What Should an Industrial AI Forward Deployed Engineer (FDE) Practice?

Original title: 工业 AI 前线部署工程师（FDE）要练什么

FDE Times · 2026-10-31 · 6 min read

Summary

Drawing on Aitomatic's public materials and TSMC's Phoenix AI Forward Deployed Engineer posting, this article argues that industrial AI deployment work lies mostly outside the model: eliciting expert knowledge from process and equipment engineers, linking data across equipment, recipes, lots, and yield, and measuring errors by defect class. Aitomatic's self-reported 94% accuracy could still mean 144 misclassifications per shift, so FDEs should build human-handoff branches and publish confusion matrices rather than rely on headline accuracy. Software engineering fundamentals remain the entry ticket; domain knowledge is what to catch up on.

Key points

  • Deployment difficulty in factory AI is knowledge elicitation, not the model itself.
  • TSMC's Phoenix FDE role still requires solid software engineering and agile fundamentals.
  • 94% accuracy can hide 144 errors per shift; analyze by defect category.
  • First-week task: trace data links across equipment, recipe, lot, process state, quality, and yield.
  • Deployment jobs may flow through system integrators like ITOCHU Techno-Solutions and FPT, not only AI companies.

Aitomatic has no public FDE job postings, but the company's materials and TSMC Phoenix's hiring already point to what to practice.

Key takeaways at a glance

  • Aitomatic's public materials put capturing expert knowledge at the start of the process, while its underlying models are released as open source.
  • The company's self-reported 94% could still mean 144 misclassifications per shift, so you need to ask where those errors fall.
  • TSMC Phoenix's FDE role still requires solid software fundamentals; don't drop your engineering skills to study semiconductors only.

"Confident wrong answers are dangerous on the fab floor." That sentence doesn't come from a safety manual; it's printed on the front page of Aitomatic's website. The company sells what it calls "Digital Engineers" to physical industries.

For anyone who wants to become a Forward Deployed Engineer (FDE), this sentence is more valuable than many job descriptions. No public FDE job posting from Aitomatic has been found, so any portrayal of that role at the company should be treated as inference.

Even so, the public materials about this company point in a fairly clear direction. In factory AI, the hard part of deployment likely lies outside the model: extracting knowledge from engineers' heads, connecting it to production data, and then proving the system knows when it might be wrong. If this is the field you're targeting, these are the things to practice.

An open-source base model doesn't tell the whole story

According to a Meta case study, Aitomatic's open-source semiconductor model SemiKong is built on Llama 3.1 70B. On its own, this doesn't reveal where the difficulty lies, because tuning a base model for semiconductors can itself be very labor-intensive.

A clearer clue lies in how outsiders describe the company. Meta writes that Aitomatic helps semiconductor companies consolidate and scale their deep expertise through Domain Expert Agents (DXA).

A 2024 case analysis on the Zilliz blog introduced the OpenSSA architecture, i.e., small specialized agents, whose process begins with capturing knowledge from process engineers and equipment experts.

Anyone can download an open-source base model, but the experience in factory engineers' heads can only be obtained on site. This is precisely why it can be inferred that this is where deployment work gets hard.

So the first task is likely a conversation, not a line of code. If you've never deliberately practiced this skill, start here.

Imagine you ask an equipment engineer: "When do you shut down the machine?" He answers: "When a signal looks a bit off." That answer isn't usable yet, and pressing for general procedures rarely helps.

Instead, ask him about the last three shutdowns: which signal looked off, off compared to what, was there ever a time it looked off but they kept running, and who had the authority to decide? Each such story gives you a draft rule and a test case drawn from a real scenario.

Phoenix reveals where the bottleneck is

Why put so much effort into expert knowledge? Zilliz's article uses TSMC as an example: even with the same technology, results still vary across fab projects, and the plant in Phoenix, Arizona struggled due to a lack of on-site expertise.

This bottleneck isn't limited to the US. In a VnEconomy article published on March 13, 2025 around AISC 2025, Aitomatic's CEO's view was outlined: Vietnam still lacks AI experts and highly skilled semiconductor engineers. The two situations differ greatly, but the key in both is people with specialized expertise.

Notably, the AI Forward Deployed Engineer position posted by TSMC Arizona is also located in Phoenix. Don't rush to treat these two facts as cause and effect. What's worth reading closely is one requirement in the posting: a solid understanding of software engineering fundamentals and agile development methodologies.

One requirement doesn't represent the whole posting, but it's enough to draw one piece of advice: to get into factory AI, you don't need to abandon your current field and learn semiconductors from scratch. Software engineering is still the entry ticket, while factory knowledge is the part you need to fill in as quickly as possible—at least enough to talk with the people who operate the machines.

The six words on the homepage are a data exercise

Aitomatic's homepage describes the context of semiconductor manufacturing with six elements: equipment, process recipe, lot, process state, quality, and yield. Through an engineer's eyes, this is almost your task brief for your first week at a customer site.

Imagine the yield of a certain lot drops, and an engineer asks the agent why. To answer this question, the system must know which equipment the lot went through, which recipe it ran, what the process state was at the time, and which run the quality results correspond to.

Now suppose the lot ID in the quality system doesn't match the one in the equipment logs. Then no matter how good the model is, the answer is built on two incorrectly linked records—and it will still sound very confident.

So the first thing to do is: pick a real problem, map out its data path, and check every join before touching the prompt.

94% could still mean 144 errors per shift

Aitomatic published one of its results in the semiconductor field: classifying 2,400 defects per shift with 94% accuracy. This is the company's own claim, and there are no independent evals yet, so treat it as a starting point for questions, not a conclusion.

The arithmetic is simple: if this rate applies across the whole shift, 6% of 2,400 is 144 misclassifications. The 94% figure says nothing about where those 144 errors fall.

Suppose 100 defects in a shift are of a type that could ruin an entire lot. In theory, even if the system misclassifies all 100 of them, accuracy is still 94%; if all 144 errors fall on less harmful defects, accuracy is also 94%.

The same number could mean either major risk or a useful tool; only an analysis broken down by defect category can distinguish the two.

At the customer site, your work starts exactly here. Ask engineers which defect types must never be missed, measure those types separately, and design a branch that hands uncertain cases to a human instead of guessing. The warning about the fab floor at the start of this article, written as code, is precisely such a branch.

The job may not be at Aitomatic alone

In February 2025, Aitomatic announced that its DXA Factory platform was selected by ITOCHU Techno-Solutions and FPT for global deployment, with FPT targeting industrial AI. If this model expands, a large share of deployment work may flow through system integrators.

This is still inference, but it suggests a broader way to look for jobs: don't just search for the "FDE" title on AI company websites.

Also read job postings from integrators and factories, looking for real signals: whether you would work directly with process or equipment engineers, who maintains the system after go-live, and whether production data falls within the job responsibilities.

The same announcement describes the final stage of the DXA lifecycle as "production deployment with continuous optimization." Treat that sentence as a resume template: a single line saying "built agents for a factory" carries little information; spelling out whose rules you extracted, what types of defects you measured, and what you fixed after go-live is what tells the full story of the job.

In interviews, you can also turn a question back on them: "What happened the last time your system gave a confidently wrong answer?" How they answer will tell you where the real work lies.

Demos can be won with fluent answers. On a fab floor, the bar is far harsher: the shift engineer still trusts your system the first time it says "I'm not sure."
