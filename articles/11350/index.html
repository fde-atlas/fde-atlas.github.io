FDE vs AI Engineer: Same Tools, Different Hard Problem (2026)

Vibe Engines · 2026-07-31 · 12 min read

Summary

This article compares AI engineers and forward deployed engineers (FDEs), arguing their technical toolboxes are nearly identical — retrieval, agents, evals, guardrails — but the hard problems differ: AI engineers optimize a system, while FDEs deliver outcomes inside a customer's organization, facing data access, security review, and stakeholder trust. Titles are merging, with Applied AI Engineer and Agent Engineer roles growing fastest. The FDE's signature skill is negotiated evals: defining "correct" with domain experts who disagree, illustrated step-by-step using Cohen's kappa (70% raw agreement is only ~0.39). FDE exits skew toward product and founding roles.

Key points

  • Same toolbox, different hard problem: system optimization vs customer organizational outcomes
  • Negotiated evals are the FDE's key skill — experts who disagree define correctness
  • Cohen's kappa example: 70% raw expert agreement is only ~0.39 chance-corrected
  • Hybrid titles (Applied AI Engineer, Agent Engineer) are merging fastest; postings grew 729% YoY
  • FDE advantages: pattern volume across enterprises; exits skew to product and founding

These two open the same tabs. Retrieval, agents, evals, context windows, latency budgets. The difference is what stands between them and success: for the AI engineer it is the system; for the FDE it is the customer’s organisation. And the titles are actively merging.

Where they overlap — which is most of the toolbox

Start with the honest part: the technical surface is nearly identical. Both roles ship retrieval over messy documents, agents that drive a multi-step workflow, tool schemas, guardrails, latency and cost budgets, and evals that decide whether any of it is good enough. Anthropic’s forward-deployed job description lists MCP servers, sub-agents and agent skills as deliverables10 — a list that would sit unchanged in an AI engineer JD.

Observability

So anyone telling you FDEs are “less technical AI people” is describing a pre-sales role, not this one. The split is not depth of AI knowledge. It is what the AI is embedded in.

An AI engineer owns a system that many users touch: make retrieval better, make the agent more reliable, make it cheaper, ship it behind a flag. The hard problem is technical, and progress is measurable in an offline eval suite.

A forward deployed engineer owns an outcome inside one customer: get access to the data, agree what “correct” means with the people who will be blamed if it is wrong, deploy where their security team allows, and get the workflow to change. The hard problem is usually organisational, and progress is measured in adoption.

Ask both roles to describe their hardest recent problem. An AI engineer tells you about a retrieval failure mode or a cost cliff. An FDE tells you about six weeks of not being allowed to touch the data, and what they built in the meantime to keep momentum.

Where the titles are merging: Applied AI Engineer

The market is converging on hybrid titles faster than the job boards can keep up. This is where the early-mover advantage is.

Watch the names. Applied AI Engineer, Agent Engineer, Forward Deployed AI Engineer and AI Solutions Engineer all describe the same emerging job: an AI engineer who is pointed at a specific customer. Anthropic’s forward-deployed role sits under Applied AI.10 Sierra hires “Agent Engineers” whose day is building and tuning customer-specific agents.28

AI engineer, customer-pointed

Usually a builder FDE with an AI-heavy mandate at a lab or model provider. Interview loops mix real coding with case and empathy rounds. If the AI work is what draws you and the customer work is acceptable, this is the sweet spot.

The narrower, newer one

Builds and tunes agents against a customer’s workflows — tools, guardrails, escalation paths, failure handling — and owns their behaviour in production.28 Interviews lean heavily on incremental coding and debugging planted failures rather than algorithms.

The other direction

Palantir’s non-engineering half of forward-deployed work: owns problem framing and stakeholders, may write little production code.13 If you are an AI engineer, this is not the adjacent move — it trades code for organisational leverage.

The strategic read: the merged titles are where hiring is growing fastest and where candidate supply is thinnest, because they demand a combination — frontier-AI fluency plus enterprise delivery nerve — that few people have deliberately built. Postings for the family grew 729% year over year on one index.1

The skill an FDE has that most AI engineers do not

If you take one thing from this page: negotiated evals.

An AI engineer usually gets to define correctness. You pick the benchmark, you curate the golden set, you decide the threshold. An FDE almost never does. The customer’s domain experts define correct, and they disagree with each other — two senior claims adjusters will label the same case differently, and both will be adamant. Turning that into a gate a contract can rest on is the highest-leverage thing an FDE does, and it is the most-cited hard skill across frontier-lab job descriptions.

with each otherfirst — if humans agree only 70% of the time, no model is getting to 95%, and now you have a defensible target. Resolve the disagreements into a written rubric. That rubric becomes the LLM-judge prompt, the golden set becomes the regression suite, and the CI gate becomes the acceptance criterion in the statement of work.

The artefact is as much a commercial document as a technical one.

From “it should be accurate” to a CI gate

The eval track on this site walks the full path: pick metrics for a fuzzy goal, label a golden set, calibrate a judge against human grades, then wire the gate.

The FDE’s structural advantage is pattern volume. Sit inside twenty enterprises and you accumulate a map of which problems recur, which are worth productising, and what customers will actually pay for. That is founder fuel, and it is why the exit list skews entrepreneurial. The AI engineer’s structural advantage is compounding depth in a field where depth is scarce and expensive.

Pick AI engineeringif your best days end with a metric moving and you resent context-switching.

Pick FDEif your best days end with a person’s work getting easier and you are bored by a quarter spent on one system. Both are AI careers; only one of them makes you good at enterprises — and, by most current hiring reports, FDE is the one with far more openings than experienced candidates.

The picture: one toolbox, two loops

Worked example: negotiating an eval, step by step

Take the page’s recipe: two experts, 40 real cases, labelled independently. Say it is a claims queue with two labels, approve or deny. Expert A approves 24 cases and expert B approves 22. These counts are illustrative; the arithmetic is not.

StepWhat you doArithmeticResult
1Cross-tabulate the labelsboth approve 17, both deny 11, only A approves 7, only B approves 517 + 11 + 7 + 5 = 40
2Raw agreement(17 + 11) ÷ 4070%
3Agreement expected by chance0.60 × 0.55 + 0.40 × 0.4551%
4Chance-corrected (Cohen’s kappa)(0.70 − 0.51) ÷ (1 − 0.51)≈ 0.39
5Resolve the disagreements7 + 5 cases, argued case by caseA written rubric
6Re-check with the rubricboth label 20 fresh cases; say they agree on 1890%
7Turn it into a gaterubric → judge prompt; resolved cases → golden setA target in the SOW, set below the 90% humans reach

Step 4 is the step most people skip. Two experts who approve most cases will agree often just by chance, so 70% raw is a kappa of only about 0.39: far from strong agreement, and no solid ceiling. Step 6 is where the rubric earns its keep. If the experts now agree on 18 of 20, you have shown that the task can be defined, and you have a human figure to set the gate against. An AI engineer usually skips steps 1 to 6 because they own the definition. For an FDE those steps are most of the job, and the result goes into the contract.

Common mistakes when crossing between the roles

  • Defining correctness yourself at a customerThe AI-engineer habit is to pick the benchmark and the threshold. Do that at a customer and the gate is yours, not theirs, so the first time it fails a case their expert cares about, they reject the whole thing. Their experts must agree to the rubric before it gates anything.
  • Calling an offline metric successA deployment can pass every eval and still change nothing about how people work. For an FDE, a good eval only gets you permission to ship. Adoption is the result. Report both.
  • Taking raw agreement as the ceilingWith two labels and unbalanced classes, 70% agreement can be mostly luck, as step 4 above shows. Report agreement corrected for chance next to the raw figure before anyone negotiates a target from it.
  • Finding out the deploy target lastAn air-gapped enclave cannot call a hosted model API. A VPC may block outbound traffic altogether. Ask where it will run in the first meeting, because the answer can rule out your model, your vector store and your observability stack.
  • Skipping the job-shape questionsIn some roles travel is 25–50% by contract, and some drift into services work. Before you accept, ask for the share of time spent on code and the travel expectation in writing.

Where this shows up in production and interviews

  • One RAG system, two jobsThe same retrieval stack ships as a product feature behind a flag, tuned against your own eval suite. It also ships into a customer’s VPC, gated by a rubric their reviewers signed. Same code, different person deciding “correct”.
  • The eval question in interviewsBoth loops ask “how would you evaluate this?”. The AI-engineer answer names metrics and a golden set. The FDE answer adds who labels it, how you measure their agreement, and who signs off on the threshold.
  • Reading a postingWords like travel, statement of work, stakeholders, on-prem and customer environment point to FDE work. Inference, serving, evaluation infrastructure and latency point to AI engineering. A posting with both is the merged role.
  • Moving from AI engineer to FDEThe technical half carries over. What to show is the customer half: a write-up of an eval you negotiated with people who disagreed, and something you deployed into an environment you did not control.

Quick answers

What is the difference between a forward deployed engineer and an AI engineer?

They use nearly the same toolbox — retrieval, agents, tool schemas, guardrails, evals — but solve different hard problems. An AI engineer owns a system many users touch and optimises it technically. A forward deployed engineer owns an outcome inside one customer, where the blockers are usually data access, security review and stakeholder trust rather than model quality.

Is Applied AI Engineer the same as a forward deployed engineer?

Usually yes. “Applied AI Engineer” is the frontier-lab framing of forward-deployed work — Anthropic’s forward deployed engineering sits under Applied AI. “Agent Engineer” is the narrower variant focused on building and operating customer-specific agents, and “Forward Deployed AI Engineer” is the same job with the older label.

Can an AI engineer become a forward deployed engineer?

Yes, and the technical half transfers completely. What has to be built is the customer half: scoping an underspecified ask, negotiating what “correct” means with domain experts who disagree, deploying into a VPC or air-gapped environment you do not control, and demoing to non-engineers. Negotiated evals is the highest-leverage of these.

Do forward deployed engineers do real AI work or just integration?

Both, in builder roles. Anthropic’s forward-deployed job description lists MCP servers, sub-agents and agent skills as deliverables, and evals are typically the contractual acceptance test. Integration work is real and substantial, but describing the role as integration-only fits the pre-sales-shaped roughly 30% of postings, not the builder majority.
