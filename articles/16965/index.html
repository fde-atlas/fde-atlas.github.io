Insight 05

Delta/Echo・Gravel Road to Superhighway・Implementation in Japan

The FDE (Forward Deployed Engineer) model, invented by Palantir Technologies in the mid-2000s, has now become the standard go-to-market model for companies seriously pursuing enterprise AI, including OpenAI, Anthropic, Google Cloud, Salesforce, and Databricks. This article systematically explains how Palantir's original model is designed and why it works, covering its origins, organization, product feedback loop, and case studies in Japan.

Palantir's FDE is a hybrid role of engineer, consultant, and PM, whose job is to be embedded directly within customer organizations to build production-ready software. Without waiting for requirements documents, they build on the spot against the customer's raw data and business workflows—the essence of this work, which may look like "premium custom development," lies in feeding back the lessons learned in the field as generalized features of the company's own platform.

Palantir's results bear this out. Q3 2025 revenue was $1.181 billion (+63% year-over-year), US commercial segment was $397 million (+121%), and the Rule of 40 was 114%. The structure of folding the "FDEs working in the field" experience back into the product to improve capital efficiency underpins this growth.

Palantir was founded in May 2003 by Peter Thiel, Alex Karp, Stephen Cohen, Joe Lonsdale, and Nathan Gettings. Its first customer was a US intelligence agency, and there were two challenges—

  • Due to confidentiality, customers could not clearly articulate "what they needed."
  • Neither the data nor the business workflows could be fully captured in advance requirements documents.

The solution chosen was to send the engineers themselves into the field. Shyam Sankar, the company's first FDE (13th employee, joined February 2006, now President and CTO), is known as the person who coined "Forward Deployed" (a military term meaning "frontline deployment").

In Sankar's own words: "Good ideas don't come while eating strawberries in Palo Alto. They are born in the combat rooms of Djibouti and on the factory floors of Detroit." The FDE model is a direct translation of this "field-first epistemology" into organizational design.

The definition in Palantir's official blog ("A Day in the Life of a Palantir Forward Deployed Software Engineer") is clear:

"While a traditional software engineer (Dev) builds a single feature used by many customers, an FDSE focuses on implementing many features for a single customer."

Palantir describes the FDE's scope of responsibility as "close to a startup CTO," and publishes that small teams take on high-stakes projects end-to-end, travel ratio is about 25%, and base salary is $135K–$200K.

Palantir assigns two types of talent in pairs to every deployment project.

  • Delta: The FDE who writes production-quality code. Technically completes the solution.
  • Echo: The Deployment Strategist. Leans toward being a domain-side insider, closer to a PM, and may not write code. Finds the right problem to solve.

Team size is roughly 3–10 people combined for Delta + Echo, and the field leader is called the "Commanding Officer" (Apoorv Agrawal, ex-Palantir FDE). "Echo discovers the problem, Delta builds the solution"—this pair operates as a mini startup embedded within the customer's organization.

FDEs are also clearly distinguished from adjacent roles:

  • vs. consultants: Consultants deliver documents. FDEs deliver production code that keeps running after they leave.
  • vs. SEs and custom development: Custom development builds to spec. FDEs go create the spec together with the customer.
  • vs. pre-sales/solutions engineers: Pre-sales stops at demos and does not write production code on the customer's infrastructure.
  • vs. core "Devs" (PDEs): PDEs build generalized features for many customers. FDEs build deeply for a single customer and send those patterns back to the Devs.

For those who want a more detailed breakdown of the differences from SEs and consultants, please also see What's the Difference Between an FDE and an IT Consultant? The Role of the Implementation Owner Who Delivers Results in AI Adoption.

What nearly all imitators of Palantir's model overlook is the productization loop. Bob McGrew, OpenAI's former Chief Research Officer (and former Palantir executive), expresses it this way:

"The FDEs in the field lay down a gravel road in the direction the product should go. And the role of the central product team is to look at it, figure out how to generalize it to the next five or ten companies, and turn that gravel road into a paved superhighway."

Marty Cagan (Silicon Valley Product Group) called this "backpropagation for humans." Every FDE project is a "learning signal" to the platform, and the platform side continuously generalizes it—without this loop, you just become a premium consulting firm.

Ted Mabrey, Palantir's Global Head of Commercial, sharply points this out: "Fake FDEs are literally doing what people misunderstood us to be doing. They've just internalized SI integration costs as FDEs and gained no long-term leverage whatsoever" ("Sorry, that isn't an FDE").

Where FDEs lay down their "gravel roads," there is a clear product architecture.

  • Gotham (post-9/11 era): For defense, intelligence, and counterterrorism. Cross-domain integration of classified data.
  • Foundry (launched 2016): A data integration "OS" for commercial and public sectors. Its core is the Ontology (a semantic layer that serves as the organization's digital twin, linking datasets to real-world objects, attributes, relations, and actions).
  • Apollo (productized in 2021): A control plane for continuous deployment across cloud, on-premises, and classified environments.
  • AIP (April 2023): An AI platform that runs LLMs on top of the Ontology. It integrates "k-LLM" (multi-model, model-agnostic) with evals, agents, governance, and a natural language workflow builder.

The "nice-to-have features" that FDEs discover in the field are absorbed as general features of Foundry and AIP, so the next customer can start from a paved road instead of re-laying the gravel road. This is the essence of scale.

Palantir's play in the generative AI era was not a new product but a new sales motion. The AIP Bootcamp, as Ted Mabrey explains, is an immersive session where customers pair with FDEs to get a use case running on the customer's own data in 1 to 5 days.

While traditional enterprise pilots took 1 to 3 months, the Bootcamp compresses this to within 5 days. As of Q4 2024, more than 1,300 bootcamps had been held, and analyst commentary cites a conversion rate of ~75% (FinancialContent). Karp's philosophy is clear: "deliver value first, then monetize."

From 2023 to 2025, three structural forces acted simultaneously.

  • Commoditization of foundation models: Value shifted from the model layer to the implementation layer. Sankar's thesis: "The model layer is largely solved. The real work is in implementation."
  • Enterprise AI PoCs overwhelmingly fail: According to MIT Project NANDA's "The GenAI Divide: State of AI in Business 2025" (July 2025), only 5% of integrated AI pilots deliver meaningful P&L results. In a16z's words, "Enterprise AI adoption is like a grandmother buying an iPhone. She wants to use it, but she needs someone to set it up for her."
  • Economic stickiness of embedded systems: Palantir stock recovered more than 1,700% from about $6 at the end of 2022, and the FDE-driven sales motion underpinned that recovery.

Actual hiring trends are dramatic. According to Salesforce's official blog citing analyses by Indeed and the Financial Times, FDE job postings grew by more than +800% from January to September 2025. a16z's Joe Schmidt IV called the FDE role the "hottest job in startups." OpenAI, Anthropic, Google Cloud (with a career ladder from FDE II to IV, base salary $183K–$265K), Salesforce (announced a 1,000-person FDE organization), Databricks, Ramp, Rippling, Intercom, Cohere, Scale AI, Adobe, and many others have all launched FDE organizations.

For another angle on the structure that stalls at the PoC stage, also see Why AI PoCs stall and AI goes unused, and how to avoid it.

To the question "Does Palantir's model really work in Japan?", a concrete answer already exists.

  • Palantir Technologies Japan K.K.: Established in November 2019 as a 50/50 joint venture between Palantir and SOMPO Holdings. Commitment of $150M; the first CEO was Koichi Narasaki (former SOMPO Group CDO). In Alex Karp's words: "The combination of decades of industry expertise from the front lines of industry with the right software will be the primary driver of future economic outperformance."
  • SOMPO: Added a direct investment of $500M; as of August 2025, more than 8,000 employees use Palantir daily, and AI agents for underwriting operations are expected to deliver improvements on the order of $10 million per year. SOMPO Care operates 1,100 locations and roughly 85,000 long-term care recipients on Foundry. CEO Mikio Okumura: "The importance of Palantir Foundry continues to grow across all of our business sectors."
  • Fujitsu: Since June 2020, the exclusive Flagship Technology Partner of Palantir Japan. Invested $50M. In December 2023, a strategic alliance integrated Foundry into Fujitsu Uvance, and in August 2025, a contract for the licensing of domestic and international distribution rights for AIP. Fujitsu has published its own use cases, such as maintenance parts management with Foundry and optimal staffing of its roughly 8,000 in-house engineers.
  • Yamato HD: In July 2020, adopted Foundry as part of its "YAMATO NEXT100" DX transformation.
  • Sumitomo Corporation: In March 2026, signed an AIP license agreement with group company Ascend SC. For SCM in its oil country tubular goods business, it will expand the 2025 European pilot to all sites from fiscal 2026, followed by a global rollout.
  • Kanagawa Prefecture (November 2021): The first Japanese local government to adopt Foundry. Used for COVID-19 data integration and EBPM.
  • Ishikawa Prefecture / Noto Peninsula earthquake response: Palantir Japan and NTT East jointly integrated disaster victim data, which had been scattered across 15 systems, into Foundry. For approximately 120,000 residents, they helped identify evacuation destinations for more than 62,000 people. Part of the Digital Agency's "Public-Private Disaster Prevention DX Promotion Council."
  • AIP Bootcamp in Tokyo: The AIP Expo in July 2023 (over 30 companies, 100+ executives), and a Bootcamp city in October of the same year. In July 2025, the AIP Developer Tier was also opened to Japan and South Korea.

Mr. Narazaki's remarks (Jiji Press, November 2, 2020) are suggestive as an expression of the FDE philosophy in Japanese business vocabulary: "Palantir can achieve the same effect as a data lake in about a week... integrating and analyzing data scattered across an organization's systems and delivering solutions to revenue improvement and strategic issues in an extremely short period" and "When we provide a system to a customer, there is only one thing we ask: 'What is your greatest management challenge?'"

The biggest pitfall for Japanese business readers is misreading the FDE as "on-site SES staff" or "premium consulting." While the two resemble each other in surface-level working style, they are structurally and decisively different.

  • SES sells "engineer man-hours." When the project ends, the relationship ends.
  • The FDE captures "product signals." Patterns gained in the field are fed back into the company's own platform, and value compounds over time.

Ted Mabrey points out the following: Companies that grant the FDE title while denying field engineers authority over the platform roadmap are, in the end, just rebuilding a consulting firm. Industry analysis is also cited as finding that "72% of companies using the FDE title do not grant architecture authority over the core product."

The individual-level skill requirements for making the FDE model work are detailed in The 3 Skills Required for FDEs: Driving Field AI with Implementation Ability, Business Understanding, and Adaptability.

The necessary conditions for the FDE model to work, as identified by a16z, SVPG (Marty Cagan), Flybridge, and Barry McCardel, ex-Palantir Deployment Strategist and current CEO of Hex, are the following four.

  • High-ACV enterprise/government customers: The model is not cost-effective unless deals exceed roughly seven figures in annual contract value. Palantir's median ACV is about $5M.
  • A platform product capable of absorbing field signals: Not merely a development company—there must be a product organization responsible for generalization.
  • High hiring standards: The budget and reputation to maintain a "level capable of working at Google / Facebook."
  • A culture of delegating authority to the field: What Sankar calls "radical deference to the field." In Japan's hierarchical, headquarters-driven culture, this tends to be the biggest bottleneck.

When companies that do not meet these four conditions adopt the FDE title anyway, they end up—as Mabrey says—as "FDE-in-name-only."

  • Structurally high cost: Barry McCardel points out that "it requires a lavish budget and high hiring standards, and doesn't work without F500-scale customers or large government agencies."
  • Career attrition and burnout: FDEs face 25–50% travel ratios, context switching across industries, and isolation from their own engineering culture. On Glassdoor, "catastrophic WLB," "political dynamics," and "rapid hiring and firing" appear frequently.
  • The wrong strategy for many startups: At low ACV, the FDE is a loss-making machine.
  • Procurement/regulatory risk: The "Land and Expand" approach with the UK MOD became the target of legal criticism (David Allen Green) as procurement that bypassed competitive bidding.
  • Valuation risk: As of the end of 2025, a P/S ratio of about 98x and a forward PER of about 226x. A price that assumes years of flawless execution.

What the Palantir Japan case demonstrates is that the FDE model can work for Japanese companies too—but only when accompanied by SOMPO-level commitment (a 50/50 JV, $500M direct investment, 8,000 daily users) and delegation of authority to the field.

For the many Japanese companies that cannot choose to build a Palantir-class platform in-house, there are two realistic options:

  • Partner with Palantir Japan or its partners (e.g., Fujitsu): Ride on an already-completed FDE operation. In exchange, accept vendor lock-in and high ACV.
  • Partner with a small firm that has adopted the FDE philosophy: One to a few field-embedded engineers who help design, implement, and embed AI tools specialized to your business in a short period. This can work even at low ACV.
