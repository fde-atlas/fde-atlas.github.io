Will the FDE role last? Lessons from the PLG hype cycle

FDE Times · 2026-11-23 · 7 min read

Summary

Drawing on the product-led growth (PLG) hype cycle as precedent, this article asks which parts of the FDE role will survive if the current boom cools. It dissects three growth figures (1,165%, over 1000%, 350%) that come from different datasets and periods, notes the 1,165% rests on a sample of roughly 1,000 postings, and cites a survey showing only about 1 in 13 to 25 FDE-style jobs carries the exact title. The PLG lesson: sales survived by owning large enterprise deals, and FDE demand today is similarly tied to large AI contracts. Roles that are mostly configuration, consulting, or sales support under the FDE label are most likely to disappear, while engineers who convert one-off customer fixes into core product improvements will endure.

Key points

  • Three FDE growth figures come from different datasets and periods, so no single number is reliable.
  • The 1,165% figure rests on a thin base of about 1,000 postings.
  • Only roughly 1 in 13 to 25 FDE-style jobs uses the exact title.
  • PLG did not kill sales; it concentrated sales on large enterprise deals, mirroring today's FDE demand.
  • Engineers who turn customer-specific work into product improvements are the ones who will last.

The PLG precedent suggests work at large customers will last, and the engineers who stay will be the ones who turn one-off fixes into product.

In brief

  • The three FDE growth figures (1,165%, more than 1000%, 350%) come from three datasets and three periods. Only about 1 in every 13 to 25 FDE-style jobs carries the exact title.
  • PLG did not replace sales teams: sales kept the large enterprise contracts. Today's demand for FDEs is likewise tied to large AI deals.
  • When the title cools, the configuration and consulting work carrying the FDE label is most likely to fall away. The engineers who stay turn work for individual customers into value for the core product.

Fortune reports that job postings for FDEs between January and August 2026 rose by more than 1000% on the same period a year earlier. A curve that steep reflects real demand, and it also invites overuse of the title.

The software industry has seen a trend like this before, with product-led growth (PLG). When the PLG fever broke, sales teams did not vanish. They held their place in large enterprise contracts, as TechCrunch noted in 2023.

For engineers in FDE roles, or aiming for one, the question worth asking is which part of the job will remain once the excitement around the title fades.

The PLG precedent points to a fairly clear answer: the work at large customers that the product cannot do on its own. That is good news, provided you build a career on the work rather than the name.

Three growth figures, three ways of measuring

The most quoted figure is growth of 1,165%. It comes from Bloomberry’s analysis of Revealera data, comparing January to October 2024 with January to October 2025, and the sample contains only about 1,000 postings.

Fortune’s figure of more than 1000% uses a different dataset and a different period. Although the two look similar, they are separate measurements, not one number repeated twice. Paraform, counting on its own recruiting platform, arrives at a much lower figure.

Source Comparison period Growth
Bloomberry (Revealera data, sample of about 1,000 postings) Jan-Oct 2024 vs Jan-Oct 2025 1,165%
Fortune Jan-Aug 2026 vs same period a year earlier More than 1000%
Paraform (own platform data) Q1 2025 vs Q1 2026 350%

All three say the market is rising. But the steepness depends on who is counting, where and from what starting point, which is reason enough to be skeptical of any slide that offers a single number as proof.

1,165% rests on a very thin base

Very large percentage gains usually come with a small starting point, and Scaler points out that the 1,165% case is no different. Run the arithmetic: growth of 1,165% means the number of postings at the end of the period is 12.65 times the number at the start.

When the entire sample is only about 1,000 postings, the starting point is small however it is split. Arithmetically, on a base like that, a few companies opening a few dozen extra positions is enough to send the curve almost vertical.

There is a bigger problem: are we counting titles or counting work? According to a Plank survey cited by Scaler, only about 1,000-1,400 postings worldwide use the exact FDE title.

Include jobs in which engineers work directly with customers under other names, and the figure rises to about 18,000-25,000. Divide one estimate by the other and only about 1 in every 13 to 25 FDE-style jobs carries that exact name.

The two curves should therefore be read separately. The title curve sits on a thin base, so it could fall quickly when the trend cools. The work curve is likely to follow its own rhythm, slower and harder to reverse.

PLG did not kill sales; it pushed sales toward big deals

What is worth learning from PLG is how it settled. TechCrunch concluded that sales still had a role, especially in winning large enterprise contracts.

Justin Bauer of Amplitude used Miro as an example. He pointed out that Miro’s million-dollar contracts were plainly not self-serve; a sales team closed them.

The example shows a division of labor. Self-serve products handle the simple deals, while large and complex deals still need people. Product-led companies did not abandon self-serve; they added a human layer on top of it.

What is FDE demand resting on?

PostHog attributes today’s demand for FDEs to large AI deals and to a simple reality: most people, especially leaders at traditional companies, are still not fully convinced that AI is useful. Part of the boom, then, is tied to the conditions of selling AI right now.

Once business leaders are more convinced, the persuasion part of the job may get lighter. But if the PLG lesson holds, large and complex deals will still need people, and that is where FDEs work.

Which parts of the FDE label are likely to fall away?

PostHog observes that the title is being used loosely: in some places an FDE is closer to a technical consultant or a sales engineer. First Round draws the line more sharply still: understanding customers deeply and deploying or configuring the product for them is important work, but it is not forward deployed engineering.

If the trend cools, this is the most exposed part: positions that use the FDE label for configuration, consulting or sales support. The FDE label will probably disappear from those postings, while the work continues to be hired under familiar names such as technical consultant or sales engineer.

What remains is something PostHog describes without any need for the hype: an engineer embedded with a customer’s team to close the gap between what the product can do and what the customer needs. Entrepreneur adds a yardstick: the question shifts from “has it been deployed successfully?” to “does it create measurable value?”

The trap that could devalue FDEs early

One risk could devalue the profession even while demand holds. First Round names it directly: FDEs can produce a string of random features without making the core product any better.

While the trend is hot, few people count how much code an FDE has written that works for only one customer. Once the boom passes, the question of each role’s measurable value will be harder to dodge.

Take a hypothetical case. Three large customers each need the product connected to a different internal system. One FDE writes three separate connectors and moves on.

Another FDE notices that all three need the same kind of data mapping and authentication, and writes a proposal for the product team to build a shared integration layer. The next time, a fourth customer takes a few days instead of a few weeks.

The two have the same title and both work at customers. But only the second avoids the one-off feature trap First Round warns about, because the work done for each customer has fed back into a stronger core product.

Where to place your bets

If you are job hunting, use the gap between postings with the exact title and the real number of jobs right away. Do not just type “Forward Deployed Engineer” into the search box. Search by job description: working inside the customer’s team, getting the product running in production in their environment, whatever the position is called.

When reading a posting, look at what most of the role’s time is spent on. If the description is mostly product configuration, pre-sales demos or consulting, it is the kind of role borrowing the FDE label, and it is likely to wobble when the trend passes.

Postings that mention enterprise customers, production deployment, measurable outcomes and a feedback loop to the product team suggest a role tied to the work most likely to remain.

On your CV, write so the reader sees both halves of the job: what measurable value you created at which customers, and which part of it became a shared feature.

A line such as “built production integrations for three banking customers and extracted the repeated parts into a shared module” carries more weight than ten lines listing technologies. It answers the question hiring managers will ask when the market cools.

When PLG lost its shine, sales teams stayed on with the large enterprise contracts, and FDE work at large customers is likely to hold on in the same way. Engineers who are good at that work will hardly care whether the title in job postings rises or falls.
