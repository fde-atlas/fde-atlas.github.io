The Missing README Book Review: How to Read It as an FDE

Original title: The Missing README 书评：FDE 怎么读

FDE Times · 2026-11-11 · 4 min read

Summary

This review of The Missing README (Chris Riccomini and Dmitriy Ryaboy, No Starch Press, 2021, 288 pages) argues the book is valuable for FDEs because every new client project puts them back in the new-hire position. Rather than reading cover to cover, the author proposes a reading plan matched to project stages: chapter 2 before onboarding, chapter 10 before the first big feature, the operable-code sections once code runs on client infrastructure, and chapter 9 before launch. It also highlights Greg Wilson's critique that the book omits privacy and legal responsibility, a gap FDEs handling client data must fill themselves.

  • Recommended order: chapters 2, 10, operable code sections, then chapter 9.
  • Chapter 2's key lesson: state clearly what you don't know yet.
  • Design docs should start with a client-confirmed problem definition.

Key Takeaways at a Glance

  • This book is written for engineers just entering the profession, and every new project puts an FDE back in the newcomer's seat.
  • Read it according to project rhythm: start with Chapters 2 and 10, then the sections on operable code, and finish with Chapter 9.
  • Greg Wilson points out that the book doesn't discuss privacy or legal liability, and that gap is one FDEs need to fill themselves.

With every new project, a Forward Deployed Engineer (FDE) becomes a new engineer all over again. The codebase is unfamiliar, the review process is unfamiliar, and there's an extra manager who doesn't appear on your own company's org chart.

Most career books assume you're only a newcomer once. "The Missing README: A Guide for the New Software Engineer" by Chris Riccomini and Dmitriy Ryaboy is no exception: No Starch Press published this 288-page paperback on August 4, 2021, aimed at readers just starting out.

The book's official website promises to teach you the software engineering skills your manager wishes you knew. Barnes & Noble's description is more blunt: writing code is only half the battle, because many of the skills and processes that determine success are taught by no school or bootcamp.

For those doing or aspiring to do FDE work, the book's value lies elsewhere. It's a "first week" checklist, and that first week is one you'll repeat at every customer. The faster that first week goes, the sooner you create value.

A Mentor Coffee Chat, Bound into a Book

Penguin Random House positions it as the book every tech lead wishes new engineers had read before onboarding, and Gwen Shapira compares it to having a coffee with a senior mentor.

According to Greg Wilson, the book covers everything from continuous integration, refactoring, and technical support to agile planning and working with your manager. These are professional habits, and they're exactly what an FDE has to relearn the way each customer does them.

Fourteen Chapters, But Don't Read Them in Order

The book has 14 chapters in total. The suggestion below is a reading plan, organized around the FDE work rhythm based on the publicly available chapter titles and descriptions — not a page-by-page summary.

A reasonable order: read Chapter 2 before onboarding, Chapter 10 before building your first major feature, the sections on writing operable code once your code starts running on customer infrastructure, and Chapter 9 before launch. The material on working with your manager is best read gradually throughout the project, since you have your own manager, and the customer has one too.

First Insight: Be Clear About What You Don't Know

Chapter 2's title alone, "Getting to Conscious Competence," is immediately actionable advice. Taken literally, it means being able to get things done while clearly knowing where your understanding ends. On a customer team, the most dangerous newcomer is the one who doesn't know what they're missing.

Imagine your first week at a retail chain that needs an inventory forecasting data pipeline. You draw a three-column table: understood (languages, frameworks), partially understood (how they deploy), unknown (which table is the single source of truth for inventory levels).

The third column is your list of questions for the first meeting, and it's also how you show the customer you're taking this seriously.

Second Insight: Design Documents Start With Problem Definition

Chapter 10's description is the clearest of all chapters: the design process, covering experiments, problem definition, documentation, and collaboration. At a customer, problem definition is where customer requirements discovery meets engineering, and it's where things most often go wrong.

Practical advice: before building your first major feature, write a short document that opens with a problem definition confirmed by the customer, then have at least one engineer on the customer's side review it. They'll surface constraints no one wrote down, and after you leave, they're the ones who will have to defend that design.

Third Insight: Who Answers the Alert at 2 A.M.?

Once your code is running at the customer, the material on writing operable code becomes very practical: logging, metrics, configuration, and defensive programming. Right after it comes Chapter 9, "Going On-Call."

A book for newcomers devoting an entire chapter to on-call sends a clear message: production systems will fail sooner or later, and newcomers must be prepared too.

For an FDE, these two parts should be read together, because your system runs on someone else's infrastructure and data. Back to the retail example: suppose the data pipeline fails the night before a promotion. With no logs, no metrics, and no agreement on who answers alerts, a technical incident becomes a trust incident.

Concrete steps: before launch, check what logs your code records and where metrics are sent, then agree in writing with the customer on who is on call and who can access the logs.

What the Book Doesn't Cover

Greg Wilson titled his review "What's Missing from 'The Missing README'". He points out that the book doesn't discuss privacy, algorithmic discrimination, workplace harassment, labor rights, or legal liability.

For an FDE, this gap matters even more than it does for internal engineers, because you handle the customer's real data, sometimes including the customer's customers' data. After finishing the book, add a question to your own day-one onboarding checklist: Whose data is this? What is it used for? Has the customer confirmed you may use it this way?

Who Should Read It, and What to Do Next

This book is best suited for software engineers transitioning into FDE roles, especially those who have spent years maintaining a single codebase. Experienced FDEs can skim it quickly, pausing only at Chapter 2, Chapter 10, the operable code sections, and Chapter 9.

When interviewing for an FDE role, you can borrow two questions directly from the book: Who reviews design documents? Who is on call after launch? The answers will help you think through how far your responsibilities extend before you accept an offer.

Don't just write "deployed system X" on your resume. If you wrote a design document together with customer engineers, or were on call for a system running on real data, say so explicitly and add a concrete detail, such as who on the customer's side reviewed the document.

The book's original purpose is to help you get through your first year on the job. An FDE relives that first year at every new customer, just with far less time available.
