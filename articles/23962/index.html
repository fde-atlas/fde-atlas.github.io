When the customer already has Copilot, how should the FDE position their solution?

Original title: 客户已有 Copilot，FDE 如何定位自己的方案

FDE Times · 2026-11-12 · 5 min read

Summary

This guide addresses how FDEs should respond when customers already own Copilot: don't compete on model quality. Citing MIT research, it argues AI pilots fail due to a learning gap around workflows, not weak models, and notes that buying from specialized vendors succeeds about 67% of the time. It proposes three questions (what work, who does it, where) to split tasks between Copilot and custom solutions, favoring delivery as a plugin inside Copilot rather than a new app. A claims-processing example, a five-step checklist, and common mistakes round it out.

Key points

  • MIT research: pilots fail from workflow learning gaps, not model quality.
  • Copilot handles general office work; core workflows and data outside Microsoft 365 need custom solutions.
  • Ask three questions: what work, who does it, where it happens.
  • Deliver as a plugin inside Copilot instead of forcing users into a new interface.
  • Vendor-built solutions succeed about 67% of the time, beating in-house builds.

This is the second demo. Your agent just ran smoothly, and someone on the customer's side speaks up: the company has already bought Copilot for every employee—what makes yours different?

If you argue about which model is smarter, you're almost certain to lose. The person asking voted to approve a significant purchase, and nobody wants to hear they bought the wrong thing. The question isn't hostile either. For anyone holding a budget, it's the most reasonable question there is.

So don't try to win the argument. What you need to do is state precisely: which tasks the existing tool already handles, which it can't yet, and where your solution plugs in.

The problem isn't the model

According to Fortune, MIT's GenAI Divide study found that enterprise AI pilots fail primarily because of "learning gaps" on both the tool and organizational sides, not model quality. General-purpose tools stall because they can't learn from workflows or adapt to them.

This gap is exactly where the Forward Deployed Engineer (FDE) comes in. A ZenML summary of a talk by Colin Jarvis notes that OpenAI describes its FDEs as people who sit with customers to build, deploy, and iterate on AI solutions. What you bring is an understanding of a specific workflow, and you turn it into software. Whether the model is good isn't the point.

Tensoria, a company that builds custom AI solutions, observes that Copilot is very good at summarizing, drafting, and rewriting. It suggests this division of labor: support functions go to Copilot, core workflows get custom solutions. This is the view of a party with a commercial stake, but the split is clear enough to test on the spot at the customer's site.

Before you speak, ask three questions

Microsoft offers a framework you can borrow directly. The first step of its Copilot plugin development lifecycle is to clarify the work to be done, who will use the plugin, and where. These are exactly the three questions to ask when the customer says "why can't we use the tools we already have?"

Asking what the work is separates general writing from domain work. Asking who does it clarifies whether the real users are general office staff or business teams with their own processes. Where the work happens is the question most often skipped, yet it determines the shape of the solution.

Then verify the data. Credal, another company with a commercial stake, claims Copilot can only integrate data within the Microsoft 365 boundary. Don't take this as fact. Treat it as a hypothesis and validate it on the customer's tenant: is the data for this workflow in SharePoint, in Outlook, or in an entirely different system?

Another check is data sovereignty. According to Tensoria, the first scenario where custom solutions clearly beat SaaS tools is when the customer demands data sovereignty and won't compromise. If the customer mandates that data cannot leave a particular infrastructure, the entire conversation changes.

A complete example

Imagine an insurance company where every employee already has Copilot. You're brought in to support the claims team. Every case file must be checked one by one against the policy, site photos, and customer history before approval.

Apply the three questions. What the work is: deciding whether a claim is approved, denied, or needs more documentation, according to the specific terms of each policy type. Who does it: claims adjusters, not general office staff.

Where the work happens: they communicate in Teams and Outlook, but policies and claims history are stored in the company's own policy management system.

The answer falls out naturally. Drafting emails asking customers for additional documents—Copilot does that well, and you should say so directly. Checking claims against policy terms using data stored outside Microsoft 365—that's the part that needs you.

Next comes the most critical step: don't build a new app that forces adjusters to open yet another tab. Microsoft defines a Copilot plugin as an installable package that bundles capabilities for a specific task. The package can contain declarative agents, skills, connectors, and connection info for MCP servers, while the servers themselves run independently outside the package.

So you write an MCP server that reads the policy system, deploy it on infrastructure the customer approves, and package the claims agent as a plugin. Adjusters keep asking questions in Copilot as usual. Ramp describes this approach as meeting customers in the tools they already use, rather than forcing them into an entirely new workflow.

The demo conversation might go like this:

Customer: We already have Copilot. What makes yours different? FDE: Copilot is still where your team works, and it drafts claims emails very well. What it can't do today is check case files against the terms in your policy system, because that data isn't in Microsoft 365. That part of the solution will run inside Copilot as a plugin, so adjusters won't need to learn a new tool. Customer: Why not have our IT team do this? FDE: They absolutely could, and after handover your IT team will take over and carry it forward. The question is who, in the first three months, can measure whether this agent has correctly learned the claims process.

That last answer is worth noting. According to MIT research, purchasing tools from specialized vendors and establishing partnerships yields a success rate of about 67%, far higher than building in-house. The same number also reminds you not to promise to build everything from scratch.

Mistakes that will cost you the customer

The most common mistake is disparaging Copilot. You may be technically right, but you embarrass the person who signed the contract, and they will defend their decision by dropping you.

The second is bringing out model benchmarks. If the real gap is learning workflows, winning benchmarks answers none of the customer's questions.

Equally dangerous is quoting vendor claims as fact. Credal and Tensoria both sell competing solutions, so treat their views as a checklist and verify them in the customer's real environment.

Finally, don't make users migrate to a new interface when they already live in Teams and Outlook all day. An agent that's more accurate but never opened is still a failed pilot.

Skills worth putting on your resume

When looking at FDE job postings, watch for phrases like Microsoft 365 extensibility, MCP, or integration with customers' existing systems. They signal that the company needs people who can put solutions into the ecosystem the customer has already bought.

Don't just write "built agents for customers" on your resume. Write that you identified work existing tools couldn't do, then integrated the solution into the same tool. This sentence shows hiring managers that you understand both the technology and the internal politics of your customers.

This week's exercise: pick one workflow at a customer you're serving, answer "why not use the tools we already have?" in five sentences, then have a colleague play a difficult CIO and read it aloud to them. If any sentence mentions the model, rewrite it.
