In short: A skill sheet is a document where IT engineers organize and present their technology stack, project history, the phases they handled, and their skill proficiency. While a typical resume tells the "story of your career," a skill sheet is read as material for judging "whether you fit this project." That's why organizing your items and presenting them with numbers matters more than anything.

Skill sheets have become widespread as career documents unique to IT engineers. In Japan's IT industry, skill sheets are central to document screening, whether for freelance projects, SES contracts, or job hunting. Here, we'll clarify the essence of skill sheets and how they differ from resumes.

The essence of a skill sheet is to "compile your technology stack and project history into a list so that fit for a project can be judged as quickly as possible." Agents and PMs skim skill sheets in about 30 seconds to 2 minutes and decide "whether to interview this person." Whether your document gets accepted depends on whether you can convey "your strengths" and "your fit with the project" within that short time.

Skill sheets are especially highly valued in the freelance world for the following reasons.

  • Need for quick project matching decisions: Agents screen dozens to hundreds of skill sheets for a single project, so structured information is essential
  • Verifying technology stack matches: They need to instantly judge whether a candidate's skills match the project's required skills (languages, frameworks, cloud, etc.)
  • Judging available phases: They want to know which phases, from requirements definition to operations, the candidate can contribute to
  • Basis for rate negotiation: When achievements, skills, and years of experience are quantified, agents can negotiate rates more easily
  • Considering multiple projects in parallel: Searchable formatting makes it easier to match candidates with multiple projects

In job hunting too, especially in the IT industry, more people submit skill sheets alongside resumes because "technical ability is unclear from a resume alone." This is even more true when technical decision-makers like CTOs or tech leads are involved in the selection process, and the quality of your skill sheet can determine whether you pass.

The work summary is a very important part that serves as the "preview" of the entire skill sheet. It is safe to say that most 30-second skim reads are decided here.

Let's organize the three elements essential to a good summary.

  • "What kind of professional you are" (specialty area): Specialized positions such as backend engineer, AI architect, etc.
  • "What scale you work at" (years of experience and scale of achievements): Years of experience, scale of projects handled, data volume
  • "Strengths and differentiators" (uniqueness): Concrete experience that differentiates you from other candidates

OK example (backend engineer)

10 years of experience in backend development for large-scale BtoB SaaS, centered on Java and Go. Over the past 3 years, led the design and operation of a microservices platform handling 10 million requests per month, with achievements including a 50% improvement in API response speed and a 30% reduction in operational costs. Also led infrastructure automation on AWS (Terraform, GitHub Actions), and can handle everything end-to-end from requirements definition to operations.

NG example

I have 10 years of experience as an engineer. I have handled a wide range of work from backend to frontend and infrastructure. Thank you for your consideration.

The differences between the OK and NG examples come down to three things: "Is it concrete?", "Are there numbers?", and "Can you see how this person differs from others?" With the OK example, readers can immediately picture "what this person can do, at what level, and what they could be entrusted with."

The points that professionals in the field always keep in mind are as follows.

  • Clearly indicate assigned phases in checkbox format: Makes it instantly clear how far you were involved, from requirements definition to operations
  • State your role in one line: Clarify whether you were tech lead, PdM, a development member, etc.
  • List technologies with specific versions and combinations: Improves the accuracy of evaluation
  • Always quantify results: Show them as numbers such as percentages, counts, or monetary amounts
  • Abstract within the bounds of confidentiality obligations: Omit project and client names while conveying the industry and scale

Even if you have all the material for your skill sheet, the pass rate of your documents can change dramatically depending on how you present and showcase it. Here, we organize the techniques that professionals in the field actually use.

If you tailor your skill sheet for each project you apply to, so that recruiters can immediately judge "this person matches the project requirements," your document pass rate will improve significantly.

Tuning Checkpoints

  • Rewriting the opening summary: Include keywords that match the project's required and preferred qualifications
  • Reordering recent projects: Move projects most similar to the one you are applying for to the top
  • Order of the tech stack: Place technologies required by the project at the top of the table
  • Emphasis in work descriptions: Thicken descriptions of the phases and areas required by the project you are applying for
  • Using the "Desired Work" field: Clearly state preferences that consciously incorporate keywords from the project you are applying for

However, over-tuning can backfire. Never "write things not in your track record" or "inflate your years of experience." The basic rule is to stay within the scope of "polishing how you present things."

The key to blurring information is to "hide company and product names while conveying the industry, scale, and the essence of the problem." If the reader can picture "how large and how complex the project was," you will be sufficiently well evaluated.

  • OSS contributions: Number of pull requests, names of OSS adopted, GitHub URL of your own OSS
  • Technical publishing: Follower counts on Qiita/Zenn/personal blogs, view counts of major articles
  • Speaking experience: Conference names, themes, dates
  • Books and article writing: Track record of technical books, serialized columns, and guest contributions
  • Personal development: MAU, GitHub star counts, monetization track record
  • Community management: Organizing study groups, running Discord/Slack communities
  • Competitions and contests: Rankings in Kaggle, AtCoder, etc.

These activities are very strong material for demonstrating "technical skill and passion that work achievements alone cannot fully convey." In particular, OSS contributions and technical publishing are easy for recruiters and agents to verify as "external corroboration," which also works in your favor when negotiating rates for freelance projects.

Please organize the following project overview into two sections for an IT skill sheet: "Work Content" and "Results." Results must always be shown as quantified figures. [Project Overview] (Describe the work content in bullet points)

Points to be careful about when using generative AI are as follows.

  • Never falsify facts: AI creating "plausible" expressions is not for inflating facts
  • Don't over-abstract: Write specific technology names and figures yourself rather than leaving abstraction to the AI
  • Compare multiple drafts: Don't settle for the first output; try multiple variations
  • Final check by a human: Always reread AI-generated text yourself and fix unnatural expressions

When applying to projects through an agent, making your skill sheet one that agents can easily "sell" greatly changes your document pass rate. Here is a summary of what agents dislike and what they prefer.

Things agents dislike (avoid these)

  • Old project history listed at the top (put your most recent project first)
  • Technology stack listed only as a list, with no proficiency level or years of experience
  • Specific company or product names beyond what confidentiality allows
  • Missing contact information or available working days
  • Inconsistent formatting (mixed fonts, colors, and sizes)
  • Descriptions of a single project that are too long (ideally one page or less per project)
  • Self-promotion focused on "motivation and enthusiasm" without specifics

Things agents prefer (incorporate these)

  • A summary at the top condensing your strengths into 3-5 lines
  • A skill table clearly showing proficiency, years of experience, and most recent usage
  • Most recent projects listed first, with a consistent format for each project
  • Assigned phases visualized with checkboxes
  • Quantified achievements
  • Work descriptions that respect confidentiality while remaining specific

Conclusion: For IT engineers, the skill sheet is the single most important document that determines "document pass rate," "rate," and "project quality"—whether for freelance projects, job hunting, or SES sales. This article has covered skill sheet templates for IT engineers, writing formats, points to emphasize by role, and techniques for improving document pass rates. Finally, let's recap the key points once more.

  • A skill sheet is a "project matching judgment document," differing from a resume in purpose, audience, and format
  • Using Excel as the base, combined with modern formats such as Notion/GitHub/LinkedIn, is the standard style from 2025 onward
  • A 3–5 line work summary at the top that instantly conveys what you can do, at what scale, and what your strengths are is the key to passing document screening
  • The technical skills table must always include three axes: "years of experience + proficiency level + most recent usage period"
  • Project history should be structured and describe "phases handled, role, technologies used, and results (quantified)"
  • Track record with generative AI (LLM, RAG, agents, MLOps) is a decisive differentiator for securing high rates
  • Techniques for conveying scale and results in abstracted form while respecting confidentiality obligations balance credibility and persuasiveness
  • We have entered an era of using generative AI such as ChatGPT and Claude to polish skill sheets
  • When submitting through an agent, formatting it in a way that is "easy to recommend" greatly improves project matching rates

A skill sheet is not something you make once and finish; it should be continually updated for each project and at each career milestone. By building the habit of putting into words the work content, results, and lessons learned each time a project ends, you can present yourself in the most persuasive form in both document screening and interviews.
