FDE as PM: Medplum's Model and Where It Breaks

FDE Times · 2026-10-08 · 4 min read

Summary

Medplum operates without product managers: its FDEs triage every customer request, rank priorities weekly in a spreadsheet, and present them to engineering on rotation, making them the main input to the roadmap. COO Rahul Agarwal admits the process breaks at scale—once ten or twenty FDEs are involved, each ticket carries too much context for the presenting engineer to answer engineering's questions. He would replace the weekly rotation with monthly or quarterly cycles tied to customer assignments, but argues the deeper problem, preserving context as knowledge passes through many hands, exists at every scale. Northzone adds an incentive critique: FDEs tied to individual accounts drift toward account management. The article urges practicing writing tickets that readers can act on without follow-up.

Key points

  • Medplum has no PMs; FDEs run the entire request intake and roadmap input.
  • Weekly rotation breaks at ten to twenty FDEs because tickets carry too much context.
  • Agarwal suggests monthly or quarterly rotations tied to customer assignments.
  • Northzone warns incentives push FDEs toward account management over product feedback.
  • Key skill: writing tickets that others can understand and act on without follow-up.

In brief

  • Medplum has no PMs: FDEs triage every request and are the main input to the roadmap.
  • The process began to break as the team grew, because every ticket came with too much context.
  • Agarwal would drop the weekly PM rotation, but the problem of sharing context exists at every scale.

Medplum has no product managers. Rahul Agarwal, its co-founder and COO, says plainly that he has never been convinced of PM as a full-time role with its own hiring profile.

At Medplum, the PM’s work goes to the FDEs. Yet Agarwal himself concedes that the process “starts to break at scale”.

For developers aiming at FDE roles, this is a lesson about the part of the job that descriptions rarely spell out: you do not only deploy for customers, you also carry the customer’s voice back to the product.

What does it look like when FDEs do PM?

Medplum’s blog says FDEs are the main input to the roadmap. The FDE team triages every issue, feature request and bug from customers and the community, which is the whole intake process that belongs to PMs at other companies.

The company wants FDEs to help decide what the product becomes, not just to help customers make good use of the product as it stands.

The mechanism Agarwal describes is fairly simple. Each week, requests are collected in a spreadsheet, the FDEs negotiate among themselves to produce a priority ranking, and one FDE, on rotation, presents it to the engineering team.

The break is about context, not headcount

Agarwal explains the failure briefly: once the team has ten or twenty FDEs, every ticket drags along too much context. The figure should not be read as a hard limit, though. He uses it to illustrate overload, and says he would fix the design rather than treat it as a fixed ceiling.

Picture this week’s on-duty FDE presenting a ticket that reads “need a date filter”. A colleague wrote it after three calls with a customer the presenter has never worked with. Engineering asks why the customer needs it, how the customer is coping now, and whether anyone else needs it. The presenter cannot answer.

Next week, someone else is presenting.

Looking back, Agarwal therefore thinks the person playing PM should not rotate weekly. He suggests a monthly or quarterly cycle, tied to customer assignments, so that presenters actually understand the tickets they bring into the room.

The hardest problem for FDEs

Changing the rotation only eases the symptoms. Agarwal believes the root problem persists at every scale: in his view, the hard problem for FDEs is preserving context as knowledge passes through hundreds of hands.

Tandem, a company building tools for FDE teams, sees the same gap: recording what is learned in the field is real work, it competes with delivery for time, and delivery always wins.

The venture firm Northzone looks at the problem through incentives. FDEs are driven to win their own customers; product teams are driven to build things many people share. When FDEs are tied to the outcomes of individual accounts, the account side wins: they stop being product scouts and become account managers.

Northzone puts it in terms of bricks: FDEs pick up bricks in the field, but if nobody carries them back to the product team, the company does not really have an FDE organisation.

What to practise

The skill Medplum is short of as it grows is one you can practise now: writing a ticket that someone who has never met the customer can read, understand and act on.

Go back to the date filter example. The bad version is a single line: “Customer X needs date filtering, high priority.” The good version, still only five sentences, answers in advance the questions engineering will ask.

Customer X’s operations team cannot filter records by date, so every morning they search by hand. They raised this on all three calls, and the call notes are attached. For now they export the data to Excel to filter it.

Two other customers have asked for something similar, so this is not one account’s peculiar need. If it is not built, Customer X will keep relying on an Excel file outside the system. The reader does not need to call you back to reach a decision.

When reading job descriptions, look for phrases such as “triage feature requests” or “input to roadmap”. They signal that the company expects you to do the PM’s work too. In interviews, ask how signal from FDEs reaches the product team, who reads it, and how often.

Companies that drop the PM role will not run short of engineers who can code. What they lack are people who know how to package context so it is not lost along the way.
