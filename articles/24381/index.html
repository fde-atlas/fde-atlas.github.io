FDE 2.0 — From AI Implementer to Leader of Enterprise Transformation

Original title: FDE 2.0——AI実装者から、企業変革の現場責任者へ

FDE Consulting · 2026-05-22 · 13 min read

Summary

The author argues that the FDE role has expanded from implementing AI to leading enterprise transformation on the ground. Tracing the model's origins at Palantir, the article surveys 2026 moves by OpenAI (its Deployment Company with ~150 FDEs via the Tomoro acquisition), Anthropic's services venture with Blackstone and others, and Accenture's partnerships with Palantir, Microsoft, and ServiceNow. It stresses that automation alone doesn't transform companies—deciding which work stays human matters more—and proposes a five-phase FDE 2.0 framework for the first 180 days of PE-backed PMI, especially relevant in Japan where headcount reduction is hard.

Key points

  • FDE 2.0 shifts from AI implementer to on-site transformation leader.
  • OpenAI, Anthropic, and Accenture all built FDE-focused ventures in 2026.
  • Success metrics should be business KPIs, not AI adoption rates.
  • In Japan, AI adoption must be paired with workforce redesign.
  • A five-phase framework targets the first 180 days of PMI.

Thinking of FDE as a Management Agenda

I want to seriously consider FDE.

The people I want to read this are not engineers interested in the FDE job title. They are executives who make decisions about deploying AI in the field: business leaders, DX officers, and the people at PE funds responsible for increasing the value of their portfolio companies.

At BCG, I have watched the process by which management strategy and corporate transformation themes land in the field. Even with a correct strategy, results will not materialize unless frontline operations, systems, people's roles, and KPIs change. Even if a document is logically sound, a company will not change unless it is embedded in frontline workflows.

Since then, the more I have worked on the front lines of business transformation using generative AI, the more I feel that this structure is exactly the same with AI.

AI adoption is neither tool selection nor a PoC. It is a rebuilding of management itself.

Standing on this premise, FDE is no longer just a new job title. It comes into view as an organizational capability in its own right, one required to make enterprise AI work in production.

FDE was originally the job title Forward Deployed Engineer.

FDEs go into the customer's field, connect data, systems, operations, and decision-making, and get software into a state where it can be used in production. It is not mere implementation support, nor is it simply being stationed at a client site. It is a model in which engineers engage deeply with business challenges until results are achieved in the field.

However, FDE in the era of generative AI does not stop there.

I now see FDE as expanding from "people who implement AI" to "people who execute corporate transformation in the field." Simply putting AI agents or LLMs into business operations is no longer a differentiator. The difference comes from how AI reshapes operations, staffing, evaluation systems, customer experience, and management KPIs.

In this sense, the FDE of the future will be a Forward Deployed Engineer and, at the same time, a driver of Forward Deployed Transformation.

The Prototype Created by Palantir

The prototype of FDE lies with Palantir.

In its explanation of its own architecture, Palantir states that its products have been continuously built through the methodology of Forward Deployed Engineering. Problems are understood in the places closest to the field, and feedback is returned to the product in collaboration with the core engineering team. Palantir positions this as something like "backpropagation by humans."

This expression is important.

FDE is not a role that listens to customer requests and customizes accordingly. It is a role that feeds the ambiguous problems arising in the field back into both the product and the operations. Data is scattered. Permissions are complex. Frontline exception handling is not documented. Decision-making is fragmented by department. FDEs convert these messy realities into working systems and working operations.

Furthermore, Palantir positions AIP, Foundry, and Apollo as "the operating system for the enterprise," expressing the philosophy of using Ontology to integrate a company's data, logic, actions, and security policies into a form that humans and AI agents can work with.

What becomes clear here is the fact that the essence of FDE is not "teaching people how to use AI tools." FDE is a role that transforms the very structure of a company's operations into a form where software and AI can function.

References: Palantir Architecture Center, Palantir AI FDE

In 2026, FDE Became the Main Battleground of the AI Industry

In 2026, this trend accelerated clearly.

In May 2026, OpenAI launched the OpenAI Deployment Company. In the announcement, it explained that it would embed Forward Deployed Engineers within companies to identify high-impact areas together with business leaders, operators, and frontline teams, and to redesign organizational infrastructure and critical workflows around AI. Through the acquisition of Tomoro, the plan also involves having approximately 150 FDEs and Deployment Specialists from day one.

Anthropic is moving in the same direction. In May 2026, together with Blackstone, Hellman & Friedman, and Goldman Sachs, it announced the launch of an AI services company to bring Claude into the core operations of mid-sized companies. Anthropic wrote that bringing Claude into a company's core operations requires hands-on engineering and a deep understanding of how each business works.

Accenture's moves are also symbolic. In December 2025, it announced the Accenture Palantir Business Group with Palantir, combining Palantir's FDEs with more than 2,000 Accenture professionals with Palantir skills. In March 2026, it announced a Forward Deployed Engineering practice with Microsoft, and in May 2026, it announced an FDE program with ServiceNow to move agentic AI from pilot to production.

What this series of moves shows is simple.

Competition in AI is no longer decided by the performance of models alone. The contest now is whether you can carry things all the way to the point where operations actually change within a company, KPIs move, and people keep using the system.

However, Automation Alone Will Not Change Companies

Here, there is one point that must not be overlooked.

Being able to automate operations with AI is not the same as a company changing.

For example, suppose a development organization has 600 outsourced engineers. With AI, parts of specification understanding, code generation, testing, review, documentation, and inquiry handling can be dramatically streamlined. In the short term, a debate arises: "Could we cut the 600 to less than half?"

But at that moment, the next question arises.

Have the eliminated tasks really disappeared? Who will watch the exception handling? Where does responsibility for quality assurance remain? How will employees be reassigned? Can people with technical skills be shifted to sales, customer success, implementation support, or business improvement? How far can AI be trusted without damaging customer experience or the brand?

AI adoption that does not address these questions is not corporate transformation. It is mere local optimization.

OpenAI's Klarna case study demonstrated just how significant an impact AI customer service can have. In OpenAI's published case, Klarna's AI assistant handled 2.3 million conversations in its first month in production—equivalent to two-thirds of customer service chats, or the workload of 700 full-time agents.

Looking at these numbers alone, it reads like a story of AI replacing headcount.

But what really matters is not "how much can we hand over to AI." It is "which work should remain with humans." In many industries, even if AI can handle 99% of inquiries, the remaining 1% of exception handling is what sustains the brand. In refunds, returns, identity verification, complaints, healthcare, finance, and high-emotion touchpoints in BtoC services, the final 0.1% of flexibility determines customer trust.

If all you want is to raise the AI adoption rate, crude automation will do. If you want to raise enterprise value, you must design where human time should be preserved.

What FDEs Should Watch in Japan, Where People Don't Disappear

At Japanese companies, this issue carries even more weight.

The US-style assumption of layoffs makes it hard to simply proceed with "cutting the headcount that AI can replace." The larger the company, the more factors are involved: employment, transfers, labor relations, internal politics, brand, and how the company appears in the hiring market. Even if AI reduces workload, people do not disappear overnight.

That is precisely why Japan needs FDEs who look at AI adoption and workforce redesign as a set.

Break down the work and decide which areas AI will replace. Define the exception handling that should remain with humans. Redeploy surplus technical talent into sales, presales, customer success, process improvement, AI operations, and data quality management. Build AI proficiency into evaluation systems—not to cut people who can't use it, but to bring them up to a level where they can.

This is not mere reskilling. It is job redesign for the AI era.

For example, moving engineers into sales cannot be achieved with old-fashioned sales training alone. Precisely because they understand the technology, they can listen to customers' operational challenges, identify the work AI can change, and explain the changes after implementation. For that, they need deal-making skills, interview design, business decomposition, AI literacy, and the ability to frame the issues—not just prompts.

AI does not make work disappear. AI raises the density of the work that should remain with humans.

On that premise, the role of the FDE expands considerably.

Entering the First 180 Days of PE/PMI

One of the areas where FDE 2.0 delivers the most value is PE funds and PMI after roll-ups.

The first 100 to 180 days after an acquisition are the most critical window for restructuring a company's operational architecture. Whether people, processes, data, KPIs, and systems can be reviewed all at once during this period dramatically changes the company's subsequent enterprise value.

Traditional PMI focused mainly on finance, governance, management control, HR systems, and system integration. Going forward, AI transformation enters that mix.

The FDE 2.0 PMI package follows a sequence like this:

PhaseObjectiveKey Deliverables
1. Process visualizationUnderstand actual frontline workProcess maps, system maps, KPI trees
2. AI classificationSeparate automation, augmentation, and human retentionAI roadmap, risk classification
3. Production implementationPut AI into critical workflowsAgents, dashboards, operating procedures
4. Talent redeploymentRedirect surplus time toward value creationTransfer plans, training design, evaluation metrics
5. KPI improvementMeasure impact continuouslyWeekly reviews, improvement logs, management reports

What matters here is not letting AI adoption close as a "cost reduction project."

Costs should come down. But that alone is not enough. Can the freed-up human time be redirected toward revenue, customer touchpoints, quality improvement, exception handling, and frontline improvement? Does AI raise the company's capabilities, rather than simply reducing headcount? That is what PE should be looking at.

The Five Jobs of FDE 2.0

The work of FDE 2.0 is broader than conventional AI implementation support.

First, process diagnosis. Go into the field and decompose who does what, on which system, and by what decision criteria. Meeting materials and process manuals are not enough. You need to look at actual chats, emails, spreadsheets, approval flows, and exception handling.

Second, workflow redesign. It is not about hunting for tasks to throw at AI. It is about rebuilding the flow of work itself into a form AI can read, judge, and execute. Here, data structures, permission design, human review, and escalation conditions become critical.

Third, production implementation. It does not end with building a chatbot. You connect internal data, business applications, approval authority, logs, auditing, and KPI measurement. AI used in production is far more unglamorous than a demo—and far more demanding.

Fourth, talent redesign. You must not leave the vacated space of AI-replaced work unattended. Where will the surplus time be reinvested? Who moves into sales? Who becomes the AI operations lead? Who handles exceptions? Which talent gets reskilled, and for which talent do you offer early retirement or external placement? Transformation includes all of this.

Fifth, KPI improvement. There is no point tracking only AI adoption rates, prompt usage counts, or chat volumes. What you should watch is lead time, gross margin, churn rate, NPS, inquiry recurrence rate, sales conversion rate, defect rate, rework rate, and management hours. Measure not whether AI was used, but whether the business actually improved.

The Success Metric Is Not "How Much You Automated"

OpenAI's State of Enterprise AI 2025 shows that enterprise usage is deepening from one-off questions into repetitive business workflows like Projects and Custom GPTs. OpenAI reports that weekly messages on ChatGPT Enterprise grew substantially, and weekly users of Custom GPTs and Projects grew roughly 19x year-to-date.

McKinsey's 2025 global AI survey likewise shows that companies achieving major impact with AI are going beyond mere efficiency gains into fundamental redesign of workflows. In that survey, AI high performers account for only about 6% of respondents, and that group is pursuing transformative innovation, workflow redesign, faster scaling, and implementation of transformation best practices.

In other words, what the world's leading companies are beginning to learn is clear.

AI has no value simply by being deployed. It only creates value when the work changes, people's roles change, and KPIs change.

References: OpenAI State of Enterprise AI 2025, McKinsey State of AI 2025

The questions decision-makers should ask

When executives or PE funds consider introducing FDEs, the question to ask is not "What can AI do?"

The question to ask is much closer to management itself.

Not "What tasks can AI automate?" but "Which tasks, if rebuilt, will move the P/L and KPIs?"

Not "How many people can we cut?" but "Where should the freed-up time be reallocated to increase revenue, quality, and customer experience?"

Not "Which vendor can build it?" but "Who will go into the field and drive weekly improvements between management and the front line?"

If AI is introduced without being able to answer these questions, projects stall at the PoC stage. Or even if they succeed at localized efficiency gains, they never become organization-wide transformation.

This is where the value of introducing FDEs lies. An FDE is not someone who builds AI, but someone who changes how a company operates through AI.

Situations where you should consult FDE Consulting

The position FDE Consulting should take is not that of an AI tool implementation company.

Nor a generative AI training company. Nor a PoC development company. Nor a prompt-writing service.

The target should be a field leader who stands between AI implementation and organizational transformation.

Executives don't just want to know "What can AI do?" What they really want to know is: "Which of our company's tasks should we change?" "How far can we reduce headcount?" "Where should we move the people we can't cut?" "What can we automate without damaging the customer experience?" "Which KPIs can we move in the first six months?"

This is where FDE Consulting's winning strategy lies.

Enter the field of AI adoption, break down the work, build the systems, get them used, monitor the KPIs, and if necessary, design everything down to personnel reallocation and sales transformation. Not just technology, not just strategy, not just HR. Treat corporate transformation as a single flow.

The companies that should consult us are not those wanting ChatGPT training. If you just want to try AI tools, there are plenty of other options.

The companies that should consult us are those that want to treat AI as a management issue.

Wanting to change the operational structure in the first six months of PMI or value creation at an investment. Wanting to rethink the staffing structure of development, sales, customer support, and back office with AI as the premise. Having finished a PoC but unable to progress to production, adoption, and KPI improvement. Not seeing how to make use of the remaining talent after generative AI-driven efficiency gains.

In such situations, AI knowledge alone is not enough. You need a team that enters the field, examines the work, thinks about people's placement, builds what's needed, and measures impact through management metrics.

This is FDE 2.0.

The winners of the future will not be the companies that automated the most tasks with AI. They will be the companies that reallocated human time to the most valuable places.
