FDE Weekly Report and Project Update Playbook: Making Invisible Delivery Work Visible

Original title: FDE 周报与项目汇报 Playbook：让看不见的交付工作被看见

FDE中国 · 2026-09-04 · 6 min read

Summary

This FDEChina playbook argues that reporting is a core deliverable skill for forward deployed engineers, equal in weight to coding, because much of their work happens invisibly at customer sites. It lays out seven steps: segmenting audiences into three tiers, fixing a weekly report structure of progress, risks, and decisions needed, making invisible work visible, using metrics with agreed definitions, tiered risk escalation with lead time, a three-layer reporting rhythm (weekly reports, biweekly demos, milestone reviews), and delivering bad news with options. It stresses that visibility is about exposing dependencies, not self-promotion, and tailors pacing to on-site engagement in China.

Key points

  • Weekly reports follow a fixed order: progress, risks, decisions needed.
  • Making invisible work visible exposes dependencies, not self-praise.
  • Agree metric definitions with customers upfront; keep 3-5 stable metrics.
  • Risks are tiered: acceptance-threatening ones get same-day direct escalation.
  • Three-layer rhythm: weekly reports, biweekly demos, milestone reviews.

An FDE's work happens largely at the client site, in places others cannot see: hunting down a hidden defect in a data pipeline, talking a client out of a technically infeasible requirement, bringing two departments that never speak to each other to the same table. If this work doesn't appear in your reports, then in everyone's eyes it never happened. Reporting is therefore not about claiming credit, but about making the information that decisions depend on actually flow. Reporting skill is thus one of the FDE's core deliverable capabilities, weighted equally with writing code and running evals. This piece lays out the method in seven numbered steps, each covering what to do, the deliverable, and common pitfalls.

Step 1: First figure out who you're writing for

What to do: The same work must face three audience tiers: the frontline contact (who cares about task details and schedules), the client-side decision-maker (who cares about risks and value), and your own team's leadership (who care about project health and reusable lessons). The three tiers don't care about the same content—the contact wants task-level progress, the decision-maker only looks at risks and matters requiring their sign-off, and your leadership also wants to know what the project has deposited for the team. Tier first, then decide what goes into the weekly report and what goes through separate communication. The tiering table isn't a one-time artifact to file away: past the project's midpoint, the client's focus drifts from "can it be built" to "can it be used," key readers may change, and you should revisit the table at every milestone to check it still holds.

Deliverable: A stakeholder tiering table: who, what they care about, through what channel, and how often they see what.

Common pitfall: Using one weekly report to serve everyone, with the result that the decision-maker finds it too verbose, the contact finds it not detailed enough, and it pleases no one.

Step 2: Fix the weekly report structure

What to do: Fix the weekly report as three sections—progress, risks, decisions needed—in an order that cannot be swapped. In the progress section, conclusion first: the first sentence is the week's one-line takeaway. The risks section contains only what the other party needs to know or can help with. The decisions-needed section has at most three items, each with options, costs, and a recommendation. Once the structure is agreed with the client, don't change it every week: readers look for information in fixed places, and speed is the entire point of reporting.

Deliverable: A weekly report structure and fixed send time confirmed with the client.

Common pitfall: Writing the weekly report as a diary, a chronological log where readers must summarize the conclusions themselves—which is as good as not writing it. Adopting the three-section weekly report template directly saves you the weekly struggle over structure.

Step 3: Make invisible work visible

What to do: Beyond progress, maintain a small section called "unplanned completions and issues eliminated," with each entry stating what the problem was, what would have happened if unaddressed, and what you did. Hidden defects fixed, infeasible requirements blocked, cross-department alignment facilitated—all belong here. Summarize this section's total once a month so the client sees the true scale of hidden costs at milestone reviews, which directly affects pricing and schedule negotiations for the next engagement. Without this section, the project's real difficulty and your actual output will be systematically underestimated.

Deliverable: An invisible-work section in the weekly report, sent regularly with it.

Common pitfall: Writing it as a self-congratulatory piece. The tone of making work visible should address issues, not people; the point is to let collaborators see dependencies and project risks, not to win yourself an award. Once readers smell self-promotion, this section's credibility drops to zero. In wording, use "what loss was prevented" instead of "what I did": the former lets readers assess project benefits, the latter lets them assess you personally, and the weekly report wants the former.

Step 4: Let the numbers speak

What to do: Translate progress into metrics wherever possible: eval set pass rates from what to what, changes in average time per task, declines in the proportion of manual intervention. Metric definitions must be aligned with the client in advance, with measurement methods noted. When choosing metrics, prioritize ones the client understands and can use for acceptance: average time to process a case, proportion of manual review, number of decisions adopted per week—all better than internal model-side metrics. Without a baseline there is no "progress" to speak of—first establish a baseline per eval baseline building, then select three to five metrics that run through the whole project per delivery metrics, and don't change them midway.

Deliverable: A fixed project-metrics section or metrics dashboard, with definitions attached.

Common pitfall: Adjective-based reporting—"significant improvement." Readers cannot judge the magnitude, and at project end you cannot settle value based on it.

Step 5: Tiering and lead time for risk escalation

What to do: Handle risks in three tiers: those affecting acceptance or contract fulfillment get raised the same day, separately, by phone or in person, not waiting for the weekly report; those with a clear mitigation and controllable within two weeks go into the report's risks section; those with no impact yet but needing a paper trail go into the risk register. Every risk entering the weekly report must carry three elements: impact, probability, and your mitigation; risk entries that only raise problems without offering mitigations drain the reader's patience. Choose escalation targets correctly too: technical risks go to the contact, risks affecting the acceptance schedule go to the decision-maker—don't let senior people get stuck in technical details they can neither judge nor solve.

Deliverable: A risk register, updated in sync with the weekly report's risks section.

Common pitfall: Reporting only good news and holding back bad until it can no longer be hidden. A risk raised two weeks early is a management issue; raised on acceptance day, it's a trust issue. Another common mistake is piling all risks into the report's risks section, which grows longer and longer until what truly matters gets drowned out—the whole point of tiering is to keep the risks section to a density of three to five items.

Step 6: Design the reporting rhythm

What to do: Make reporting a three-tier rhythm: weekly reports (written, sent on a fixed day), biweekly demos (letting the client see real things moving—even if changes are small, show the movement in eval numbers), and milestone reviews (formally checking against baselines and acceptance criteria). Each tier has its own job: the weekly report keeps information flowing, demos keep trust accumulating, reviews make conclusions officially take effect; remove any one tier and the other two must shoulder functions they're not good at. Demos are always more persuasive than text: for a trust problem solvable with a fifteen-minute demo, don't grind it out with a five-thousand-word weekly report. Brief the contact on demo content in advance to avoid them seeing surprising conclusions for the first time in the meeting; also include a fixed metrics-comparison page in the demo so numbers and demos corroborate each other rather than talking past each other. The three-tier rhythm should be written into the communication plan at project kickoff, not arranged on a whim.

Deliverable: A three-tier reporting rhythm table written into the project communication plan.

Common pitfall: Weekly reports without demos. The client's perception of progress is always separated by a layer of text, trust builds slowly, and milestone reviews lack直观 evidence.

Step 7: Deliver bad news with a plan

What to do: Report bad news with a fixed structure: what happened, how big the impact is, what you've already done, what decisions remain for the other party, and which option you recommend. State the facts in the first sentence—don't bury the bad news in the third paragraph, and don't dilute its weight with qualifiers. What the other party really wants to confirm is "whether you have things under control," and that sense of control comes from structured narration and options you can actually offer.

Deliverable: A bad-news briefing (email or in-person outline), organized in the five sections above.

Common pitfall: Both extremes are harmful: reporting only facts without options throws the decision pressure straight back at the client; or, conversely, absorbing it on your own and temporarily concealing it, letting a small problem grow into a big one. Timing matters too: sending a bad-news email on Friday evening means the client carries it through the weekend—if it can be explained face-to-face the same day, say it that day; don't let a written document absorb the first round of emotional reaction for you.

Summary

Reporting is the last mile that turns delivery work into trust. The core deliverables are three: a stakeholder tiering table, a fixed three-part weekly report structure, and metric definitions agreed by both sides. Upward, it supports reviews and decisions at every stage of the project lifecycle; downward, it relies on eval baselines and delivery metrics to provide the numerical foundation. When what needs managing is expectations rather than facts, use it together with expectation management. For an FDE who reports well, the first reaction when a project hits problems is often "let's figure this out together" rather than "why didn't you tell me earlier"—that's the difference.
