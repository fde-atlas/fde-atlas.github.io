How to Develop FDEs: Organizing a 90-Day Roadmap and Evaluation Rubric for Practical Use | FDE Research Institute

FDE総研 · 2026-08-26 · 15 min read

Summary

This article from FDE Soken argues that hiring experienced FDEs in Japan is nearly impossible because the job title, popularized by Palantir, is too new and the talent pool too thin. Companies must instead develop engineers internally or convert adjacent roles (SIer presales, consultants, PdM). The core challenge is teaching judgment about how deeply to engage with customer operations, which classroom training cannot transfer. The authors propose a 90-day roadmap: days 0-30 for observation and business understanding, 31-60 for a small closed-scope delivery, and 61-90 for independent customer-facing decisions. Skills are layered into technical foundations, domain translation, and relationship design. Evaluation should use customer-side changes as the primary metric plus a five-level autonomy scale, with the 90-day target around levels 2-3. Three failure patterns are highlighted: stationing people at clients without reflection, lacking a dedicated mentor, and reusing developer evaluation rubrics.

Key points

  • Experienced FDEs barely exist in Japan's job market; companies must grow them internally or from adjacent roles.
  • The core skill is judgment about engaging with customer operations, not technical ability alone.
  • A 90-day roadmap moves from observation, to a small closed-scope delivery, to independent customer-facing decisions.
  • Skills are layered: technical foundations, domain translation, and relationship design, built bottom-up.
  • Evaluate via customer-side changes and a five-level autonomy scale; avoid reusing developer rubrics.

Why is developing FDEs harder than mid-career hiring?

According to the FDE Research Institute's assessment, the difficulty of FDE development lies not in technical skill, but in conveying the judgment to engage deeply with customer business without teaching materials.

FDE (Forward Deployed Engineer) is a term popularized by Palantir as a job title, referring to engineers who embed themselves at customer sites, connect their company's product to the customer's business, and drive it to results (for details on the definition, see What is an FDE Engineer?). When searching for talent with this title in the hiring market, you hit the structural wall of a shallow talent pool. As a result, many companies choose to develop their in-house engineers into FDEs.

"Experienced FDEs" barely exist in the market

There are only a handful of people in Japan who claim FDE experience, so it is best to assume that filling positions through mid-career hiring alone is practically unfeasible.

The job title FDE only began to spread after the concept was introduced through Palantir's recruiting pages and engineering blog, and it has a short history in the Japanese labor market. It has no independent category in the Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare's occupational classification. Even if a job posting says "3+ years of FDE experience," applicants will mostly be cross-border entrants from adjacent roles.

  • Pre-sales engineers at SIers
  • Engineers from consulting firms
  • Customer success engineers at product companies

Hiring from adjacent roles is effective, but in reality it is not experienced-hire recruitment but "mid-career development of people with good foundations." Since a development plan is needed from the moment of hiring, it is almost the same as internal development. The hiring-side procedure is organized in How to Conduct FDE Hiring.

Because what needs to be developed is "judgment to engage deeply" rather than "technology"

The core of FDE development is the judgment of how deeply to engage with customer business, and this judgment cannot be transferred through classroom learning or a two-day training alone.

Where differences emerge in the field is in the response when a customer says "I want this feature." The value of deliverables six months later differs greatly between someone who converts the request directly into implementation specs and someone who digs one level deeper into the business problem behind the request. This digging-in is hard to capture in a procedure manual.

Therefore, FDE development centers on designing how to position experience within real projects rather than on training curricula. The following sections cover how to arrange this in 90-day units and how to design evaluations that measure post-development proficiency.

Should FDE development use external training, internal OJT, or hiring?

According to the FDE Research Institute's comparison framework, external training is suited to sharing concepts, internal OJT to solidifying judgment, and hiring to securing foundations; the three methods are meant to be used in combination.

The three methods have different roles and cannot replace each other

External training, internal OJT, and hiring are in a division-of-roles relationship, not a substitutable one, so avoid a design that completes development with just one of them.

MethodWhat you gainWhat you don't gainSuitable organizations
External trainingCan quickly share FDE concepts, terminology, and frameworksJudgment experience in front of your own customersStage where you want to align FDE thinking internally
Internal OJT (90 days)Accumulation of judgment and reflection on real projectsExternal case studies and systematic organizationOrganizations with customer projects that can assign a mentor
Mid-career hiringFoundations built in adjacent rolesUnderstanding of your own business and customersOrganizations without the capacity to develop the first layer (technical fundamentals) internally

Training companies' development programs often do not cover plans to continue internally after training or completion criteria. How you design the 90 days after external training determines the success or failure of development.

The starting point of development differs by prior role

Because the missing layers differ depending on the FDE candidate's prior role, the emphasis of the 90-day plan must be adjusted for each role.

Conditions of people and organizations unsuited to FDE development

Those unsuited to FDE development fall into two categories: people who want to focus on implementation rather than judgment in front of customers, and organizations that cannot assign a mentor.

  • People who feel time spent on the customer's business is "time not spent developing"
  • People whose Layer 1 technical fundamentals are not yet solid (the load of customer interactions actually slows their learning)
  • Organizations that cannot separate the project owner and the mentor roles
  • Organizations unwilling to change their developer evaluation framework

How should the 90-day FDE development roadmap be structured?

According to the FDE Research Institute's recommended 90 days for FDE development, the allocation is: business understanding in days 0–30, small ownership in days 31–60, and judgment in front of customers in days 61–90.

There is no absolute basis for the 90-day division, but it aligns easily with quarterly project cycles and evaluation interview cycles, and works well as a unit for reporting development progress to management.

PeriodMain themeTrainee's practical tasksDeliverablesPeople involvedMilestone
Days 0–30Observation and business understandingCustomer shadowing, one-on-one interviewsBusiness flow diagram, glossary, list of discrepancies between specs and dataMentor (accompanying)Can explain the customer's business in their own words
Days 31–60Small ownershipRequirements definition → implementation → delivery within a limited scopeOne small, self-contained deliverable, pre-engagement measurementsMentor (review), project owner (scope approval)Can complete a full cycle on a small scope
Days 61-90Judgment in front of the customerMeetings alone, deciding which requests to accept or declineJudgment records and reflection notesMentor (before and after), supervisor (evaluation interview)Can respond with something other than "I'll take it back and check"

Days 0-30: Focus entirely on observation and understanding the business

For the first 30 days, do not demand implementation. The goal is reaching a state where they can explain the customer's business flows and terminology in their own words.

You will be tempted to ask for deliverables during this period, but the more you rush them, the more rework you will face later. For the business flow diagrams and glossary, do not hand over existing materials; instead, have the trainee write them up themselves based on interviews with the customer. Through this work, both the trainee and the mentor can see "what is not yet understood."

  • Attend the customer's regular meetings as an observer (no speaking required)
  • Ask on-site staff about business flows in one-on-one conversations
  • Compile customer-specific abbreviations, report names, and system names into a glossary
  • Look at actual existing data and identify discrepancies with the specification documents

The last item is especially important. The places where specifications and actual data diverge are precisely where the most effort will be consumed in later projects.

Days 31-60: Hand over a small, self-contained scope

From day 31 onward, it is effective to carve out a scope where failure will not derail the project, and have the trainee go through one full cycle from requirements definition to implementation.

The key point is not to "split the process" but to "split the scope." If you only hand over implementation, they will never accumulate requirements definition experience. Even if small, have them go through the full cycle of asking the customer → deciding → building → showing.

Choose a scope that can be completed in one to two weeks and where failure will not halt the customer's production operations. Something like rebuilding a single existing report or automating a manually run aggregation is a manageable size. Also hand over the pre-start measurements to the trainee during this period.

Days 61-90: Make them make decisions in front of the customer

From day 61, deliberately create experiences where they stand alone in front of the customer and decide themselves which requests to accept or decline.

The biggest factor separating FDEs from ordinary development roles is the number of times they have made decisions in front of customers. When a supervisor attends, both the customer and the trainee naturally look to the supervisor. As long as this structure continues, judgment experience is hard to accumulate.

That said, handing everything over at once is risky. In practice, a realistic approach is to not attend the meetings themselves, but to invest heavily of time in pre-meeting strategy sessions and post-meeting reflections. The division of labor becomes: the trainee makes the decisions in the field, while the mentor reviews the quality of those decisions.

How are the skill layers developed in FDE training divided?

The FDE Research Institute divides FDE skills into three layers—technical fundamentals, domain translation, and relationship design—and evaluates them on the premise that they are built up from the bottom layer in order.

Evaluating the three layers together causes the training plan to collapse into "vaguely doing everything." Setting separate achievement criteria for each layer raises the resolution of the training.

LayerContentCore of how to develop itWhat happens when it is lacking
Layer 3: Relationship designManaging expectations, deciding when to declineHandling customers aloneAccepts every request and breaks down
Layer 2: Domain translationBusiness language ⇄ system languageCreating business flow diagrams and glossaries themselvesSpecifications drift from the field
Layer 1: Technical fundamentalsData preparation through implementation and operationsOrdinary development projectsFreezes up in front of the customer

Layer 1: Engineering fundamentals to build things end-to-end alone

The foundation is the ability to handle everything alone from data preparation through implementation and operations; if this layer is missing, they will be unable to act in front of the customer.

FDEs are frequently asked on-site, "Can you do this right now?" Whether they can estimate feasibility on the spot is directly tied to the breadth of their technical skills. In particular, data preprocessing and operational design for keeping things running are essential regardless of whether they work with platforms like Palantir Foundry or build their own systems. Conversely, avoid placing people lacking Layer 1 into FDE roles.

Layer 2: The translation ability to move back and forth between business language and system language

The core of the FDE role is translation ability: rendering field-level phrasing into data structures, and translating system constraints back into business language.

Translation is meaningful because it is bidirectional. If you only train the direction of converting the customer's words into specifications, you end up with an engineer who can only say "that's not possible." Only when they can explain constraints in business language and present alternatives in terms of business benefits can they participate in the customer's decision-making. A circuit that works well is having the customer's on-site staff read the specification documents the trainee wrote and point out anything that feels off.

Layer 3: The ability to design relationships and expectations with the customer

The top layer is relationship design: the skill of drawing one's own lines on what to promise and what to decline, and managing the customer's expectations.

This layer develops last. Deciding when to decline requires an understanding of the overall profitability of the project and the company's policies. If you overemphasize "not upsetting the customer" early in training, you will produce an FDE who cannot say no. It is appropriate to position this as a challenge beyond the 90-day period.

How does the FDE training approach change based on years of experience, current role, English ability, and company size?

According to the FDE Research Institute's framework, differences in prerequisites do not change the 90-day allocation, but rather which layers to emphasize and who serves as the mentor.

Differences by years of experience and current role

The basic principle is to emphasize Layer 1 for candidates with less experience, and Layers 2 and 3 for candidates who have built experience in adjacent roles.

PrerequisitesStarting layerAdjustments to the 90-day plan
Limited development experienceLayer 1Solidify technical fundamentals through ordinary development projects before assigning them as an FDE
SWE with sufficient development experienceLayer 2Protect the 0-30 day observation period without cutting it
From consulting, SIer, or PdM backgroundsLayer 1 or Layer 2Always include a "build it alone end-to-end" scope in days 31-60

Individuals considering a career change with no prior experience can refer to Can you become an FDE without prior experience?, and those in their 30s considering a transition can also refer to Changing careers to FDE in your 30s.

Differences by English ability and company size

English ability requirements are determined by the languages used with the customer and in materials, while company size affects whether a dedicated mentor can be assigned.

  • English ability: If the customer and materials are primarily in Japanese, English does not need to be included in the 90-day goals. If overseas customers or English technical documentation are the focus, English is added to the Layer 2 translation targets
  • Large companies: It is easier to separate the mentor from the project lead. Since revising evaluation tables takes time, proceed with revising the grade tables in parallel with the start of training
  • Small and medium-sized companies and startups: It is difficult to assign a dedicated mentor. However, someone other than the project lead must always handle the weekly 30-minute reflection session

What metrics should be used to evaluate FDE training outcomes?

In the FDE Research Institute's evaluation design, FDE training is measured on two axes: changes on the customer side rather than hours worked, and a five-level scale of the scope that can be delegated.

FDEs spend long hours working at customer sites, making their activities hard to see from within the company. Trying to fill this visibility gap with activity reports only multiplies reports made for reporting's sake. What should be measured is how the customer's business has changed.

Make customer-side change the primary metric

The primary metric is change in the customer's business; define it in a form that can be measured before work begins, such as task time or days to decision.

Metric categoryExamplesMeasurement timing
Business efficiencyTask time for the target work, remaining manual tasksBefore start and after 3 months
Decision-makingDays until data needed for decisions is completeBefore start and after 3 months
AdoptionNumber of actual users after delivery, continued usage rate1 month and 3 months after delivery
InternalizationNumber of modifications the customer completed on their ownAfter 6 months

What matters is measuring before work begins. On projects with only post-improvement numbers, you cannot speak to results.

Evaluate the scope that can be delegated on a 5-point scale

For tracking development progress, it is practical to evaluate on a 5-level scale based on the expansion of discretion: whether accompaniment is required or the person can be entrusted to work alone.

  • Accompaniment level: The mentor's presence is required. Speaking is limited.
  • Takeaway level: Can talk with the customer alone, but brings decisions back.
  • Judgment level: Can triage requests on the spot.
  • Design level: Can design the project's approach and scope itself with the customer.
  • Development level: Can train the next FDE.

The 90-day milestone usually falls around the boundary between levels 2 and 3. It is more realistic as an expectation to consider level 4 and above as a matter of years.

Checklist template for the 30-, 60-, and 90-day marks

In evaluation meetings, checking tier-specific attainment criteria and period-specific checkpoints in the same table makes judgments less likely to waver.

Point in timeTier 1: Technical fundamentalsTier 2: Domain translationTier 3: Relationship designScope of delegation
30 daysExamined actual existing data and identified differences from the specificationCreated their own business flow diagrams and glossary, and can explain the customer's businessAttends regular meetings and can record speakers and discussion points1: Accompaniment
60 daysImplemented and delivered a small scope on their ownField staff read their specification and their confusion was resolvedAgreed with the customer on pre-start measurements1–2: Accompaniment to takeaway
90 daysCan explain a design that accounts for operationsExplained constraints in business terms and proposed alternativesCould triage requests in a solo meeting2–3: Takeaway to judgment

Record each item on a 3-level scale of "done / partially done / not achieved," and reflect unmet items in the next quarter's assigned scope. Even when adding numeric targets later, limit them to forms comparable with pre-start measurements.

What are common failure patterns in FDE development?

FDE Soken identifies three typical failures in FDE development: leaving people unattended on-site, absence of a dedicated mentor, and reusing developer evaluation sheets.

Failure 1: Believing people will grow simply by being placed at the customer site

On-site placement is only one condition for development; without a designed reflection process, experience flows by without being organized in the person's mind.

Spending long time at the customer site and having experience structured are different things. Set aside time—even just 30 minutes once a week—to verbalize questions like "What did the customer say this week that I couldn't make sense of?" and "Where did I hesitate to make a judgment?" Without this reflection, even after 3 months understanding of the business tends to remain superficial.

Failure 2: Not assigning a mentor and leaving everything to on-the-job training on projects

If the project manager and the mentor are the same person, deadlines take priority and development gets postponed, so it is better to separate the two roles.

The project manager is accountable for deadlines and quality. Structurally, there is no incentive to allow inefficient approaches for the sake of development. If possible, assign a mentor who is one step removed from the project, and include development outcomes in that person's evaluation items. This is an organizational design problem that individual effort cannot solve.

Failure 3: Applying existing developer evaluation sheets as-is

Reusing developer evaluation sheets means the value of customer engagement goes unrecognized in evaluations, making developed talent more likely to leave first.

With evaluation sheets centered on code volume and architecture design, time spent untangling the customer's business is treated as "time not spent developing." We want to avoid the pattern where developed FDEs return to the development organization or leave the company because they feel undervalued.

The countermeasure is to explicitly add items such as "understanding the customer's business," "designing expectations," and "progress of internalization handover" to the evaluation sheet, and to write out attainment criteria for each grade. For companies with in-house FDE-like roles, including VACAN Technologies, what should be established first is this evaluation sheet, ahead of training curricula. How to launch an internalization team as a whole is covered in How to Advance DX Internalization.

FDE development is not completed in 90 days. However, 90 days is enough to judge "whether this person will grow as an FDE" and to carry the development plan into the next quarter. A three-stage placement of observation, small assignments, and judgment in front of the customer, plus evaluation measured by customer-side change. Prepare these two first, then send out your first FDE candidates.
