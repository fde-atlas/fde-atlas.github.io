Forward Deployed Engineer responsibilities: what an FDE manages from pre-sales to renewal

Original title: 前线部署工程师职责：从售前到续约 FDE 管什么

FDE Times · 2026-10-08 · 5 min read

Summary

This guide maps out what a forward deployed engineer (FDE) owns across the contract lifecycle. Whether FDEs join pre-sales varies by company: PostHog's handbook excludes it, while a Pluto job post requires it. After signing, the FDE owns technical outcomes end to end, starting with a scoped brief that states what is out of scope. Success is measured by production usage and feedback routed to the product team, not demos. Customer Success owns renewals, but renewal evidence comes from adoption of the FDE's system, making handoff documentation a real deliverable. The article lists four failure modes and shows how to rewrite resumes around lifecycle outcomes with concrete numbers.

Key points

  • Pre-sales involvement varies: PostHog excludes it, Pluto requires it.
  • Post-signing, FDEs own technical outcomes within a clearly scoped brief.
  • Success means production usage and product feedback, not demos.
  • CS owns renewals, but relies on adoption evidence from the FDE's system.
  • Resumes should show lifecycle ownership with real numbers, not tool lists.

Key Takeaways

  • The pre-sales boundary varies by company: PostHog doesn't let FDEs participate in pre-sales, while a Pluto job posting requires it.
  • After signing, the FDE is accountable for technical outcomes: the system runs in production, and feedback flows back to the product team.

Picture a Monday morning. Your account manager messages you: "The deal is signed, you're on-site with the client next week." The client is a logistics company that wants an agent that can read import documents. You open the contract, and on scope there's a single line: "Automate document processing."

The first question isn't which model to use. You first need to figure out who owns what between now and the client's renewal decision: which tasks are yours, which belong to Sales, and which belong to Customer Success (CS). Get this wrong, and there are usually two outcomes: either you absorb the entire client relationship yourself, or you disappear once the system is built.

The skill to master here is knowing exactly what you must deliver at each stage of the contract. Get this straight, and your collaboration with Sales and CS will go more smoothly, you'll pick the right roles when reading job descriptions (JDs), and you'll be able to write a resume that Forward Deployed Engineer (FDE) recruiters want to see.

Pre-sales: some companies give it to FDEs, some don't

The first thing to look for when reading a JD is whether the FDE gets involved before the contract is signed, because this varies by company. PostHog's employee handbook states that FDE projects begin where pre-sales ends, and its FDEs currently don't do pre-sales; Kunal Ganglani likewise places FDEs in the post-signing phase, distinct from Solutions Engineers.

But a Pluto FDE job posting requires working directly with clients in both pre-sales and post-sales. That role follows the client from proof of concept (POC) to pilot to production launch.

So don't assume anything when reading a JD. Watch for the terms "pre-sales," "proof of concept," "pilot," and "post-sale implementation," then ask directly in the interview: Will the FDE be involved before the contract is signed? If so, what are the deliverables for that phase?

If the role does include pre-sales, what you're responsible for is an honest POC. It should accurately demonstrate what the client wants to see, and document the assumptions that haven't been validated yet, so whoever implements it (sometimes you yourself) doesn't have to guess from scratch all over again.

After signing, technical outcomes are yours

Once the deal is signed, you're accountable for technical outcomes end to end—that's how Palantir's job posting describes the FDSE role—but only if the scope has clear boundaries. PostHog calls this kind of work scoped engagements, with the goal of removing barriers to adoption or expansion.

"Scoped" is the key word. Underdog.io observed that clients rarely state root causes; they describe symptoms. "Automate document processing" is a symptom. Your first job is to turn it into measurable requirements.

Back to that logistics client. Suppose you sat with the data entry staff for two days and found they spend most of their time manually entering invoices and packing slips from two major suppliers into the ERP. The brief you send back to the client and Sales can be as short as this:

项目简报 — 单据读取 Agent
问题：发票手工录入 ERP
范围：发票 + 装箱单，2 家大供应商
不在范围内：报关单
产出：Agent 向 ERP 写入草稿，由人审批
衡量：每天经 Agent 处理的单据数、人工修正率
移交之后：CS + 客户 IT 团队

Before finalizing, ask three questions in the same meeting. Does the ERP have an API for writing data, or only file imports? Who grants access permissions, and how long does it take? Who on the client side approves drafts each day?

"Out of scope" is the most valuable line in the brief. It locks down the project scope, and when the client asks about customs declarations, Sales has a ready answer: that's a follow-up project, not extra work slipped in on the side.

The metric is production, not demos

Stackmatix draws a clear line between two sets of metrics. The FDE's metrics are the system running in production and the feedback brought back to the product team. Customer Success's metrics are retention, expansion, and net revenue retention (NRR).

Recruiting from Scratch describes FDEs as typically embedded with client teams for anywhere from a few weeks to a few months, responsible for deployment and customization, and for keeping systems running smoothly after the sale. In other words, a demo that works in a conference room doesn't count as done. Done means the data entry staff use the agent every morning without you sitting next to them.

Product feedback is the other half of the metric. Suppose both suppliers send skewed scanned PDFs, and the agent keeps misreading the quantity column. You patch it for this client, but you must also write a clear ticket for the product team, because the next client will very likely hit the same problem.

Renewal isn't your job, but it depends on you

At many companies, CS and Technical Account Managers (TAMs) own long-term client relationships, track client health, and handle renewals. Even so, adoption, satisfaction, and renewal often still appear in FDE performance metrics.

That's not a contradiction. FDE effectiveness directly affects renewal rates and expansion opportunities: CS sits at the renewal negotiation table, but what they bring is evidence that the client is using the systems you built.

So the handover is a deliverable, not a formality. When the work is done and the FDE hands the client relationship back, it should come with a record: what was built, what the client should do next, and the follow-up work.

For this logistics client, the handover document should include the number of documents the agent processes daily, the ticket for the skewed PDFs, and customs declarations flagged as a candidate for the next project.

Four mistakes that derail projects

First, holding the client relationship in your own hands. If the client still comes to you for everything after launch, CS can't see the client's health, and you have no time for the next client.

Second, no "out of scope" line in the brief. Every meeting, the scope grows a little, and by week five you have three half-finished projects, none in production.

Third, measuring by demos instead of usage. A smooth demo reassures everyone, but it can't tell CS whether the client actually uses the system every day.

Fourth, handing over only a code repository with no next steps for the client. Then CS walks into the renewal meeting empty-handed, and no one proposes follow-up projects.

Rewrite your resume around the lifecycle, not the tech stack

When FDE recruiters read a resume, they're looking for signs that you've been accountable for outcomes. A sentence like "Built an LLM-based document processing agent for a logistics client" only shows you know how to use tools.

Using the hypothetical example above, you could rewrite it as: "Led a 6-week project for a logistics client: scoped to 2 document types, launched the agent to production in week 4, 70% of invoices from 2 major suppliers now processed by the agent, handed over to CS with 3 follow-up items." The numbers must be your own real data, but the sentence structure stays the same.

If you've never worked on-site with a client, you can apply this framework to internal projects too: who the users are, how long until production launch, how many people actually use it, and who takes over after handover. The last two are usually exactly what's missing from developer resumes.

This week's exercise: take a project you're currently working on and write a seven-line project brief following the pattern above, with "out of scope" and "after handover" as required lines. If you can't fill in the last line, you'll know who gets the phone call when the system breaks.
