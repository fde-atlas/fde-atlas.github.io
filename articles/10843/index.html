Also called FDE, Palantir is building products, while teams in China are burning through their youth.

Original title: 同样叫 FDE，Palantir 在做产品，国内团队在消耗青春

Summary

The author argues that most Chinese FDE teams fail not because of the job title but because of accounting: their headcount sits under project delivery costs, so KPIs prioritize acceptance and payment, leaving no room to turn field experience into product. Palantir succeeds because it places forward deployed engineering in the engineering organization under the CTO, treating delivery spend as R&D investment that feeds the platform. The piece also criticizes domestic use of FDE as marketing to inflate delivery quotes, and offers three questions to judge whether an FDE role is real: reporting line, share of reusable assets in evaluation, and whether field requests have an SLA into product planning.

Full text

The debate over real vs. fake FDEs has never been a word game about changing job titles—it's a question of which line item the cost gets booked under.

The purpose of this article is not to teach you any skills or provide interview materials; it is purely my personal, subjective take on the current blind herd-following around the FDE phenomenon in China

Target audience: AI industry practitioners, job seekers for FDE roles, enterprise technology decision-makers, and industry observers following AI implementation.

Most people only see domestic vendors uniformly adopting the FDE title and mock it as "rebranded on-site staffing." But few point out the core issue: 90% of domestic FDE teams are doomed from day one of the project to never achieve a true closed loop—because from the very start, they are booked under "project delivery costs" rather than "product R&D investment."

How you book the accounts determines behavioral logic. When FDE headcount is counted as delivery cost, the team's first KPI is on-time acceptance and fast payment collection, and knowledge accumulation is always an extra burden that eats into the schedule. Frontline engineers aren't unwilling to feed insights back—it's that project closing deadlines loom and client change requests keep coming, leaving them no time to even write a complete retrospective, let alone abstract scenarios into reusable components that flow back into the product roadmap.

More realistically, product teams have their own priorities and OKRs—why should they take on the scattered requirements thrown over by delivery teams? Without organizational mandate and SLA agreements, so-called feedback relies entirely on personal favors.

Palantir's FDE model works not because its engineers are better, but because it fundamentally places forward deployed engineering within the engineering organization, with the CTO overseeing both the platform and the frontline. The pitfalls the frontline encounters are the product's source of requirements, and delivery investment is part of R&D investment.

They use one client project after another to feed and strengthen an increasingly powerful platform foundation; many of our teams use one project after another to burn through batches of engineers' youth, and when a project ends, nothing remains except the payment collected.

More ironic still, the greatest use of the FDE concept in China is often as marketing packaging to inflate delivery quotes. Repackage a 20,000 person-month implementation project as "deep co-creation with Forward Deployed Engineers," and the quote can double.

As for what gets accumulated after delivery or what gets iterated in the product, nobody really cares. The hotter the concept, the more willing clients are to pay for an "advanced model," and the more motivated vendors are to rebrand and raise prices—everyone's happy, except two parties who suffer: the engineers who thought they'd learn something real, and the clients who thought they'd bought advanced capabilities.

A word of honest advice for frontline engineers: to judge whether an FDE role is the real thing or a pit of fire, don't ask about salary—just ask three questions:

Does this team report to the head of product or the head of delivery?

What proportion of the performance metrics is reusable assets?

Is there a clear SLA for frontline requirements to enter the product roadmap?

If these three questions can't be answered, no matter how impressive the pitch sounds, it's essentially high-end on-site staffing.

What AI implementation lacks has never been on-site headcount, but the organizational capability to institutionalize and accumulate frontline experience. If you can't learn to do this accounting, no matter how many trendy titles you adopt, it's just outsourcing wearing a new coat.
