Clarifying questions for an FDE decomposition interview

FDE Interview Prep · 2026-09-25 · 13 min read

Summary

This post offers a question bank for the clarifying phase of an FDE decomposition interview. Its core rule: only ask questions whose answers would change your first version; state everything else as an assumption. Questions fall into four ordered areas—goal, users and deciders, data, and constraints—each with examples showing which choice the answer flips. It lists wasteful questions and habits to avoid, citing Palantir's careers guidance, Blind reports from former employees, OpenAI's Colin Jarvis, and Ramp's Leo Mehr. The author recommends finishing questions by minute eight of a 45-minute case, committing to a first version by minute ten, and closing with a template sentence before building, demonstrated on a food bank spoilage case.

Key points

  • Ask only questions whose answers change your first version.
  • Order questions by goal, users, data, then constraints.
  • Last question by minute eight; first version by minute ten.
  • When told 'you decide', choose, justify, and label what flips.
  • Sources include Palantir guidance, Blind reports, OpenAI and Ramp practitioners.

The interviewer reads you a one-line problem and waits. Ask too few questions and you spend the round solving the wrong problem; ask too many and the clock is gone before you have built anything. This post is the question bank for that moment, and it sits inside the decomposition interview guide, which covers the whole round.

The short answer: ask only the questions whose answers would change your first version. Take them from four areas, in the order our method uses: the goal, the users and who decides, the data, and the constraints. Say an assumption out loud for everything else. Then say one sentence that ends clarifying and starts the build.

Why the first questions shape the whole round

A decomposition prompt is a request, not a requirement. “A regional food bank says donated food spoils before it reaches families” could be a warehouse problem, a routing problem, a forecasting problem or a pantry problem. Each one leads to a different build. Your first questions decide which of those you spend the round on.

Palantir says both halves of this out loud. Its careers site tells onsite candidates to talk through how they plan to approach analytical and technical problems and to ask questions if necessary. Our rule for every question

Before you ask, name the choice the answer would flip. If you cannot name one, don’t ask it: state an assumption instead. This test is ours, not an interviewer’s rubric, and it is the first step of our decomposition method. It cuts wasted questions before you say them.

Questions about the goal

Start here. The goal decides what “done” means.

  • “If this works, I’d expect fewer pounds binned. Is that the win, or is it more families fed?” Flips the whole target. The two lead to different builds.
  • “When you say spoils, what counts?” Flips the metric. Food binned in the warehouse and food that arrives too short-dated to hand out are two different losses.
  • “How do you measure it today, if at all?” Flips your first week. A number that already exists is your baseline; no number means your first version starts by collecting one.
  • “Why now?” Flips the scope. A new grocery partnership, a grant report or a bad month each point at a different slice.

Notice the first one offers a guess. An open “what does success look like?” hands the work back to the interviewer.

“Why now?” comes straight from how FDEs work. Ramp’s Director of Engineering, Leo Mehr, said in an AI Engineer talk published in July 2026 that when a sales rep urgently demands an integration, a well-trained pauses to ask what is driving the urgency. Questions about the users and who decides

The goal says what must change. The users say who has to change their day for it to happen, and the decider says whose “yes” you need.

  • “Who would use the first version, and what do they do today?” Flips the interface. A warehouse manager on a tablet and an operations director reading a weekly report need different things.
  • “Who owns this problem and the budget?” Flips who you design for when two groups want different things.
  • “Who else loses if we change this?” Flips your guardrail. If donors see their gifts binned, they may stop giving, so pounds received becomes a number you must not hurt.
  • “Is there anyone whose sign-off could stop it?” Flips the order of work. A compliance team, a union rule or a clinical lead can make part of the problem off limits.

For an AI product prompt, also ask: “Is a wrong answer worse than no answer?” It flips everything about review, fallback and how much the model decides alone.

Say the prompt is “support wants an AI to answer refund emails.” If a wrong refund costs money and a slow one costs only patience, your first version drafts replies for a person to approve rather than sending them.

Questions about the data

In our practice cases, the twist often hides in the data: the request assumes data that is missing, late or owned by someone who has not agreed to share it.

First Round Review quoted Palantir’s former FDE recruiting lead giving this as an example scenario: explain insider trading to the candidate, then ask them to design a solution, including what data they would need and what they would ask the customer. Source 8So You Want to Hire a Forward Deployed EngineerPublisherFirst Round ReviewSource typenews report On Blind in July 2022, a poster who had sat a Palantir interview wrote that “clarifying the question and data schema/source is super important.” Source 3Palantir FDSE Interview (Blind)PublisherBlind (Teamblind)Source typecandidate report on BlindSource 4Update: interview experience - Palantir new grad FDSE interview (Blind)PublisherBlind (Teamblind)Source typecandidate report on Blind

  • “What records already exist about this?” Flips build versus collect. An inventory system that logs arrivals and shipments already tells you how long food sits.
  • “Who owns that data, and can we get it this week?” Flips your first version. Data behind a slow approval means your first version runs on something else.
  • “How fresh is it?” Flips batch versus live. A nightly export is fine for a daily pick list and useless for a same-hour alert.
  • “Can I see one real record?” Flips your assumptions. One example row tells you more about fields, gaps and free text than any description.

For enterprise prompts, add “Which system is the source of truth when two disagree?” On the hospital readmissions question, that could be the admissions record and the discharge notes telling different stories about the same stay. For AI prompts, add “Do we have examples with the right answer attached?” The first flips your joins; the second flips whether you can measure the model at all.

Questions about constraints: time, money and rules

Constraints matter only when they rule something out.

  • “Is anything off limits to automate?” Flips the design. A rule that a person must approve each decision turns an automated system into a review queue.
  • “Can we spend on new tools, or only use what you already pay for?” Flips build versus reuse. If the food bank already pays for a warehouse app with a disposal log nobody uses, turning it on is the first version.
  • “What has already been tried?” Flips your pitch. Proposing the thing that failed last year costs you the room.
  • “Is there a deadline that isn’t moving?” Flips the size of the first version, not its shape.

Ask a constraint question as a choice, not an open door. “Is there a budget?” flips nothing yet. “Can we buy scales, or only use the tablets you have?” flips the plan.

Questions that waste the clock

These sound diligent and change nothing. Each one has a fix.

Instead of Do this
“What’s the budget?” Ask the spend choice it would decide
“What tech stack do you use?” Assume a common one, unless integration is the problem
“Any other constraints?” Ask about the one rule that could rule out your idea
“How many requests per second?” Ask only once you know it’s a live system
“What should success look like?” Propose a metric, then ask if it’s right
“So you want less spoilage, right?” Restate with a choice: “less binned here, or less that arrives too short-dated?”

Three habits waste time too:

  • Stacking questions. Five questions in one breath get one vague answer. Ask one, listen, then use the answer in your next question.
  • Edge cases before the core flow. “What about donations that arrive on a holiday?” can wait until the main path exists.
  • Asking what you could assume. If any reasonable answer leads to the same first version, say “I’ll assume X” and keep moving.

The mirror mistake is not asking at all. The decomposition interview mistakes post covers that one and how to recover mid-round.

The sentence that ends clarifying and starts building

Stop asking when the next unknowns are cheap to check in the first week of real work, or when two answers in a row have not changed your plan. Our rule of thumb, from our lesson on clarifying before you solve: in a 45-minute case, ask your last question by minute eight and commit to a first version by minute ten. Then close the phase in one sentence, so the interviewer knows you have moved on and what you are building on.

The template:

“I have enough to start. I’ll build for [user] to move [metric]. I’m assuming [A] and [B]; if either is wrong, I’ll say what changes. Here’s the first version.”

Here is what that looks like on the food bank prompt:

You: When you say spoils, what counts: food binned in the warehouse, or food that reaches a pantry too close to its date to hand out? Interviewer: Mostly what we throw out here.

Changed: the target is warehouse loss, measured in pounds discarded.

You: Who would use the first version day to day? Interviewer: The warehouse manager. The operations director owns the budget.

Changed: build for someone on the floor, and report to the director.

You: If this works, I’d expect less in the dumpster. Is there anything it must not hurt? Interviewer: Less in the dumpster, without turning donors away.

Changed: pounds received and distributed become the guardrail.

Three questions, three changes. The data questions I turn into assumptions:

“I have enough to start. I’ll build for the warehouse manager, to cut pounds discarded at each stage, with pounds received and distributed as the guardrails. I’m assuming the inventory system records when each lot arrives and ships, and that discards aren’t recorded by reason today. If discards are already recorded, I skip the new form. Here’s the first version: a weigh-in at the disposal point that writes the stage and the reason.”

That lands the point Palantir makes: arrive at a concrete approach and deliver a functioning idea first. When the interviewer says ‘you decide’

Sometimes the answer is “you tell me” or “whatever you think.” Treat it as a prompt to decide, not a trap: the answer probably isn’t in the case, so choosing is the useful move.

Use the same shape every time: choose, give one reason, label it, and say what flips.

“Then I’ll assume the food bank can’t buy new software this year, because that’s the cheaper guess to get wrong. If that’s wrong, a vendor tool goes on the list before a custom form.”

Offer two options only when the choice is expensive to reverse, and even then say which one you’d take. Don’t ask the same question again in different words. And write the assumption where you can both see it, so you can come back to it when a later answer changes it.

A quick check before your next round

Clarifying, in order

  • For each question, the choice its answer flips
  • A goal question that gives you a metric
  • A user question that names who decides
  • A data question: what exists, who owns it, how fresh
  • A constraint question, only if it could rule something out
  • Your last question by minute eight
  • Every other unknown said as “I’m assuming X; if not, Y changes”
  • The closing sentence, out loud, then the first version

Questions people ask

What if the interviewer won’t answer my clarifying questions?

Treat that as an invitation to decide. State the assumption you are making, say why it is reasonable, note what would change if it were wrong, and move on to a first version.

Why do clarifying questions matter so much for FDE roles?

Because the job starts from vague requests. How long should I spend on clarifying questions?
