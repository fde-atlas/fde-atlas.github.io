Lean is an English adjective meaning "slim" or "waste-free." In the context of software development, Lean Software Development can be defined as "a development philosophy that eliminates all waste that does not create value and minimizes lead time to delivery to customers."

Lean development was systematized by the husband-and-wife team Mary Poppendieck and Tom Poppendieck. In 2003, they published the book "Lean Software Development: An Agile Toolkit," translating manufacturing philosophy into software development. The origin of their thinking lies in the "Toyota Production System (TPS)" established by Toyota Motor Corporation in Japan.

The Toyota Production System (TPS) is a manufacturing philosophy built by Taiichi Ohno starting in the 1950s, with "Just-in-Time (JIT)" and "Jidoka (automation with a human touch)" as its two pillars. The core of TPS was the thorough elimination of the 7 types of waste (overproduction, waiting, transportation, over-processing, inventory, motion, and defects) lurking in manufacturing lines.

The Poppendiecks applied this philosophy to software development, reinterpreting things like "unstarted code," "excessive documentation," and "pull requests in a waiting state" as "waste." This shift in perspective is the starting point of lean development.

Additionally, in 2007, Eric Ries proposed "Lean Startup," applying lean development thinking to startup management, and the concept of lean spread beyond engineering to business as a whole (explained in detail in Chapter 2).

Lean development consists of the following 7 Principles of Lean Software Development. Each is not an independent concept but part of an organic system where they complement one another.

In modern software development, the assumption that "requirements are fixed from the start" has collapsed. The speed of market changes, the diversification of user behavior, and competitors' moves—all of these increase uncertainty.

If you proceed sequentially through "requirements definition → design → implementation → testing → release" as in waterfall development, gaps with market needs tend to emerge by the time of release. Lean development confronts this uncertainty head-on with the philosophy of "form hypotheses, try small, learn, and improve."

The following three points are particularly noteworthy.

  • A great fit for the context of DX promotion: In digitizing existing operations, requirements are fluid, so Lean's "defer commitment" and "fast delivery" prove powerful
  • Accelerating development cycles with AI and LLMs: With generative AI advancing code generation and test automation, the barriers to realizing Lean's "deliver fast" principle are lowering
  • Applicable from startups to large enterprises: The lean startup approach using MVPs has also been applied to internal new business ventures (intrapreneurship)

This chapter covers the point where most people get confused. The four concepts have a hierarchical structure of "philosophy → framework → method," and understanding it resolves the confusion all at once.

Key clarification: Lean development and Agile development have a "sibling-like relationship"; neither is a superset of the other. Lean development traces its origins to the Toyota Production System, while Agile development began with the 2001 "Agile Manifesto." While the two overlap philosophically, each has evolved independently.

Common misconception: The belief that "Lean development is an older method than Agile, so there's no need to use it" is wrong. Lean thinking (especially waste elimination and whole-system optimization) still works powerfully today, complementing Agile's weaknesses (local optimization and a focus on velocity alone).

This is another pair that is easily confused. In short, "Agile is the philosophy, and Scrum is a concrete framework for realizing Agile."

Agile methods other than Scrum include Kanban (visual task management derived from Lean), XP (Extreme Programming) (focused on technical practices such as pair programming and TDD), and SAFe (Scaled Agile Framework) (for large organizations).

Lean Startup is a business development methodology proposed by Eric Ries in his 2011 book *The Lean Startup*. It applies Lean development thinking to business model validation, repeating the cycle of "quickly releasing a product built with minimal features (MVP), learning from user reactions, and improving."

An MVP (Minimum Viable Product) is "a product with the minimum features necessary to validate a hypothesis." By testing the market before building a polished product, it prevents wasteful development.

From here, we'll dig deeper into the seven principles introduced in Chapter 1 at the practical level. Rather than simply introducing concepts, we'll focus on "how to actually use them in projects."

In Lean development, "waste" is defined by translating the seven wastes of the Toyota Production System into software development terms.

Waste in TPS (manufacturing)Translated to software development
OverproductionDeveloping features no one uses, creating unnecessary documentation
WaitingWaiting for code reviews, approvals, or deployments
TransportationHandoffs of information, manual data integration
OverprocessingOver-engineering, unnecessary refactoring
InventoryUntouched tickets, work-in-progress code (WIP)
MotionTask switching, inefficient tool operation
DefectsBugs, technical debt, misunderstood specifications

Practical tip: WIP (Work In Progress) limits are the waste-elimination measure with the fastest results. By setting an upper limit on the number of "in progress" tasks on a Kanban board, you prevent the team from "spreading itself thin" and help it focus on completing one item at a time. This can easily be implemented with GitHub Projects or Jira's Kanban view.

"Build Quality In" means embedding mechanisms that create quality within the development process itself, rather than leaving quality assurance to the QA team "after the fact."

Here are the specific practices:

  • TDD (Test-Driven Development): Write test code before implementation. Since the code grows in a "state protected by tests," regressions drop dramatically.
  • Pair Programming: Writing code with two people generates real-time code reviews, preventing bugs that would otherwise be found later.
  • CI/CD (Continuous Integration/Continuous Delivery): Set up a mechanism where automated tests run every time code is merged. GitHub Actions and CircleCI are typical examples.
  • Establishing a Definition of Done: Explicitly document "what counts as done" as a team and share quality standards with everyone.

Case study: A toB SaaS startup reported that after introducing TDD, the frequency of production incidents dropped by about 60% compared to before. Although it involves initial costs, it can greatly reduce the cost of fixing bugs in later stages.

"Create Knowledge" is a particularly unique principle among lean development principles. It views software development itself as "a learning process for dealing with uncertainty."

The key to practice is combining synchronous and asynchronous learning.

  • Synchronous: Pair programming, mob programming (the whole team works on a single task), sprint retrospectives
  • Asynchronous: Recording architectural decisions with ADRs (Architecture Decision Records), maintaining knowledge bases with wikis or Notion, and recording intent through code comments

Failure pattern: On teams with a "just rush to implement" culture, learning stays inside individual heads, and knowledge disappears when members leave. Lean development emphasizes documenting and sharing learning as a team asset.

"Defer Commitment" is one of the principles that runs counter to intuition. We tend to think "the faster the decision, the better," but lean development says "postpone irreversible decisions until the information needed to make them is available."

The concept that expresses this way of thinking is the Last Responsible Moment. The idea is to hold off on decisions until "the last moment at which not deciding would negatively impact the project," allowing you to make the best decision based on more information.

Practical examples:

  • Database selection (RDB vs NoSQL): Don't decide hastily before requirements are settled; validate through prototyping and then decide.
  • Microservices vs monolith: Start with a monolith and consider splitting when scaling issues actually become visible (the so-called "modular monolith" strategy).
  • Cloud vendor selection: Compare multiple vendors through a PoC (proof of concept) before deciding on the production configuration.

"Deliver Fast" does not mean "just build quickly." It means shortening the delivery cycle in order to shorten feedback loops.

Here are representative measures for shortening lead time.

"Respect People" is the most "cultural" of the seven principles. Lean development is based on the premise that engineers on the front lines hold the most detailed knowledge, and it prioritizes the team's autonomous judgment over top-down directives.

This principle translates into the following concrete codes of conduct:

  • Delegation to the team rather than task assignment: The product owner decides "what to build (What)" and leaves "how to build it (How)" to the engineering team.
  • Ensuring psychological safety: A culture where "reporting a bug gets you blamed" lowers quality. Foster a culture that treats failure as learning.
  • Regular 1-on-1s and retrospectives: Provide forums to separately discuss individual growth issues and team improvement issues.
  • Involvement in engineers' career path design: Create an environment where engineers can have ownership of the product, rather than being mere "people who write code."

"Optimize the Whole" is the idea that optimizing only a particular team or process is meaningless if the overall value stream is congested.

The following cases are commonly seen as typical "local optimization traps":

  • The development team's velocity is high, but QA is a bottleneck and releases are delayed.
  • The frontend can be developed quickly, but backend APIs are always delayed due to dependencies.
  • Individual engineers are excellent, but deployment permissions are concentrated in a few people, stalling releases.

The practical tool for solving these is Value Stream Mapping. It visualizes all steps until value reaches the customer (coding → review → testing → approval → deployment) and identifies where waiting time occurs.

Lean × Agile combination patterns: In actual practice, the combination of "the Scrum framework + Kanban WIP limits + Lean waste-elimination philosophy" is particularly effective. This is called Scrumban, and it is widely adopted by development teams from startups to large enterprises.

Case 1: Manufacturing × Lean Development (SIer affiliated with a major manufacturer)

An in-house system development team at a major manufacturer transitioned from waterfall development to Lean × Agile. Using value stream mapping to identify bottlenecks, they found that about 40% of the overall lead time was spent waiting for code reviews. By limiting WIP to 2 items per person and introducing a pair-review system, they shortened the release cycle from 4 weeks to 2 weeks.

Case 2: Startup × Lean Startup (BtoC SaaS)

When a Japanese fintech startup developed an MVP for a new feature, they released only the core functionality in the first 3 sprints (6 weeks) and measured behavioral data from 100 real users. As a result, they discovered that the usage rate of the "notification feature" was 5 times higher than the originally anticipated "dashboard feature," and immediately concentrated development resources on expanding the notification feature. Compared to developing all features over 6 months with waterfall, they are estimated to have reduced customer acquisition cost by about 35%.

Case 3: Large Enterprise × SAFe (Scaled Agile Framework)

In large-scale projects where multiple Scrum teams collaborate on development, there are cases where SAFe (Scaled Agile Framework) was adopted and Lean portfolio management principles were incorporated to reduce waste in budget allocation.

Failures in introducing Lean development and Agile share typical common patterns.

Failure Pattern 1: Scrum in form only (Scrum-but)

Daily scrums are held but become mere progress report meetings. Customers and stakeholders do not attend sprint reviews. The same issues come up in every retrospective.

→ Countermeasure: Clarify the Scrum Master's role and promote the team's autonomous improvement. In retrospectives, decide on action items and make sure they are implemented in the next sprint.

Failure Pattern 2: Lack of alignment in upstream processes

The development team operates in an Agile manner, but the requirements definition, contracting, and budget approval flows remain waterfall-style.

→ Countermeasure: Provide Agile education to product owners and stakeholders, and review contract formats to Agile-compatible types (e.g., quasi-mandate contracts).

Failure Pattern 3: Wrong direction in waste elimination

Misunderstanding "reducing meetings is Lean" and omitting even important retrospectives and design discussions.

→ Countermeasure: Clearly distinguish between "waste that does not create value" and "investment that creates value." Position activities related to learning cycles not as waste but as investment that "generates knowledge."

As companies accelerate their DX initiatives, the proportion of projects based on Agile and Lean development is rapidly increasing. According to surveys by the Information-technology Promotion Agency (IPA), a Japanese independent administrative agency, the adoption rate of Agile development is rising year by year, and the trend toward in-housing and Agile adoption has become irreversible even in large enterprises.

The following factors underlie this trend.

  • Acceleration of DX in-housing: More companies are seeking to break free from dependence on external vendors, and having in-house development teams that can work in an Agile manner has become a management challenge
  • Spread of cloud-native and CI/CD: Infrastructure as code (IaC) and deployment automation have dramatically lowered the cost of realizing the "deliver fast" principle
  • Expansion of the startup ecosystem: The growth of VC (venture capital) investment has led to a rapid increase in startups adopting Lean Startup methods

Engineers and PMs who can practically apply frameworks such as Lean development, Agile, and Scrum are highly valued as "immediately effective talent who can participate from upstream processes." This is a major asset when going independent as a freelancer.

There are three reasons why Lean and Agile skills are effective for freelancers seeking to win high-rate projects.

1. More opportunities to participate in upstream processes

Engineers and PMs who can engage in upstream processes—such as requirements definition, process design, and team building—rather than simple implementation work have high market value, and their rates naturally rise. Lean's philosophy of "optimizing the whole" and the perspective of "eliminating waste" directly translate into stronger proposal capabilities in upstream processes.

② It leads to continuous project acquisition

Since agile development assumes continuous cycles, projects tend to become long-term engagements once you join them. By building trust as a member of a Scrum team, your chances of contract renewal increase.

③ You are valued in the context of "organizational transformation"

DX promotion projects require not only technical skills but also "people who can transform teams." Freelancers with experience in agile coaching and as Scrum Masters are highly valued as "transformation drivers from an external perspective" that in-house staff cannot provide. To fully leverage the combined effect of "Lean" and "Agile," the relationship with "Design Thinking" is also essential to understand.

"Design Thinking" is a way of thinking that involves observing consumers and users to understand their needs and derive the ideas necessary to create new value and products.

Abilities cited include "problem-solving thinking," "prototyping," and "new values." It is a way of thinking that finds meaning in things, frames problems, explores latent needs, and explores people's problems and solutions.

It is not a choice between "Lean" or "Agile"—combining them, and adding "Design Thinking" on top, amplifies the effect.

The three methods share commonalities in their processes. They all share the approach of "define the problem → understand the problem → form a hypothesis → prototype → verify the hypothesis → analyze results → adjust and improve," aiming to enable rapid testing of problem-solving proposals using the shortest and simplest methods possible.

While the thinking is common, the means of verification differ.

Lean: Online ads, service LPs, and other materials suited to test marketing Agile: An MVP with the minimum necessary features Design Thinking: Sketches and other visuals that convey the image at a glance

The reason each produces different outputs is that the issues to be verified and the situations in which they are used differ. Lean was created, primarily around startups, as a process for addressing business challenges and understanding user needs.

Agile was created, centered on engineers, as a process for solving product challenges. Design Thinking was created, centered on designers, as a process for verifying challenges faced by general users.

When choosing among "Lean," "Agile," and "Design Thinking," it is best to select based on what you want to verify and what problems you want to solve.

Lean development: Whether your idea matches the market. Use Lean development when there is a solid market for your service and you want to discover new possibilities.

Agile development: Whether the product delivers benefits to users. Use Agile development when you want to get the product to users quickly and confirm how it actually feels to use.

Design Thinking: Whether the service has value for users. Use Design Thinking when you want to understand users' latent needs, communicate ideas in an easy-to-understand visual way, and gauge user response.

Once you understand the differences in focus of value, choose the method based on what you want to measure. In actual product development, the methods overlap considerably, so they need to be used in combination. You will need to build a framework that allows flexible adaptation.

Finally, let us summarize the content covered in this article.

What Is Lean Development: A development philosophy originating from the Toyota Production System that pursues "eliminating waste that creates no value" and "optimizing the whole" in software development. Its seven principles (eliminate waste, build quality in, create knowledge, defer commitment, deliver fast, respect people, optimize the whole) form the core of the system.

Summary of the Relationships Among the Methods:

QuestionAnswer
Is Agile or Lean the higher-level concept?Neither. They are sibling concepts that developed independently while sharing philosophical overlap.
Is Scrum something separate from Agile?Scrum is one of the frameworks for practicing Agile.
Is Lean Startup the same as Lean development?No. Lean Startup is the application of Lean philosophy to business model validation.
Why build an MVP?To validate hypotheses at minimal cost and prevent wasteful development.

Lean development, Agile, and Scrum are universal development philosophies that remain applicable wherever you work or whichever industry you move to once you have mastered them. Engineers and PMs who deeply understand these and can put them into practice gain a powerful asset: not only for career advancement within a company, but also for continuously winning high-rate projects as a freelancer.
