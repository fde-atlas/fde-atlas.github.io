How to Hand Over an FDE Project: A Handover Guide for Forward Deployed Engineers

Original title: FDE 项目交接怎么做：前线部署工程师交接指南

FDE Times · 2026-10-21 · 5 min read

Summary

Drawing on Tandem, PostHog, Rocketlane, and ClientSuccess, this guide explains how forward deployed engineers should hand off projects mid-engagement. Its core argument: handover documents are point-in-time snapshots that quickly go stale, so engineers should instead share links to continuously updated customer records (ticket boards, CRM, repos) plus a short delta note covering commitments, blockers, risks, and assigned tasks. Since business relationships stay with sales and customer success, handoffs involve three parties, not two. The introduction call should be led by the incoming engineer while the departing one observes, letting the client verify the new person understands the work. The article also lists common mistakes and suggests framing handover experience for resumes and interviews.

Key points

  • Static handover docs go stale; share living record links plus a short delta note instead.
  • Delta notes should capture commitments, blockers, risks, and tasks with owners and deadlines.
  • Business relationships stay with sales/CS, so handoffs involve three parties, not two.
  • The incoming engineer should lead the client introduction call; the departing one only observes.
  • Common mistakes: treating merged code as done, or still answering client DMs after leaving.

Customers only trust a successor who has proven themselves: at handoff, share a link to a continuously updated record, plus a short incremental brief.

Key takeaways

  • When engineers are swapped carelessly, a project quietly resets to the starting point while the customer believes it's already halfway done.
  • A handoff document is only a snapshot of a single moment. Instead, hand over a link to a continuously updated customer record, along with an incremental brief and clearly defined tasks.
  • Trust must be earned by yourself: at the introduction meeting, the successor speaks and the outgoing engineer listens.

Imagine this scenario. A data integration project is in its sixth week when the company reassigns you to another customer. You pushed your code, wrote a long README, and said goodbye in the shared Slack channel. Three weeks later, the successor asks the customer the very questions you asked in week one.

An article by Tandem introducing its FDE handoff playbook describes exactly this kind of failure. The project didn't collapse; it quietly restarted, while the customer still believed it was halfway done. From the customer's perspective, their money and time were being charged twice.

When you leave a project is often not up to you. So the skill to practice is not avoiding engineer swaps, but handing off in a way that doesn't make the customer feel pushed back to square one. And the hardest part isn't in the code.

Why long documents won't save you

An engineer's first instinct is to write more. But Tandem points out a weakness: a handoff document is only a snapshot of a single moment, and once the author leaves, no one can verify it. A week later, half of it may already be wrong, and the successor has no way of knowing which half.

Their alternative: hand over a link to a continuously updated customer record, plus a short incremental brief. The link points to the ticket board, the customer record, and the code repository—places that keep updating after you leave. The incremental brief captures the things that exist only in your head.

PostHog's employee handbook has a similar rule for all information that others will need to find again: it should go into durable deliverables, not private Slack messages or threads that become impossible to trace later. Outstanding work should be written as explicit tasks, not buried in paragraphs of prose.

Who actually owns the customer relationship?

Before writing the incremental brief, figure out what you're actually handing over. According to PostHog's employee handbook, the FDE owns the technical outcomes, while the commercial relationship always belongs to the sales and customer success (CS) teams. Once the technical work is done, the FDE hands the customer relationship back to the account owner and documents what was done and why.

That rule targets project wrap-up, but it also offers a lesson for mid-project engineer swaps: the commercial relationship sits with the account owner from start to finish.

So a mid-project handoff usually involves three parties, not two: you, the successor, and the account owner. If the account owner is absent, the customer can easily end up hearing two versions of "who's actually running our project now."

What does an incremental brief look like?

Back to a hypothetical example. Lan is an FDE integrating a logistics company's warehouse management system with her company's product. Starting next Monday, Minh takes over. Lan's handoff has two parts.

The link section is only a few lines: the customer's ticket board, the CRM customer record managed by CS, the code repository, and the page documenting technical decisions. Lan didn't copy any of their contents, because copying would just create another snapshot that quickly goes stale.

The incremental brief follows Rocketlane's advice for handoffs between customer success managers (CSMs): record commitments, blockers, and risks, then list tasks.

ItemExample from Lan's incremental brief
Commitments madePromised the head of operations a pilot run of inventory sync by the end of the month
BlockersThe customer's IT team hasn't yet granted read access to the returns table; the person to follow up with is the sysadmin
RisksThe head of operations was burned by a previous vendor's delays and is highly sensitive to schedule slips
Follow-up tasksThree tickets created, each with an owner and a due date

The "risks" line matters most. The code will never tell Minh that the primary user fears delays above all, and that detail shapes how Minh should open the first call.

The introduction meeting must be led by the successor

Documents can only transfer information. Tandem's article notes that customer relationships are transferred through earned trust, not through a handbook. So they recommend a single introduction meeting, led by the incoming engineer, with the outgoing engineer only listening in.

It sounds backwards at first, since the customer knows the outgoing engineer. But if Lan narrates the whole meeting from start to finish, the customer will keep treating Lan as their point of contact and will keep DMing her afterward. When Minh personally reaffirms the end-of-month commitment, the customer sees that the new person already owns the work. Trust starts building from that moment.

Minh's opening could go like this:

Lan: "Starting next week, Minh will own the integration work. I'm moving to another project, and as before, Ha from CS will remain your contact for contract matters." Minh: "I've gone through all the tickets. As I understand it, the goal is a pilot run of inventory sync by the end of the month, and we're currently blocked on read access to the returns table. Is that right?"

Lan's line accomplishes what Rocketlane recommends: tell the customer why the swap is happening. Minh's confirmation question turns the incremental brief into something the customer can verify on the spot—which is exactly what a document alone cannot do.

Concrete steps

Step one: tell the customer early. ClientSuccess recommends introducing the new person as soon as possible to avoid confusion later, because how the handoff is conducted shapes the entire long-term relationship. A week's notice beats a last-day email.

Step two: the outgoing engineer writes the incremental brief personally. Rocketlane stresses that this document must be prepared by the person doing the handoff. Then clean up Slack DMs and convert every commitment into a ticket. Next, hold a three-party meeting led by the successor. Finally, confirm with the account owner that they know who now owns the technical work.

Common mistakes

The most common mistake is assuming the handoff is done once the code is merged. The second is the outgoing engineer dominating the introduction meeting to avoid awkward silence, making the successor look like an assistant. Harder to spot: the outgoing engineer keeps answering the customer's DMs after reassignment, so the relationship never truly changes hands.

Another mistake is forgetting the account owner. It's easy for an FDE to treat the handoff as a matter between two engineers, but the person who owns the commercial relationship needs to know first.

How this skill shows up on your resume

Prepare a specific story in case an interview asks how you once left a customer mid-project: how you wrote the incremental brief, who led the introduction meeting, and whether the project stayed on schedule.

A single resume line like "handed a customer over to a successor mid-project with no promised milestones delayed" shows you understand a part of this job that few people are taught.

When reading job descriptions, watch for phrases like "ownership," "handover," or "collaboration with CS." They reveal whether the company separates technical work from the commercial relationship. You can ask about it directly in the interview.

When a handoff is done well, the customer barely notices the engineer changed. They only see that the end-of-month milestone still lands on time.
