How to Define P1-P4 Incident Severity Levels: FDE Incident Severity Table

Original title: P1-P4 故障等级怎么定：FDE 事件严重度表

FDE Times · 2026-11-13 · 5 min read

Summary

This guide argues that severity labels like P1-P4 have no industry-wide standard, so without a written agreement, FDEs end up arguing with clients over every bug at 11pm. The author recommends separating severity (measurable impact, e.g. more than 25% of users affected) from priority (response speed), and drafting a four-level table with the client before launch, using scenario-based questions to let the client set thresholds themselves. A worked example uses an insurance deployment with 400 claims adjusters. Common failure modes: labels without numbers, reluctance to downgrade, tables disconnected from alerting, and too many levels.

Key points

  • P1-P4 labels have no universal standard; agree on definitions with the client before launch
  • Severity measures impact; priority measures response speed — keep them separate but on one row
  • Use countable thresholds, e.g. over 25% of affected users defines P1
  • When unsure, classify higher, then downgrade with a recorded reason
  • The table must be wired into alerting and on-call schedules or it becomes worthless

Agree on a four-level table before go-live, so everyone knows which incidents are worth a midnight phone call and which can wait until morning.

Key takeaways

  • There is no universal standard for P1, P2, and P3. If you don't write it down, the customer and your team will each have their own interpretation
  • Every level needs a measurable impact criterion (e.g., more than 25% of users) and a committed response speed
  • When in doubt, classify higher; adjust the level once the situation is clear, and treat reclassification as normal practice

It's 11 p.m. and your phone buzzes. The customer's head of operations messages: "P1, the export report button is misaligned." You open your laptop and confirm the button is indeed a few pixels off, and realize this is the third time this week you've been woken up for an issue that didn't need fixing before morning.

The customer isn't trying to be difficult. They use the term P1 according to their own understanding, while you use your team's. The incident management platform Rootly puts it bluntly: labels like P1/P2/P3 or SEV0/SEV1/SEV2 have no universal standard.

For a Forward Deployed Engineer (FDE), this problem is trickier than for an internal SRE team, because you sit between two organizations. OneUptime writes that without clear definitions, every incident turns into an argument.

The way to avoid this: before go-live, sit down with the customer and write out the four levels from P1 to P4, and have both sides sign off.

One label, two questions

When a customer says "P1," they're actually answering two questions at once: how bad is it, and how fast must it be handled. Rootly separates the two: severity measures the impact of an incident, while priority determines the speed of action. People often conflate these two concepts, and midnight arguments usually start right there.

Severity should be measured in countable terms. PagerDuty recommends that each organization define its own levels, usually based on the percentage of affected users or accounts. OneUptime offers an example threshold for high impact: more than 25% of users affected, or degraded performance of a revenue-generating feature.

How many levels? According to incident.io's experience, 3-5 levels work well; any fewer and you can't distinguish between different incidents. Four levels, P1-P4, fall right in that range, enough to separate "wake someone up now" from "put it in the next iteration."

A table tailored to a specific customer

Suppose you're deploying an agent that reads claims documents for an insurance company, used daily by 400 claims adjusters. For this customer, the 25% threshold means more than 100 people. The table you bring to the meeting could look like the one below, with each row specifying both impact and response speed.

LevelImpactResponseInsurance company scenario example
P1The agent stops running, or more than 100 of the 400 claims adjusters cannot process claimsImmediate response, 24/7, pause planned work, notify management on both sidesAfter a model update, every claim returns an error
P2A smaller group is blocked on a core featureRespond during business hours, queued after P1 work30 people cannot upload a certain type of scanned form
P3Degraded quality, but workarounds existInclude in the regular work schedule; no off-hours calls unless the situation worsensThe agent misclassifies a few rare claims, which reviewers can correct manually
P4Minor or cosmetic issues that don't prevent anyone from using the productOpen a ticket; call no oneThe export report button is a few pixels off

Notice how this table separates the two questions while using a single label: the "Impact" column answers the severity question, and the "Response" column answers the priority question. Putting both on the same row means both sides have agreed in advance that this scale of impact corresponds to this speed of response, and no one needs to renegotiate in the middle of the night.

The "Response" column isn't made up either: it aligns with Rootly's definitions of P1, P2, and P3, with OneUptime's rule that lower levels don't warrant off-hours calls, and with PagerDuty's practice of converting cosmetic issues into tickets.

Notifying management at P1 corresponds to PagerDuty's SEV-1, the incident level that requires public communication and direct contact with executives.

Now plug in the numbers. The form issue blocks 30 people, which is 7.5% of 400, still below the 25% threshold. So it's a P2, even if those 30 people have a rough day.

If the same issue spreads to 120 people, or 30%, it crosses the threshold and becomes a P1. The level is determined by the numbers, not by the tone of the message. As for that misaligned button at 11 p.m., it's a P4: open a ticket and no one gets woken up.

The pre-launch meeting

A drafted table only has value when the customer sets the thresholds themselves. Don't ask "What do you want P1 to be?"—that only invites the answer "everything matters." Ask about people and timing:

You: "If the agent stops running at 3 a.m. on a Sunday, who do you want called? Would you take the call yourself?" Customer: "Of course, the entire claims department would be down." You: "And if only one type of form breaks, blocking about 30 people, should we wake someone up in the middle of the night, or can it wait until Monday morning?" Customer: "Monday morning is fine, as long as someone handles it that day."

After these two answers, the customer has drawn the line between P1 and P2 themselves. All you need to do is record it as a number.

The process goes like this: before the meeting, count the real number of users, identify which features are tied to revenue, and fill in the draft table with examples from the customer's own systems. In the meeting, use the questions above to adjust the thresholds.

Before signing, add two rules at the bottom of the table. The first comes from PagerDuty: when unsure which level an incident belongs to, treat it as the higher level and review it during the retrospective. The second comes from Rootly: the initial classification is only provisional, and adjusting the level is a sign the process is working.

Why do so many tables end up as waste paper?

The most common mistake is having labels without criteria. A row that says "P1: critical" helps no one at midnight, because "critical" is exactly what the two sides are arguing about. Every level needs a number or a specific feature that the on-call engineer can verify within minutes.

The second mistake is reluctance to downgrade. If an incident opened as P1 turns out after 20 minutes to affect only ten people, it should be downgraded to P2, with the reason recorded so both sides can see it. If no one dares to downgrade, the customer learns that labeling something P1 gets people running over. Within weeks, the table becomes useless.

The third mistake is writing the table and then shelving it. It must stay consistent with alerting configurations and on-call schedules. If P3 still triggers a call at 2 a.m., the table is just a promise on paper. Finally, don't add P5 or P6 just because each side wants an exception: with too many levels, the on-call engineer spends time picking labels instead of solving problems.

Turning this table into a career asset

When "on-call," "incident response," or "SLA" appears in an FDE job description, it's the perfect moment to ask in the interview: who is responsible for defining incident levels with the customer? Where is that definition documented? The answer will tell you quite clearly what your on-call nights will look like.

On your resume, a specific statement carries more weight than adjectives: "Co-developed a P1-P4 severity table with the customer based on the percentage of affected users, and wired it directly into the paging configuration." If you have before-and-after data on off-hours call volume, include that too.

In interviews, describe a time you downgraded an incident and how you explained it to the customer.

This four-row table won't make your system fail less often. It only guarantees that the next time your phone buzzes in the middle of the night, it's for something genuinely worth getting up for.
