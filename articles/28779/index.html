What Is the Challenger Sale and How Can FDEs Make Good Use of the Three-Step Method

Original title: 挑战者销售是什么 FDE 如何用好三步法

FDE Times · 2026-11-05 · 5 min read

Summary

This article applies The Challenger Sale to the work of forward deployed engineers, arguing that simply fulfilling customer requirement lists rarely moves metrics leadership cares about. CEB (now part of Gartner) research found relationship builders make up only 7% of top performers while challengers approach 40%. The author maps the book's three techniques to FDE practice: Teach (bring insights tied to how customers save or make money), Tailor (present the same finding differently per stakeholder), and Take Control (push back on weak requirements with options and deadlines). It also warns against gut-based objections, condescension, and opposing without alternatives.

Key points

  • CEB research: challengers are ~40% of top performers, relationship builders only 7%.
  • Teach: insights must connect to customer cost savings or revenue.
  • Tailor: keep a stakeholder map; vary the framing per audience.
  • Take Control: when saying no, offer alternatives and a decision owner.
  • The data measures salespeople, so engineers must adapt it themselves.

Key Takeaways

  • CEB's research (now part of Gartner) divides B2B sales into five profiles. Challengers perform best, while relationship builders make up only 7% of top performers
  • Being agreeable isn't enough: insights must change how the customer thinks about saving or making money
  • For FDEs, the three moves are: bring insights from data, present the same finding differently to different people, and proactively clear obstacles

In the research behind The Challenger Sale, relationship builders ranked last: they made up only 7% of top performers. Challengers, who dare to push back on customers, accounted for nearly 40%.

At first, this sounds like a sales department matter. But consider a week all too familiar to a Forward Deployed Engineer (FDE): the customer sends a list of requirements, you implement them one by one, the demo goes smoothly, yet months later the project still hasn't moved any number management cares about.

This book explains why simply going along with the customer isn't enough, and offers three moves for doing things differently. Engineers working on-site with customers should read it alongside their technical books, because it fills exactly the gap technical books leave: customer needs discovery.

A sales book built on data

The book's full title is The Challenger Sale: Taking Control of the Customer Conversation, published by Portfolio on November 10, 2011. The original research was conducted by CEB (now part of Gartner), initially as a study on customer loyalty.

The authors found that every B2B seller fits into one of five profiles, with Challengers performing best. The exact percentages vary by sample: nearly 40% comes from the original study, while Challenger Inc.'s website now cites about 54%. So remember the trend, not the precise numbers.

For engineers who believe they're "just not cut out for sales," there's good news: according to the publisher, the traits of Challenger sellers can be replicated and taught to average sellers.

Teach customers what they don't yet know

The first move, Teach, is about bringing insights that change how the customer thinks. Richardson, the firm that trains this methodology, emphasizes that this value lies beyond a list of products and features. The publisher puts it more concretely: it's insight into how the customer can save or make money.

Imagine a logistics company asks you to build an agent that answers "Where is my order?", with one requirement: answer faster. Suppose that after a week of reading ticket logs, you discover most inquiries happen because shipping notification emails are sent too late.

At that point, what's worth bringing to the meeting isn't "the agent is up and running," but a proposal to adjust email timing that reduces inquiries at the source. There's a simple test: if an insight can't be tied to the customer saving or making money, it doesn't carry enough weight.

One finding, many ways to present it

The second move, Tailor, is about adjusting the message to each customer's specific needs and goals. Octave applies the Challenger method to GTM engineers, recommending that insights be adjusted to each stakeholder's situation, and linking Take Control to stakeholder mapping.

The chart at the top of this article shows three ways to present the email-timing finding from the logistics case. The insight itself doesn't change; only the opening line does, depending on what each person is accountable for.

A habit worth building: maintain a stakeholder map and base every progress update on it, rather than sending one generic email to the whole group.

Take control even when saying "not yet"

The third move, Take Control, is about being assertive, pushing back when necessary, and owning the direction. If you're used to waiting for tickets to be assigned, this is probably the hardest move to practice. Challenger Inc. later added a fourth skill, Constructive Tension, a name that captures that uncomfortable moment: saying directly what the customer doesn't yet want to hear.

A 2026 practical guide to the FDE job puts it in industry terms: you're not there to accept every field and every button the customer asks for, but to push back on requirements that don't hold up.

Suppose access to production data has been stuck for two weeks. The order-taker writes "blocked" and waits. The Challenger explains the cost of waiting, offers two options (anonymized data or a staging environment), and asks who will make the call by Friday.

The order-taking FDE

  • Implements exactly what the customer's requirements document says
  • Sends the same generic progress update to the whole group
  • Writes "blocked" and waits

The Challenger FDE

  • Brings insights from the customer's own data
  • Presents the same finding differently to different people
  • Offers options with a decision deadline attached

Challenging isn't arguing

The most common beginner mistake is pushing back on gut feeling. Saying "I don't think this requirement is right" only invites a battle of opinions. Saying "the logs from the past two weeks show most tickets come from late-sent emails" pulls the conversation back to the customer's own data—that's what the book calls Teach.

The second mistake is challenging to the point of condescension, treating the customer as someone who doesn't understand their own business. Remember that Tailor sits between Teach and Take Control: insights should be framed around the listener's goals. The word "constructive" in Constructive Tension is also a reminder: the tension exists to help the customer make a decision, not to help you win an argument.

The third mistake is objecting without offering a way forward. After saying "not yet," always provide an alternative and the person who needs to make the call, as in the data access example above.

Who should read it, and in what order?

Start with the section on the five sales profiles to see which type you lean toward, then read the three moves. After that, check out Challenger Inc.'s page on sales profiles to learn about today's four skills.

Read with some skepticism. The data measures salespeople, not engineers, and translating it to deployment work is up to you. This book suits those who have just moved from software engineer (SWE) to FDE, and anyone who feels they're just building to spec.

For career development, rewrite a line on your resume, changing "implemented X per customer requirements" to a sentence that shows how your insight changed the project's scope. Before interviews, prepare a story: a time you pushed back on a customer requirement, what data you based it on, and what happened as a result.

Before closing the book, do an exercise: take a recent requirement from a customer and write three lines. The first line states what the customer wants, the second states what the data shows the real problem is, and the third offers an option that helps the customer save or make money. If the second line just repeats the first, you haven't found the insight yet.

If a customer only needs someone to build to spec, there's little reason to send an engineer on-site. The reason worth sending one for is pointing out where the requirements are wrong.
