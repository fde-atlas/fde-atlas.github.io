What Is AI Governance? Five Things Companies Should Decide, and Frameworks in Japan and Abroad

Original title: AIガバナンスとは？企業が決めるべき5つのことと、日本・海外の枠組み

Summary

This article defines AI governance as an organizational system deciding who decides, how to protect, and how to keep correcting—not just writing policy documents. The author argues 2026 turned governance from optional to necessary: Japan's AI law took effect, the EU AI Act is being applied in stages, and shadow AI appeared in breach statistics. AI governance is the system an organization defines for who decides what, how it is protected, and how it is continuously improved when using, building, or providing AI. It is not about writing a document of usage rules. A document is only one part of the system.

We see 2026 as the year AI governance shifted from "nice to have" to "essential." In Japan, the AI Act was enacted and the AI Business Guidelines were revised to version 1.2. In Europe, the AI Act began applying in stages, and shadow AI appeared in data breach statistics. Meanwhile, even among companies that have prepared guidelines, nearly half of frontline staff are using AI that is not officially sanctioned by the company.

Here, from the perspective of someone implementing AI in the field, we organize the definition of AI governance, why it became necessary in 2026, the frameworks in Japan and abroad, and the five things companies should actually decide.

What Is AI Governance?

AI governance consists of three layers.

LayerWhat is decidedWhat it takes shape as
PolicyWhy AI is used. Which tasks, which AI, and with what data it may be usedAI usage policy, classification of information that may be input
SystemWho approves and who stops it. How inputs and outputs are recorded and verified. Who to report to when a problem occursApproval flows, authority design, logs, evaluation criteria, incident response procedures
OperationsHow to review the policy and system in line with changes in how AI is usedMonthly reviews, training, audits

In many companies, what is called "governance" is only the first-layer document. Without the second layer, rules depend on people's goodwill. Without the third layer, rules become outdated from the day they are created.

Why It Became Necessary in 2026

1. Employee AI use spread outside of management control

According to a survey by Kadokawa ASCII Research Laboratories (featured in the "AI White Paper 2026," report), even among companies with company-wide guidelines in place, 46.9% of people had experience using non-official AI for work, little different from the 50.0% at companies with only partial guidelines. Even at companies with full company-wide guidelines, 45.1% answered that "rules have not kept up with the reality on the ground."

In a survey by Okta Japan (report), 84.6% of Japanese executives answered that they "can visualize AI tool usage," while 78% of Japanese employees answered that they "cannot find the official AI usage policy." Management thinks it has decided; the frontline doesn't know. This disconnect is the starting point of AI governance.

2. AI appeared in data breach statistics

IBM's "Cost of a Data Breach Report 2026" showed that the share of security incidents involving shadow AI (AI used by employees without permission) doubled from 20% the previous year to 43% (report). In IPA's "Information Security 10 Major Threats 2026," "cyber risks surrounding AI use" entered the organizational category for the first time, at 3rd place.

3. Laws and guidelines moved

In Japan, the AI Act (Act on Promotion of Research and Development and Utilization of AI-Related Technologies, Act No. 53 of 2025) was enacted in 2025, and an AI Strategy Headquarters was established within the Cabinet. On July 14, 2026, the AI Basic Plan (Phase II) was approved by the Cabinet (Cabinet Office). The MIC/METI "AI Business Guidelines" have been revised almost annually, from version 1.0 in April 2024 to version 1.2 on March 31, 2026 (METI).

In Europe, the AI Act entered into force on August 1, 2024, with provisions on prohibited uses applying from February 2, 2025, and obligations for general-purpose AI models applying from August 2, 2025. Japanese companies are also covered if they ship products or services incorporating AI to the EU.

4. AI agents went from reading to acting

The governance required differs between an AI that answers questions and an AI agent that reads and writes internal systems to carry out work. Agents are given authority. If you start using them without deciding the scope of authority granted, how actions are logged, and how to stop them, the exits for leaks increase. Microsoft 365 Copilot's EchoLeak, which I wrote about in "8 Examples of Generative AI Information Leaks," and the intrusion into Hugging Face by an AI agent are problems of this kind.

There are many frameworks, but if a Japanese company wants to organize its internal AI use, building around the 10 guiding principles of the AI Business Guidelines is sufficient. ISO/IEC 42001 and the EU AI Act become necessary at the stage of releasing AI as a product externally.

Five things companies should decide

Even after reading the frameworks, it is hard to know what your company should decide. We believe the following five things are what companies should decide first.

#What to decideSpecificallyWhat happens if it is not decided
1Purpose and permitted scopeWhich tasks, with which AI, and with which data use is allowed. Which tasks will not use AIEnds up at one of two extremes: "anything goes" or "nothing is allowed"
2Responsible parties and decision flowWho approves the use of new AI. Who stops it when a problem occursApplications are cumbersome, and frontline staff drift to personal accounts
3Data and permissionsThe three categories of information that may be entered (public, internal, confidential). Settings ensuring inputs are not used for training. The scope of permissions granted to AI agentsInformation that should not be entered gets entered. Agents can read too much
4Records and evaluationRecords of inputs and outputs. Verification before outputs are used in work. Criteria for what accuracy level warrants production useCauses of problems cannot be traced later. Erroneous outputs enter decision-making
5Review and incident responseMonthly review of usage status. Updating rules. Reporting destinations and initial responseRules become outdated. Small incidents go unreported

Of the five, 1 and 2 are the policy layer, 3 and 4 are the system layer, and 5 is the operations layer. The 10 guiding principles of the AI Business Guidelines work well as checkpoints when filling in these five items. For example, "3. Data and permissions" corresponds to privacy protection and security assurance, and "4. Records and evaluation" corresponds to transparency and accountability.

Why governance ends up as mere "documents," and how to make it effective in the field

When rules are created but not used in the field, it is not because the rules are bad. It is because the official route is less convenient than personal accounts. In a survey by SIGNATE Lab, shadow AI users cited dissatisfaction with the limited functionality of company-standard AI and the hassle of applications (news coverage).

To make governance effective in the field, follow three steps in order.

  • Build from actual conditions. Do not write rules first. Grasp who is entering what into which AI for which tasks. This reveals the tasks where the field actually needs AI
  • Embed into systems. Rather than making "do not enter confidential information" a rule, ensure that tasks handling confidential information can only use the official AI environment. Rather than urging people to "verify outputs," build verification procedures into the workflow templates. Rules protected by systems are stronger than rules people must follow
  • Fix monthly. AI tools and features change every few months. Review usage records and feedback from the field, identify missing official features and new shadow AI, and update the rules

Step 2, "embedding into systems," cannot be done without examining the actual work. We believe this is the dividing line between work that merely creates documents and work that goes into the field and implements.

Where should small and medium-sized enterprises start?

No elaborate structure is needed. The following three steps form the initial shape.

OrderWhat to doEstimated time required
1Understanding actual conditions. Anonymous surveys and an inventory of SaaS, AI features, and browser extensions in use2-3 weeks
2A one-page rule. The three categories of information that may be entered, and which AI may be used for each category1 week
3An official AI environment. Corporate contracts, company accounts, logging, and templates for frequently used tasks1-2 months

We described the approach for step 1 in "What is shadow AI? Risks that bans cannot prevent and countermeasures that work in the field", and an overview of generative AI risks in "Generative AI Security Measures Guide".

Our practice of FDE (Forward Deployed Engineer) is a way of working where engineers go into the field, break down business processes, build AI systems that are actually used, and iterate improvements until they take hold. In terms of AI governance, it plays the role of building the second layer, the "systems," within actual field operations. We wrote about FDE itself in "What is FDE?
