Range by David Epstein: what it teaches FDEs

FDE Times · 2026-10-26 · 6 min read

Summary

This article applies David Epstein's Range (Riverhead Books, 2019) to the FDE role. The book argues early specialization is the exception among top performers, and that most valuable skills develop in "wicked" learning environments where deliberate practice alone fails—client deployment being a prime example. The author suggests separating rule-bound from ambiguous work onsite, distilling client observations into abstract, transferable lessons, and testing fit with the FDE role through a four-week experiment. It warns breadth must rest on real technical depth, not replace it.

Key points

  • Range argues early specialization is the exception among top performers.
  • Deployment is a "wicked" environment where deliberate practice alone falls short.
  • FDEs should distill client observations into name-free, transferable lessons.
  • A four-week experiment can measure your match quality with the FDE role.
  • Breadth rests on technical depth and cannot replace it.

In brief

  • Range (Riverhead Books, 2019) argues that among top performers, early specialization is the exception, not the rule.
  • What an FDE should take from it: most work worth doing sits in "wicked" learning environments, where deliberate practice alone falls short, and deployment is one of them.
  • The book does not dismiss deep expertise, so use it as a lens on the work rather than a license to stay shallow.

In its review of February 14, 2019, Publishers Weekly wrote that anyone without 10,000 hours to devote to a single skill still has reason for hope. Many FDEs need to hear it. They know enough about data, APIs, infrastructure and talking to customers, yet in no single area are they as deep as someone who does only that one thing.

The book is Range: Why Generalists Triumph in a Specialized World by David Epstein, published by Riverhead Books. The hardcover, ebook and audiobook came out on May 28, 2019, and the paperback on April 27, 2021.

It was not written for FDEs. It does, however, supply a vocabulary for explaining why breadth is a real capability, and for choosing what to practice next.

What does the book actually argue?

Penguin Random House sums up the central claim in one sentence: among top performers, early specialization is the exception, not the rule. On his own site, Epstein writes that generalists often find their path late, juggle many interests at once, and are the ones with the advantage in complex, unpredictable fields.

The recurring image is the pairing of Tiger Woods and Roger Federer. Federer played many sports before concentrating on tennis, and according to the Wikipedia summary, Epstein finds that the stories of successful people tend to contain more “Rogers” than “Tigers”.

Publishers Weekly also notes that the book does not wholly dismiss the value of expertise. Read Range as a lens on the work, then, and not as a call to give up depth.

Idea one: deployment is a “wicked” environment

Yevgeniy Brikman, an engineer who reviewed the book, took from it that most of the things we want to be good at sit in “wicked” learning environments. There, deliberate practice alone is not enough; you need breadth.

Take two tasks in the same week. The first is writing a data transformation function that already has a test suite. You run the tests and see red or green at once. The rules are clear.

In the second, the client says they need the dashboard to run faster. Three weeks later you discover the real problem lies in the data-entry process of a department nobody invited to the meetings.

The first thing to do on site is to separate these two kinds of work. Follow the playbook for the rule-bound parts and automate them. For the wicked parts, resist assuming you have understood the requirement, and design a small experiment to check.

Idea two: what travels to the next client is a way of thinking

If the generalist’s advantage lies in unpredictable fields, the practical question for an FDE is what that advantage looks like day to day. The most plausible answer is the ability to carry a lesson from one client to the next.

Follow one lesson across two engagements. At a hospital, the data specification says the “discharge date” field always has a value. When you test the pipeline, you find a large number of records left blank, because nurses enter the date only at the end of their shift.

That is a raw observation, and it holds only for that hospital. The next step is to turn it into a sentence with no proper names left in it: “a data spec describes intent, not reality; to learn the reality, ask who fills in the field and when”.

Six months later you move to a bank. Different table names, different systems, but in the first week you profile the fields the spec calls mandatory, before writing a single line of transformation, and you ask exactly those two questions. A problem that took a full week to surface at the hospital is now one item on a day-one checklist.

Breadth is only valuable when you can distill it into sentences like that. Two FDEs work with the same five clients: the one who records patterns carries five lessons to client number six, while the other has five anecdotes.

Idea three: try the FDE role the way you would try a relationship

The book’s case on career changes turns on “match quality”, which economists define as the degree of fit between a job and the person doing it.

Commoncog stresses that exploration is one of the main benefits of learning, far more than a side dish for fun. With few chances to try things out, many students rush down a narrow path before they know whether it suits them.

Shelf Awareness observes that this very search for fit is what late bloomers who succeed have in common. Brikman frames the advice more practically: treat university and your first jobs like dating, that is, test and learn, instead of plan and execute.

Say you are a backend engineer, hypothetically, weighing the FDE role. Instead of analyzing it, you run a four-week experiment. In week one, sit in on a technical meeting with a client; in week two, build a demo for someone who does not code; in week three, take a customer escalation ticket; in week four, shadow a colleague on an onboarding session.

After each task, write exactly three lines: whether you finished energized or drained, whether the ambiguity made you curious or irritated, and whether you wanted to solve that particular client’s problem or go back to building something shared. Those three lines measure your own match quality, in line with the definition of “fit” above.

At the end of the month, reread the twelve lines. If most lean toward energy and curiosity, ask your manager about an internal transfer or start applying. If most say drained, that is a good result too: you have avoided a narrow path before going too far down it.

On your CV, present range as evidence rather than a list of keywords. A line such as “worked in three different data contexts and derived the same source-data checking process” is more persuasive than ten technologies lined up side by side. In job descriptions, words like “ambiguous” or “customer-facing” signal that the work belongs to a wicked environment.

Who should read it, and in what order?

The book suits engineers with two to eight years of experience who are torn between going deeper in one area and moving to a hybrid role such as FDE. It also suits working FDEs who need language to explain their value to their managers.

Read according to what you need to do. For the overall argument, start with the Tiger and Roger example; if you are about to plan an engagement, the idea of wicked environments is immediately useful; save the idea of match quality for when you are considering a change of role.

If time is short, read the Commoncog summary and Brikman’s review first, then decide whether to read the whole book.

Three common mistakes when bringing Range to work

The first is treating the book as a license to go deep in nothing. The hospital and bank example above works only because you are deep enough to profile data and write transformations; breadth rests on a solid technical foundation and does not replace it.

The second is treating everything as wicked. Bringing experiments and open questions to parts that already have a test suite only slows the engagement down. The third is moving through many clients without drawing out a single abstract statement, and ending up with nothing more than extra anecdotes.

Range will not teach you to deploy any system. After reading it, though, it becomes hard to see the ambiguous weeks at a client site as failures, because those are the weeks that build your breadth.
