FDE is not on-site development: How does Palantir turn projects into products?

Original title: FDE不是驻场开发， Palantir如何把项目变成产品？

53AI · 2026-09-22 · 10 min read

Summary

Using Palantir as a case study, this article argues that what distinguishes FDEs from on-site contractors is not solving problems in the field, but systematically feeding field experience back into the core product. It covers Palantir's engineer rotation between deployment and R&D, its Ontology-based platform (Foundry, Apollo, AIP), and the Acquire-Expand-Scale framework, citing 2020 S-1 data such as customer data onboarding dropping to 14 days and ERP mapping from up to 45 days to as fast as 4. It cautions that AI companies hiring FDEs have not necessarily built equivalent feedback loops, and offers four checkable questions for evaluating whether FDE work creates reusable product value.

Key points

  • FDE value lies in knowledge flowing back into products, not just delivery.
  • Palantir rotates engineers between field deployment and core R&D.
  • Six reusable platform capabilities, including Ontology, absorb recurring field problems.
  • Scale means declining relative cost and customers becoming self-sufficient.
  • Four evidence-based questions help judge whether FDE models work.

Full text

One customer project is delivered, the next customer arrives, yet the team still has to reconnect the data, sort out permissions, and rebuild workflows from scratch. As projects pile up, why doesn't product capability accumulate alongside them?

Understanding Palantir's FDE model can start from this question. Its key is to let problems discovered by engineers in the field continuously flow back into the core product, so that a single customer delivery becomes a capability that later projects can reuse.

FDE stands for Forward Deployed Engineer, often translated as "前沿部署工程师" (Forward Deployed Engineer). Today, you can find related positions on the recruiting pages of OpenAI and Anthropic. [6][7] Large language models make general capabilities easier to obtain, but enterprises still have to solve problems of data, permissions, workflows, and production stability. FDE is therefore worth revisiting: after engineers go deep into a customer's business, how can field work leave behind lasting product value?

Palantir was founded in 2003, initially developing software for counterterrorism investigations and operations for U.S. intelligence agencies. Its first core platform, Gotham, served defense and intelligence analysts. [1]

In scenarios like these, it is hard to write requirements up front and then deliver against a feature checklist. Data is scattered across different systems, with varying formats, trustworthiness, and access permissions; the software also has to adapt to classified networks, on-premises data centers, and even disconnected environments.

"Integrating intelligence data" and "discovering potential connections" can be written into a requirements document, but they are not enough to guide development. How does an analyst judge whether a piece of information is credible? Which fields change decisions? How should results be presented so they can feed into actual action? The answers often lie in daily operations, edge cases, and organizational divisions of labor.

Palantir's IPO filing mentions that FDEs went into military bases in Afghanistan and factories in the American Midwest, understanding users' difficulties while deploying software. [1]

The value of field work is, first of all, filling in the knowledge missing from product definitions. Engineers need to figure out how users judge and act before they can decide what capabilities the software should provide, and what counts as solving the problem.

Take a factory that wants to reduce downtime. Where the equipment data lives, whether maintenance records are reliable, and whether downtime is actually caused by parts, scheduling, or the supply chain—all of this needs to be investigated one by one.

Even if the model can already produce risk scores, the work is not done. Who receives the alerts? Can the responsible person adjust maintenance plans accordingly? If alerts never change any decision, the system going live does not equal business improvement.

FDEs work along this chain: understand the goal, connect real data, build analyses or applications, put the results into workflows, and then observe whether they change decisions. Palantir's job descriptions also emphasize that small teams need end-to-end ownership from the first customer conversation to product delivery. [5]

This requires engineers to continuously translate field judgments into working software and adjust solutions based on actual usage. But improved business outcomes only complete the task for the current customer. To further build product accumulation, another question must be answered: how can the experience from solving this problem be put to use by other teams and customers?

Project-based teams can also solve complex problems. The difficulty usually appears after delivery: the customer cannot do without the few people familiar with the site, while other teams find it hard to directly reuse the experience they have accumulated.

Someone wrote a custom interface, someone memorized the permission exceptions, and someone else knows what a certain metric really represents. The system runs, but the knowledge is scattered across people, documents, and customer-specific code. When another team takes on a similar project, they have to understand and develop everything all over again.

What makes Palantir worth studying is that it brought field engineers into the product development feedback loop.

Its 2020 IPO filing disclosed that software engineers rotate between field roles and product development functions; field engineers are treated as an extension of the R&D system, and the improvements they discover are systematically incorporated into Gotham and Foundry. [1]

Palantir's architecture documentation likens this approach to "backpropagation for humans": engineers stay close to real problems and, together with core R&D, continuously digest feedback and ship new capabilities. [2] In organizational terms, the field identifies shortcomings, R&D distills commonalities, and then more customers' actual usage tests these improvements.

However, customer needs do not automatically become product capabilities. The team still has to make judgments: which are special rules of a single customer, best kept in configuration or application layers; and which problems recur often enough to be worth abstracting further into core platform capabilities.

If every requirement adds a long-term-maintained custom branch, the more customers you have, the heavier the maintenance burden may become. Effective knowledge backflow requires turning field experience into capabilities that other customers can use and the R&D team can maintain sustainably.

Factories care about equipment and work orders, airlines care about aircraft and flights, hospitals care about beds and patients. Their business objects and processes differ, yet they often need to answer similar questions: how does data come in, how are objects related, who has permission to operate, and how do judgments trigger action.

These recurring engineering problems provide the foundation for platform reuse. Drawing on Palantir's architecture documentation, the relevant capabilities can be organized into six categories. [2][3]

Differences at customer sitesReusable platform capabilitiesValue of reuse
Different data sources and formatsData ingestion, transformation, lineageReduce repeated data integration and provenance work
Different permission rules and organizational boundariesFine-grained permissions, auditing, governanceUniformly enforce access and operational constraints
Different industry objects and relationshipsOntology business object modelingExpress different businesses with a shared mechanism
Different business logic and modelsRules, models, and application development capabilitiesReuse runtime and development mechanisms
Different decision processes and actionsWorkflows and action interfacesConnect analysis results to business operations
Different deployment environments and update conditionsDeployment and delivery across cloud, on-premises, classified, and disconnected environmentsReduce repeated effort in environment adaptation and operations

Among these, Ontology organizes data, business objects, logic, actions, and security policies into a single business representation. [2] Engineers can build systems around objects such as "factory," "order," and "equipment," defining relationships between objects, executable operations, and their constraints.

This way, teams can organize applications around business objects and their behaviors, instead of starting from isolated data tables and interfaces every time. But how "equipment" is defined, who can modify its state, and which actions require approval still have to be determined by the customer's actual business.

The shared modeling mechanism can be reused, but the specific business judgments still need to be made separately.

Palantir's IPO filing gives an example: capabilities originally built to process streaming time-series data from oil and gas wells were later used to improve Formula 1 race car speeds, increase fleet utilization, and enhance quality control on automotive production lines. [1] What carries across industries is the underlying data processing capability; each industry's goals, constraints, and decision logic still differ.

From a product division-of-labor perspective, Foundry handles data management, logic, Ontology, workflows, and other capabilities; Apollo manages deployment and continuous delivery; and AIP provides large model integration, agents, and automation development capabilities. [3] Together, these platforms absorb the needs that recur in the field.

Therefore, customization is still necessary; the key is deciding which layer of the architecture it belongs in. Recurring parts can continue to be abstracted, while customer-specific business rules stay in the corresponding configurations or applications, avoiding a separately maintained system for every project.

FDE field investment is not cheap. If every new customer requires permanently adding an engineering team, the company still struggles to escape the link between revenue growth and delivery headcount growth.

So, a growing customer count alone is not enough to prove that this model has achieved economies of scale.

In its 2020 IPO filing, Palantir described the development of customer relationships using Acquire, Expand, and Scale. [1] Understanding this framework requires observing how the relationship between investment and revenue changes as platform usage expands.

Acquire: prove value first. This usually starts with a short-term pilot, where the customer pays a lower fee or even uses it for free. Palantir bears the upfront investment to verify whether the platform can solve a problem important enough.

Expand: get the platform into more of the business. The team continues to identify major pain points, connecting more data, processes, and departments. Engineering investment at this stage can still be substantial, as single-point applications gradually become part of daily operations.

Scale: verify whether relative investment declines. As customer usage matures, the vendor continues to provide operations and support, but the associated investment cost declines relative to revenue. Platform reuse, along with customers gradually gaining the ability to expand on their own, creates the conditions for reducing dependence on the delivery team.

Note that these three stages have clear statistical definitions: the listing documents divide stages by customer revenue and contribution margin, and point out that customers do not necessarily go through each stage in sequence. [1] Signing a large deal or entering more departments does not by itself prove that scale has been achieved.

Whether customers can become self-sufficient is another piece of evidence to check. Palantir's Foundry adoption guide describes the mature state as "essentially self-sustaining": daily operations and new development are handled by the customer's internal teams, while Palantir engineers step in mainly for breakthroughs on new use cases or advisory support. [4]

This means that after an FDE goes deep into the field, they should also help the customer gradually develop the ability to use and extend the platform independently. In existing scenarios, if daily operations and new requirements always depend on the original team, you need to check whether knowledge has sufficiently entered the product, documentation, and the customer's team.

The platform itself should also absorb the repetitive work in deployment, data onboarding, and upgrades. Palantir's 2020 listing document provides three sets of historical data: [1]

  • From Q2 2019 to Q2 2020, the average time for customers to start using data on the platform dropped to 14 days, less than one-fifth of the original.
  • The process of mapping ERP data and starting to build applications improved from up to 45 days in some cases in Q2 2019 to as fast as 4 days in Q2 2020. These are time descriptions of different scenarios, not an average comparison.
  • Over the same period, the number of software upgrades that engineers could manage across installation environments increased from about 20,000 per week on average to over 41,000. This is overall management capacity, not the workload of a single engineer.

These are figures the company disclosed at the time and cannot be taken directly as delivery commitments for all projects today. The direction of observation they offer is: whether repetitive work has entered the product and infrastructure, thereby reducing the time and resources required for deployment and management.

For other enterprises, when requirements complexity and quality standards are comparable, they should likewise compare repetitive development, deployment time, and subsequent support needs across new projects. What scale must prove is that after the business expands, the corresponding investment no longer grows at the original rate.

Large models make general capabilities such as generation, summarization, and programming easier to obtain, but they do not complete the building of business systems for enterprises.

Which internal data should the model read, and how are permissions inherited? How do results enter approval, production scheduling, or customer service workflows? Who discovers errors, and at what step do humans take over? These questions directly affect whether the system is usable, and they determine whether a demo can become a continuously running production system.

This is how we understand AI companies' emphasis on the FDE: once general capabilities become easier to obtain, the work of embedding them into specific businesses and continuously validating results becomes more prominent. However, recruiting pages can prove the role exists, but not that the relevant companies have established the same product feedback loop as Palantir. [6][7]

Take downtime reduction again as an example: AI can help read maintenance records, summarize failure causes, and propose repair recommendations. But who can view which records, under what conditions work orders may be generated, and which recommendations require human approval—all of this must be built into the system design.

Among these, data connections, permission inheritance, evals, exception handling, and human takeover can potentially be consolidated into reusable capabilities; the maintenance rules of a particular production line, however, may need to stay in the customer's application layer. If every project assembles models, interfaces, and workflows from scratch, delivery speed may improve, but the product will not necessarily accumulate accordingly.

Learning from the FDE requires building both the ability to solve problems on-site and the mechanism for bringing common problems back into the product. The former makes one project a delivery success; the latter allows more projects to share that investment. Adding a new job title cannot substitute for these two parts of the work.

For business managers and technical leaders, the most direct way to judge whether an FDE is effective is to take a real project and look for evidence.

What was discovered in the field? Which improvements entered the product? Who has already reused them? Can the customer take over? The following four questions can ground the discussion in concrete work.

Question to assessEvidence that can be verifiedWarning signs
Can field work influence the core product roadmap?Find an improvement that went from field feedback into a core release, and confirm the adoption process and the responsible team.Feedback sits in project groups for a long time; the product roadmap is disconnected from the field.
Do projects leave behind reusable modules or bespoke branches?Check records of the same module being used by other customers, and how it is maintained afterward.The more customers there are, the more independent branches and duplicated code there are.
Is the team accountable for business outcomes?Check the goals set before launch, the business metrics after launch, and records of system adjustments based on them.Acceptance only checks feature completion; business outcomes are not tracked continuously.
Can customers gradually become self-sufficient as they mature?Check whether customers can operate and build new applications on their own, and whether daily support is decreasing.New requirements must always be handled by the original team.

A missing piece of evidence on any one item points to a specific place for improvement. If field input cannot reach the product roadmap, check the feedback and decision-making mechanisms; if reuse stays on paper, check whether maintainable shared capabilities have been formed; if customers can never take over, check whether platform usability and knowledge transfer are in place.

You can go further and put the first and second similar projects side by side: what exactly was done less?

The answer should come down to specific modules, engineering hours, deployment cycles, or support needs. When comparing, you must control for project scope and quality requirements; doing fewer features must not be counted as an efficiency gain.

Looking back at Palantir, the hard-to-replicate part is continuously bringing experience from different customers and industries back into the same product architecture, while controlling the fragmentation caused by customization. This requires field teams to influence product decisions, core R&D to make trade-offs, and continuous maintenance of the distilled general capabilities.

The value of the FDE must be validated both in the business results of this engagement and in the reduced repetitive investment of the next. After the customer's problem is solved, what the product retains is the question enterprises must pursue to the end when learning this model.
