Chapter 12 · Chinese-Style FDE: Driving the Piles and Doing the Interior Design

Original title: 第 12 章 · 中国式 FDE：既要打桩，也要装修

FDE 的来路 · 2022 · 6 min read

Summary

This chapter examines how FDEs operate in China, where enterprise digital foundations vary widely even within the same industry. The author argues workload depends on company size, industry constraints, software maturity and organizational readiness—not a simplistic "China lags, US is complete" framing. Doing foundation repair and visible delivery simultaneously creates three pressures: runaway timelines, blurred accountability, and non-reusable costs. The chapter recommends keeping customer-specific rules on-site while distilling reusable assets like connectors, eval structures, industry templates and control policies, and urges FDEs to decline excessive customization, sometimes downgrading scope to advice-only or single-branch deployments.

Key points

  • Enterprise differences stem from size, industry, software base and maturity, not nationality
  • Simultaneous foundation repair and delivery causes timeline, accountability and cost problems
  • Customer-specific rules and approval authority must stay on-site, not be abstracted away
  • Reusable assets include connectors, eval sets, industry templates and control policies
  • Good FDEs refuse unreasonable customization and sometimes scope down to advice-only

Facing Chinese enterprises with uneven foundations and complex systems, the FDE must both shore up the data and engineering groundwork and deliver business results quickly. This chapter discusses the balance between reuse and on-site work.

The team arrives at the customer site with an AI application, originally planning two weeks to connect data and two weeks to go live.

On day one, they discover that three branch companies use inconsistent customer codes; key approvals still happen in chat apps; the legacy system has no interfaces, only file exports; and there is no clear answer on who can approve write-backs to production.

Before the application has even started, the project has already turned into data cleaning, process mapping, interface retrofitting, and permission confirmation.

This is not a case of "Chinese companies all lack foundations." Within the same industry, some companies already have stable data platforms and clear processes, while others have only just moved core operations off paper. Even inside large conglomerates, different subsidiaries can seem separated by two eras.

When discussing the Chinese-style FDE, we cannot lazily assume "China doesn't have it while the US already has it all." What truly determines the workload is company size, industry constraints, software foundations, and organizational maturity.

Some need models; some need foundations first

A company with a high share of online business and well-built interfaces may quickly plug a model into its existing workflows. Data is generated in real time and actions happen inside software, so the FDE's main work is defining use cases, controlling risk, and validating adoption.

A manufacturer's key facts may be scattered across ERP, MES, equipment logs, and crew experience. Before the system can offer production scheduling or equipment disposal recommendations, it must first resolve whether codes, timestamps, and statuses can be aligned.

A regional service company may not even have stable records of some processes. Deploying an AI agent directly at that point means the system can only guess at reality from scattered files and chat logs.

The Ministry of Industry and Information Technology's guidance documents on SME digital transformation specifically emphasize differences in foundations, differences in needs, and phased advancement, and also encourage lightweight, subscription-based products and full-process companion services. The policy language is deliberately restrained: there is no single roadmap on which all companies start running at the same time.

So there is nothing shameful about an FDE shoring up some groundwork first upon arriving on site.

The question is how much to shore up.

If a claims-assist project requires rebuilding customer master data, overhauling all approvals, and replacing core systems first, the original use case cannot bear that cost. The team must either shrink the scope of action or admit the customer isn't ready yet.

"We can do anything" is not a sign of capability; it may be a refusal to do the math.

The cost of driving piles and doing interior work simultaneously

Traditional projects usually build the foundational platform and the upper-layer applications in separate phases. AI projects chase quick wins, and customers won't wait two years, so the FDE is asked to do two things at once: finish a visible room while patching the foundation beneath it.

This creates three kinds of pressure.

First, timelines spiral out of control. Every field you connect may drag in legacy systems and departmental definitions behind it.

Second, responsibility loses focus. The original goal was to solve one operational problem, but the project's success ends up depending on company-wide data governance.

Third, costs fail to compound. The customer sees one application, while the team has sunk in massive on-site adaptation work.

The FDE's value lies in shortening the distance between a problem and a product. If every day is spent manually moving files, fixing one-off interfaces, and chasing approval stamps, the role degenerates into a faster-reacting but more expensive on-site crew.

This doesn't mean on-site grunt work shouldn't be done. Some temporary scaffolding exists to validate value quickly, which is entirely reasonable. The dividing line is whether the team knows it's scaffolding, whether they've agreed on when to tear it down, and whether recurring parts get consolidated.

Which differences should stay on site

Customers will not all become the same company.

Industry regulations differ, organizational authority differs, legacy systems differ, and risk appetites differ. Trying to eliminate every difference only produces an abstract product nobody is satisfied with.

What should stay on site is usually the customer's unique business rules, division of responsibilities, process rhythms, and change management. For example, who can approve a special payout, or which level of staff takes over a production anomaly—these cannot be decided by the vendor on the customer's behalf.

What must be reused are the recurring technical and methodological assets:

  • connectors and data mapping approaches for common systems;
  • eval set structures covering typical errors, edge cases, and attacks;
  • industry templates for objects, states, actions, and permissions;
  • control strategies such as limits, approvals, circuit breakers, and replay;
  • delivery playbooks from problem discovery to production adoption.

Reuse is not copying the previous customer's configuration. It means keeping the "why we did it this way" and extracting the customer-specific values.

For example, two manufacturers' inventory codes will never be identical, but when identifying available inventory, both must distinguish book quantity, frozen quantity, and committed quantity. The specific fields belong on site; this set of questions can go into an industry template.

What to distill from a single customization

One retrospective after the project ends is not enough.

The team needs to take the deliverables apart and examine them.

Which code serves only the customer's legacy systems, and which could become a universal connection layer; which prompts depend on individual tricks, and which business judgments should become rules or models; which exceptions will recur and could go into the eval set; which approvals were purely ad hoc coordination, and which should become product capabilities.

This step is often squeezed out by sales pressure. The previous customer just went live and the next one is already onboarding, so the fastest path is to clone a team rather than distill a product.

Project-based work thus develops strong inertia: revenue tracks person-days, the organization rewards firefighting, and the most capable people stay permanently bound to the hardest customers. Every delivery succeeds, and every delivery feels like the first.

Whether the FDE can be the antidote to project-based work depends on whether there is a real feedback loop between product and service.

The field discovers problems; the product absorbs the commonalities. As the product takes on more commonality, the field team gains slack to tackle new hard problems. If only the first half happens, the FDE is just custom development carrying AI tools.

Stay close to the field, but also know how to say no

Chinese-style field work often interprets "taking responsibility" as going one step further.

If no one builds the interface, the FDE builds it; if no one defines the metrics, the FDE helps define them; if no one coordinates the business side, the FDE chases them down. In early projects, this initiative is precious—it gets things moving that would otherwise be stuck.

But going one step further must not become walking the customer's path for them forever.

Business rules need confirmation from accountable customer owners; production permissions require explicit authorization; and when foundational upgrades exceed the use case's returns, it's time to stop. Declining unreasonable customization is not lecturing the customer from a product manager's pedestal—it's laying out the long-term costs openly for discussion.

Sometimes the best solution is to step down one level: make recommendations first, not automatic write-backs; cover one branch first, not unify the whole group; solve the high-frequency, well-evidenced parts first, and leave extreme exceptions to humans.

This restraint is harder to sell than "end-to-end intelligent automation," but it is closer to real delivery.

Will the FDE eventually disappear?

If a problem becomes standardized enough—connectors, models, controls, and delivery processes all mature—will FDEs still be needed?

Perhaps not as many.

The mature parts will move into the product, and the field role will shift toward harder problems. Excellent FDEs may also turn recurring pain points into their own products and companies.

But companies will not stop being different, and technology will not stop changing. As long as new capabilities must enter old organizations, someone who understands both software and business will still be needed at the boundary.

Whether that role keeps the name FDE doesn't really matter.

The opportunity of the Chinese-style FDE comes from a vast and unevenly digitized landscape; its bar is not the ability to stay up later or stay on site longer, but staying close to the field while preventing every difference from hardening into permanent customization.

At this point in the book, the assets and debts left by generations of technology are all on the table. The final question to answer is no longer whether this role will be hot, but what those who walked the old road should take with them—and what they should leave behind.
