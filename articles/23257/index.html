A framework is a systematized "template" of the thinking, analysis, and processes required for product development and business design.

Instead of building your thinking from scratch, you can borrow "frameworks for high-probability thinking" distilled from the countless failures and successes of those who came before. To use a cooking analogy, it's like cooking not without a recipe, but with a proven cooking recipe at hand.

Business frameworks are mainly used for the following three purposes.

PurposeSpecific examplesRepresentative frameworks
Current state analysis and issue identificationUnderstanding the market environment, confirming your company's positionSWOT analysis, 3C analysis, PEST analysis
Strategy planning and decision-makingDefining what to build and who to deliver it toLean Canvas, OKR, positioning map
Development and operations process managementAdvancing development efficiently as a teamScrum, Kanban, PDCA

While the word "framework" is a familiar term for engineers, it is fundamentally different from "frameworks" in the programming context (React, Rails, Django, etc.).

Comparison itemBusiness/development frameworksProgramming frameworks
PurposeOrganizing thinking, decision-making, process managementEfficient code implementation and standardization
Target usersPdMs, engineers, PMs, executivesMainly engineers
DeliverablesStrategy documents, roadmaps, analysis materialsWorking software and systems
How to learnApplication in practice and reflectionCoding and studying documentation
Usage scenariosMeetings, strategy planning, hypothesis testingImplementation, testing, deployment

This article covers the former, namely business frameworks for organizing the thinking and processes of product development.

If you advance product development without using frameworks, the following problems are likely to occur.

  • Building a product no one uses: You build "what you want to build" without validating user needs, and the market doesn't accept it after release
  • Frequent rework: Development proceeds with vague requirements, leading to major revisions in later stages
  • Fragmented team understanding: Without a common language or frame of reference, discussions don't align and decision-making is delayed
  • Not knowing what to improve: Post-release evaluation metrics aren't defined, so improvement priorities can't be set

Conversely, using frameworks correctly is a means of structurally avoiding these risks.

One of the most wasteful costs in product development is "the time spent thinking itself." If you build "what to analyze" and "what questions to ask" from scratch, resources that could be used for actual development and validation are reduced.

By using frameworks, "what to think about" is defined in advance, allowing you to focus on essential decisions.

For example, using the Lean Canvas, you can organize the 9 elements of problems, customer segments, solutions, revenue models, and KPIs on a single sheet in 30 to 60 minutes. If you tried to organize the same content without a framework, you would have to start from the stage of defining what to write, taking several times longer.

Frameworks have comprehensiveness built into their design, meaning "if you use this framework, you will inevitably cover these perspectives."

For example, using 3C analysis means you will always consider the three perspectives of Customer, Company, and Competitor. If you conduct competitor analysis without a framework, you're more likely to end up in a situation like "I researched the competition, but overlooked what customers actually think."

Frameworks also function as "checklists to prevent overlooking important questions."

Product development is a collaborative activity involving members with different backgrounds, such as engineers, designers, PdMs, marketers, and sales. Even when using the same words, if each person has a different image in mind, discussions become unproductive.

The results of analysis and organization using a framework become a visually visualized common language. If everyone shares the same information in the same format—such as "Our North Star metric is DAU" or "The top of the product backlog is this feature"—the quality and speed of decision-making improve dramatically.

Even when freelance engineers join a project externally, using a framework to organize the current state allows them to understand the project's context in a short time and contribute as immediately effective members.

In today's development environments, "pivot capability"—the ability to quickly shift a product's direction in response to market changes and competitor movements—is in demand.

By using a framework to structure and understand the current state, when change occurs you can quickly identify "what has changed" and "which decisions should be reconsidered."

The success or failure of a pivot depends not on how quickly you detect change, but on whether you can instantly judge what should be changed in response to it. Regularly organizing your situation with frameworks is a habit for keeping that decision-making foundation constantly up to date.

The starting point of product development is discovering the "problem to solve." The depth of analysis in this phase determines the direction of all subsequent development.

[Definition] A framework for clarifying your company's strengths and differentiators compared to competitors, and visualizing an advantageous market position on a two-dimensional map.

[How to Use]

  • Set two axes that are important in the market (e.g., "high vs. low price" × "many vs. few features")
  • Plot competing products and services on the axes
  • Discover "blank areas" with few competitors and define the position your company will target

[Example] When planning a matching service for freelance engineers, if you set the vertical axis as "ratio of direct end-client projects (low to high)" and the horizontal axis as "level of support (automated to extensive)," the "direct end-client × extensive support" position emerges as a blank area, clarifying the axis of your differentiation strategy.

[Use Cases] Formulating market entry strategies for new products; considering rebranding of existing products

[Definition] A framework for analyzing your company and market environment across four elements: Strengths, Weaknesses, Opportunities, and Threats. By combining the internal environment (strengths and weaknesses) with the external environment (opportunities and threats), it derives the direction of strategy.

[Four Strategic Options]

CombinationStrategic DirectionExample
SO strategy (Strengths × Opportunities)Maximize opportunities by leveraging strengthsEnter the AI market by leveraging technical expertise
WO strategy (Weaknesses × Opportunities)Overcome weaknesses to seize opportunitiesCompensate for the AI talent shortage through stronger hiring to capture market opportunities
ST strategy (Strengths × Threats)Avoid or counter threats with strengthsCounter newly entering competitors with brand power
WT strategy (Weaknesses × Threats)Minimize weaknesses and threats, or decide to withdrawConsider cost reduction and withdrawal from a market crowded with competitors

[Use Cases] Early stages of new product development; annual strategy reviews

[Definition] A framework for analyzing the business environment from three perspectives—Customer, Company, and Competitor—and deriving Key Success Factors (KSF).

[Analysis Order and Key Points]

  • Start with Customer: Understand market size, growth rate, customer needs, and purchasing behavior. Analyzing customers first establishes the criteria for evaluating your company and competitors afterward
  • Analyze Competitors: Understand competitors' strengths, weaknesses, strategies, and market share
  • Evaluate Company: Based on customer needs and competitor strategies, organize your company's resources, strengths, and constraints

[Use Cases] Deciding whether to enter a market when developing a new product; building the rationale for "why we should be the ones to build this product"

[Definition] A framework for analyzing four macro-environmental factors: Politics, Economy, Society, and Technology.

FactorFocus Points in Product DevelopmentExample
PoliticsRegulations, legal revisions, subsidiesImpact of the invoice system on freelancers
EconomyEconomic conditions, exchange rates, pricesRising SaaS usage costs due to the weak yen
SocietyDemographics, values, work stylesIncreased demand for communication tools due to the establishment of remote work

[Usage Tips] Use this in addition to short-term competitor analysis (3C analysis) to understand medium- to long-term market trends. It is particularly effective for determining the market launch timing of a new product and forecasting market size several years ahead.

[Definition] A framework for defining a target user's typical profile as a fictional persona described as concretely as a real person. It goes beyond basic demographic information (age, gender, occupation) to describe behavior, thinking, pain points, and goals.

[Main Elements to Include in a Persona]

  • Basic information: age, occupation, place of residence, family structure, annual income
  • Digital behavior: frequently used services, information-gathering methods, devices
  • Challenges and pain points: Frustrations experienced in daily life
  • Goals and motivations: What they want to achieve or solve
  • Relationship with the product: Usage context, frequency, and expectations

[Example]

"Tanaka Hiroki, 34 years old. Backend engineer at an SIer in Tokyo (7 years of experience). Aiming to increase his rates, he is considering going independent as a freelancer, but has no sales experience and doesn't know how to acquire projects. He has a track record of earning 50,000–100,000 yen per month on the side, but feels anxious about taking the step to full-time freelancing. He collects technical information on Qiita and follows engineers' social media on Twitter."

Having a concrete persona like this creates consistency across feature design, UI/UX design, and marketing messages.

Once you understand the market and challenges, this is the phase where you define "what product to deliver, to whom, and how," and quickly validate hypotheses.

[Definition] A framework specialized for startups and new product development that visualizes a business model on a single sheet. Developed by Ash Maurya, it is an adaptation of the Business Model Canvas for new ventures.

[The 9 components and tips for filling them in]

ComponentContentTips for filling it in

[Tips for use] The purpose of the Lean Canvas is not to "complete it," but to "write it as a hypothesis, validate it, and keep updating it." Reviewing it about once every two weeks deepens your understanding of the business model as the product evolves.

[Definition] A framework that visualizes, in chronological order, the entire series of experiences from when target users first become aware of the product to when they use it, continue using it, and recommend it. It organizes user actions, thoughts, emotions, and touchpoints on a single sheet.

[Components of a customer journey map]

  • Phases: Each stage from awareness → consideration → first use → continued use → recommendation
  • Actions: What users specifically do in each phase
  • Thoughts: What users are thinking
  • Emotions: Changes in emotions such as satisfaction, dissatisfaction, expectations, and anxiety (easy to understand when visualized as a graph)
  • Touchpoints: Which channels provide points of contact
  • Issues and improvements: Points where drop-offs or dissatisfaction occur

[Specific use cases in product development]

  • Improving user onboarding: When there are many drop-offs in the "registration complete → first use" phase, identify where and what users are struggling with
  • Prioritizing feature additions: Identify the phase where emotions drop the most and prioritize improving it
  • Planning marketing initiatives: Optimize which messages to deliver at which touchpoints

[Definition] An agile development method for visualizing the goals users want to achieve through the product as "stories" and determining development priorities. Proposed by Jeff Patton.

[Basic format]

"As a [type of user], I want to [feature/action] so that I can [achieve a goal]"

[Example (for a service for freelancers)]

"As a backend engineer with 5 years of experience, I want to use a feature that instantly shows the appropriate rate range when I enter my skill set, so that I can accurately understand my market value"

[How to determine MVP scope] Prioritize user stories into the following three tiers, and make only the Must items the MVP scope.

  • Must: Without it, the service cannot function
  • Should: Improves the experience if included
  • Could: Nice to implement if there's room

[Definition] A goal management framework that links Objectives (qualitative goals you want to achieve) with Key Results (quantitative metrics indicating goal achievement). Born at Intel and popularized by Google.

[Design principles for OKRs]

  • Objectives should be inspiring and written qualitatively (not "achieve X" but "reach a state where X")
  • Key Results should be measurable numerical targets (not "improve" but "improve by X%")
  • 3 to 5 Key Results per Objective is appropriate
  • For OKRs, 60–70% achievement is ideal (100% achievement is a sign the goal is set too low)

[Examples of OKRs in product development]

ObjectiveKey Results
Create a product users want to use every day・DAU/MAU reaches 40% or higher ・7-day retention rate reaches 60% or higher ・NPS (Net Promoter Score) reaches +50 or higher
Successfully release the MVP in the first month・Acquire 100 beta users ・Release with zero critical bugs ・Conduct 10 initial user interviews

[Implications for freelance engineers] Engineers who can introduce and operate OKRs are more likely to be entrusted with leading projects by clients. This increases their evaluation in projects where they also serve as tech lead or development PM, and works to their advantage in rate negotiations.

Once idea validation is complete, you move into the actual development phase. Frameworks for efficiently managing the development process itself become important.

【Definition】 An agile development framework that continuously improves the product through repeated short development cycles of 1-2 weeks called "sprints." It emphasizes adaptability to change and transparency.

【Key Scrum Roles and Responsibilities】

RoleResponsibilitiesCases Where Freelancers Take It On
Product Owner (PO)Maximizing product value and managing the backlogSometimes taken on by engineers who also serve as PdM
Scrum Master (SM)Supporting the team in practicing Scrum correctlyExperienced SMs sometimes join as freelancers
Development TeamHandles actual development, testing, and releasesThe main form of engagement for freelance engineers

【Key Scrum Events】

  • Sprint Planning: Planning what to develop in the next sprint
  • Daily Scrum: A 15-minute session for sharing progress and identifying blockers
  • Sprint Review: Demo of sprint deliverables and gathering feedback
  • Retrospective: A reflection for process improvement (teams that skip this event tend to repeat the same problems)

【Pitfalls of Introducing Scrum】 Scrum is not about "following the rituals for their own sake." If you run the ceremonies without understanding "why each event exists," you end up in a state of "doing Scrum but development is still slow."

【Definition】 A framework that visualizes the flow of work using columns such as "To Do," "In Progress," and "Done," and optimizes the flow of work. It originated from the Toyota Production System and was later applied to software development.

【Choosing Between Scrum and Kanban】

FeatureScrumKanban
Suitable situationsMainly new development and feature additionsMainly operations, maintenance, and improvement
時間軸スプリント（固定期間）継続的なフロー（期間なし）
Role definitionsStrict (PO, SM, development team)Flexible (no specific roles)
Accepting changesChanges are restricted during a sprintAdditions and changes possible at any time
WIP limitsManaged via the sprint backlogExplicit WIP limits are set

【The Importance of WIP Limits (Work In Progress Limits)】 Setting an upper limit on the number of tasks that can proceed simultaneously makes development bottlenecks visible and prevents a pileup of work in progress. For example, setting "In Progress: max 3 items" creates a culture of completing existing work before starting new work.

【Definition】 A framework that quantitatively evaluates the priority of features and initiatives using a score combining four metrics: Reach (number of users affected), Impact (impact per user), Confidence (level of certainty), and Effort (workload). Developed by Intercom.

【RICE Score Formula】

RICE score = (Reach × Impact × Confidence) ÷ Effort

Feature proposalReachImpactConfidenceEffortRICE score
Push notification feature5,000 people/month2.080%3 weeks2,667
Dark mode support5,000 people/month0.560%2 weeks750
Bulk CSV export feature500 people/month3.090%1 week1,350

In this example, the "push notification feature" can be judged as the top priority. It is a powerful tool for eliminating emotional and political debates and making data-driven prioritization decisions.

A product release is merely a "beginning." This is a framework for achieving PMF (Product Market Fit) and sustaining continuous growth.

【Definition】 A framework that drives continuous improvement by repeating the cycle of Plan → Do → Check → Action.

【Concrete Example of PDCA in Product Development】

StepContentConcrete example
PlanSetting improvement hypotheses and defining KPIs"Reducing onboarding steps from 3 to 2 will raise the 7-day retention rate by 10%"
DoImplementing and releasing the initiativeReleased a new version of onboarding in an A/B testing environment
CheckMeasuring data and evaluating resultsAfter 2 weeks, confirmed the new version's 7-day retention rate was 63% (old version: 55%)
ActionRolling out successful initiatives to production and setting the next hypothesisApplied the new onboarding to all users. Next, set an improvement hypothesis for the "first-use completion rate"

【Note】 Running PDCA in 1-2 week cycles (sprint units) is the standard in modern product development. Quarterly PDCA is too slow to respond to change.

【Definition】 The "single KPI" that best represents a product's long-term growth. Just as the North Star always points north, it is the single metric the entire team should use as its basis for judgment. Also called the "One Metric That Matters."

【Examples of North Star Metrics for Representative Products】

ProductNorth Star MetricReason for selection
SpotifyNumber of monthly active listenersBest represents the value delivered by the music experience
AirbnbNumber of nights guests stayedSimultaneously represents value for both sides (guests and hosts)
SlackNumber of messages sent by weekly active teamsProof of continuous value delivery
SaaS for freelancersNumber of monthly deals closedDirectly shows the results users achieved

【Criteria for Setting a North Star Metric】

  • It reflects the value delivered to users
  • It correlates with long-term business growth
  • It can be intuitively understood by the entire team
  • The results of initiatives are reflected within a few weeks

Once the North Star Metric is defined, every decision can be judged on a consistent axis by asking, "Will this feature improve the North Star Metric?"

Trying to use all frameworks at once is counterproductive. It's important to pick one or two frameworks that most directly address "what the team is currently stuck on" and "what needs to be decided now," and use them deeply.

When filling out a framework becomes the goal itself, your thinking gets constrained by the format. The Lean Canvas in particular works not as "something you make once and finish," but as "something you make, validate, and keep updating." It's fine for the first version to be incomplete.

Frameworks deliver their maximum effect not when completed by one person, but when filled in through team discussion. When members with different roles and specialties discuss while looking at the same framework, oversights and assumptions can be prevented.

【Background】 A case of a five-person team launching a SaaS that auto-generates portfolios for freelance engineers.

【Phase 1: Market Validation (2 weeks)】

  • Defined with the Lean Canvas: "Problem = creating a portfolio takes over 10 hours each time," "Target = engineers 1–2 years after going independent," "Value proposition = a portfolio completed in 5 minutes just by linking GitHub and work history"
  • Wrote detailed personas and created a landing page. Ran test marketing to 300 people. Confirmed an 18% email signup rate, completing validation

【Phase 2: MVP Development (4 sprints = 8 weeks)】

  • Used user story mapping to decide the MVP scope would include only 4 features: "GitHub integration → template selection → preview → published URL generation"
  • Ran 2-week sprints with Scrum, interviewing 5 registered users after each sprint
  • In the third sprint, feedback concentrated on "too few template types," so in the fourth sprint templates were expanded from 3 to 8

【Phase 3: Growth】

  • Set the North Star Metric as "monthly number of published portfolios"
  • Ran PDCA weekly, and adding a "template preview feature" improved the publication completion rate from 42% to 67%

【Background】 A case of a major IT company's new business division developing a business automation SaaS for existing customers.

【Setting OKRs (quarterly)】

Objective: Deliver a product that improves operational efficiency for existing customers and reduces churn

  • KR1: Acquire 50 beta user companies (within 3 months)
  • KR2: Retention rate of 70% or higher 30 days after first use
  • KR3: Customer satisfaction (NPS) score of +40 or higher

【Combining with PDCA】 For KR2 "70% retention rate," hypotheses such as "improve onboarding" and "introduce support chat" were set → executed → measured → improved in cycles. After 8 weeks, KR2 reached 68% (97% of the target), and adding onboarding videos brought it to 73% the following month.

Amid this change, the market value of "engineers, PMs, and product managers who can think together about product direction using frameworks" has risen significantly compared to people with only pure implementation skills.

【Concrete Added Value Created by Framework Utilization Skills】

  • In initial client meetings, can use the Lean Canvas to organize problems and ask "Is this product really necessary?"
  • In sprint planning, can use user story mapping to organize the backlog and make evidence-based proposals on "what should be built now"
  • In KPI-setting discussions, can propose OKRs or North Star Metrics and build team consensus on "where to aim"
  • Can use the RICE prioritization framework to explain with data "why build that feature first"

All of these are skills that enable the shift from "the party asked to implement" to "a partner who builds the product together."

Framework utilization skills function not as a "substitute for technical skills" but as a "multiplier on technical skills." Even with the same technical ability, engineers with framework utilization skills can often negotiate monthly rates 100,000–400,000 yen higher.

The reason rates rise is that the rates client companies pay freelancers are proportional to "the added value brought to the team." Someone who can not only "write code" but also "simultaneously propose and implement improvements to product KPIs" also reduces PM/PdM workload for the client, creating a rational reason to pay higher compensation.

Here is a step-by-step roadmap for mastering framework utilization skills and going independent as a high-rate freelancer.

【PHASE 1: Building the Fundamentals (While at Your Current Job or Doing Side Work: 3–6 Months)】

  • Actually write out a Lean Canvas for your own side project or personal development work
  • Learn the basics of Scrum from books (e.g., "SCRUM BOOT CAMP THE BOOK") and propose introducing it to your current team
  • Set OKRs for your personal goals and build a habit of reviewing them quarterly

【PHASE 2: Building a Track Record (Hands-On Practice Through Side Work and Small Projects: 3–6 Months)】

  • Take on the Scrum Master role in side projects, leading sprint planning and retrospectives
  • Propose and implement user story mapping during requirements definition with clients
  • Add achievements to your portfolio such as "defined MVP scope using a Lean Canvas" and "improved team development velocity by X% as Scrum Master"

【PHASE 3: Maximizing Market Value (After Going Independent as a Freelancer: Ongoing Skill Development)】

  • Obtain official Scrum certifications (PSM I, PSPO I, etc.) to demonstrate credibility externally
  • In tech lead projects, take responsibility for OKR design and KPI setting, building a track record of being "able to handle not just development but product strategy as well"
  • Specifically list the "frameworks and methods you can apply" on your skills sheet

【Example Skills Sheet Entry】

"Hands-on experience with Scrum development (sprint planning, running retrospectives, facilitating sprint reviews). Experience designing MVP development using Lean Canvas. Experience managing development goals with OKRs (consistently achieving KR completion rates of 80% or higher). Experience managing product backlogs using the RICE prioritization framework."

In this way, engineers, PMs, and product managers who can write concrete achievements in framework utilization on their skills sheet can clearly differentiate themselves from "professionals with only technical skills" in their proposals.
