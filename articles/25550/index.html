Quick Answer

A forward deployed engineer (FDE) is a software engineer embedded with a customer who ships production code inside that customer's environment — running discovery with the people who will use the software, scoping a narrow first slice, building it, integrating it with their identity and data systems, and then operating it.

The role started at Palantir in the early 2010s and is now standard at AI labs and enterprise AI companies. It is an engineering job, not a sales job: you write code most of the week and usually carry the pager for your own deployment.

Almost every article about the forward deployed engineer role answers the same question — what is it? — and then stops. That question takes two sentences. The questions people actually have take longer: what does the week look like, where does my code physically run, what happens when the customer's security team sends a 200-row spreadsheet, what does it pay, what is in the interview loop, and how do I get there from an ordinary backend job?

This guide answers those. It is written from the same material as our free 13-module forward deployed engineer course, which ends in a server-graded exam and a certificate anyone can verify. Where a number comes from someone else's research, it is cited and linked. Where a framework is ours, it says so.

What a forward deployed engineer actually is

A forward deployed engineer is a software engineer whose requirements arrive as a person's frustration rather than a ticket, and whose software runs in someone else's environment. Those two shifts — the source of the requirement and the location of the runtime — explain almost every other difference in the job.

The role was created at Palantir in the early 2010s, where the internal title was "Delta" — the engineer who closes the gap between what the product does and what a specific customer needs. Until around 2016, Palantir employed more forward deployed engineers than traditional software engineers. The pattern spread because the underlying problem spread: powerful, general software sold to organisations whose data, workflows and compliance rules are all unique.

The reason it exploded again in 2025 and 2026 is the same problem in a new form. An LLM-based product is maximally general and minimally configured — it does not know the customer's ticket taxonomy, their escalation rules, their document formats or their tolerance for a wrong answer. Somebody has to go and find out. That somebody is an FDE.

The three things that make it a distinct role

  • You own an outcome, not a component. Your success measure is "the support team's median handle time fell", not "the endpoint shipped".
  • You have no spec, and writing it is your job. Nobody hands you acceptance criteria. You extract them from people who describe symptoms, not requirements.
  • You run what you build, in an environment you do not control. Their VPC, their identity provider, their proxy, their change-freeze window.

Forward deployed engineer vs product engineer vs solutions engineer

Three titles get blurred constantly, and the difference matters when you are reading a job description. Here is the honest separation.

Requirements come from Users you sit withPM and roadmapSales opportunity

The one-line test. If your code gets deleted when the deal closes, you are doing solutions engineering. If it is still running eighteen months later and someone pages you about it, you are a forward deployed engineer.

How an engagement actually runs

Every forward deployed engagement moves through five phases in the customer's world, and one loop back into your product. The last arrow is the one that decides whether the business scales or drowns in bespoke work.

1. Discover. You sit with the people doing the work and watch, rather than asking what they want. What you are hunting for is one workflow that is painful, frequent, and measurable.

2. Wedge. You scope down to a slice that can ship in about two weeks. The wedge is not a demo — it is the smallest thing that changes a real number for a real team. Most engagements die here, not in the code, because the scope was set to impress rather than to ship.

3. Prototype. Working software in front of the users, fast, then iterated in front of them. Speed is a correctness property in this job: a prototype in week two tells you what six weeks of requirements documents never will.

4. Integrate. Their SSO, their data, their network boundary, their vendor security review. This is where optimistic timelines go to die, and it is the phase every "what is an FDE" article skips.

5. Operate. Dashboards someone actually opens during an incident, an SLO, a runbook, and a rotation. If nobody is on call for the thing you built, it is a pilot, not a deployment.

The loop. Everything you learned goes back into the product as a ladder: bespoke script → shared library → config flag → product feature. Work that never climbs that ladder is technical debt that happens to be profitable this quarter.

What a forward deployed engineer's week actually looks like

Roughly 55–70% of a forward deployed engineer's week is hands-on-keyboard engineering; the rest is the discovery, demo and integration work that produces the requirements. Below is the model we teach in the FDE course — it is our own framework for a steady-state week in the middle of a deployment, not survey data, and it shifts hard toward calls in week one and toward code in weeks three to six.

ActivityTypical hours/weekWhat it looks like
Building the deployment18–22Services, integrations, data pipelines, UI, IaC
Customer calls & shadowing6–8Discovery, weekly working session, ad-hoc questions
Debugging in their environment4–6Their proxy, their auth, their data, their timezone
Demos & written updates3–4Friday demo, one written status their exec reads
Security, legal & procurement2–3Questionnaires, DPAs, review calls, evidence

Two things surprise people about that table. First, how much of it is normal engineering. Second, how much of the non-coding time is not sales — it is procurement, security review and status writing, which are the actual overheads of shipping into another company.

Where your code runs: the four deployment topologies

The single biggest predictor of how hard a deployment will be is where the software is allowed to run. Ask this in week one, in writing. No mainstream article on this role covers it, and it changes your architecture, your timeline and your on-call story more than any other decision.

TopologyWho operates itWhat it costs youTypical trigger
Your multi-tenant SaaS YouNothing extra — the easy caseMid-market, low data sensitivity
Single-tenant, your cloud YouPer-customer infra, per-customer deploys, drift"Our data can't share a database"
Customer's cloud (their VPC) Them, with your helpNo prod access, log requests, remote debugging, their change windowsRegulated industries, large enterprise
Air-gapped / on-prem ThemNo egress, manual upgrades, versions frozen for months, offline model weightsDefence, some public sector, some health

Three consequences engineers learn the hard way:

  • Your observability plan must survive the topology. In a customer VPC you may never see a log line. Design for exportable, redactable telemetry from day one — see our guide to how data flows through a pipeline for the same discipline applied to data.
  • Every hop right on that table roughly doubles your release cycle time. Plan feature flags and backwards-compatible schemas accordingly.
  • Air-gapped means "the model too". If your product calls a hosted LLM API, an air-gapped customer is a different product, not a different config.

The security questionnaire is part of the job

A forward deployed engineer is usually the person who answers the customer's vendor security review, and three answers end more engagements than any technical failure. They are: where the data physically resides, whether customer data trains any model, and who at your company can access production.

Prepare these before you are asked:

  • Data residency and retention — region, storage duration, deletion path, sub-processors.
  • Model and data usage — a written, unambiguous "no training on customer data" statement if it is true, and precision about what is sent to which provider if it is not.
  • Access control — how production access is granted, logged, and revoked; whether you can see their data.
  • Tenant isolation — the actual boundary, named. "Logical isolation with row-level policies" is an answer; "we're secure" is not.
  • Incident response — notification window in hours, contact path, and one real post-incident example.

If your product sends customer data to an LLM, expect follow-up questions about prompt injection and data exfiltration specifically. Our write-up on AI agent security and prompt injection covers the threat model the reviewer will be working from.

Practical rule: keep a maintained answer bank. The second questionnaire should take two hours, not two days. Engineers who build that bank become the person every deal routes through — which is exactly the leverage that gets you promoted in this role.

Shipping AI features at a customer: the 80/20

In an "AI project" at a customer, the model is roughly 20% of the work; the other 80% is data access, evaluation, escalation paths and the interface. Teams that invert that ratio ship an impressive demo in week two and nothing in month six.

What the 80% is made of:

  • Getting to their data at all — the extract, the permissions, the refresh, the quality. Most enterprise AI failures are data quality failures wearing an AI costume.
  • An evaluation set built from their tickets, not from a benchmark. Fifty labelled real examples beat any public eval for deciding whether you can ship.
  • The escalation path — what happens when the model is unsure, and who sees it. Design the human handoff before you tune the prompt.
  • Retrieval and context. Whether you need RAG or fine-tuning is a five-minute decision; assembling the context the model needs is the actual project — see context engineering.
  • The surface. A slow, ugly interface makes a correct model useless. Adoption is the deliverable.

If the customer's systems need to be reachable by the model as tools, this is where MCP servers have become the default integration shape, and the difference between MCP servers and AI agents is worth being precise about in front of a customer architect.

What forward deployed engineers get paid

Verified data is thinner than the internet suggests, so here is what is actually sourced and what is not. Levels.fyi, which is built on self-reported offer data, is the most checkable source for this role.

SourceScopeReported range
Levels.fyi Palantir FDE, total comp~$185K – $631K, median ~$278K
Third-party aggregatorsFrontier AI labs, mid-level total compCommonly quoted $350K – $550K
Third-party aggregatorsFrontier AI labs, staff+Commonly quoted $600K+

Read that table with three caveats:

  • Only the first row is a real dataset. The frontier-lab bands circulating in 2026 come from recruiter blogs and compensation "reports" whose methodology is not published. Use them as a directional signal, never as a negotiating anchor.
  • Equity dominates at the top and it is not cash. A large fraction of a frontier-lab package is illiquid equity with a valuation you cannot verify.
  • The premium is for scarcity, not for the title. Companies pay it because very few engineers will do integration work, security review and a customer demo in the same week. If you only want the first of those three, the premium is not for you.

On location: most of the highest-paying roles are in US and European hubs. Pragmatic Engineer reports that OpenAI runs its forward deployed team across eight cities on three continents — New York, San Francisco, Dublin, London, Munich, Paris, Tokyo and Singapore. For engineers in India, the realistic near-term paths are the same work under different titles — deployment engineer, solutions architect, applied AI engineer at enterprise SaaS vendors and global capability centres — or a remote FDE role at a startup that already sells internationally. The skills transfer completely; only the title and the band change.

The interview loop, stage by stage

Forward deployed engineer loops test three things software interviews normally test separately: can you code, can you design an integration, and can you hold a room. The loop is usually five stages.

StageWhat it isWhat they are really testing
1. Recruiter screen 30 minDo you understand that this is a field role, and do you want it
2. Technical screen 60 min, practical codingFluency in a real language on a real problem — not competitive puzzles
3. Take-home or pairing Build a small integration or pipelineJudgement under ambiguity: what you left out, and why
4. Customer simulation Roleplay: an angry or vague stakeholderCan you run discovery, say no, and reframe without losing the room
5. Architecture + past project Deep diveWhether you actually owned an outcome or just a component

The stage that fails most candidates is stage 4, and it fails strong engineers. The common mistakes: jumping to a solution before the problem is stated, agreeing to everything, and answering "can you build X?" with a yes instead of "what would X let your team stop doing?"

The reliable pattern for stage 5 is a narrative in this order: the customer's situation, the wedge you chose, what you deliberately did not build, the integration that turned out to be hard, what broke in production, and what became product. That last item is the one that separates a senior FDE story from a mid-level one.

How to move into FDE work from a normal software job: a 90-day plan

You do not need customer-facing experience to get hired; you need evidence that you can operate without a spec. Here is the plan we recommend to engineers making the switch, built to be done alongside a full-time job.

Days 1–30 — Get the vocabulary and one artefact.

  • Work through the free forward deployed engineer course modules on the role, the stack, discovery and prototyping.
  • Volunteer for the most integration-shaped work available in your current job — the SSO migration, the partner API, the customer data import. Anything with another organisation on the other end.
  • Write one requirements document from a conversation rather than a ticket, and get it approved.

Days 31–60 — Ship it and instrument it.

  • Take that integration to production yourself, including the dashboard and the alert.
  • Do the course modules on integrations, deployment and security, and answer a real security questionnaire if your company has one in flight.
  • Build a small end-to-end project with a customer-shaped brief — ingest messy external data, apply an LLM step with an evaluation set, and expose it behind a simple UI. The course capstone gives you exactly this brief if you do not have one at work.

Days 61–90 — Prove the narrative and interview.

  • Sit the certification exam and put the certificate on your profile.
  • Rewrite your CV around outcomes rather than components — "cut reconciliation time 40% for a partner integration" beats "worked on the payments service". Run it through our free ATS resume checker before you send it anywhere.
  • Practise the stage-4 roleplay out loud with someone. This is a performance skill and it does not improve by reading.
  • Apply to Series A–C companies with named enterprise logos, not only to frontier labs. That is where the role is learnable and the loop is less brutal.

Common mistakes that end forward deployed engagements

  • Scoping to impress. A six-week first deliverable is a six-week window for the sponsor to change jobs. Ship in two.
  • Treating the security questionnaire as somebody else's problem. It is on your critical path whether or not it is in your job description.
  • Building for the person who bought instead of the person who uses. The buyer signs; the users decide whether it survives renewal.
  • Never climbing the productization ladder. Ten happy customers on ten bespoke branches is not a business, and it is not a career either.
  • Going native. After six months embedded, you can end up advocating for the customer against your own product. The useful version of that is upstreamed feedback; the harmful version is a private fork.
  • No pager, no ownership. If ops handed off to someone who was never in discovery, you will be back in month four anyway.

Frequently Asked Questions

What does a forward deployed engineer do day to day?

A forward deployed engineer spends most of the week writing production code for a specific customer's deployment, and the rest running discovery sessions, demoing progress, debugging inside the customer's environment, answering security and procurement questions, and pushing what they learned back into the core product. They usually carry the pager for the system they built.

Is forward deployed engineering a good career move?

For engineers who want ownership of outcomes and unusual exposure to how businesses actually work, yes — it compresses years of product, integration and operations experience into months, and it pays a scarcity premium. It is a bad move if you want deep specialisation, predictable weeks, or distance from customers. The exit paths are strong: product engineering, engineering management, solutions architecture and founding.

What is the difference between a forward deployed engineer and a DevOps engineer?

A DevOps or platform engineer owns the reliability of your company's own infrastructure. A forward deployed engineer owns a customer outcome end to end and deploys into an environment they do not control. They overlap on containers, CI/CD, observability and on-call, but the FDE also runs discovery, integration and customer communication, and their success is measured by a customer metric rather than uptime.

Do forward deployed engineers write code every day?

Yes. In a steady-state deployment week, roughly 18 to 22 hours go to building — services, integrations, data pipelines, interfaces and infrastructure. The role is sometimes mistaken for a sales-engineering job because it is customer-facing, but the deliverable is production software that stays running after the engagement ends.

What programming languages do forward deployed engineers use?

Python and TypeScript dominate, with SQL everywhere. Python for data work, integrations and LLM pipelines; TypeScript for the interfaces you will end up building because the customer needs one; SQL because you will be querying their warehouse by week two. Add containers, one cloud, and enough Bash and Terraform to deploy without waiting for someone else.

Can I become a forward deployed engineer as a fresher?

It is uncommon. Most FDE postings ask for around five or more years of experience because the job requires making irreversible scoping decisions alone in front of a customer. The realistic path for a junior engineer is two to four years shipping production software — ideally with integration work — and then moving across. The course and certificate help you make that move credibly, but they do not replace the shipping experience.

Conclusion

The forward deployed engineer role is having a moment for a real reason, not a hype reason: general-purpose AI products are unusually hard to make useful inside a specific organisation, and somebody with real engineering ability has to go and do it. The job is discovery, a narrow wedge, fast prototypes, painful integrations, honest operations, and the discipline to turn custom work into product.

If that description sounds like the parts of engineering you already enjoy, the path is straightforward.
