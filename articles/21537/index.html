Vulnerability management services fall into 4 types—tools, assessment firms, managed operations, and FDE-type—and the difference lies in "who handles things through to fixing what is found."

Finding holes (vulnerabilities) in systems is something any type can do.

The problem is the state where a list arrives but no one fixes anything.

As a guideline: if you have no in-house fixers, choose FDE-type; if you only want detection and reporting, choose managed operations; if you need assessment results for reporting to business partners, choose an assessment firm.

This article, aimed at IT and development leaders looking for outsourcing partners, presents in order: a comparison of the 4 types, published costs, 6 selection criteria, and items to include in quote requests.

Sources include IPA (Information-technology Promotion Agency), CISA (the U.S. cybersecurity agency), FIRST (an international security organization), and each company's official pages. Prices reflect only publicly available information as of November 2026.

Vulnerability management is a series of activities: continuously finding vulnerabilities in the systems and software your company uses, ranking them by risk, proceeding through fixes and workarounds, and confirming that they are fixed.

IPA's "Effective Approach to Vulnerability Countermeasures (Practical Edition)" also describes the flow of collecting vulnerability-related information, assigning priority using CVSS and similar methods, and proceeding to countermeasures (Source: IPA "Effective Approach to Vulnerability Countermeasures (Practical Edition), 2nd Edition").

The starting point is that this is not a one-time inspection, but work that runs every time a new vulnerability is published.

Vulnerability assessment is a task that examines a defined scope on a set date and ends with a report of the holes found.

Vulnerability management is work that continues until fixes are complete: after receiving assessment or scan results, it involves re-prioritizing, fixing, verifying, and detecting the next round.

If you contract only for assessment services, the report arrives, but the fixing work remains with your company.

Vulnerability scanning refers to automated checks used as the entry point to assessment and management.

Scanning alone cannot determine how dangerous a found hole is for your company.

IPA's public materials (such as the vulnerability countermeasure information database "JVN iPedia") can be used as material for grasping the basics before placing orders. Note that as of November 2026, JVN iPedia and MyJVN are announced as temporarily suspended (scheduled to resume) on their official pages. Please check the latest status on JVN.

Even when outsourcing, the following 5 things remain in-house.

If you contract while leaving these vague, you can easily end up in a state where you've outsourced but nothing moves.

  • Identifying which systems exist and who owns them (the outsourcing partner won't know your internal owners)
  • Deciding when systems may be taken down
  • Approving the release of fixes to production
  • Deciding to accept the risk of vulnerabilities you've decided not to fix
  • Serving as the point of contact that acts on reports from the outsourcing partner

What is easy to outsource: information gathering, drafting priorities, performing fix work, and verification and periodic reporting.

The scope you delegate varies greatly across the 4 types. The next chapter lays them out.

Outsourcing changes the following 3 things.

  • Staff workload: information gathering and report creation can be handed off externally
  • Basis for prioritization: what to fix first can be explained using established metrics (CVSS, KEV, etc.; Chapter 4)
  • Material for explanation: records that can be shown to business partners and auditors are retained

However, how much can be outsourced differs by type. Compare them in the table below.

An FDE (Forward Deployed Engineer) is an engineer who works on-site with customers, handling everything from organizing issues to implementation and adoption, or a form of support based on that model.

In vulnerability management, this article calls the type that works hands-on through all four stages on-site—"find → narrow down → fix → keep it running"—the FDE type.

The classification into four types is an organization by this article's editorial team and may differ from each company's own terminology.

"Vulnerability scanning tool," "vulnerability checking tool," "vulnerability detection tool," "vulnerability management system," and "vulnerability notification service" are similar terms but refer to different things.

Despite the different names, most differ only in how far they cover "finding, notifying, and recording in a ledger," and do not include "fixing." When evaluating products, judge not by the name but by whether fixing is included.

It is insufficient for managing servers or an entire internal system.

What makes the difference in vulnerability countermeasures is how you decide which discovered vulnerabilities to fix first.

The longer the list, the less you can fix everything. The central factor in choosing a service is what criteria the outsourcing vendor uses to narrow things down.

MetricWhat it representsSource
CVSSExpresses vulnerability severity as a number from 0 to 10.0. Under IPA's classification, 9.0–10.0 is "Critical," 7.0–8.9 is "Important," 4.0–6.9 is "Warning," and 0.1–3.9 is "Caution"IPA CVSS v3 Overview
EPSSThe probability (0–1) that a published vulnerability will actually be exploited within the next 30 days. Updated daily and available for freeFIRST EPSS
KEVA list of "vulnerabilities with confirmed exploitation in the wild" maintained by CISA. CISA recommends using it as an input for prioritizationCISA KEV
SSVCA decision-making method that classifies responses into four levels—Track, Track*, Attend, and Act—based on five factors: exploitation status, technical impact, ease of automation, business impact, and public impactCISA SSVC

CVSS base scores are standardized numbers designed to yield the same score regardless of whose environment is being evaluated, and they do not take your own systems' circumstances into account.

IPA explains that the risk in your own environment should be reassessed by users using environmental scoring metrics.

In other words, ranking solely by published CVSS scores does not reflect your own environment or actual exploitation status.

Vulnerability triage is the work of sorting discovered vulnerabilities by how urgently they need to be addressed.

The following is an example of combining the four metrics; use it as a guideline when asking an outsourcing vendor "how would we triage in our case?"

  • Check whether vulnerabilities on the KEV list exist in software your company uses (if so, they are top-priority candidates. Since KEV is a list from US CISA, absence from it does not guarantee safety)
  • Separate assets exposed to the internet from internal-only assets
  • Next, rank items with high EPSS values
  • Rank the rest by CVSS classification and by when systems can be taken down for maintenance

When asking about prioritization methods, an answer of just "in order of CVSS score" may indicate they haven't looked at your environment.

The difference among the four types is most pronounced in ① (whether they handle remediation). In the table in Chapter 4, the "Fix" row is where the responsible parties diverge.

When requesting quotes, providing the following items allows you to compare responses from multiple vendors under the same conditions.

  • Number of target assets (number of domains, servers, applications) and whether they are public or internal
  • Main technologies in use (OS, cloud, languages, library management methods)
  • Whether you want remediation included, or only detection and reporting
  • Desired reporting frequency and report recipients (IT department, management, business partners)
  • Number of contact persons on your side, and when systems can be taken down
  • Budget ceiling and desired contract period
  • Whether past diagnostic results exist

Even when proposals use similar language, their contents differ. Upon receiving quotes, verbally confirming the following five points will reveal differences in what is included.

  • Is the "remediation work" for discovered vulnerabilities included in the contract scope? If not, who is assumed to do it?
  • What is the basis for prioritization? CVSS scores only, or also exploitation status (KEV, EPSS) and your exposure situation?
  • When an urgent vulnerability is published, when and by what means will the first contact arrive?
  • Is the contact person fixed? If the contact changes, how is the handover handled?
  • After the contract ends, will detection settings, lists, and work records remain with your company?

The fifth point is easily overlooked. If lists and settings don't remain in your hands after the contract ends, switching to the next outsourcing vendor requires the effort of identifying everything from scratch.

Even with the same "X yen per month," the content differs entirely depending on whether the target is 10 domains or 100 hosts.

For deciding whether to adopt FDE, you can also use the checklist in 6 Principles for Judging FDE Consultants.

A failure where introducing managed operations or tools only increases the number of detections, fixes remain with your company, and the list keeps growing.

The countermeasure is to decide "who will do the fixing" before signing the contract. If there is no one responsible for fixes, get an estimate that includes the scope of option ④ or fix-support options.

A failure where a diagnostic was performed before release and a report was received, but no one addresses new vulnerabilities afterward.

The countermeasure is to accept that a diagnostic is a one-time task, and separately decide on post-diagnostic operations (a mechanism for continuous detection and fixing).

A failure where a list sorted by CVSS score arrives and everything on it is requested as-is.

The countermeasure is to use the criteria in Chapter 4 and ask before signing the contract, "How will items be triaged based on our environment and actual exploitation status?"

A failure where, when a vulnerability listed in KEV is found, no one is designated to act at night or on weekends, delaying the response.

The countermeasure is to specify emergency contact methods, response times, and fix approvers in the contract or operational agreements.

A failure where the list of targets provided at contract time omits decommissioned servers or systems set up independently by departments.

Anything not on the list will not be subject to detection. Outsourced providers have no way of knowing on their own what exists inside your company.

The countermeasure is to conduct an inventory of domains, servers, and applications in-house once before signing the contract. It does not need to be perfect. Establish a process to add items whenever omissions are found.

Some vulnerabilities have a large business impact and cannot be fixed immediately. They are left unaddressed without documentation, and six months later a different person cannot explain the history. This is a failure.

The countermeasure is to add a "deferred" column to the list, recording the reason, alternative measures (such as communication restrictions), and the month for review in a single line. This keeps you in a state where you can explain when asked by business partners or auditors.
