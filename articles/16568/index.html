What is an FDE (Forward Deployed Engineer)? The role of engineers who solve business problems in the field, and key points for hiring

This Japanese article from LAPRAS HR TECH LAB explains the FDE (Forward Deployed Engineer) role for recruiters. FDEs, originating at Palantir Technologies, work on-site at customer locations, customizing their company's product into systems that deliver real business value rather than simply shipping software. The role is gaining attention because generative AI must be deeply integrated with clients' proprietary data, legacy systems, and tacit knowledge—something generic SaaS cannot do. Recently, one of the job titles you may have seen more often in engineering recruitment circles and on social media is "FDE (Forward Deployed Engineer)." Many recruiters may feel they have heard the term somewhere but cannot quite picture what it actually involves.

In this article, we will explain what kind of engineer an FDE is and what points to keep in mind when hiring one.

What is an FDE (Forward Deployed Engineer)?

In a nutshell, an FDE is "an engineer who is deployed (Deployed) to the front lines (Forward) where customers implement the company's product and directly takes on the creation of business value."

Turning the company's product into "value" on-site at the customer

The defining characteristic of an FDE lies in where they work. While a typical software engineer focuses on development within their own company, an FDE works directly on-site where the company's product is implemented—at the customer's offices, factories, and so on.

The FDE's job is to properly customize the company's product to fit each customer and the environment where it will be used, and fully implement it as "a system that actually generates business value." It is not simply a matter of "installing the product in the customer's environment and calling it done"; the FDE must decipher the complex data structures that exist on the customer's site and the tacit knowledge of the people working there, and reflect them in the system.

The roots and origins of the FDE

The FDE role was first created at Palantir Technologies, a US big data analytics company.

The company served customers such as defense organizations and financial institutions that handled extremely complex, highly sensitive data. In such environments, there is a reality that simply "selling excellent software" cannot fully solve the customer's problems.

So the company adopted an approach of "dispatching engineers who knew the core functionality of its product to the customer's site and adapting the system to their individual problems." This is considered the origin of the FDE role.

A hybrid of engineering and business problem-solving

An FDE needs two broad sets of skills. One is the ability to organize the customer's business concerns and translate them into concrete solutions. The other is the ability to implement those solutions into actual systems with their own hands.

Having a strong sense of ownership—"using technology to directly solve the business problem in front of me"—and seeing the customer's business transformation through to the end via the system. That is the role of the FDE.

Why is the FDE attracting attention now?

FDEs have been attracting significant attention in recent years. Behind this lies the evolution of technologies such as generative AI and the resulting surge in demand from companies that want to "incorporate AI into their operations to grow their business."

The on-site barriers that generic products alone cannot overcome

With the evolution of AI technology, many companies are considering "AI-driven productivity improvements." However, to bring out AI's true value and solve individual business problems, AI must be deeply integrated with the customer's proprietary data and existing systems. There are many cases where simply implementing a packaged SaaS product is not a sufficient solution.

For example, in fields such as government, finance, and healthcare, strict standards are required for data handling. As a result, it is difficult to use generic external cloud AI as-is, and it becomes necessary to individually build and implement mechanisms that enable AI to be used in a secure environment while ensuring robust security and data sovereignty.

The "gap" between desk simulations and on-site reality

Furthermore, it is not uncommon for there to be a difference between the preliminary simulations done before implementing a product and the way work is actually done on-site.

Some companies still have many company-specific barriers, such as "personalized Excel files on individual PCs," "paper slips," and "unspoken rules among the people in charge."

How should inconsistent, fragmented data and long-used legacy core systems be integrated? An approach is needed in which engineers go into the field themselves, observe the actual workflows, and implement AI and systems to fit the on-site reality.

The reason the FDE role is attracting attention from many companies is precisely this "growing demand driven by the evolution of AI technology."

How FDEs differ from other roles

To sharpen your understanding of the FDE role in recruitment activities, it is useful to clarify how it differs from existing IT-related roles. Here, we explain the differences from three representative roles.

Differences from product development engineers

Between FDEs and the more common "engineers engaged in product development," there are differences in who receives the value and in the depth of "domain knowledge" required.

Product development engineers aim to increase the value of the product as a whole through generic feature development, in order to solve the problems commonly faced by the many unspecified users of that product. FDEs, on the other hand, engage deeply with a specific customer and take the approach of adapting the product individually in order to directly solve that "customer's specific business problems."

There is also a clear difference in terms of domain knowledge. Product development engineers need a bird's-eye view of domain knowledge for the entire target industry. FDEs, by contrast, are expected to quickly pick up—and translate into system implementation—more individual, concrete, and deep domain knowledge, such as "the unique workflows that exist in the specific company in front of them" and "the tacit knowledge of the people in charge," on top of industry knowledge.

Differences from IT consultants

The difference lies in whether they write code themselves and carry the work through to implementation.

An IT consultant's main role is to organize a customer's business challenges and propose solutions and IT strategies. In contrast, an FDE is responsible for going one step further: understanding those strategies and then personally writing code and adapting systems to the field.

Rather than stopping at concepts and proposals, an FDE builds AI and systems as working deliverables and embeds them in actual operations. The clear difference lies in taking ownership of implementation all the way through to the "last mile of business transformation."

Differences from SES and SIers

While SES engineers primarily "provide technical expertise as members of a customer's development team," an FDE's role is to "adapt the company's own product to the customer's environment and deliver direct business results."

In addition, SES engineers and SIers tend to support development on projects with clearly defined requirements. An FDE, by contrast, embeds themselves in the field from the stage where specifications are unsettled and requirements are unclear. They are expected to define the required requirements themselves while repeatedly testing hypotheses through hands-on coding, and to shape the system to fit reality.

Skills expected of an FDE

An FDE needs not only coding skills but also comprehensive abilities: analyzing business challenges and moving projects forward in collaboration with field stakeholders. Specifically, the following three broad skill sets are expected.

Technical ability to integrate complex systems and AI in the field

A customer's environment is not always equipped with the latest technology. Full-stack development and implementation skills are required, such as connecting the company's product with legacy core systems or integrating fragmented databases.

In projects that use AI, technical skills are also needed to incorporate generative AI into operations at a practical level, such as building RAG (retrieval-augmented generation) and optimizing prompts. The ability to quickly build "systems that reliably work" even in environments with many undocumented constraints is put to the test.

Problem-solving ability to uncover customer pain points and propose technical solutions

Customers themselves may not be able to clearly articulate "what the real problem is." An FDE must discern the true problem through field observation and dialogue with stakeholders, and possess the skill to translate abstract business challenges into concrete system requirements.

An attitude of respecting industry-specific rules and tacit knowledge, and fitting systems seamlessly into actual business workflows is essential. In addition, the ability to persuade customers by demonstrating return on investment—showing what business outcomes (such as cost reduction or revenue growth) the system will deliver—is also important.

Customer-facing ability to adapt steadily under high uncertainty

In FDE projects, specifications are rarely fully defined in advance. Flexible adaptability is required to turn highly uncertain situations—changing requirements or unexpected data shortages—into opportunities to create value.

Communication skills are also indispensable: explaining technical constraints and solutions clearly to non-technical customer stakeholders and smoothly building consensus to move the project forward. Rather than building something once and being done, an FDE is expected to iterate quickly based on field feedback, with a willingness to experiment.

How to find candidates with FDE qualities

In the current Japanese market, candidates who already have "hands-on FDE experience" are extremely rare. Therefore, rather than searching for the narrow job title of "FDE experience," it is more effective to look for candidates who meet the three qualities required of an FDE (technical skill, problem-solving ability, and customer-facing ability). Specifically, consider broadening your search to candidates with specialized expertise whose roles and responsibilities partially overlap with those of an FDE.

Three talent pools with high FDE aptitude

First, look for people with the potential to thrive as an FDE among those with the following three backgrounds.

1. Former SIer and IT consultants

These are people with experience in "building consensus" while structuring the complex business workflows of large enterprises and driving projects from requirements definition through system deployment. Among them, those with a strong development orientation—wanting not just to design and propose but to see implementation through with their own hands—show high potential as FDEs.

2. Former software engineers (SWEs)

These are people with full-stack development skills, proficient in languages such as Python. Those who aspire beyond building predefined features in-house—wanting to "engage directly with customers' business challenges and solve them with technology to deliver business results"—tend to transition smoothly into the FDE role.

3. Data professionals (data scientists, etc.)

These are people with specialized knowledge in building AI models and data analysis. Those with a strong interest in practical application—wanting not to "share analysis results as reports and stop there" but to "embed improvements into actual business processes and systems and put them into operation"—have the potential to thrive as FDEs who implement AI in the field.

Reading the traces of trial and error from "past output"

When searching for candidates with FDE aptitude, relying solely on the skills and past positions listed in their résumés may not be enough. Ownership backed by business understanding, and soft skills such as "the adaptability to move ambiguous situations forward," rarely show up on a résumé.

That is where "past output" serves as a valuable clue: published code on GitHub, posts on tech blogs, personal development projects, and so on. By reading the traces of "experience in identifying problems around them and experimenting to solve them with technology," you can see the qualities indispensable to an FDE.

Sending a scout message that references the content of such output conveys respect for the candidate's skills and past experience, and creates an opening for a high-quality conversation.

Points to note when hiring FDEs

To hire an FDE and ensure they thrive after joining, there are several points to keep in mind throughout the process, from recruitment through onboarding.

Make clear that the role differs from "implementation leads" and "sales engineers"

An FDE's role differs from that of a "sales engineer," who communicates product specifications based on technical expertise, or a "product implementation lead," who supports smooth adoption in the customer's environment.

The mission of an FDE is to "write production code themselves and fully solve customer problems." During the hiring process, you need to clearly explain this distinction and align expectations with candidates. If the role definition is vague or candidates join with misaligned expectations, it can lead to gaps in expectations and mismatches such as early turnover or poor performance.

Assess aptitude for customer dialogue and business problem solving

FDEs need the ability to translate vague customer requests into technical requirements, and the resilience to navigate messy on-site coordination and troubleshooting. Such aptitude is difficult to evaluate through standard coding tests that only measure whether a candidate can implement a fixed specification.

In interviews, it is important to design a selection process tailored to actual FDE work, for example by incorporating work sample tests based on realistic customer problems or by digging deep into "how candidates have collaborated with others in the past to resolve complex issues."

Building a framework for smooth collaboration after joining

After hiring, creating a mechanism that allows FDEs to collaborate effectively with internal product development teams is also essential.

If FDEs are completely separated from the development team as "dedicated customer-facing staff," there is a risk that customer-specific customizations become siloed with individuals and long-term technical debt accumulates.

It is important to establish a framework that allows FDEs to stay connected with the company and continue delivering value, for example by creating a flow for feeding insights gained at customer sites back into the product as generic features, or by regularly setting aside time for refactoring to clean up code.

FDEs are key partners driving business growth

As AI technology evolves, the areas where SaaS products can solve business problems continue to expand. Along with this, the role of the FDE—embedding themselves in the field and directly solving complex problems with technical skill—is becoming increasingly important to corporate growth.

However, in the Japanese engineer hiring market, talent with hands-on FDE experience is still rare. For this reason, as introduced in this article, the key is to broaden your view to engineers in adjacent specialized fields and identify "the potential to proactively solve problems" from their past output.
