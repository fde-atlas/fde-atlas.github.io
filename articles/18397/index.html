FDE vs Sales Engineer: A Comparison Across 8 Dimensions

Original title: FDEとプリセールスエンジニアの違い｜8軸で比較

Summary

This FDX article compares FDEs and presales engineers across eight dimensions. The core distinction: presales works pre-contract (feasibility, proposals, estimates), while FDEs work post-contract (implementation, production launch, handover to in-house teams). Presales handles 30-50 deals yearly; FDEs embed with 1-2 clients for months or years. The article stresses handover design as the most failure-prone point, offers aptitude guidance for individuals, hiring-order advice for companies, and ends with FDX's own job listings (¥8M-18M for FDE, ¥8M-16M for presales, both Tokyo-based).

Key points

  • Presales covers pre-contract; FDE covers post-contract implementation.
  • Presales runs many parallel deals; FDEs go deep with few clients.
  • Presales' key value: spotting deals that should be declined.
  • Handover must be a defined deliverable, not personal goodwill.
  • Hire based on bottleneck: stalled deals need presales, stalled delivery needs FDEs.

FDEs (Forward Deployed Engineers) and Sales Engineers are both professionals who "understand technology and can talk to customers." Because their job postings read similarly, it is easy to be unsure which one to apply for, or which one a company should hire.

However, the dividing line between the two is clear. Sales Engineers handle the pre-contract phase, while FDEs handle the post-contract phase. From this single point, everything diverges: scope of responsibility, success metrics, required experience, and how time is spent.

Who this article is for

  • Engineers considering a move to customer-facing roles
  • Those deciding whether to apply for a Sales Engineer or FDE position
  • Executives and HR professionals deciding which role to hire first when building an AI implementation team

The two roles split along "pre-contract" and "post-contract"

PhaseRoleKey question
Sales meetings and proposalsSales EngineerCan it be done / at what price, and how
Post-contract implementation and handoverFDEMake it actually run and leave it with the client's team

The implementation approach the Sales Engineer sketches during sales meetings becomes the starting conditions for the FDE. Conversely, if the Sales Engineer's assessment is too optimistic, the FDE's work on the ground falls apart. The two are linked as sequential stages of the same process, not opposing roles.

The most commonly overlooked difference is how each role engages with customers. Sales Engineers run many sales meetings in parallel, so they are judged on speed and consistency of judgment rather than depth per engagement. FDEs stay with a small number of customers for a long time, so they are judged on depth of business understanding and persistence in the field. This difference directly affects your preferred working style.

The role of the Sales Engineer

They attend sales meetings and, on the spot after hearing the customer's business challenges, sketch an implementation approach and PoC scenario. They communicate "what AI can do" with the same level of resolution to both executives and IT departments, converting vague inquiries into implementable scopes.

Specific duties include the following.

  • Assessing technical feasibility and proposing architecture
  • Defining PoC scope (articulating effort, team structure, and success criteria)
  • Building estimate rationales
  • Responding to RFPs, handling security checklists, negotiating with IT departments
  • Designing the handover to the post-contract implementation team (communicating assumptions and technical risks)
  • Turning recurring patterns into proposal templates

This role delivers its greatest value when it can decline deals that should be declined. Spotting technically impossible conditions, assumptions based on nonexistent data, or application scopes that will yield no results, and reflecting them in negotiations before the contract is signed. If these slip through, every downstream stage becomes a liability.

The role of the FDE

FDEs enter the field after the contract is signed and take end-to-end responsibility, from strategy development through solution design, implementation, and operational handover. Embedded with or accompanying 1–2 clients, they complete business interviews, requirements definition, PoCs, and production deployment, then leave the client's in-house team able to operate independently.

For a definition of the FDE role itself and how it differs from SES, SIers, and strategy consulting, see "What is an FDE (Forward Deployed Engineer)?" and "FDE vs SES vs SI vs Strategy Consulting".

Handover design is where the two roles meet

In organizations that separate the two roles, the most common problems arise in the handover from sales to implementation.

If implementation begins without the assumptions discussed in sales meetings being communicated to the implementation team, situations like the following occur.

  • Data said to be "retrievable from the existing system" during the proposal turns out to be a manually maintained ledger
  • The success criterion was agreed as "making the work easier," so completion cannot be determined
  • Requests added verbally during sales meetings remain out of scope while expectations linger

To prevent this, the handover must be defined as a deliverable, not left to individual goodwill. The Sales Engineer's deliverables should include "assumptions" and "communication of technical risks," with a process to review them with the FDE at the time of contract signing. This connection design is the first mechanism an organization employing both roles should establish.

Part of the reason PoCs fail to reach production lies in this handover gap. The related structure is laid out in "5 Structural Reasons AI PoCs Fail and How to Avoid Them".

Which role suits you

People suited to being a Sales Engineer

  • You feel a sense of fulfillment explaining technology to strangers in their own language
  • You have no trouble running multiple projects in parallel
  • You find more value in "shaping what can be built" than in "building it"
  • You have the implementation ability to write a prototype on the spot and get it running in front of others

People suited to being an FDE

  • You are interested in going deep into a single site and fully understanding the business
  • You want to see things through from implementation to release and beyond, until they take hold
  • You find meaning in developing customers' in-house teams
  • You can accept measuring results on a timescale of months to years

When you are unsure which way to go, a good rule of thumb is: "do you want to build working things yourself, or set up the conditions for them to be built?" If you don't want to leave implementation behind, an option is to handle pre-sales while partially joining projects after they are won.

From the company's perspective: which should you hire first?

When building an AI implementation team, which role to hire first depends on where your current bottleneck is.

SituationRole to hire first
Sales meetings are coming in, but deals are lost or drag on because technical conditions can't be fully nailed downSales Engineer
Deals are won, but implementation is delayed, never reaches production, or fails to take holdFDE
The assumptions of won deals repeatedly fall apart on siteSales Engineer (starting with handover design)
Few projects, and you are at a stage where you want to make one project a sure successFDE

In early-stage organizations, the FDE often doubles as pre-sales. The dual role breaks down when the number of sales meetings grows and implementation time starts to be squeezed. If you observe a state where "the person who can implement is stuck in sales meetings all day," you can treat that as the timing to separate the roles.

Frequently asked questions (FAQ)

Q1. Are Sales Engineers and Sales Engineers the same thing?

A. Companies use the titles differently, so you cannot judge from the name alone. What you should check is the job content. If the role involves pre-contract technical judgment, proposals, and building the basis for estimates, then regardless of the title it corresponds to the Sales Engineer role in this article. If the main duties are post-implementation technical support and maintenance, it is closer to a different role (post-sales, customer engineer, etc.). In job postings, it is good to check "is it pre-contract or post-contract?" and "is the success metric contract wins or usage?"

Q2. Can you not be a Sales Engineer without implementation ability?

A. There are situations where you cannot. To judge feasibility in a sales meeting, you need to understand the practical realities of implementation. FDX's job posting also requires 3+ years of professional experience as an SE or engineer, and lists as a preferred qualification the ability to write a prototype on the spot and get it running. However, it does not require implementation depth on the level of an FDE. What is required is not the ability to build deeply, but the ability to quickly assess feasibility.

Q3. Can you move from pre-sales to FDE, or the other way around?

A. Yes. The two are adjacent stages of the same process, and the required foundations (technical understanding and customer dialogue) overlap. What you need to fill in when transitioning differs by direction. From pre-sales to FDE: experience completing long-term projects and handing over to operations. From FDE to pre-sales: the ability to juggle many projects in parallel, and templates for estimates and proposals. At FDX, it is also possible to discuss a working style where you handle pre-sales while partially joining post-contract projects as an FDE.

Q4. Why are the salary ranges so close?

A. Because both roles have a clear path to contributing to the business and are hard to replace. Pre-sales directly determines whether deals are won; FDEs directly determine whether delivery and continued use succeed. They simply cover different stages, and we position their business impact as equal. Decisions within the range are based not on years of experience, but on the difficulty and repeatability of the projects you can handle.

Q5. If your company has a pre-sales role, should it belong to the sales organization or the engineering organization?

A. There is no single answer, but the deciding factor is the evaluation metric. If you evaluate based on contribution to winning deals, the sales organization is more consistent; if you evaluate based on the quality of technical judgment and scope accuracy, the technical organization is. A common failure pattern in practice is placing the role in the sales organization and evaluating it only on the number of deals won. This creates an incentive to push through deals even when conditions cannot be met, and downstream stages break down. You need a design that includes both the number of deals won and the success of post-contract projects in the evaluation.

Q6. While the number of projects is small, shouldn't the FDE just also handle sales meetings?

A. In the early stage, that arrangement is reasonable. The problem arises after the number of sales meetings grows and implementation time starts to be squeezed. If the people who can implement are stuck in sales meetings all day, deals increase but delivery stalls. The rule of thumb is "the share of the implementer's time taken up by pre-contract activities." If this ratio keeps rising, it is time to consider separating the roles.

  • Sales Engineers handle pre-contract work, while FDEs handle post-contract work. From this single point, everything diverges: scope of responsibility, success metrics, and way of working
  • Sales Engineers run many deals in parallel (on the order of 30-50 per year), requiring speed and repeatability in judgment. FDEs go deep with 1-2 companies, requiring deep business understanding and the persistence to see things through to adoption
  • A Sales Engineer's value shines most when they spot deals that should be declined before signing and reflect that in negotiations
  • In organizations with both roles, the first thing to establish is the design of the handover. Define the transfer of prerequisites and technical risks as deliverables, not as individual goodwill
  • A rough guide to aptitude: "Do you want to build working things yourself, or set up the conditions that make building possible?"
  • The hiring order for companies depends on the current bottleneck. If deals aren't flowing, hire a Sales Engineer; if implementation is stalling, hire an FDE
  • The limit of combining both roles comes when the share of pre-contract activities in the implementation engineer's workload keeps rising
