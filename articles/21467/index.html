How to hire your first FDE and build an FDE team

Original title: 如何招聘第一位 FDE，组建 FDE 团队

FDE Times · 2026-10-21 · 5 min read

Summary

Drawing on Paraform, Rocketlane, Recruiting from Scratch, Cursor and Palantir, this guide explains when and how an AI startup should hire its first FDE. Its core argument: a stalled PoC, not a hiring plan, is the clearest signal to hire. Teams should start with two people—one senior enough to write a deployment playbook, one to operationalize it—define code boundaries and reporting lines before the first project, and keep FDEs under engineering rather than sales. It also proposes interviews built around real work instead of LeetCode, and lists common failure modes.

Key points

  • A stalled PoC is the clearest signal to hire the first FDE.
  • Start with two hires: one writes the playbook, one executes it.
  • Separate customer-specific code from core product before the first project.
  • FDEs should report to engineering, not sales.
  • Interview by having candidates build something and explain it to non-technical stakeholders.

Imagine you're the fifth engineer at an AI startup. The demo works, an enterprise customer agrees to a trial, and then the proof of concept (PoC) sits idle for six weeks because their data looks nothing like anything your team has tested with. Sales calls every week, the product team is busy pushing the roadmap, and you're patching things in a rush at night.

According to the recruiting platform Paraform, this is almost the clearest signal to hire your first Forward Deployed Engineer (FDE): a stalled PoC deal. It's not a sales problem. The problem lies in the engineering work between the product and the customer's reality, and no one has been assigned to do it.

This article is for two audiences. If you're an engineering leader asked to build the team, here are the steps to follow. If you want to become an FDE, here's how companies will evaluate you and what questions you should ask in return.

Which kind of FDE are you hiring?

Paraform says the most common reason FDE hiring drags on is that the people involved disagree on which kind of FDE to hire. Rocketlane puts it more bluntly: there are five distinct types of FDE, and picking the wrong one for the deal size and pricing model means the math doesn't work.

The actual scope of the work explains why. At Cursor, a project might be a few weeks of gap analysis, or months of building an entire SDK. These two kinds of work require two different kinds of people.

Pricing also shapes the role's positioning. Rocketlane argues that if you charge for outcomes, the FDE becomes the team that delivers those outcomes directly to the customer, part of high-end sales.

So before posting the job, write a one-pager answering the five questions below, and have sales, product, and engineering all read it. If the three answers differ, you've found why the hiring will drag.

QuestionExample answer from the startup above
What signal is prompting the hire?A PoC with a logistics customer stuck on data integration
How long does a typical project last?Weeks of integration work, not yet months of building
How do we sell?A trial with success criteria, then outcome-based pricing
Where does the FDE's code live?A separate repo per customer, no direct changes to core code
Who does the FDE report to?The engineering lead, not the sales lead

The first writes the playbook, the second executes it

Recruiting from Scratch's guide recommends starting with two FDEs. The first must be senior enough to define the deployment playbook; the second turns that playbook into daily practice. The same guide warns: hiring more than about three FDEs before you have a reusable playbook creates chaos.

What does "senior" mean here specifically? Cursor requires 5+ years of software engineering experience, strong system design intuition, and experience working directly with customers.

Palantir's job posting describes the role this way: working in small teams with little supervision, owning important projects end to end.

Paraform also notes that candidates who need detailed requirements before starting will struggle in this role. So the first hire can't be a great engineer who just waits for tickets. At this stage, there are no tickets at all.

As the team grows, Recruiting from Scratch targets one FDE per strategic enterprise customer. Treat this as a later goal, not a starting point.

Interview with real work scenarios

The test Recruiting from Scratch proposes is simple: have the candidate build something small, then explain it to a non-technical stakeholder sitting right there, replacing the usual LeetCode or system design round. It tests exactly the two things an FDE does every week.

Using the startup above as an example, a 90-minute interview could go like this. For the first fifteen minutes, the interviewer plays the customer's operations lead, describing the problem in the user's language, e.g. "whenever a driver loses connectivity, orders get duplicated."

The candidate can ask questions freely. For the next hour, they build a working version from a sample CSV file with deliberately planted errors.

In the final fifteen minutes, a sales or operations colleague joins and listens to the candidate's explanation. Evaluate three things: whether the candidate asks clarifying questions before writing code, whether they spot the dirty data rows on their own, and whether they can explain the limitations of what they built without jargon.

A candidate who stops to ask "what's your definition of a duplicate order?" is usually more valuable than the one who finishes fastest.

Boundaries to draw before day one

Rocketlane recommends drawing an architectural boundary between customer-specific builds and the core product before the first project begins. Otherwise, the first FDE will modify core code to hit a deadline, and three customers later you'll have three divergent product branches.

In the example above, the rule could be: connectors and data-cleaning scripts live in each customer's repo, and any change to core code goes through product team review.

Reporting lines matter just as much. Recruiting from Scratch argues FDEs should belong to engineering, because reporting to sales pulls them into pre-sales work.

Cursor takes a middle path: the VP responsible for FDEs positions the team between engineering and product on one side and go-to-market on the other. Both approaches share one thing: FDEs must stay close to the code.

Mistakes that sink the first FDE team

The first mistake is expecting to hire quickly. Paraform recommends budgeting 8 to 12 weeks for a full FDE hire, with most of the time spent proactively reaching out to candidates rather than waiting for applications. If you need someone handling a stalled PoC next week, temporarily pull an engineer from your existing team while continuing to recruit.

Recommendations like hiring timelines and starting with two people should be treated as recruiting practitioners' experience, not industry standards.

The second mistake is hiring a third and fourth person before the first has finished writing the playbook. The third is having FDEs report to sales, then being surprised they spend all week doing demos. The fourth is harder to spot: hiring an excellent engineer who needs clear requirements, then placing them in a role with none.

If you're the one applying

Read the job posting carefully. If it specifies project length, who you report to, and where the code lives, the company has done its homework. If not, ask these three questions in the first interview.

Don't just write "worked with customers" on your resume. Describe a time you took over a problem without a requirements doc, built a solution yourself, then explained it clearly to non-technical people and got them using it. That's exactly the test companies will give you, so prove you've already passed it in real work.

A good FDE team doesn't start with headcount. It starts with a stalled PoC, a one-pager that clearly defines the role, and one person steady enough to turn the first deployment into the method for every deployment after it.
