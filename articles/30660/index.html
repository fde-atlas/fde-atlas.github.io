What is an FDE? A Complete Guide to the Forward Deployed Engineer: Definition, Origins, What a Day Looks Like, and Why It's Suddenly Blowing Up

FDE stands for Forward Deployed Engineer, sometimes also called a Forward Deployed Software Engineer. Simply put, it is a role that "places engineers next to the customer": the person sits inside the customer's team, works with the customer's real data and real processes, and shapes the product into something the customer can actually use.

This article answers five questions: what an FDE actually is, where the role came from, what a typical day looks like, how it differs from other roles, and why it has been repeatedly brought up since 2025. After reading, you should be able to judge whether this is the role you are looking for, or whether your company needs people like this.

1. What is an FDE? A one-sentence definition

According to the PostHog blog post "WTF is a forward deployed engineer?", an FDE is an engineer embedded in the customer's team who bridges the gap between "what the product can do" and "what the customer needs". There are three key phrases in this definition, and each needs to be unpacked to be understood.

  • Embedded: not a support role that only answers tickets from behind a layer of separation, nor someone who leaves after a week-long business trip. It can mean being on site for weeks, sharing communication channels with the customer, or working deeply in the customer's environment through remote collaboration; data access methods and permissions depend on what both parties agree on.
  • Bridging the gap: the product is generic, but the customer's business is specific. The FDE is responsible for translating generic capabilities into concrete business outcomes, filling in whatever is missing, including writing code, integrating systems, and changing processes.
  • Engineer: this is the most fundamental difference from pre-sales and consulting. An FDE has to build things and get them working with their own hands, not just produce proposals and demos.

2. Where did the FDE role originate? What is its relationship with Palantir?

According to the PostHog blog, this role originated at Palantir. Palantir's customers include intelligence agencies, and a single product demo for such customers required weeks of NDAs and security clearances first. Remote communication and repeated demos were impractical, so Palantir's solution was to "forward deploy" engineers to the customer's site and work directly in the customer's environment.

According to eWeek, Palantir popularized this role, and the idea was later borrowed by many enterprise software companies, becoming a widely discussed role in the era of large language models. Different sources phrase "originated" and "popularized" differently; this article only adopts two claims: the FDE role originated at Palantir (per PostHog), and Palantir popularized it (per eWeek).

From this account by PostHog, it is clear that the starting point of this role was not "great customer service", but that the product was too far from the customer's real environment and would not run unless someone was sent in. Scenarios where the product must work within the customer's private data and complex systems to produce results usually need this kind of role even more.

3. What does an FDE do all day?

It varies greatly between companies. The following is a common day, meant to help you build a mental picture, not a standard process.

  • In the morning, review yesterday's results with the customer's business owner: which answers were correct, which fell far short of business reality, and record the issues as to-dos.
  • In the morning, hands-on work: connect data from one of the customer's internal systems, or modify processing logic so the AI outputs according to the customer's rules. Dirty data, mismatched fields, and missing API documentation are the most time-consuming parts.
  • Around midday, attend the customer's business meeting and listen to frontline staff complain about where the current process gets stuck; these remarks carry more information than requirements documents.
  • In the afternoon, run small-scale trials: get two or three real users using it, and sit nearby watching where they hesitate and where they give up.
  • In the evening, consolidate: record the pitfalls encountered today and the generic parts built, and pass them back to the product team to see which can be turned into features the next customer can also use.

An FDE's work usually involves both understanding the customer's business and engineering development, and the exact time split varies by role. According to the article, common requirements in job postings include: customer empathy, communication and expression, product sense, an awareness of turning solutions into reusable accelerators, and industry-specific knowledge, plus a willingness to mix engineering, consulting, and delivery work.

4. How is an FDE different from a software engineer or a product engineer?

According to PostHog's comparison, software engineers spend most of their time building reusable generic features; product engineers pursue reusable solutions for many users; an FDE focuses on one customer at a time and spends most of their time on the customer's side. A table can lay out the differences clearly.

DimensionFDESoftware Engineer / Product Engineer
Who they work forOne specific customerMany users or the entire product
Where the time goesMostly on the customer's sideMostly within their own team
What they pursueGetting the customer's business running in the real environmentReusable, scalable features
Typical outputLaunched integrations, custom workflows, internal customer toolsProduct features, platform capabilities, infrastructure
Common difficultiesMessy customer data, legacy systems, complex organizational politicsArchitecture trade-offs, iteration cadence, technical debt

An FDE may overlap considerably in responsibilities with AI application engineers and AI implementation engineers; the difference is that an FDE places more emphasis on "being on the customer's side". For a more detailed distinction between these roles, see another article on this site, "What is the difference between an FDE and a Solutions Engineer".

5. Why has FDE hiring heated up since 2025?

According to an eWeek report from November 2025 citing Financial Times research, Palantir popularized this role; OpenAI formed an FDE team in early 2024 and planned to expand it to about 50 engineers by 2025; Anthropic planned to grow its Applied AI team (including FDEs and product engineers) fivefold that year — that is the entire team including FDEs and product engineers, not FDEs alone growing fivefold, and it was only a plan; Cohere was also expanding its FDE team. Indeed data shows that FDE job postings grew more than 800% from January to September 2025. According to the same report, OpenAI also deployed FDEs to John Deere to build custom tools.

Why this point in time? PostHog's article offers several explanations, which we summarize in three points:

  • No matter how strong the models are, they cannot get into enterprises: FDEs help open up the "application layer", turning model capabilities into enterprise value; what is often missing is not the model but the segment that connects it to the business.
  • Data that customers won't hand over: according to PostHog, customers are unwilling to give sensitive data to vendors, while FDEs can obtain long-term access permissions and work within the customer's environment. Whether data concerns are truly eliminated still depends on the access methods and permissions agreed by both parties.
  • Value must be proven with real data: many executives doubt AI's value, and FDEs prove it with real data rather than demos.

As quoted in the PostHog article, a16z called it "the hottest job in startups". In the report, Palantir's Nic Prettejohn described it as "doing product discovery from the inside". In other words, an FDE is both delivery and the product's frontline radar.

6. Do companies in China need FDEs? Who is suited for this role?

Chinese job postings may use titles like "AI implementation engineer", "AI application engineer", or "AI deployment engineer"; whether these are close to FDE depends on the specific responsibilities: whether they go deep into the customer's environment, whether they write code themselves and ship it, and whether they are accountable for business outcomes. For companies, the most practical question is: do you have AI use cases complex and valuable enough to justify assigning someone to dig into them long term.

  • People suited for this role: those who understand an industry or a type of business process, are willing to write code or get hands-on with AI coding tools, are not afraid of communicating with frontline staff, and have patience for details.
  • People less suited: those who only want to do pure deep technical work, dislike dealing with people, or only want to produce proposals without seeing things through to completion.
  • Signals from the enterprise side: usable models and platforms already exist, but launch gets stuck on concrete issues like data, system integration, and business processes.
