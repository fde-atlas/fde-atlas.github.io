OpenAI Forward Deployed Engineer: The Role and How to Prepare for the Interview

Prepare.sh · 2026-11-04 · 5 min read

Summary

This guide from Prepare.sh describes OpenAI's forward deployed engineer (FDE) role based on job postings from November 4, 2026, when 33 of 812 open roles were forward deployed positions. FDEs lead technical discovery, architecture, implementation, evaluations, productionization, and handoff inside customer organizations, without owning the commercial relationship. Posted US salaries range roughly $145,800–$325,000 plus equity, with up to 50% travel. OpenAI also cited about 150 FDEs and Deployment Specialists in its deployment company announcement. The article admits almost nothing verifiable exists about the FDE interview loop, recommends asking recruiters specific questions, and offers practice questions derived from the postings, emphasizing shipped LLM projects with evals.

  • FDEs own the technical solution end to end but not the commercial relationship.

OpenAI's forward deployed engineers (FDEs) work inside customer organizations to get its models into production, handling everything from technical discovery to handoff. On November 4, 2026, its careers search returned 33 forward deployed jobs out of 812. The postings are specific about the work and silent about the interview, so this guide is built from the postings, and it's upfront about what isn't known.

What the role looks like

OpenAI's FDE posting for healthcare says you "lead technical discovery, architecture, implementation, evaluation, productionization, and handoff". That's a good summary of the job.

Evaluation gets real weight. The same posting asks you to "Define and operationalize evaluations, validation evidence, human-review workflows, escalation paths, and launch criteria". The Forward Deployed Software Engineer posting describes "coding side-by-side to drive projects to completion on their infrastructure", and asks you to "Prepare detailed scopes of work and project plans".

One useful limit from the healthcare posting: "This role owns the technical solution; ownership of the commercial or executive relationship is not required." So it isn't a sales job, though you'll work with people who hold that relationship.

OpenAI is also expanding this work beyond its own payroll. Its deployment company announcement from May 2026 describes embedding engineers "known as Forward Deployed Engineers, or FDEs" and mentions "approximately 150 experienced Forward Deployed Engineers and Deployment Specialists".

The SWE role in New York and Seattle starts lower, at $153,000 to $325,000, and the Gov role is $145,800 to $280,000. Deployment Leads run from about $200,000 to $295,000. Non-US FDE roles don't show pay on the board.

Take the travel line seriously. Up to 50% is a big commitment, and it's on both postings we read.

What we know about the interview

Very little that's specific. Neither posting mentions the interview process, OpenAI doesn't publish an FDE-specific loop that we could find, and we found no readable first-hand report of one. Most of what's online is from prep sites, which we can't verify, so it isn't repeated here.

OpenAI's general process is covered in our OpenAI interview guide. Expect FDE to differ, and ask your recruiter these directly:

  • Which rounds are in my loop, and how long is each?
  • Is there a customer role-play or a scoping exercise?
  • Is there a take-home, and may I use AI tools?
  • Is the coding round practical or algorithmic?

Practice questions

We wrote these from the job descriptions. They aren't reported interview questions, but each one tests something the posting names.

"A hospital network wants to use our models to summarize clinical notes. Walk me through your first two weeks."

This tests "technical discovery". A strong answer starts with questions, not architecture:

  • Who uses it and what decision does it change? A summary for a discharge nurse has different failure costs than a billing summary.
  • What's the worst outcome? A summary that invents a medication is much worse than one that omits a preference. So you design for that failure first.
  • What are the constraints? Where can the data go, who reviews outputs, and what's their existing system?
  • What's the first slice? One note type, one department, read-only, with a clinician reviewing each output.

Then define launch criteria before building: for example, "no fabricated medications in a reviewed sample of N notes", with N agreed with the customer's clinical lead. End with who owns it after you leave, since the posting expects handoff.

Follow-ups to expect: How do you measure a fabrication rate at scale? What happens when a clinician disagrees with the rubric? When would you recommend not launching?

"How would you evaluate this system, and how do you decide it's ready?"

The posting's wording is your outline: evaluations, validation evidence, human review, escalation paths and launch criteria. Say what each is here.

  • Evaluations: A test set of real notes with expert-written expectations, run on every change to a prompt or model.
  • Validation evidence: A record you can show a compliance team, including what was tested, on what, and with what result.
  • Human review: Start with review of every output, and reduce it only with evidence.
  • Escalation: Low-confidence or high-risk cases go to a person, with a defined path.
  • Launch criteria: Numbers agreed in advance, so that "ready" isn't decided by whoever is most impatient.

Our AI engineer interview questions go deeper on evals and retrieval.

"The customer's data can't leave their network. What changes?"

This comes from "on their infrastructure". Don't guess at what's possible: say you'd first check which deployment options are available and what the customer's security team requires. Then cover how it changes the design: logging and evals have to run inside their boundary, your debugging access may be limited, and you'll need to build with their team and not just for them.

"Write a scope of work for a six-week pilot."

From "Prepare detailed scopes of work and project plans". Show the outline: the goal in the customer's terms, what's in and out of scope, milestones, dependencies on the customer (access, a named contact, review time), success metrics, and risks. Explicitly naming what you need from them is what separates a good scope from a wish list.

Coding and system design

The FD SWE posting asks for full-stack engineering, so prepare practical work: API clients, retries, data parsing, and a small end-to-end service. Our system design guide covers the design side. OpenAI's hub of practice questions has hands-on options such as cleaning null values in a dataset and extracting domains with regex, which are close to the data clean-up you'd do on a customer project.

How to stand out

  • Show a shipped LLM project with an eval. The posting's emphasis on evaluation makes this your best evidence.
  • Bring a customer story. Scoping, a difficult stakeholder, or a project that changed direction once you understood the real problem.
  • Know the domain a little. For a healthcare role, you don't need clinical credentials (the posting says so), but do read about how notes and review work.
  • Use the product. Read OpenAI's current API docs and build with them, so your answers refer to how it works today.
