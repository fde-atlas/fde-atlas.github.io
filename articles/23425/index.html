How to choose an FDE offer: the proportion of coding in a Forward Deployed Engineer role

Original title: FDE offer 怎么选：前线部署工程师写代码占比

FDE Times · 2026-11-18 · 5 min read

Summary

This article warns engineers holding FDE offers that the same title can mean very different jobs. An analysis of 1,000 job postings by Bloomberry found build-focused roles spend 70-90% of time coding, while "Sales Engineer+" roles spend only 30-40% and carry sales quotas. Jobs with the same Forward Deployed Engineer (FDE) title can involve anywhere from 30% to 90% coding per week, and some roles involve no development at all.

Key takeaways

  • The 25% coding, 50% integration, 25% meetings split is one author's interpretation of a single Google Cloud job posting, not survey data.
  • An analysis of 1,000 FDE job postings found: builder roles spend 70-90% of their time coding, while "Sales Engineer+" roles spend only 30-40% and carry sales quotas.
  • Some jobs titled FDE involve no software development at all. Always ask about the coding situation before signing.

Bloomberry analyzed 1,000 Forward Deployed Engineer (FDE) job postings and found that the same job title covers at least two very different jobs. The first type spends 70-90% of its time coding. The second, which it calls "Sales Engineer+", spends only 30-40% coding and carries sales quotas.

This gap is far wider than the commonly cited FDE work split: roughly 25% code-related, 50% on integration and connecting systems, and 25% on meetings and direct customer support.

That split comes from The Pragmatic Engineer, but it is one author's interpretation of a single Google Cloud job posting, not a survey and not official data.

If you are holding an FDE offer right now, do not apply industry averages. The actual coding time for this specific role determines whether you will still be an engineer two years from now, so you must work it out yourself before signing.

The 25/50/25 split is just one interpretation

Even taken as illustrative, the split is still useful. On a 40-hour week, that is roughly 10 hours code-related, 20 hours on integration, and 10 hours dealing with customers. For someone who spends entire weeks shipping features in a familiar codebase, this is a completely different rhythm.

But those 20 hours of integration may not be real engineering work. In an article discussing whether FDE roles are losing their appeal, The Pragmatic Engineer quoted a developer's warning: at some companies, FDEs do almost nothing that qualifies as software engineering beyond integrating software that product teams have already built.

So the integration question must be asked specifically: are you writing code to connect systems, or just configuring things others have built? Job postings call both of these "integration".

The week is not evenly distributed

There is another reason not to trust any average: an FDE's coding time comes in clusters, not spread evenly. A Palantir FDE told The Pragmatic Engineer that some weeks he spends most of his time writing and reviewing the team's code, no different from an ordinary software engineer.

An FDE at Wonderful described to Insight Partners a rhythm of alternating days: some days spent entirely coding and fixing bugs, others spent entirely on-site with customers. Even on-site time varies: Palantir expects FDEs to spend about 25% of their time with customers, while at Commure that figure can reach 50%.

In practice, asking "roughly what percentage is coding?" often gets you a tidy but meaningless answer. Instead, ask one of the FDEs on the team to walk you through their last four weeks: which weeks were spent coding, which were spent with customers, and where the code they wrote ended up.

Less travel is not necessarily a good thing

There is one detail in Bloomberry's data that is easy to misread. Builder roles with 70-90% coding spend 30-50% of their time traveling. "Sales Engineer+" roles with 30-40% coding travel less than 20%, but carry sales quotas.

Many engineers would find an offer with less travel more comfortable. But if low travel comes with a quota, it likely means you were hired to support sales rather than to build systems that run at customer sites.

When reading a job posting, look at three signals together: the coding share, the on-site time, and whether quotas, commissions, or revenue are mentioned. Any one signal alone means little; the three together are telling.

When the FDE title hides a different job

The biggest risk is not 30% or 25%, but zero. In the same article discussing whether FDE roles are losing their appeal, The Pragmatic Engineer quoted an engineer's experience: the company wanted to use him primarily as a project liaison, giving him no software development work at all.

At the other extreme, First Round Review reported that Ironclad's "legal engineers" merge code to production and do code reviews with the CTO every week. The value of this example is that the coding is verifiable: there are commits, there is a production environment, there are reviewers.

That is the standard to apply. Companies that genuinely treat FDEs as engineers can explain where FDE code gets reviewed and how it reaches production. The table below turns the signals from these sources into questions you can ask employers directly.

Question to askSignal of a builder roleWarning sign
How much of the last four weeks did an FDE on the team spend coding?Most of the time, close to the builder range of 70-90%Around 30-40%, or nobody can give a number
Are there sales quotas or commissions?No quota mentioned in the offerA quota, combined with less than 20% on-site time
Does FDE code get merged to production and reviewed?Yes, weekly, like IroncladOnly configuring and integrating what product teams have already built
Which repository would you commit to in your first week?A specific repository, with reviewersOnly docs, demos, and a meeting schedule
Why is this role being opened?A technical problem at a customer that no one has been able to solveA need for one more person to coordinate customer projects

The last question itself comes from First Round Review. The publication advises hiring managers to think through: why open this role, who is doing this work now, and what happens if they cannot hire anyone. Turn those questions back on them: the answers will reveal whether the company needs an engineer or a customer relationship manager.

Scarcity gives you standing to push for answers

Executive search firm Christian & Timbers says it mapped more than 17,000 FDEs in the US, of whom about 2,000 meet its standard of "elite".

The original research states "Not 2,000 available, 2,000 total", which TechCrunch interpreted as the number of engineers with the required expertise, not the total size of the FDE population.

The firm also predicts that by the end of 2026, overall demand for FDEs will grow by about 2,100%. This is its own forecast and is best treated as a signal of market direction.

However cautiously you read them, both numbers point the same way: the market is competing for people with deep technical foundations, not anyone carrying the FDE title.

A role that stops you from coding will pull you out of that group, and the current scarcity gives you enough leverage to demand clear answers before signing.

Keep your resume an engineer's resume

Whichever offer you accept, write your resume to the same verifiable standard. Every line should state: what code you merged into production, who reviewed it, which customer the system runs at, and which part was yours.

To anyone reading a resume, a line like this instantly distinguishes builders from coordinators. If, six months into a new job, you can't write even one line like that, it's time to reconsider your original choice.

When you're holding an offer, that's exactly when you have the most leverage to ask questions. A company that truly treats FDEs as engineers will show you what team members have worked on over the past four weeks, as well as the code repository you'll be committing to in your first week; if they dodge both, the answer is clear.
