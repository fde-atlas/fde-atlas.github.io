How to Calculate ROI for AI Projects: How an FDE Proves Value to the CFO

Original title: AI 项目 ROI 怎么算：FDE 如何向 CFO 证明价值

FDE Times · 2026-10-08 · 5 min read

Summary

This guide explains how forward deployed engineers can prove AI project ROI to a CFO with measured data rather than user impressions. Citing a METR trial where developers felt 20% faster but were actually 19% slower, it argues for baseline measurement before deployment, a net-savings formula that subtracts mandatory review time and probabilistic rework time, and a per-successful-task cost metric. An invoice-processing example shows gross savings of 435 seconds per invoice dropping to 351 after deductions. It also lists common mistakes and warns that payback typically takes years, per Deloitte data.

Key points

  • METR trial: developers felt 20% faster but were actually 19% slower.
  • Measure a baseline before deployment; use a control group when possible.
  • Net savings must subtract review time and probability-weighted rework time.
  • Example: 435 seconds gross savings per invoice becomes 351 net.
  • Deloitte survey: typical AI use cases take two to four years to pay back.

Key Takeaways

  • Self-reported time is unreliable: in the METR experiment, developers actually took 19% more time yet still believed they were 20% faster.
  • Count only work completed by AI that meets quality standards, then subtract review time (which always happens) and rework time (calculated by probability).
  • Measure the baseline before deployment, and be honest with the customer: payback usually takes years, not months.

Three months after the system went into production, a new face appeared at the quarterly review meeting: the customer's Chief Financial Officer (CFO). She didn't ask which model was being used, nor what the latency was. She asked only one question: "Is this project worth the money we invested?"

If all you have at that moment is a screenshot of a dashboard and a few glowing user quotes, the project is already half lost. Forward Deployed Engineers (FDEs) are not paid to ship features.

Palantir puts it bluntly in the job description for its Forward Deployed Enablement Engineer: the role exists to maximize the output of deployed products and workflows and tie them to the customer's most important business outcomes.

So measuring ROI is not just a sales or finance matter. It is an engineering capability that requires data, well-designed measurement methods, and honesty. Using an invoice-processing data pipeline as an example, the following walks through the calculation from baseline all the way to the final number presented to the CFO.

Why can't you trust the feeling of "being faster"?

The first trap is asking users. In 2025, METR ran a randomized controlled trial with experienced open-source developers. The result: when using AI tools, they took 19% more time to complete tasks than when not using them.

What's more notable is the detail that follows. Before the trial, these developers expected to be faster. After the trial, despite the measurements showing the opposite, they still believed AI had made them 20% faster.

The two numbers use different measures: one is the measured extra time, the other is the speedup users believed they gained. But they point in opposite directions, and that is what should raise alarm.

For an FDE, the lesson is clear. Questionnaires like "How much time do you think you saved?" help understand user experience, but cannot serve as financial evidence. What goes to the CFO must be measured per-task time, with a before and an after number.

Start from goals and baselines, not from the model

Paul Parks of AICPA & CIMA wrote in the Journal of Accountancy about an ROI process whose first step is clarifying objectives: what is the use case, and what does management hope to achieve with this investment. His framework also accounts for benefits that cannot be measured in money.

For an FDE, this is customer requirements discovery: before writing the first line of code, you need to know which number the customer will use to judge success or failure.

The next step is the baseline. Delos recommends using the first 30 days of a 90-day framework to establish baseline metrics before reporting any ROI numbers. The logic is simple: without measuring "before," there is nothing to compare "after" against.

Trying to find a baseline after the system is already running is usually too late, because the old process no longer exists.

Parks also points out that the benefits side is inherently hard to measure, because most organizations run multiple projects at once. An increase in invoice processing volume might be thanks to your system, or to the finance team having just hired new staff.

When conditions allow, keep a control group, such as a branch office or a category of documents not yet connected to the system, to isolate the effect of the project itself.

Example: an invoice-processing data pipeline

Delos gives an intuitive before-and-after comparison: processing an invoice drops from 8 minutes to 45 seconds. This framing is right because it counts per task rather than vaguely claiming "efficiency gains." But if you stop there, the numbers are still inflated.

GSPANN calls the missing piece the repair burden: the time spent manually reviewing and correcting AI output. Their summary: review is a certainty, rework is a probability.

To that end, GSPANN offers a net savings formula. In words: gross labor hours saved, minus repair hours, minus the system's other costs.

Apply a hypothetical scenario: the customer processes 10,000 invoices per month, each invoice requires 1 minute of human review of the results, and 5% of invoices are wrong and need manual redoing, taking 8 minutes each.

Component (per invoice)CalculationSeconds
Gross savings480 − 45435
Review (always happens)1 minute × 100%−60
Rework (by probability)8 minutes × 5%−24
Net savings351

Across 10,000 invoices, that's 3,510,000 seconds of net savings, or 975 hours per month. Delos recommends that when converting labor hours into money, multiply the saved time by the fully loaded hourly cost of the people who originally did the work, meaning salary plus insurance, benefits, and overhead.

Then subtract the costs of the model, infrastructure, and operations staff.

The second number to hand to finance is cost per successful task. Following GSPANN's method, count only work completed by AI that meets the agreed quality threshold, sum all the costs of producing that work, and divide.

In the example above, if manually redone invoices are assumed not to count as AI-completed work (even though after correction they may meet the standard), the denominator is 9,500, not 10,000.

Applying this in your own projects

Start with a meeting with the budget owner, not the end users. Ask which business metric they want to improve, and what quality threshold counts as "done." Write it down and have them confirm it.

Then measure the baseline on the old process: time each task, count the business volume, and record the current error rate. After the system goes live, record three things for each task: processing time, human review time, and whether the task required rework. Without the third column, the repair burden can never be calculated.

Finally, talk about payback period from the start. A Deloitte survey cited by ACCA found that a typical AI use case takes two to four years to achieve ROI, with only 6% of respondents breaking even within a year. Promising payback in a quarter is digging your own grave.

Common mistakes that get your numbers rejected

The most common mistake is reporting gross savings: taking 8 minutes minus 45 seconds and multiplying it out, ignoring review time. The finance people will immediately ask: who is reviewing these outputs? At that point, your numbers fall apart.

The second mistake is counting failed tasks in the denominator, making the per-task cost look cheaper than it really is. The third is crediting all improvements to your project while the customer is simultaneously running three other projects.

The fourth is relying on perception surveys. METR has already shown that perception can be wrong not just in magnitude, but even in direction.

Where should this skill go on your resume?

For developers looking to transition into an FDE role, this skill can make your resume stand out. When reading job descriptions, watch for phrases like "business outcomes," "value realization," and "customer success." They signal that the company wants people who can measure results, not just people who can build things.

On your resume, replace descriptions like "built an invoice OCR data pipeline" with statements that include the baseline, post-deployment data, and pass rates.

This week's exercise

Take a feature you've delivered, whether a work or personal project, and build a table like the invoice above: gross savings, inspection time, rework time, net savings. Any cell left blank because you lack measured data is exactly the data you must start collecting from day one of your next project.

When the CFO shows up at the retrospective, the winner is usually not the person with the flashiest demo, but the one who measured a baseline on day one.
