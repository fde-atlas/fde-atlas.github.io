What Is Human-in-the-Loop? Designing AI Operations Where Humans Make Decisions and the Role of FDEs

Original title: ヒューマンインザループとは？AI運用で人が判断を担う設計とFDEの役割

FDE総研 · 2026-11-02 · 12 min read

Summary

This Japanese article explains Human-in-the-Loop (HITL): designing AI workflows so humans approve, correct, or reject AI outputs and remain accountable for final decisions. It distinguishes HITL, HOTL, and full automation, proposes choosing among them based on error cost and daily volume, and identifies four intervention points (input validation, low-confidence outputs, high-impact actions, exceptions). It argues HITL design is well suited to FDEs, who decompose on-site workflows, surface implicit rules, implement thresholds, review UIs, and logs, then tune intervention rates. It also covers pitfalls like rubber-stamping, automation bias, and regulatory context (EU AI Act, GDPR, NIST AI RMF).

Key points

  • HITL inserts human approval into AI decision loops; frameworks like EU AI Act Article 14 and NIST AI RMF require human oversight.
  • Choose HITL, HOTL, or full automation by weighing error cost against processing volume.
  • Focus human review on four points: inputs, low-confidence outputs, irreversible actions, and exceptions.
  • Beware rubber-stamping and automation bias; design around the time humans actually have to judge.
  • FDEs span SWE, consulting, and PdM roles: decomposing workflows, implementing thresholds and logs, and iterating with customers.

What Is Human-in-the-Loop (HITL)?

Human-in-the-Loop (HITL) is a design approach in which human review, correction, and approval are inserted into the AI processing flow, with humans holding responsibility for the final decision.

Human-in-the-Loop (HITL) originally referred, in the field of machine learning, to a mechanism where humans label data and evaluate models to improve them. Now that generative AI and AI agents have entered business operations, the meaning has broadened somewhat. It is now commonly used to include "designing business workflows so that humans make decisions at defined points rather than executing AI output as-is."

Definition: A mechanism where human judgment enters somewhere in the AI decision loop

The core of HITL is inserting human judgment between AI output and execution, allowing approval, correction, or rejection at each step.

The HITL mechanism is easier to organize by breaking it into the following three components.

ComponentDescriptionExample
TriggerConditions for routing a decision to a humanConfidence falls below a threshold, amount exceeds a limit
Intervention actionOperations a human can performApprove, correct, reject, escalate
FeedbackPath for returning human decisions to the AI or business rulesUse correction history as evaluation data, update rules

If feedback is missing from these three components, HITL becomes "a mechanism where humans do cleanup work manually." Whether HITL is properly designed depends on whether human corrections are retained as data and used for the next round of improvement.

Why HITL Is Needed Now: Trends in Regulations and Guidelines

HITL is attracting attention because the scope of decisions AI handles in business has expanded, and laws and guidelines have begun requiring human oversight.

Public frameworks explicitly state the following regarding human involvement and oversight:

FrameworkIssuing body and dateMain points regarding human involvement
EU AI Act (Regulation (EU) 2024/1689), Article 14European Union, adopted 2024High-risk AI systems must be designed so that natural persons can effectively exercise human oversight
GDPR, Article 22European Union, effective 2018The right not to be subject to decisions based solely on automated processing that produce legal effects
AI RMF 1.0NIST (US), January 2023Requires clarifying human roles and responsibilities in AI risk management
AI Business GuidelinesMinistry of Internal Affairs and Communications and METI, April 2024 (Version 1.0)Requires humans to be appropriately involved in AI decisions under the human-centric principle

The scope of application and degree of obligations differ across frameworks. Whether your company's systems fall under them requires checking the original texts and consulting legal counsel. However, the direction is clear: "creating a structure where humans can take responsibility for AI-generated results" is becoming a prerequisite.

How Does HITL Differ from HOTL and Full Automation?

HITL is a method where humans are involved in each decision; HOTL is a method where humans monitor and stop only when necessary; full automation involves no human involvement.

Three Levels Based on Depth of Human Involvement

Human involvement in AI operations falls into three categories: "humans present before the decision, monitoring alongside it, or outside it."

MethodHuman positionHandling of AI outputExample use cases
HITL (Human-in-the-Loop)Inside the decision loopExecuted after human approvalCredit decisions, finalizing medical/legal documents, sending external responses
HOTL (Human-on-the-Loop)Monitoring above the loopExecuted automatically; humans intervene or stop on anomaliesFraud detection alert operations, monitoring high-volume routine processing
Full automation (Human-out-of-the-Loop)Outside the loopExecuted without human interventionSpam detection, internal summaries and classification

Some frameworks add Human-in-Command (HIC) to these three. HIC refers to higher-level control where humans decide whether to use AI and in what scope. While HITL and HOTL concern day-to-day operational levels, HIC concerns governance levels—keeping this distinction in mind helps avoid confusion.

Selection Criteria: "Cost of Errors" and "Processing Volume"

FDE Soken recommends comparing two factors when choosing a method: "the cost when an error occurs" and "the number of items processed per day."

  • High error cost, low volume → HITL (feasible even if humans review every item)
  • High error cost, high volume → Combination of HITL and HOTL (humans review only high-risk items, monitor the rest)
  • Low error cost, high volume → HOTL or full automation
  • Low error cost, low volume → First evaluate the ROI of automation

In practice, a common approach is to "start with HITL for everything and expand automation as the team gains experience." While this seems safe, caution is needed. Unless you establish upfront criteria for when humans will be removed based on results, human review will remain indefinitely, and operational costs will become fixed without realizing the benefits of introducing AI.

Which Process Steps Should HITL Place Humans In?

The basic approach for HITL human-in-the-loop steps is to narrow them down to four: input verification, low-confidence outputs, high-impact executions, and exception handling.

Four decision points for involving humans

Rather than inserting humans into every step, concentrate them at the point just before errors affect external parties and in situations where AI struggles.

Intervention pointWhat humans reviewExample triggers
① Input verificationWhether the data and instructions passed to the AI are correctNew data sources, inputs with many missing values
② Low-confidence outputsDecisions where the AI is uncertainConfidence score below threshold, small differences among multiple candidates
③ High-impact executionsOperations that are hard to reverseMoney transfers, sending to customers, updating contracts or production data
④ Exceptions and unknown casesUnexpected patternsClassifications not in the training data, inputs that don't match any rules

Among these, ③ "just before hard-to-reverse operations" is where keeping human approval is highly valuable regardless of AI accuracy. In configurations where AI agents call external tools or APIs, a practical approach is to automate read operations and insert approval only for write and send operations.

Steps for designing HITL

HITL design starts with breaking down the business process, then decides thresholds, screens, and logging in that order, and proceeds by reducing the intervention rate while operating.

  • Break down the target process into decision units and estimate the cost of errors for each
  • Assign each decision unit to one of the four intervention points
  • Tentatively set thresholds for routing to humans (confidence, amount, target customers, etc.)
  • Build a review screen where the approver can quickly verify the rationale (displaying AI output, rationale, and source data side by side)
  • Log approvals, corrections, rejections, and their reasons
  • Regularly review intervention rate, correction rate, and processing time, and revise thresholds and scope

Design to prevent "approval fatigue" is also essential

If too many items are routed to humans, reviews become mere formal approvals, defeating the purpose of HITL.

When the volume routed to humans grows too large, reviewers start pressing the approve button without reading the content—the so-called rubber-stamping problem. To prevent this, consider the following measures.

  • Narrow down the volume of items routed to humans itself
  • Specify review criteria concretely with checklists
  • Review only the cases where AI and human judgments diverged

From a practical standpoint, designing HITL based on "the time humans have available to make judgments" rather than "the number of items humans review" tends to make it sustainable over the long term.

What role does an FDE play in HITL design?

An FDE goes into the customer's site, breaks down the business processes, decides where humans intervene, the thresholds, and the screens, implements them, and is responsible through operation and improvement.

FDE (Forward Deployed Engineer) is a role popularized by Palantir. FDEs are stationed at or deeply involved with customer sites, integrating the product into actual business operations. HITL design involves many decisions that require understanding both technology and business, making it a natural fit for the FDE role.

Break down on-site operations and decide where humans come in

The success of HITL depends on which judgments are left to humans. Drawing that line requires interviews with on-site staff and observation of the actual work.

The procedures written in business manuals often differ from how judgments are actually made. For example, a veteran employee may operate under an implicit rule like "for this customer's cases, route them to a supervisor just in case." If AI is introduced without noticing this, cases that should be reviewed by humans end up processed automatically. The FDE's role is to trace the actual decision flow together with on-site staff, make implicit rules explicit, and translate them into trigger conditions.

Implement thresholds, UI, and logs, and improve through operation

The FDE doesn't just hand over a design document; they build the threshold settings, review screens, and judgment logging mechanisms themselves, adjusting based on the numbers.

Right after introducing HITL, thresholds tend to skew one way or the other: too strict, increasing the human burden, or too loose, letting errors through. The FDE monitors intervention and correction rates and adjusts thresholds in agreement with the customer's staff. Feeding human correction history back into AI evaluation and prompt improvements is also within the scope expected of an FDE.

FDE Research Institute's comparison axis: Differences in involvement by role in HITL design

In HITL design, the FDE is not just one of SWE, IT consultant, or PdM, but covers parts that span across each of them.

SWE (Software Engineer)Implementation to spec, such as review screens and logging infrastructureLimitedYes

For engineers aiming to become FDEs, the differentiator is not just "being able to write code" but also "being able to elicit the on-site decision flow and explain which judgments to leave to humans and which to automate."

When is HITL unsuitable, and what are its limits?

For processes requiring real-time responses, or situations where reviewers lack sufficient knowledge or time, HITL struggles to function as a safety measure.

Cases where adding humans doesn't make things safer

The biggest pitfall of HITL is when the mere presence of a human creates a false sense of security, and in reality the person simply rubber-stamps the AI's output.

CaseLikely problemAlternative approach
Processes requiring millisecond-level responsesHuman judgment can't keep upUse HOTL instead, with mechanisms for post-hoc monitoring and shutdown
Reviewers lack specialized knowledgeUnable to detect AI errorsCreate an escalation path to experts
High volume with little time per itemRubber-stampingNarrow the scope or switch to sampling-based audits
Situations where people tend to trust AI output unconditionallyAutomation bias (the tendency to over-trust machine judgments)Try a procedure where a person makes the primary judgment before the AI's judgment is shown

Also, while HITL is a mechanism for clarifying accountability, it must not become a mechanism that shifts responsibility onto the person who approves. A design that assigns responsibility without providing the information and time needed to make the judgment will undermine both organizational trust and AI adoption itself.

The optimal design changes depending on conditions

The optimal form of HITL varies with company size, business risk, and the experience of the people involved, so it is important not to simply copy other companies' examples.

  • Small organizations and small teams: Since the number of people who can make judgments is limited, applying HITL to everything tends to make the person in charge a bottleneck. It is realistic to start by limiting it to high-impact executions (③) only.
  • Regulated industries (finance, healthcare, public sector, etc.): Laws, regulations, and supervisory authority guidelines may require human involvement, so design with regulatory compliance prioritized over operational efficiency.
  • Engineers and aspiring FDEs with little AI implementation experience: Starting by identifying intervention points and designing judgment logs makes it easier to grasp the overall picture of HITL.

At FDE Soken, we recommend viewing HITL not as a "temporary measure while AI accuracy is insufficient" but as "a mechanism for continuously reviewing the division of roles between humans and AI." Deciding in agreement with the customer which judgments to leave to humans and which data to use to expand automation. We believe that this process itself is one of the jobs where the value of the FDE profession is most evident.
