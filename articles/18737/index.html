Frontend FDE job requirements: Forward Deployed Engineers also need to understand the backend

Original title: 前端 FDE 岗位要求：前线部署工程师也要懂后端

FDE Times · 2026-11-11 · 6 min read

Summary

This article compares two job postings to show that even frontend-titled FDE roles demand far more than UI skills. Foxglove hires a "Frontend Engineer, Forward Deployed" but requires Node.js or Python backend experience plus 3 years in robotics or autonomous driving, with code deployed on customer cloud or on-prem infrastructure. Lovable is building its first FDE team, asking for 8+ years of production experience and expecting engineers to turn recurring customer needs into platform features. The author advises frontend engineers to build and operate their own backend, develop industry expertise, and document scope, timelines, and success metrics in their resumes.

Key points

  • Foxglove's frontend FDE role also requires Node.js or Python backend skills.
  • Foxglove demands 3+ years of robotics or autonomous driving experience.
  • Lovable's FDE team is new; the bar is 8 years of production experience.
  • Lovable FDEs decide which customer needs become platform features.
  • Frontend engineers should add backend, domain, and scoping skills.

Engineers building interfaces at customer sites also handle scoping, deployment, and bringing lessons back into the product.

Key takeaways

  • Foxglove puts "Frontend" in the job title, yet also requires Node.js or Python backend skills plus 3+ years of experience with robotics or autonomous driving systems.
  • Lovable is hiring the first members of its newly formed FDE team, with work spanning frontend and backend, an 8+ year experience bar, and responsibility for turning customer needs into platform features.
  • To move from frontend to FDE, you need a backend you've deployed yourself, a domain specialty, and resume bullets that spell out scope and success metrics.

Foxglove puts "Frontend" right in the title of its Forward Deployed Engineer (FDE) role. Yet in the same job posting, the company requires at least 3 years of robotics or autonomous driving experience, plus backend development experience with Node.js or Python.

Lovable's FDE job title doesn't mention "frontend" at all, but the job description stretches from frontend all the way to backend, with an 8-year bar of production-grade software development experience. Two companies, two naming approaches, one shared signal: the person building interfaces at the customer site can't just build interfaces.

For frontend engineers eyeing FDE as a career path, this detail is worth a close read. "Frontend" in a job posting sounds like an easy entry point. In reality, the role makes you accountable to the customer, from the first page of the scope document all the way to going live on the customer's servers.

Why hire along the interface axis?

The most plausible explanation: the interface is where customers see value. A dashboard that correctly displays the customer's own data, a workflow that fits how the customer's team actually works—these are exactly what gets a pilot renewed.

But to get that dashboard running in the customer's environment, someone has to connect it to data sources, handle authentication, complete the deployment, and keep it running.

Foxglove is blunt about it: candidates need to master React/TypeScript on the frontend and have Node.js or Python backend experience. The full-stack requirement is fully intact—only the emphasis is on the side the user can see.

Lovable goes further. It requires its FDEs to design, build, test, deploy, and operate solutions on both Lovable's frontend and backend. That describes an engineer who owns things end to end, not someone who turns design mockups into pages.

Lovable: the first cohort sets the rules

Lovable's biggest difference is that this role has no template yet. The job posting states that whoever joins will be one of the first builders on the company's FDE team. No internal playbook, no senior FDE colleagues to ask how a collaboration should run.

Lovable needs such a team because of its growth. According to the company, since launching in November 2024, users have built over 60 million projects on Lovable, with customers ranging from individuals to major brands.

Once large customers come in, their needs rarely fit inside a self-serve product.

Lovable also clarifies a common misconception upfront: this is a hands-on software engineering role where you write code—not sales engineering, not technical support. The goal is stated plainly: identify what should become part of the Lovable platform, then build it yourself.

This detail is the core of the role. Lovable's FDE doesn't just solve one customer's problem—they also judge when that problem is common enough to be worth building as a general feature. That may explain the 8-year bar: product judgment is far harder to teach than React.

Foxglove: writing code on someone else's infrastructure

Foxglove paints a different picture. Its FDEs work directly with strategic customers to integrate and extend the Foxglove platform in customer environments. Code is written alongside the customer's engineers and deployed on the customer's own cloud or on-prem infrastructure.

Doing frontend work on someone else's infrastructure is a craft of its own. You don't have your familiar CI, you can't install tools freely, and every environment failure happens in front of the customer's engineering team. The ability to debug a build that runs on your machine but breaks on their servers is worth more than any component library.

Foxglove also requires domain expertise: 5+ years of JavaScript/TypeScript experience, plus 3+ years of robotics or autonomous driving experience. Put the two numbers together and it's clear the company isn't planning to hire a frontend ace and slowly teach them robotics. It needs someone who can speak the same language as robotics teams from the very first meeting.

The role is also embedded in the commercial process. Foxglove's FDE shares responsibility for customer accounts with the sales and customer success teams, and must write the scope, timeline, and success metrics for everything from proof of concept (PoC) to production launch. The person writing code is also the person writing commitments to the customer.

Read down the columns: Lovable needs someone to bring customer-side experience back into the product; Foxglove needs someone to carry the product deep into the customer's world. Read across the rows: neither company pays just for building pages.

One dashboard, from PoC to production

Imagine a hypothetical scenario with exactly the kind of customer Foxglove describes. A robotics team wants a custom dashboard to view data from an autonomous vehicle fleet. A pure frontend engineer would ask for design mockups first; an FDE would ask first: where is the data, who opens this dashboard every morning, and what are they using as a substitute today.

From there, the PoC scope document could read: two weeks, one data source, two metrics—the ops team replaces their old scripts with the new dashboard, and time to locate an incident shrinks compared to the current approach. These numbers are just illustrative, but the structure matches the requirement to "write out scope, timeline, and success metrics."

The hard part comes at the production stage. The PoC that runs on your machine must be deployed to the customer's on-prem infrastructure, the backend must integrate with their authentication system, and it has to keep running reliably even after you've moved on to other customers.

If three customers in a row all want the same kind of dashboard, at a company like Lovable that's the signal to build it into a platform feature. Only one link in that whole chain is truly "frontend"—the rest is requirements discovery, scoping, deployment, and product feedback.

What frontend people should practice

Your advantage is real: people who excel at interfaces understand how users work, and that's the first thing customers see. Priority number one: own a Node.js or Python backend that you write, deploy, and operate yourself.

Second priority is industry. Foxglove shows that interface-focused FDEs can be industry experts: today it's robotics, and at another company it might be a different field. If you've spent a few years in an industry, put it at the top of your resume—don't let it hide behind a list of framework names.

Third priority is proving you can handle business-level work. A resume bullet like "built a React dashboard" is far less persuasive than one that spells out scope, timeline, success metrics, and post-launch results.

When reading job postings, words like Sales, Customer Success, or customer environment will tell you what the interview will ask about beyond code.

Lovable's 8-year threshold and Foxglove's 3 years of robotics experience aren't rejections of less experienced engineers. They describe the endpoint most clearly: an engineer whom a customer trusts with both the interface and the servers.
