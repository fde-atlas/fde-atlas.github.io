Case Studies of Palantir Implementations by Domain | How Japanese Companies Should Proceed and the Role of FDEs

Original title: Palantir導入事例を領域別に解説｜日本企業の進め方とFDEの役割

FDE総研 · 2026-08-21 · 16 min read

Summary

This article surveys Palantir deployments by sector: Gotham serves government and defense clients integrating scattered intelligence, while private-sector cases cluster in aviation, pharma, energy, and manufacturing, almost all built on Foundry. Notable examples include Airbus's Skywise (2017), the Athinia joint venture with Merck KGaA (2021), BP's collaboration, and NHS England's Federated Data Platform contract (2023, reported up to £330 million), which also sparked debate over patient data. In Japan, Palantir entered via a joint venture with SOMPO Holdings (2019) and a strategic partnership with Fujitsu (2020). The author argues most results stem from methodology—narrowing to one daily decision, securing named owners and data-sharing agreements—rather than the product itself, and that FDEs succeed by embedding on-site, defining problems, and being judged on whether the application keeps being used.

Key points

  • Product choice splits by classification and buyer: Gotham for government, Foundry for nearly all private cases.
  • Key cases: Airbus Skywise, Merck KGaA's Athinia JV, BP, and NHS England's FDP (up to £330m).
  • Japan entry ran through SOMPO's joint venture (2019) and Fujitsu's partnership (2020), not direct large contracts.
  • The hardest step is moving from PoC to production, which requires named operators and governance, not technology.
  • FDEs are measured by continued field usage and changed decisions; most case results are reproducible through methodology without Palantir.

In which domains are Palantir case studies most common?

Palantir case studies are concentrated in government and defense, and in industries with heavy operational data such as aviation, pharmaceuticals, and energy.

Palantir's product line is broadly divided into two: Palantir Gotham for government, defense, and intelligence agencies, and Palantir Foundry for the business operations of private companies (Source: Palantir official website "Platforms" https://www.palantir.com/platforms/). The distribution of case studies largely follows this division. One is government projects that integrate highly sensitive, dispersed information. The other is private-sector projects involving physical operations such as facilities, supply chains, and inventory.

Palantir case studies are rarely described as "BI tool replacements." Most published cases go beyond merely viewing analysis results, extending to front-line decision-making and writing back to operational systems. The fact that FDEs (Forward Deployed Engineers) work embedded at customer sites is thought to be because Palantir implementations reach deep into front-line operations.

How is Palantir used in government and defense?

In government and defense, Palantir is used to integrate dispersed information into a common model and accelerate decision-making.

Palantir Gotham has been used to handle data spanning multiple information sources on a single common model. The goal is to enable analysts and commanders to grasp the situation on the same screen (Source: Palantir official website "Gotham" https://www.palantir.com/platforms/gotham/). Contracts with the U.S. Department of Defense and the U.S. Army are continuously cited as major revenue sources in Palantir's financial reports. It can be confirmed from Palantir's public information that public-sector revenue is one of the pillars of its business (Source: Palantir Investor Relations https://investors.palantir.com/).

In the UK, in November 2023, NHS England announced that it had awarded the Federated Data Platform (FDP) contract to a consortium centered on Palantir. The contract was widely reported as a multi-year contract worth up to £330 million. It is a frequently referenced case as an example of integrating public healthcare data.

On the other hand, the NHS project also sparked social debate over the handling of patient data. When reading Palantir case studies, it is practically useful to look not only at success factors but also at the cost of building consensus on the receiving side. Government and public-sector cases take longer than private ones for reasons related to consensus-building rather than technical requirements. The success or failure of public projects depends on whether there is a structure capable of proposing solutions that include the design of consensus-building.

In which industries do Palantir case studies stand out among private companies?

In the private sector, Palantir case studies stand out in industries with facilities and supply chains, such as aviation, pharmaceuticals, energy, and manufacturing.

A representative example is "Skywise," an aviation industry platform built by Airbus with Palantir. Announced in 2017, it is known as a mechanism for sharing aircraft operation and maintenance data among airlines. Its main uses are anomaly prediction and maintenance planning (Source: Skywise official website https://www.skywise.com/). In the pharmaceutical and materials field, Merck KGaA and Palantir established the joint venture Athinia in 2021, which handles semiconductor materials supply chain data. In the energy field, a long-term collaboration between BP and Palantir has been announced, used for data utilization in upstream operations.

Looking at the "mainly used product" column, all private-sector cases use Palantir Foundry. The choice of product is determined less by industry than by security classification and the procuring entity. Confirming first which category your organization falls into as the procuring side makes it easier to keep comparison targets consistent.

What do the operational challenges that give rise to Palantir case studies have in common?

The common factor is a state where front-line data scattered across multiple systems cannot be used for daily decision-making.

Although the cases listed in the table above vary by industry, the structure of their challenges is very similar. Core systems, equipment logs, Excel files, and external data exist separately, reconciliation is done manually, and decisions rely on experience. Moreover, those decisions occur daily and hourly, not in monthly analyses.

Conversely, for tasks that can be completed within a single SaaS, or for uses that only involve viewing monthly reports, there is little reason to introduce a large-scale platform like Palantir. When interviewing on-site staff, requests such as "It seems like it can do anything, so we want to use it company-wide" sometimes come up. Even in such cases, it is easier to move forward with implementation if you first check whether the initial target task meets the following three conditions.

ConditionWhat to checkWhat tends to happen if it is missing
FrequencyThe decision occurs daily or hourlyEffects are hard to see, and motivation to keep using it does not emerge
Data fragmentationThe data needed for the decision is split across two or more systemsExisting tools suffice, weakening the case for an integration platform
OwnerA person responsible for that decision can be identified by nameSomething gets built, but no one keeps using it

If you choose a task that lacks even one of these conditions as the first project, you may build something that works but is never used. Simply applying these three conditions to candidate tasks early in the evaluation process and eliminating those that fail will narrow down the targets considerably.

How do Palantir implementation cases at Japanese companies proceed?

In Japan, many cases start with joint ventures or partnerships with major domestic companies such as SOMPO and Fujitsu, expanding from internal use to external offerings.

Palantir's launch in the Japanese market has distinctive features. Rather than only signing large direct contracts with customer companies as in the US, it built a foothold through joint ventures and partnerships with major Japanese operating companies. When Japanese companies introduce a platform from an overseas vendor, they go through procedures such as review by the information systems department, security requirements, and sorting out relationships with existing SIers. It is natural to interpret the joint venture and partnership approach as a way of having domestic partners take on these procedures.

What are representative Palantir implementation cases in Japan?

Japan's representative examples are the establishment of a joint venture with SOMPO Holdings and the strategic partnership with Fujitsu.

In November 2019, SOMPO Holdings established the joint venture Palantir Technologies Japan with Palantir and announced a policy of advancing data utilization in the nursing care and insurance fields (Source: SOMPO Holdings press release https://www.sompo-hd.com/). It is often described as an initiative to integrate field data accumulated in the nursing care business and use it to improve service quality and operational efficiency.

In 2020, Fujitsu announced a strategic partnership with Palantir. Since then, it has advanced the domestic provision of Palantir Foundry and its application to Fujitsu's own business transformation (Source: Fujitsu press release https://pr.fujitsu.com/jp/news/). The sequence in which a vendor uses the product internally before rolling it out to customers tends to carry persuasive power in the Japanese market.

CompanyPeriodFormMain target areas
SOMPO HoldingsNovember 2019Establishment of joint venture Palantir Technologies JapanNursing care and insurance
Fujitsu2020Strategic partnershipDomestic provision of Palantir Foundry, internal business transformation

In both cases, public information centers on explanations of policy and structure, and only a limited number of cases disclose specific cost reductions or effectiveness metrics. When preparing internal materials, it is realistic to treat quantitative information from overseas cases separately from information about structure and approach from domestic cases. Mixing the two tends to create the mistaken expectation that the same numbers can be achieved in Japan.

In what order do Japanese companies proceed with Palantir implementation?

It often proceeds in four stages: limiting target tasks, PoC, production operation in one department, and horizontal expansion to other departments.

Data platform implementations at Japanese companies, not limited to Palantir, often go through stages like the following. Note that the decision-maker changes at each stage.

StageEstimated durationMain workDecision-makerCommon failures
1. Task identification2 to 6 weeksNarrowing down target tasks and decisions, field observationHead of the business departmentScope is too broad and focus is lost
2. PoCSeveral monthsData connections, ontology design, prototype appsBusiness department + information systems departmentEnds with a flashy demo and is never connected to actual work
3. Production operationSeveral months or morePermission design, operational structure, integration with existing systemsInformation systems department + field staffNo operations owner is assigned and the project is shelved
4. Horizontal expansion6 months or moreApplication to other departments, development of in-house talentManagementEach department rebuilds from scratch and assets are not reused

The most difficult step is often the transition from stage 2 to stage 3. This is a widely shared feeling among field stakeholders. A PoC can win agreement with "we built something interesting." But production operation will not move unless you decide who looks at that screen every day and who handles exception processing. What surfaces here is not a technical issue but an organizational one.

As the stages progress, the decision-maker shifts from the field to management. For this reason, you cannot get past stage 3 with only the people involved in stage 1. If you have the information systems department present from the beginning, you can avoid putting off discussions about permission design.

What are stumbling blocks specific to Japanese companies?

The main barriers are three: dispersed decision-makers, sorting out roles with existing SIers, and building consensus with departments that own the data.

Stumbling blockWhat happensPreventive measures
Dispersed decision-makersApprovals are split among the business department, information systems department, and management, so no one can move things forward aloneExplicitly state the decision-maker for each stage at the outset
Sorting out roles with existing SIersCoordination at the data connection stage consumes timeDecide the division of roles for maintaining core systems before starting work
Resistance from data-owning departmentsPushback over other departments being able to see their numbersInvolve the owning department's manager from an early stage and reach agreement on permission design

The third issue cannot be solved by technology and must be treated as a problem of permission design and governance. If you skip this and proceed with "let's collect data first," you will be sent back later. Involving the heads of the departments that own the data from the early stages of consideration often turns out to be the shorter path in the end.

What do FDEs do in Palantir implementation cases?

FDEs go into the customer's workplace, translate the language of operations into data models, and see the work through to building working business applications.

Behind the fact that Palantir implementation cases are described not as "tool adoption" but as "business transformation" is the role of the FDE. FDE stands for Forward Deployed Engineer, referring to engineers deployed forward to the customer's site. Rather than receiving a requirements specification and developing from it, their role is to go into the field, define the problems themselves, implement solutions, and bring things to a state where they are actually used (Source: Palantir official website "Careers" https://www.palantir.com/careers/).

What do FDEs start with in the early stages of implementation?

In the early stages of implementation, FDEs start by narrowing down to a single decision to improve, before designing the data platform.

The first task is identifying who decides what, and when. In a factory, they check what information the production planning staff look at and what decisions they make each day; in logistics, it is the dispatch staff. Once the target is narrowed down to one, they work backward to determine and connect the necessary data.

This order is the reverse of the approach often seen in Japanese system development of "first build a company-wide data platform." However, neither approach is inherently correct.

ApproachWhat you gainWhat you trade off
Business-first (FDE style)Results become visible quicklyOverall design becomes an afterthought
Platform-first (company-wide data platform first)Consistency of overall designIt takes time before results become visible

The sense of speed described in Palantir implementation cases is best understood as the result of choosing the business-first approach.

How are FDE results measured?

FDE results are measured not by deliverables, but by whether the field continues to use what was built and whether decisions have changed.

Comparing with typical contracted development makes the difference in FDE evaluation criteria clear.

PerspectiveTypical contracted development / SIerPalantir-style FDE
Starting pointRequirements specification written by the customerProblems defined by themselves through field observation
Main deliverablesA system built to specificationBusiness applications actually used in the field and changed operations
Success criteriaAcceptance and deliveryContinued use, changed decisions, expansion to the next business process
Distance from the customerCentered on the point of contactDirect access from field staff to management
Required mindsetAdherence to scopeProposals that include redefining scope

Under Palantir-style FDE standards, if what was built is not used, the result is considered zero. While the FDE way of working is often described as attractive, its high demands are also pointed out. Much of the reason is thought to lie in the strictness of these evaluation criteria.

What skills do FDEs need, as seen from Palantir implementation cases?

From implementation cases, it can be read that FDEs need, in addition to technical skill, business understanding, communication skills, and the judgment to narrow scope.

On the technical side, fundamentals such as SQL, Python, data modeling, and API integration form the foundation. However, following implementation cases shows that what separates success from failure is not technical sophistication. Can you draw out the field staff's honest concerns? Can you tell management "this time we will do this much, and we will not do that"? Even with the same product, whether you can do these two things changes the outcome.

SkillWhere it matters in implementation cases
Technical skill (SQL, Python, data modeling, API integration)Data connection and business application implementation
Business understandingIdentifying who decides what, and when
Communication skillsDrawing out field staff's honest concerns
Judgment to narrow scopeShowing management what will and will not be done

For engineers aiming to become FDEs, rather than immediately getting hands on Palantir products, it is easier to transition by building experience interviewing business staff directly at their current workplace and building small things for them to use. Even if job postings list product names among the requirements, interviews often focus on this kind of experience.

What is needed to replicate Palantir implementation cases in your own company?

What replication requires is not the product itself, but limiting the target business, people who go into the field, and a design that extends through to writing back.

Companies referencing Palantir implementation cases usually arrive at the question "Do we need Palantir to achieve the same results?" The product certainly has inherent value: an integrated platform encompassing ontology, permission management, and write-back. However, a considerable portion of the results in these cases comes from the approach. As for the approach alone, there is room to do the same with an existing technology stack.

What parts can be replicated without using Palantir?

Most of the approach can be replicated, but differences from Palantir emerge in the consistency of an integrated platform and the depth of permission management.

CategoryContentDependence on the product
Easy to replicateNarrowing down to one target business, going into the field to define problems, building small things for people to use, designing through to write-backNo dependence
Where differences emergePlacing multiple data sources on a common business model and handling permissions, auditing, applications, and AI consistentlyBuilding it yourself requires considerable effort

The areas where differences emerge are also the main reasons for choosing a paid platform. The criteria for judgment are three: the number of systems to integrate, the complexity of permission management, and the number of planned horizontal deployments.

SituationSuitable choice
Target is one or two systems, confined to a single departmentExisting platforms and application development are often sufficient
Crossing departments, with a need for fine-grained control over visibilityBuilding it yourself leads to heavy operational burden later, so consider an integrated platform

What should be decided in the first 90 days?

In the first 90 days, solidify four things: the target business, the responsible person, success metrics, and agreement with the departments providing data.

Items to decide early in the implementation consideration, organized together with rough timing.

  • Weeks 1-2: Identifying the target business — Choose one business that occurs daily, has a clear decision-maker, and currently relies on Excel and experience.
  • Weeks 3-4: Confirming the responsible person — Decide by name the decision-maker on the business division side and the person handling daily operations. Concurrent roles are acceptable, but leave no blanks.
  • Weeks 5–6: Agree on success metrics — Narrow down to one or two metrics that frontline teams will accept, such as "time to decision" or "number of rework incidents."
  • Weeks 7–10: Agreement with data-providing departments — Obtain agreement from the departments that own the target data on the scope of data provision and what can be made visible.
  • Weeks 11–13: Deliver the first prototype to the frontline — Prioritize a state where the frontline can actually touch it and give feedback, rather than completeness.

Some people may feel uneasy that much of the 90 days is spent on building consensus rather than technical validation. However, looking back at stalled projects, cases where the technology was simply impossible are rare. In my experience, more cases failed because one of the items above was left blank.

What metrics should be used for the investment decision on a Palantir implementation?

In practice, it is more realistic to work backward from the decision frequency of the target operations and the improvement per case, rather than from license fees.

In investment decisions for platform implementations, attention tends to go to initial costs and annual license fees. Setting the comparison baseline as "current labor costs plus opportunity losses" makes the discussion more concrete. For example, assuming a decision that takes 30 minutes a day is reduced to 10 minutes, and this occurs across 3 sites × 250 business days, the calculation would look like this:

ItemValue (estimate based on assumptions)
Reduction per instance30 min → 10 min (20 min saved)
Annual number of decisions3 sites × 250 business days = 750 instances
Annual hours saved20 min × 750 instances = approx. 250 hours (estimated value calculated from the assumptions above)

Furthermore, if decision quality improves, stockouts, excess inventory, and equipment downtime will also decrease.

However, this estimate cannot be made accurately before implementation. That is precisely why the order matters: narrow down the target operations for the first phase, collect actual measurements, and then decide whether to roll out horizontally. The large-scale results often cited in Palantir case studies are, in many cases, built on accumulated results from a single operation. Understanding this makes it easier to manage internal expectations.

Note that the company names, dates, and contract sizes mentioned in this article are based on publicly available information from the respective companies and public institutions. For cases where specific effect figures have not been disclosed, no figures are given. When considering implementation at your own company, please verify using each company's primary information and estimates.
