Tencent, ByteDance, and Kimi have all started sending people to customer sites

Selling a large model is only the beginning; the real challenge is getting it into an enterprise's ERP, permissions, and business processes. From Tencent's FDE certification to ByteDance building its own team, Kimi leveraging service providers, and OpenAI spending $4 billion to build its own deployment company, a quiet war over delivery costs has begun. Whoever can turn on-site experience into reusable product capabilities will be the one to truly achieve scale.

At 10 a.m. on March 6 this year, a line began forming outside Tencent's building in Shenzhen. People brought their laptops, waiting for Tencent Cloud engineers to help them install OpenClaw. More than eighty people lined up starting at 10 a.m., and by 11 a.m., hundreds of appointment slots had been handed out. According to information provided by Tencent, nearly a thousand developers and AI enthusiasts came to the site that day. The line included a retired aviation engineer and a fourth-grade elementary school student.

OpenClaw is open-source software. Tencent's Lighthouse cloud deployment solution takes as little as five minutes. Meanwhile, some people on social platforms have already started a paid installation business. Remote installation typically costs 50 to 100 yuan, while on-site service usually runs to several hundred yuan. The software is free to obtain and deployment tools are getting easier, yet people are still willing to pay for installation and configuration.

Six months later, Tencent did something similar. On September 10, Tencent Cloud launched the FDE engineer certification, training partners in requirements analysis, solution design, application building, delivery implementation, and security compliance for agent projects. On the same day, Moonshot AI announced the Kimi enterprise partner program, working with traditional IT service providers and system integrators to cultivate Forward Deployed Engineers.

On Volcano Engine's public recruitment page, several different types of FDEs have appeared: some are responsible for agent development and deployment, some for model selection and inference optimization, and others go specifically into customer sites to figure out what problems customers actually need solved.

In the commercialization of large models, there has always been a piece of work that is hard to eliminate: the model can be delivered via API to thousands of enterprises, but each enterprise has its own ERP, databases, permission systems, and business processes. Models can be upgraded simultaneously, but customers' systems and organizations cannot change in sync; after a model is sold, someone often still needs to follow up and help it enter the specific working environment.

Therefore, the problem exposed by the "FDE craze" is: if every additional customer requires several more engineers on site, then what kind of company will a large model company ultimately become?

01 The same model can't plug into the same company

Large models initially looked like a nearly standardized software business.

Wrap the capabilities behind an API, set uniform pricing and calling conventions, and let customers do their own development. Each time the model is upgraded, all customers using the same version benefit.

But once the model enters an enterprise, the work is usually not that simple. Suppose a company wants AI to help review contracts. When engineers actually start implementing, they first have to figure out where the contracts are stored, who can read them, which policy document the review standards come from, which departments have the authority to make changes, and who gives final approval.

Even if the model accurately detects anomalous clauses in a contract, it may not have permission to access the original files, let alone decide on the company's internal approval procedures on its own. Switch to another customer, and these questions often have to be answered all over again.

The traditional software industry has long faced the problem that "the larger the enterprise, the more legacy systems it has, the more complex the customization requirements, and the heavier the delivery work". Developing a product may only need to be done once, but deploying it to different enterprises requires repeatedly investing large amounts of manpower.

Large models have not eliminated these problems; they have merely re-exposed them.

Palantir turned solving this difficult problem into a product development methodology early on. "human equivalent of backpropagation" — the human version of backpropagation: engineers stay as close as possible to the problems customers actually face, work together with the core R&D team, and feed the constant stream of on-site feedback back into the platform, forming new product capabilities.

To understand this methodology, one can start with Palantir's three roles. Echo finds the problems customers genuinely need solved and coordinates with business staff and management; Delta makes the technical solution run, including data infrastructure and actual applications; Dev collaborates with the first two to develop already-validated solutions into platform capabilities that can serve more customers.

The three roles overlap, but together they form a collaborative chain from discovering problems in the field to product development.

The FDEs Volcano Engine publicly recruited this year reflect a similar division of labor. General FDEs connect APIs and data systems in customer environments, take projects from application prototypes all the way to production-grade agents, and also build evals and observability systems. FDEs on the algorithm side are further responsible for model selection, fine-tuning, and inference optimization, and translate customers' technical problems into product requirements for Ark and Seed.

Volcano Engine also specifically recruits FDE Echo, a role focused on interviewing and observing customers, organizing vague, scattered business feedback into verifiable product opportunities — the job posting even explicitly requires at least five years of solutions consulting experience.

The emergence of these roles is largely about shortening the distance that customer requirements travel within the organization. In the past, when an enterprise said "month-end reconciliation is too much of a hassle," the requirement might first be received by a salesperson, then passed to pre-sales, product managers, and the R&D team; after several rounds of handoffs, what R&D received might be reduced to "need to add a finance interface."

But the problem the customer encountered may have nothing to do with the interface itself. Is it that two departments use different data standards, or that historical records are missing? Should an interface be added, a process adjusted, or should AI replace manual verification? If engineers are too far from the customer, it is hard to judge.

The farther engineers are from the field, the more their judgment of problems depends on secondhand descriptions.

FDEs try to push engineers back in front of the business. They participate in actual processes, build solutions with their own hands, and bring recurring problems back to R&D. If this mechanism works properly, experience left over from one project has the chance to become product capabilities that the next project can directly call upon.

This is where FDEs most easily distinguish themselves from ordinary project outsourcing. When a project is finished, if all that remains is revenue and a set of customer-specific code, then delivery is just delivery; but if it also leaves behind connectors, workflow templates, eval methods, and new platform capabilities, then this labor cost can potentially be amortized across future customers.

At the same time, this is the commercial reason large model companies have begun to take FDEs seriously again. Simply increasing token call volume only shows that enterprises are using more model capabilities; once models truly enter core processes like customer service, finance, R&D, and supply chain, enterprises also need long-term maintenance of data interfaces, permission rules, eval systems, and employee operating procedures.

These factors determine whether AI can become part of an enterprise's daily operations, and also determine how much delivery resource a model company must invest per customer. But then, the more customers there are, the more engineers may be needed in the field. One model can serve a hundred thousand enterprises, but one engineer cannot simultaneously complete deployments for a hundred thousand enterprises.

Therefore, Tencent, ByteDance, Kimi, and OpenAI have taken different paths.

02 Tencent, ByteDance, Kimi — who bears the delivery costs?

On September 10, Tencent Cloud launched the FDE certification and simultaneously started partner recruitment. Tencent organized requirements analysis, solution design, agent building, delivery implementation, and security compliance into training and assessment content, and relying on the ADP platform, helps partners build enterprise-level agent delivery capabilities. The official positioning is clear: this certification is mainly aimed at professional engineers at partner companies and individual developers.

This is actually something IBM, Microsoft, and SAP have all done before: channelizing delivery.

Tencent's current choice primarily solves the delivery radius. ADP does not need to assign its own engineers to every enterprise; the more partners there are, the more industries and customers can be reached. For Tencent, with its vast cloud ecosystem, this system fits its existing commercial foundation better than expanding entirely with its own FDEs.

But while the channel can replicate engineers, project experience does not replicate itself.

If one partner developed a special permission workflow for one bank, another partner serving a second bank might run into similar problems all over again. As long as project experience stays siloed in different providers' codebases and delivery teams, what the platform can rapidly add is mainly headcount for delivery.

Tencent's next challenge is to turn its FDE ecosystem into a system where "the more you deliver, the easier it gets."

Whether problems repeatedly encountered by partners on-site can reliably flow into ADP, and whether ADP's updated capabilities can flow back into the entire partner network, will determine whether this delivery network ultimately accumulates headcount or product capability.

ByteDance chose a path closer to internal R&D.

ByteDance publicly recruited a group of FDEs with different specializations: Volcano Engine's general, algorithm, and Echo roles cover application engineering, model optimization, and customer demand discovery respectively. Feishu's commercial team is also hiring FDEs, sending engineers into customers' work environments to build workflows using Feishu's AI tools, and to accumulate standardized solutions and templates.

The biggest advantage of an internal team is the short feedback loop. When an engineer finds retrieval performance unsatisfactory at a customer site, they can bring the problem straight back to the model and platform teams; if the issue lies in collaboration workflows, there is also the option of seeking a solution from the Feishu product side. With one less layer between the customer site and product R&D, information loss is naturally smaller.

But this path also means more delivery costs must be kept inside the organization.

After completing the first banking project, how much workload can be reduced for the second bank? After entering automotive and retail, how much of the experience accumulated from banking projects can still be reused? These questions will ultimately show up in FDE productivity.

For companies building in-house FDE teams, the growth in engineer headcount itself means little. The more important metric is whether the person-days required for similar projects keep declining.

If projects multiply and the FDE team expands at a similar rate, the business scales up, but the organization grows heavier too. Only when field experience continuously turns into standard components, platform capabilities, and industry templates can the in-house team's proximity to R&D truly pay off.

Kimi simply skipped this step and placed partners at the core of its enterprise delivery plan. On September 10, Moonshot AI announced partnerships with Teamsun, Kingsoft Cloud, Yakang, AsiaInfo, and ChinaSoft International to jointly build FDE teams. Kimi provides the models, the Hosted Agents runtime foundation, and engineering methodology, while partners contribute their existing industry knowledge and customer service capabilities to jointly deliver projects.

Kimi can leverage their established customer relationships and delivery teams to enter industries it has not yet penetrated. But in this kind of collaboration, when an Agent fails to run smoothly at a customer site, whether it's a model problem, a workflow design problem, or a legacy system problem—the partner is usually the first to know. How information from the customer site flows back to Kimi becomes a key link in this partnership model.

In January this year, Hayden Krush, an FDE at healthcare AI startup Lamar Health, described his daily work in an interview with FDE Hub. He is responsible for integrating AI into the prior authorization process for specialty pharmacies. Some weeks, he writes code for a week straight to complete a customer's initial system integration; other days, he spends the entire day answering emails, handling the stream of new issues customers raise.

An important part of the job is figuring out exactly how customers want the system to handle extremely specific exceptions. For example, if two patients have identical names and dates of birth, whom should the system choose? Hayden's team decided to leave that call to the customer rather than let the AI decide on its own. He also found that bigger features usually require more customer-specific customization, while small suggestions from customers—like tweaking an interface—can quickly benefit all customers.

This is what Kimi must face too: Kimi can use its partners' people to enter more enterprises. As for how much of what these people learn in the field ultimately makes it into Kimi's product, only future projects will tell.

OpenAI took the classic brute-force approach, simply building a separate company for the task. On May 11, OpenAI announced the creation of a Deployment Company, majority-owned and controlled by OpenAI, with plans to invest over $4 billion in initial funding. It also announced its intent to acquire Tomoro, an AI consulting and engineering firm; once the deal closes, it will bring roughly 150 FDEs and deployment experts to the new company.

It adopts the structure of an independent company while staying connected to OpenAI's research, product, and internal deployment teams. This allows it to build an organization dedicated to enterprise delivery while preserving a path for field experience to flow into model and product development.

The investment of over $4 billion alone illustrates the current cost structure of enterprise AI: model R&D still burns cash, and actually putting models into a large enterprise is equally a business that demands heavy investment.

OpenAI did not leave this entire segment to Accenture, IBM, or other system integrators—it chose to control a dedicated deployment company itself. It wants to own the enterprise field presence, yet does not want massive project delivery to be fully absorbed into the core organization of a foundation model company.

This is actually one of the most interesting aspects of the FDE boom: the closer you get to enterprises' core processes, the more foundation model vendors need to fill in capabilities that used to belong to enterprise software companies, consultancies, and system integrators.

Tencent delegates part of these capabilities to its ecosystem, ByteDance keeps more in-house, Kimi relies on traditional IT service providers, and OpenAI simply founded an independent company.

The organizational forms differ, but in the end all must reckon with three accounts: who bears the delivery headcount, who holds the information from the customer site, and after finishing a project, how much can be carried directly into the next one.

These three accounts will also determine whether FDE ultimately becomes a scale lever for AI companies, or a cost center that balloons along with revenue.

03 Closing Thoughts

Palantir has already started letting software take over part of the work once done by field engineers. This year, AI FDEs have gradually gained more Foundry operational capabilities, able to handle data, code, evals, and platform resources. On September 8, Palantir went further and launched AIP Evolve, which lets multiple AI FDE Agents collaboratively optimize already-running AI systems. Users can specify targets such as cost, latency, and eval performance, set test data and modification boundaries, and then have the Agents propose improvement plans.

Palantir itself put it this way when introducing AIP Evolve: You can't scale what only humans can fix. (A system only humans can fix is very hard to truly scale.)

The same applies to today's FDE. How many certifications Tencent issues this year, how many engineers ByteDance hires, how many partners Kimi signs—these are all just early numbers. Once enterprise AI truly enters the scaling phase, growth in project count alone won't prove the model works.

If the first banking project takes ten people three months, and the second still takes ten people three months, the company's revenue grows, but labor costs rise along with it. If the second project needs only five people and one month; and further on, data interfaces, permission rules, industry evals, and common workflows have all become standard platform capabilities—only then does this business begin to show the replicability that software should have.

The greatest value of FDE is turning one expensive field experience into something that can be directly reused the next time.

Following this path, in the future, when an FDE returns from a banking project, they won't have to write data interfaces, configure permissions, or handle those all-too-familiar exceptions from scratch for the next bank.

The pitfalls from the last project are already written into the product. Next time, the engineer will still walk into the customer's site—but with far less already-done work to redo.

For today's large model companies, this is probably where the FDE ledger ultimately has to balance: customers can be won one by one, but delivery can't start from scratch, one customer at a time, forever.
