What Is a Business Architect? Roles, Skills, and Criteria for Deciding Between Internal Development and External Expertise

Original title: ビジネスアーキテクトとは？役割・スキルと育成/外部活用の判断軸

デファクトリーNote｜事業開発/ソフトウェア/プロダクト開発ならDefactory株式会社 · 2026-08-26 · 21 min read

Summary

This article by Shohei Tokumitsu, CEO of DeFactory, explains the Business Architect, a talent category defined in Japan's METI Digital Skill Standard (DSS): a role that connects business goals and IT implementation end-to-end, from goal setting through execution to verifying results. It covers the April 2026 DSS ver.2.0 revision, which reorganized the category into three roles (Business Architect, Business Analyst, Product Manager), and distinguishes the role from PMs, SEs, planning staff, and technical architect positions. Using data from DeFactory's freelance database BizDev Tech, it notes that as of August 2026 no listings use the title itself, while adjacent upstream roles command roughly ¥750,000–1,150,000 per month. The conclusion: internal development suits long-term cultural fit; external hiring suits immediate projects, and the two can be combined.

Key points

  • Business Architect is a METI DSS talent category driving DX from goal setting to execution and verification.
  • DSS ver.2.0 (April 2026) redefined the category into Business Architect, Business Analyst, and Product Manager roles.
  • Unlike PMs, SEs, and planners, Business Architects own the goal-setting stage itself.
  • BizDev Tech data shows zero listings under the exact title; adjacent upstream roles run about ¥750k–1.15M monthly.
  • Internal development is cheaper long-term but slow; external hiring is fast but costlier over time.

A Business Architect is a talent category defined by METI's Digital Skill Standards: in corporate DX initiatives, this role drives everything end-to-end, from goal setting through execution to effectiveness verification, while coordinating stakeholders.

It is a relatively new concept, established when METI announced the "DX Promotion Skill Standards" in December 2022, and amid the shortage of DX talent, many companies are focusing on developing and securing such professionals.

This article is aimed at corporate DX promotion and HR personnel, as well as those considering this role as an extension of their own careers. Beyond explaining the official definition and role, it also covers the decision criteria of whether to develop such talent in-house or rely on external resources, incorporating real talent market data.

The Digital Skill Standards (DSS) are guidelines developed by METI and the Information-technology Promotion Agency (IPA) that define the skills and roles that companies and individuals should acquire to drive DX.

The DSS consists of two main components.

  • DX Literacy Standard (DSS-L): Shows the foundational digital knowledge and mindset that all business professionals should acquire
  • DX Promotion Skill Standard (DSS-P): Defines the specific roles and skills required of professionals who drive DX in a specialized capacity

DSS-P classifies the talent needed for DX promotion into five categories: "Business Architect," "Designer," "Data Scientist," "Software Engineer," and "Cyber Security."

Among these five categories, the Business Architect is positioned as a core role that serves as the "bridge between business and IT."

The DSS was developed against the backdrop of many Japanese companies struggling with DX promotion, with one contributing factor being the shortage of talent capable of driving DX.

The DSS is widely used as a common language when companies consider their in-house talent development plans and hiring criteria.

The definition of a Business Architect, as stated by METI, is as follows.

In DX initiatives, a professional who sets what they want to achieve through business and operational transformation (i.e., the objective), coordinates stakeholders and leads the building of collaborative relationships among them, and realizes the objective through consistent advancement of the process toward achieving it (Source: METI "Digital Skill Standards" https://www.meti.go.jp/policy/it_policy/jinzai/skill_standard/main.html)

In simpler terms, this role envisions the company's desired future, concretizes the business models, business processes, and ideal state of the required information systems to realize it, and plans and drives the projects to make it happen.

It can be described as a specialist who not only has deep technical knowledge but also integrates business strategy and IT strategy to lead company-wide transformation.

Note that with the April 2026 revision of the "Digital Skill Standards (DSS) ver.2.0," a role with the same name, "Business Architect," was newly established within this talent category. The "Business Architect" as a category (the theme of this entire article) and the "Business Architect" as one role within it are concepts at different levels. See heading 3 below for details.

There are several job titles with "Architect" in the name that we cover on this site, but all of them are engineering roles responsible for technical design, and their areas of responsibility differ from that of a Business Architect.

ArticleMain area
IT ArchitectTechnical specialist responsible for technology selection and design of the entire system
Solution ArchitectDesigner who solves business challenges using cloud technology
Technical ArchitectOverall lead for technical decision-making and architecture design
AWS Solution ArchitectCloud design specialist qualification and role based on AWS certification
AI ArchitectDesigner who integrates AI models with business systems

All of these roles focus primarily on "how to design and implement technology."

In contrast, the Business Architect is a management/business-side talent category whose main focus is not technology implementation itself, but "how to define business objectives and engage stakeholders to lead them to realization."

With the "Digital Skill Standards (DSS) ver.2.0" published on April 16, 2026, the role structure of the Business Architect category was revamped.

In the previous version (DSS-P v1.2), the areas of activity for business architects were classified into three roles: "new business development," "advancement of existing businesses," and "improvement and efficiency of internal operations." In ver. 2.0, with the promotion of business model transformation as the central axis, these have been redefined into the following three roles.

  • Business Architect: A role that, from a perspective overlooking the organization and business, translates corporate strategy into an optimally structured business model and formulates a transformation roadmap
  • Business Analyst: A role responsible for analyzing operations, organizations, and systems within products and programs, organizing requirements, communicating them to implementers, and coordinating stakeholder interests
  • Product Manager: A role that, as the person in charge of a specific product (goods or services), manages the team across the entire lifecycle, from planning through development, continuous improvement, and business expansion

What is confusing here is that the term "Business Architect" (the concept explained in this article), which refers to the entire talent category, shares the same name as the "Business Architect" newly established as one of the roles within it. The former is a broad grouping referring to one of the five talent categories, while the latter is a role name for a role within that category that specializes specifically in "translating corporate strategy into business structure and transformation roadmaps."

What remains unchanged from the previous classification is that all of these roles share a common pattern: "defining objectives and driving realization while engaging stakeholders."

(Source: Information-technology Promotion Agency, Japan (IPA), "Press Release: Digital Skill Standard ver. 2.0 Released" https://www.ipa.go.jp/pressrelease/2026/press20260416.html)

The biggest reason business architects are needed is that many companies lack a role that connects "business objectives" and "technical implementation" end to end.

"I was appointed by management to lead DX promotion, but I don't know where to start." This is a common concern faced by many corporate staff members.

Until now, many companies have viewed DX as "the introduction of the latest systems."

However, there are many cases where simply introducing a system does not take root in the field and fails to deliver the expected business results.

Behind the misalignment between sales departments and IT departments, or initiatives ending as partial implementations, there is a common cause.

It is the absence of a role within the organization that connects "business objectives" and "technical implementation" end to end.

To survive in a market with accelerating change, talent capable of drawing a transformation roadmap from an overall optimization perspective, rather than short-term technology adoption, is indispensable.

The business architect is precisely the one who plays this role of "connecting objectives and implementation."

Advancing DX without this role risks ending up as an accumulation of suboptimal initiatives, resulting in DX with low return on investment.

Companies facing the wall of "why doesn't the technology we introduced translate into business results?" are the ones most likely to appreciate the need for this role.

The skills required of a business architect vary somewhat depending on the role, but the following skill areas are common to all.

  • Objective setting and strategy building: The ability to clarify the objectives of a business or operations and to formulate strategies for achieving them
  • Data-driven decision making: The ability to formulate visions based on objective data and analysis, rather than intuition or past successes
  • Stakeholder coordination: The ability to lead consensus building among stakeholders with different positions, such as business departments, IT departments, and management
  • Basic knowledge of IT and data utilization: Knowledge sufficient to understand the potential use of systems and data and to discuss on equal footing with experts, even without being a technology specialist
  • Basic understanding of security and governance: In the area of internal operations efficiency in particular, consideration for information security is also required

In a nutshell, the difference between business architects and other talent types is that, compared to the other four categories of the DX Promotion Skill Standard, they serve as a "bridge connecting multiple areas of expertise," and compared to existing PMs, SEs, and planning staff, the biggest dividing point is whether they are responsible for objective setting itself.

Below, we look at each in detail.

The biggest difference between business architects and the other four talent categories is that, rather than being responsible for a specific technical domain, they serve as a bridge connecting multiple areas of expertise.

In addition to business architects, the DX Promotion Skill Standard defines four other talent categories: "Designer," "Data Scientist," "Software Engineer," and "Cybersecurity."

Talent categoryMain role
Business ArchitectDrives everything from objective setting to realization end to end, while engaging stakeholders
DesignerDesigns services and experiences from the user's perspective
Data ScientistAnalyzes data and applies it to business decision making
Software EngineerImplements the concepts envisioned by the business architect as actual systems
CybersecurityManages security risks in DX promotion

The business architect is positioned as the bridge and overall coordinator of these specialists; rather than deeply specializing in a particular technical domain, its distinguishing feature is the role of connecting multiple areas of expertise.

The biggest difference between existing PMs, SEs, and planning staff and business architects is whether they are responsible for objective setting itself, that is, "what should be achieved in the first place" before a project begins.

A question frequently asked by corporate staff is, "We already have PMs (project managers), SEs (system engineers), and planning staff; can't they serve as substitutes?"

  • Difference from PMs (project managers): PMs are responsible for executing a given project according to plan. Business architects, by contrast, are responsible for objective setting itself, that is, "what should be achieved in the first place," before the project begins
  • Difference from SEs (system engineers): SEs are technical specialists mainly responsible for system design and development. Business architects do not need to be technology specialists; rather, their central role is that of a "translator" between the business side and the technical side
  • Difference from planning roles: Planning roles focus mainly on envisioning businesses and services, whereas business architects differ in that they design those visions with the use of IT and data as a premise and consistently drive them through to realization.

It is not always necessary to create a completely new position; in many cases, existing PMs, SEs, and planning staff can take on business architect-like functions by reinforcing their skills based on the DX Promotion Skill Standard.

For those currently building careers as PMs, SEs, or planners, this role can be considered one of the options on the extension of their own career path.

To be successful as a business architect, obtaining certifications is an effective way to acquire systematic knowledge.

The following certifications are considered particularly useful.

  • IT Strategist Exam (IPA / an advanced information processing engineer exam that evaluates the integration of business strategy and IT strategy)
  • Systems Architect Exam (IPA)
  • Project Manager Exam (IPA)
  • Applied Information Technology Engineer Exam (IPA)
  • SME Management Consultant (a national certification for systematically learning knowledge of general management)

However, these certifications are merely a means of organizing knowledge; obtaining them is not the goal in itself.

It is the experience of producing results while engaging stakeholders in actual business transformation efforts that shapes one's true capability as a business architect.

Even if certifications are obtained, that alone does not guarantee that business architects will take root in the organization.

Next, we compare the two options: developing talent internally and leveraging external resources.

In conclusion, if there is a project you can act on right away and you have no time to wait for development, external hiring is the way to go; if you want to develop talent rooted in your company's culture even if it takes time, internal development is the better fit.

For those in charge of building a DX promotion structure, the biggest concern is the decision of "should we develop this role in-house, or should we rely on external resources?"

Here, we compare both options fairly based on actual labor market data.

As a conclusion, as of August 2026, there were zero listings under the job title "business architect" itself, according to our job site BizDev Tech, operated by our company.

When we searched our BizDev Tech, a database of freelance and contracted work listings, no matching projects were found either under the katakana term "ビジネスアーキテクト" itself or via a partial-match search for "アーキテクト" (architect).

On the other hand, for upstream job types that perform functions close to those of a business architect, many projects actually exist under keywords such as "consulting," "requirements definition," "DX," and "upstream."

The average rates for the projects found under these keywords (excluding projects with undisclosed rates and duplicates) are concentrated roughly in the range of 750,000 to 1,150,000 yen per month, with a central value of around 750,000 yen (as of August 2026, according to BizDev Tech).

In other words, while the new talent category of "business architect" defined by METI is becoming increasingly recognized in corporate systems, it has not yet permeated the language of freelance market job listings.

This fact is an important premise when considering whether to choose internal development or external hiring going forward.

In conclusion, internal development allows you to cultivate talent rooted in your company's culture at low cost over the long term, but it takes time to become immediately productive, and because there are no established hiring criteria in the market, the hurdle for mid-career hiring is also high—this is the trade-off.

Advantages

  • You can develop talent with a deep understanding of your company's business characteristics and organizational culture
  • Over the medium to long term, total costs may be lower than continuous payments to external talent
  • During the development process, DX knowledge tends to spread to other employees as well

Disadvantages

  • It takes time to become fully capable, and it often will not be in time for immediate DX projects
  • Since no established job title of "business architect" exists in the market, even if you try to hire ready-to-work talent externally as full-time employees, building a candidate pool itself is difficult
  • Securing a development budget for a newly created position faces internal approval hurdles

In conclusion, external hiring allows you to flexibly procure practitioners from adjacent fields at market rates of roughly 750,000 to 1,150,000 yen per month, but it takes time for them to catch up on your company's specific circumstances, and if the engagement becomes long-term, costs can end up higher than developing full-time employees—this is the trade-off.

  • As seen in section 8-1, even if you cannot search under the title "business architect" itself, practitioners in adjacent fields such as consulting, requirements definition, DX promotion, and upstream processes can be procured right away at market rates of roughly 750,000 to 1,150,000 yen per month
  • Because contracts can be flexibly made for just the required period and workload, DX projects can move forward without waiting for a development period
  • Results are easy to evaluate on a per-project basis
  • Understanding your company's specific circumstances requires time for external talent to catch up as well
  • If engagements continue, long-term costs can sometimes be higher than developing full-time employees

The more of the following apply, the more viable external hiring becomes as an option.

  • There is a DX project you can act on right away, and you have no time to wait for development
  • There is not yet an internal candidate who can dedicate themselves to "bridging business and IT"
  • You want to verify effects with a small-scale project first before deciding on full-scale investment in development

In conclusion, business architect is a "classification of roles," while FDE and Development Strategist are "terms for ways of working or job types" that embody that function as external talent.

Having read this far, some of you may be wondering, "I've heard the terms FDE and Development Strategist—how do they differ from business architect?"

To summarize, the relationship is as follows.

TermPositioning
Business architectA talent "category" (classification of roles) in DX promotion defined by METI's Digital Skill Standard
FDE (Forward Deployed Engineer)A term referring to a "way of working" of solving problems alongside customers on-site while personally working on technical implementation
Development StrategistA "job title" referring to a role that bridges business strategy and system development

In other words, if the Business Architect is a "classification of functions and roles to be fulfilled in promoting DX," then FDE and Development Strategist are names that indicate "how external specialist talent actually embodies those functions through their way of working."

When in-house development is difficult, leveraging external talent who work in these ways becomes a realistic option for quickly filling the functions a Business Architect should perform.

When developing Business Architects in-house, the following steps are generally taken.

  • Visualizing skill gaps: Using the DX Promotion Skill Standard as a benchmark, clarify the difference between the candidate's current level and the required level
  • Participation in small-scale DX projects: Gain experience not only through classroom learning but also through OJT on actual projects
  • Experience as a cross-departmental bridge: Deliberately create opportunities to stand between business and IT departments
  • Systematizing skills through certification: Acquire the certifications introduced in Chapter 7 alongside practical work experience

The development period varies depending on the candidate's experience and the scale of the project, but it is important to understand that bringing someone to a near-professional level requires substantial medium- to long-term investment.

If you don't want to halt current projects, combining this with the external hiring introduced in Chapter 8 is also an option.

Q1. What is the difference between a consultant and an architect?

The biggest difference between consultants and Business Architects is whether they stop at making recommendations or take responsibility through execution.

Management consultants are specialists who primarily recommend strategies and solutions to companies from a third-party standpoint, and often do not participate in the entire execution phase.

Business Architects, on the other hand, are "execution drivers" who promote everything in one continuous flow from goal setting through execution to post-implementation effectiveness verification, and are characterized by taking responsibility through execution as internal stakeholders.

For the difference from DX consultants, please also see the article introduced in Chapter 6.

Q2. What is the relationship between Business Architects and DX?

The relationship between Business Architects and DX is that of core talent who ensure DX leads to business outcomes rather than ending with technology adoption.

In METI's DX Promotion Skill Standard, they are positioned as the parties responsible for "goal setting" and "driving execution," making them a critical position that determines the success or failure of DX.

As explained in Chapter 4, proceeding with DX without a Business Architect increases the risk that efforts end up as merely locally optimized initiatives.

Q3. How long does it take to develop one in-house?

There is no uniform benchmark data, but going through the steps introduced in Chapter 9 (visualizing skill gaps, participating in actual projects, cross-departmental experience, and certification) to reach a professional level in practice takes considerable medium- to long-term time.

If you want to advance DX projects without waiting for internal development, combining this with the external hiring introduced in Chapter 8 is also an option.

Q4. What is the market rate when entrusting work to external talent (contracted work)?

The market rate for external talent (contracted work) in adjacent upstream roles (consulting, requirements definition, DX promotion, upstream processes) is 750,000 to 1,150,000 yen per month.

As of August 2026, there were 0 projects under the exact title "Business Architect" according to a BizDev Tech survey, but in adjacent fields, many projects actually exist at these rates.

Q5. Can a Business Architect role be fulfilled on a freelance or contracted basis?

Yes, it can.

While recruitment under the exact name "Business Architect" is still rare in the market, there are many actual examples of similar functions being fulfilled through contracted work in forms such as consultants, upstream-process engineers, and technical advisors.

One way of working that bridges technology and business, FDE (Forward Deployed Engineer), is also spreading.

For details, please also see the terminology overview in Chapter 8.

The Business Architect is a talent category defined in METI's digital skill standards as a bridge that ensures a company's DX leads to "business outcomes" rather than ending with "technology adoption."

They drive everything in one continuous flow from goal setting through execution to effectiveness verification, standing between business and IT and engaging stakeholders.

However, the reality is that the title "Business Architect" itself has not yet become established in the freelance and contracted-work market.

While in-house development takes considerable time, external talent is actually active in adjacent fields such as consulting, requirements definition, DX promotion, and upstream processes at rates of around 750,000 to 1,150,000 yen per month.

Whether to develop talent in-house over time or first leverage external help to move projects forward—there is no need to commit to just one.

Depending on your company's situation, use the checklist in Chapter 8 as a reference and consider the optimal combination.
