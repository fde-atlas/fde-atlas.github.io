What You Should Know Before Outsourcing FDE | Evaluation Criteria and Preparation Before Requesting

Original title: FDEを外注する前に知りたいこと｜判断基準と依頼前の準備

AI Ops Manager · 2026-10-11 · 27 min read

Summary

This Japanese article guides companies considering outsourcing Forward Deployed Engineers (FDEs). It argues that vendors can handle business analysis, implementation, and adoption, but decisions about which processes to tackle and how to measure success must stay in-house. It compares contractor (ukeoi), quasi-mandate (jun'inin), and dispatch contracts, warning that directly instructing external staff risks disguised contracting under Japanese labor law. Citing government surveys and a Gartner prediction that 70% of enterprises will abandon vendor-built agentic AI by 2028, it analyzes job postings from 15 SI/consulting firms—only one commits to making clients self-sufficient. The author recommends contractually securing data/prompt ownership, success metrics, and handover plans before engagement.

Outsourcing FDE allows you to entrust practical work from organizing business processes through implementation and adoption, but decisions about which tasks to start with and how to measure results require your company to take an active role. This article explains contract types, points to note when collaborating with external FDEs, criteria for deciding whether to outsource, and preparation before engagement, together with an analysis of FDE job postings from 15 SI and consulting firms.

Published date Last updated

"We want to incorporate AI into our operations, but no one in-house can handle implementation, so we'd like to engage an external FDE who works on-site through implementation." For companies thinking this way, the first issue is drawing the line between what can be delegated and what should be handled in-house.

Practical work from organizing business processes through implementation and embedding them into daily operations can be delegated to the outsourcing partner. On the other hand, decisions such as which tasks to start with and what counts as success must be made by your company, taking an active role while receiving proposals from the outsourcing partner. If you leave decisions entirely to the partner, the system may run, but your company will be unable to decide which process to change next, becoming dependent on the outsourcing partner for every improvement.

Also, to ensure your company can continue operating the system after the contract ends, it is essential to agree at the engagement stage on what will be left in-house, such as the reasons for selecting particular tasks and where data is stored.

What Is FDE Outsourcing?

FDE outsourcing is not about buying a finished product; it is about bringing in from outside an executor who handles everything from organizing business processes through implementation and embedding them into daily operations.

Regarding FDEs, a previous article, "What Is an FDE (Forward Deployed Engineer)? Salary, Skills, Career, and How Companies Can Secure One", covered the job details and salary. To reconfirm the definition, an FDE (Forward Deployed Engineer) is originally an engineer who belongs to a company with its own product, works on-site in customers' operations, and handles everything from identifying issues to implementing AI and establishing its operation. In Japan, SI and consulting firms that do not assume their own products also recruit for similar roles under the FDE name.

For this reason, external FDEs can be broadly divided into two types depending on their employer: FDEs at product-providing companies and FDEs at SI and consulting firms. The former is suitable when the product to be introduced is already decided; the latter when you want to delegate even product selection. In a previous article, "Why Are FDE Needs Surging? An In-Depth Analysis of Job Postings from 73 Japanese Companies", we organized what remains after a project ends and the three points to decide at the time of ordering, for each type of engagement. This article mainly treats the latter as "outsourcing," but the thinking about what to leave in-house after outsourcing ends is the same regardless of which type you engage.

The scope that can be delegated to the outsourcing partner is roughly as follows.

  • Inventorying target business processes and organizing candidates for initial implementation
  • Tool selection and implementation of a working system
  • Adjusting it into a form usable on-site and checking usage
  • Improvement after operations begin

You can make the request even without in-house engineers, and there is no need to start by hiring technical staff. The outsourcing partner can propose which tasks to tackle, with supporting rationale. However, your company must take the lead in deciding its own priorities from among those proposals, in discussion with the partner.

Differences from Contract Development and SES

Besides FDEs, other ways to outsource development include contract development (ukeoi) and SES. The dividing lines are whether they can get involved before what to build is decided, and what you receive when the contract ends.

With contract development, you receive a system completed according to the agreed requirements. With SES, what is provided is the time technical staff worked within a defined scope. With FDE outsourcing, by contrast, you receive a system refined to the point of being actually used in operations. Contract development is efficient when what to build and the requirements are clear, but in cases like AI adoption, where you must think from "what to build to achieve results," fixing specifications in advance is itself difficult. FDE outsourcing differs in that it can get involved from the stage before requirements are settled.

Differences from other occupations such as SES and SIers are organized in the "Differences from Other Occupations" chapter of "What Is an FDE (Forward Deployed Engineer)? Salary, Skills, Career, and How Companies Can Secure One", and the scope each type of provider takes on is organized in a table in the previous article "How to Advance AI Initiatives | 5 Steps to Turn Individual AI Use into Organizational Results".

Differences Between Contract for Work (Ukeoi), Quasi-Mandate (Jun'inin), and Dispatch

Once you decide to outsource, the next consideration is the contract type. Contracts for commissioning work to external parties include ukeoi (contract for work), which aims at completion of the work, and jun'inin (quasi-mandate), which aims at performance of the services*1. Worker dispatch, which involves accepting personnel, is a separate mechanism*2. Organizing each type's purpose and who gives work instructions to the workers, based on explanations from the Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare's Q&A collection and METI's AI contract checklist, and adding suitable situations, we get the following.

Contract typeWhat it aims atWho gives work instructions to the workersSuitable situations
Ukeoi (contract for work)Completion of the workThe vendorWhat to build and the completion criteria are decided
Jun'inin (quasi-mandate)Performance of the services (some types pay fees based on results)The vendorDeciding what to build anew as work progresses
Worker dispatchWorking for the dispatch destination under its directionThe dispatch destination (your company)Your company can direct the details of the work

Under ukeoi and jun'inin, the vendor's responsibilities also differ. METI's checklist treats as a generally important difference the point that under jun'inin the vendor bears a duty of professional care (the obligation to perform services with due care as a professional), while under ukeoi it bears an obligation to complete the work and liability for non-conformity (liability when the delivered item does not match the contract terms). It further points out that this distinction under the Civil Code is merely a supplementary rule for when there is no agreement between the parties, and that how much to demand in terms of the content and level of results is often the more important issue*1.

The model contract for agile development published by IPA (Information-technology Promotion Agency) and METI assumes jun'inin*3. The reason is that for approaches where features and priorities change during development, jun'inin, which pays for the performance of services itself, fits better than ukeoi, which pays for completion of agreed deliverables. FDE outsourcing, which proceeds while determining what to build on-site, involves similar circumstances.

Points to Note When Collaborating with External FDEs

When you outsource FDE work, the vendor's staff will often join your internal regular meetings and chat channels and work as part of the same team as your employees. What you need to watch out for here is having your own staff give direct work instructions to the vendor's personnel engaged under a contract for work (ukeoi) or a quasi-mandate (jun'inin). The Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare's Q&A collection treats cases where, despite taking the form of a contract for work, the client in practice "gives direct and specific instructions to the workers employed by the contractor to have them perform work" as so-called disguised contracting (giso ukeoi), in violation of the Worker Dispatch Act. The same applies to quasi-mandate contracts; whether something constitutes dispatch is judged by the actual situation, not by the form of the contract *2.

On the other hand, the Q&A collection does not deny collaboration itself. Although the Q&A was originally written with agile-style development in mind, in May 2026 an answer was added stating that it also applies to system development other than agile-style development performed under a contract for work *2. The key points are as follows.

  • Even if the parties share information, hold technical discussions, and offer advice and suggestions, it is not deemed disguised contracting as long as they collaborate as equals and the vendor's staff make decisions autonomously and proceed on their own (Q2, Q5)
  • Even if the client's person in charge gives explanations or provides information to clarify requirements, this alone does not immediately constitute disguised contracting (Q4)
  • The same thinking applies even when all members of both parties participate in meetings, chat, and project management tools (Q6)
  • When instructions are needed regarding task allocation, ordering, or urgent adjustments, the vendor side should appoint a management supervisor and give the instructions itself (Q3)
  • If the client specifies a particular individual or refuses the participation of a particular individual, the arrangement is not recognized as a proper contract for work or the like (Q7)

As a way to prevent situations from being judged as disguised contracting, the Q&A collection recommends clarifying and agreeing in advance on the roles and authority of both parties and how work will proceed within the team *2. Applied to FDE outsourcing, this means agreeing before work begins on a division of responsibilities in which the client's person in charge decides the requirements for what is to be built and their priorities, while the vendor's staff decide for themselves how the work is carried out and in what order (Q4). If instructions on task allocation or ordering become necessary, the vendor's management supervisor issues them (Q3). Since whether individual contracts and operations are proper depends on their content and actual practice, consult your legal department or the prefectural labour bureau if you are unsure *4.

Criteria for deciding whether to outsource

For Japanese companies, entrusting development to external parties is nothing unusual. According to a survey by the Ministry of Internal Affairs and Communications, 25.0% of Japanese companies answered that they "outsource almost all development to external vendors," far exceeding the 2.6% in the United States. Meanwhile, while companies answering that they develop in-house under their own direction stood at only 46.0% in Japan, the figures reached about 80% in the US, Germany, and China *5. Respondents were managers and above at companies with 10 or more employees that had begun digital transformation initiatives by 2025. The questions covered system development in general, not limited to AI.

Given this high degree of external dependence, if you commission work without deciding which decisions will be handled in-house, you may end up delegating even decision-making to external parties. In a survey by the Japan Users Association of Information Systems (JUAS) asking IT department heads at listed companies and similar organizations, 70.9% of companies handled system planning (formulating systemization concepts) mainly in-house, while 63.4% mainly outsourced design, implementation, and testing *6. In-house leads on what to build; external parties handle the building. Whether you can establish this division of roles when embedding AI into your business is the criterion for deciding whether to outsource.

Is there someone in-house who can evaluate the vendor's proposals?

Most important is whether you have someone in-house who can evaluate whether the vendor's proposals fit your business and decide priorities together with the vendor.

The person in this role does not need to be an engineer. Anyone who can determine, through discussion with the vendor, which parts of the vendor's proposals are necessary for your company and which can be deferred can fill the role. A manager who knows the operations well can also take it on. The model contract for agile development by IPA and METI likewise assumes a structure in which the client appoints a "product owner," and lists among the pre-contract confirmation items "whether an appropriate product owner can be appointed and authority delegated" *3. A product owner is the client-side person in charge who decides the list of requirements for what is to be built and their priorities *2.

If you commission work without such a person, you will keep entrusting the work without ever being able to verify the quality of the proposals. Outsourcing is still possible in that case, but you should include developing someone who can evaluate proposals within the scope of the engagement.

Is it a business that will be continuously improved, or a temporary one?

If the business will continue to be improved going forward, position the outsourcing as a period until it can be operated in-house, and commission it on the premise of developing internal owners of the work. The more a business is iteratively improved, the more the reasons behind design decisions and the experiments that failed to produce results become material for the next decisions, which makes having someone in-house who holds that knowledge increasingly valuable.

Conversely, for operations that occur infrequently or are expected to shrink in the future, it is more flexible to commission external help only when needed. If you hire staff and move to in-house production without deciding whether the business will be continuously improved, you may end up with a single target operation while labor costs keep accruing every month. Before deciding between outsourcing and in-house production, start by estimating how much the volume and importance of that operation are likely to grow.

Cases where alternatives to outsourcing are better suited

There are also situations where outsourcing FDE work is not necessarily optimal. If what is to be built and the requirements are already fixed, contract development is sufficient. If the product to be introduced is already decided, it is appropriate to request the FDE of the company that provides that product. If you want to continuously rebuild your business operations with AI as the premise, limit outsourcing to the launch period and consider keeping the owners of the work in-house. I organized how to balance hiring, training, and external resources in a previous article, "A Thorough Explanation of FDE Training: Balancing Hiring and External Resources, and How to Approach Training".

What to retain in-house even after the outsourcing ends

The month after the outsourcing ends, can your company start work on the next operation? What determines this is not so much the mechanisms in place as whether the material for decision-making remains in-house.

There are also external forecasts showing the outlook when everything is left to the vendor. In September 2026, the research firm Gartner published a prediction that by 2028, 70% of enterprises will abandon agentic AI built by vendors' FDEs*7. The reasons cited were soaring costs and the inability to develop it on their own. As countermeasures, Gartner recommends narrowing the scope of tasks delegated to FDEs, transferring deliverables and knowledge, deciding intellectual property and responsibilities at termination at the contract stage, and having FDEs work alongside in-house business owners and engineers during implementation. It is a forecast rather than actual data, but it aligns with the approach of this chapter: deciding what will remain after the contract ends before making the request.

In the previous article "How to Advance AI Adoption | 5 Steps to Turn Individual AI Use into Organizational Results", we organized what should be retained when leveraging external help as the deliverables of five steps. For outsourcing in particular, the following four items should be secured through the contract and day-to-day progress management.

Why that task was chosen

First, keep within the company the decision-making process behind why that task was originally chosen.

Even if you only receive the system itself, you cannot decide on your own which task to expand to next. If the decision-making process is documented, you can select the second and third tasks using the same criteria. The record should include a list of candidate tasks, the tasks that were not chosen and why, and the criteria used for the decision (time spent, frequency of occurrence, likelihood of errors, etc.).

How to measure results

You may decide how to measure results while drawing on the vendor's expertise. However, the decision should be made with your company taking the lead while discussing with the vendor, and the records of metrics and measurements should be kept by your company. If you leave even the evaluation framework entirely to the vendor, you will be unable to judge for yourself whether results were achieved, and decisions about continuing the contract will depend on the vendor's reports.

Simple metrics are sufficient. Time spent on processing, the number of missed cases, and whether the task can now be run by people other than the original owner. Just agreeing on metrics at this level before starting work will allow you to judge contract continuation based on your own numbers.

How to decide when exceptions occur

Have rules in place within your company for who makes what decisions when unexpected cases arise.

Even if routine processing can be left to the system, if every exception requires contacting the vendor, operations will depend on the vendor's availability. When an exception occurs, record the decision made and its reasoning, and build a state where your company can make such decisions itself from the second time onward.

Ownership and storage location of data, settings, and prompts

Decide at the time of contracting who will own the business data, settings, and prompts, and where they will be kept.

If you try to move them later, you will have to ask the vendor where everything is. Even if the system is running, if your company cannot make changes to its internals, improvements will remain dependent on the vendor.

METI's "Contract Checklist for AI Use and Development" cites the handling of provided data (input) and development results (output) as points to note when commissioning AI system development*1. Regarding data, it states that if the vendor wishes to use it for their own technology development, it is necessary to consider whether to permit this and, if so, to what extent. Regarding results, it positions the attribution of rights and conditions of use as "matters where the interests of users and vendors tend to sharply conflict." Especially when developing something to be used in combination with the vendor's AI systems or services, distinguishing newly created results from what the vendor already had can become a point of dispute later, so it is considered important to align understanding early in development.

The model contract for agile development by IPA and METI takes as its main draft the transfer of copyright for newly created works to the ordering party. However, generically usable programs created by the vendor are excluded from the transfer. Alternative drafts assigning copyright to the vendor or making it shared are also presented*3. Which draft to adopt depends on negotiation, but at minimum confirm the following points before contracting.

  • Storage location of data, settings, and prompts
  • Whether the vendor will use the provided data for their own development
  • Which party will own the rights to the development deliverables
  • Handling of data and deliverables after the contract ends
  • Whether your company's staff can read and edit the contents

Preparation before making the request

A requirements specification document is not necessary. What is required is the ability to explain how the task is currently being run.

What to decide before bringing in external help (scope of access to operations and data, scope of delegated decision-making, how to measure results, handover goals) was organized in the previous article "Complete Edition | 50-Point Checklist for Hiring, Training, and Utilizing AI Talent | All Steps from Design to Delivering Results". What to prepare before the request is the material for working through these items with the vendor.

What to prepare and what you don't need to prepare

Before making the request, prepare the following five items.

  • Current procedures (who processes what, in what order, using what)
  • Number of people involved and monthly work hours
  • What would count as success (a provisional goal writable in one sentence)
  • Scope of operations the vendor may see and data they may access
  • The in-house point of contact

Conversely, a list of features, screen mockups, and tool selection results are better not prepared in advance, as this makes progress easier. If the ordering party decides these on their own, discussion tends to solidify in a direction that builds something deviating from the actual problem.

For example, suppose that behind a request like "we want a case management screen" lies the problem that input granularity differs by person. If current procedures and work hours are shown first, such discrepancies can be found before the discussion turns to screens.

What is written in the vendor's job postings

How vendors perceive the scope of their own work is also reflected in FDE job postings. We read job postings from 15 companies in Japan recruiting FDEs, including SI and consulting firms, and counted what they listed as the work FDEs would undertake for client companies.

Assessment of vendor FDE job postings (October 2026) / surveyed by AI Ops Manager, as of October 1, 2026

  • Scope: 16 companies classified as "SI, consulting, and contract development" among posting issuers in the periodic tally (September 2026) from the previous article "Why Is Demand for FDEs Surging? An In-Depth Analysis of Job Postings from 73 Japanese Companies"
  • Sources: the body of job postings on each company's recruitment pages (retrieved October 1, 2026)
  • Counting method: by company, one job posting per company. Whether the posting describes all four aspects of the work an FDE handles for a client company: organizing operations, operation and adoption, success metrics, and handover to the company.
  • Exclusion: one company whose job posting had already closed
  • Judgment method: two independent judgments were made—one counting postings where the intent was described in the explanation of the work or role, and one counting only those where it was explicitly stated as a duty—and only postings that met both were counted *8

13 companies stated that they start by organizing operations, and 11 stated that they handle operation in production through to adoption into the business. On the other hand, only 4 companies stated that they set or measure success metrics with the client, and only 1 stated that making the client able to operate and improve on their own is part of the work. That one company says it builds "self-sustaining teams" through training and workshops.

A job posting is a recruitment document and is a different thing from what is promised in a contract. A company that does not mention it does not necessarily skip handover. Even so, it is safest for the client to request that handover and how results are measured be included in the scope of the engagement.

What to check with the outsourcing vendor

What you should check is not "will they work on-site" but "what will remain in your company when the contract ends." Asking the following questions will reveal differences between vendors.

  • Do they start by listening to the business flow, rather than features?
  • Do they propose starting small from one business process and expanding?
  • Does the estimate show which parts—from inventorying operations to post-launch improvement—are included, and can the client verify the breakdown of work hours?
  • Do they define how results are measured together before starting, and report against those metrics?
  • Do they deliver the results of organizing operations in a form the client can read?
  • Will they stay involved until in-house staff can make decisions themselves, and can that goal be stated explicitly in the contract?

Compare costs not by the size of the amount, but by how much of organizing operations, implementation, and adoption support is included in that amount. Even at the same monthly rate, a contract covering only implementation means something different from one that includes organizing operations through to adoption on-site. There are also ways to link results to compensation. Even quasi-mandate contracts can have a performance-based compensation model; IPA's model contract commentary gives as an example a scheme that defines metrics such as sales revenue or reduced expenses and pays a success fee in addition to the normal compensation upon achievement *3.

How to hand over to the in-house team

Once daily processing starts running alongside the vendor, you enter the stage of deciding what to take over in-house. What should be taken over is not the work but the judgment. If you take over only the tasks and cannot decide what to change and how, operations will eventually stall.

For the order of transfer, starting with lighter, standardized tasks is a manageable approach.

StageWhat your company handlesWhat the vendor handles
1. Build the system and confirm resultsDecide priorities and how to measure results together with the vendorOrganizing operations, implementation, and adjustments to fit the workplace
2. Transfer daily processingStandardized daily tasks (verified by running alongside the vendor)Running alongside and fixing stalled points
3. Transfer judgment on exceptionsJudgment on unexpected cases and records of themConsultation on judgment and improvement of the system
4. Decide the next business process in-houseJudgment on which process to expand to nextConsultation on specialized areas such as designing new systems and selecting technologies

Decide the timing of transfer not by the calendar but by whether operations have stabilized. Transferring before preparations are complete just to meet a deadline means going back to square one.

Designing new systems and selecting technologies are areas that can remain with external parties. They are not everyday work and require high expertise, so consulting externally when needed is more realistic than keeping them in-house at all times. Rather than trying to bring everything in-house, deciding in advance how much to keep relying on external parties lets you leverage external knowledge when needed.

When it comes to securing AI talent, hiring, training, and dispatching come to mind. However, with a form of outsourcing where you pair with people embedded in the business who handle implementation through to adoption, where your company takes the lead in discussing priorities with the vendor from the start, and where judgment on exceptions and selection of the next process are transferred in-house one by one, bringing people in from outside itself becomes a way to develop in-house talent.

Summary

Outsourcing FDE is not buying deliverables; it is bringing in from outside executors who handle everything from organizing operations to adoption.

  • Practical work can be left to external parties. Which process to start with and what counts as results should be decided with your company taking the lead, while receiving the vendor's proposals
  • Do not give direct work instructions from in-house to staff hired under a contract for work (ukeoi) or quasi-mandate (jun'inin). Agree on roles, authority, and procedures before starting
  • Keep in-house, through daily practice, the reasons for choosing each process, how results are measured, and the rules for judging exceptions, and decide by contract the ownership and storage location of data, settings, and prompts

Before looking for a vendor, first pick one target business process, write out the current procedures and monthly work hours, and place one in-house person to work with the vendor on prioritization. That alone will clarify both the criteria for comparing estimates and what to check with vendors. Do not leave AI initiatives to outsiders and wait; get inside and move them forward together. Outsourcing can also proceed in that form.

Frequently asked questions

Q. Can we outsource FDE even without engineers in-house?

Yes. Since the vendor handles implementation, there is no need to hire technical staff before starting. However, you do need an in-house person to evaluate the vendor's proposals and think through priorities together. This role can be filled by someone who is not an engineer.

Q. Can we commission work even if requirements are not finalized?

Yes. The difference from contracted development is that they can get involved in organizing operations from a stage where what to build is not yet defined. Being able to explain how the business currently runs is more useful than a thick requirements specification.

Q. Can in-house staff directly ask the outsourced FDE to do tasks?

If in-house people directly instruct staff of a vendor hired under a contract for work or quasi-mandate on how to do tasks or how long to spend, there is a risk of disguised contracting. On the other hand, the Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare's Q&A responses state that even when information is shared and technical discussions take place, it is not considered disguised contracting if the parties collaborate as equals and the vendor's staff proceed with autonomous judgment *2. Agree before starting that task allocation is handled by the vendor's manager.

Q. If we outsource, will no know-how remain in-house?

It depends on how the contract is designed. If you only receive the mechanism, nothing remains in-house. But if you proceed on the premise of keeping in-house the reasons for choosing the task, how results are measured, and the rules for handling exceptions, and if the ownership and storage location of data and prompts are specified in the contract, you can continue outsourcing while accumulating decision-making capability within your company.

Q. What is the difference between outsourcing FDE and contracting out work?

"Contracting out work" is the name for an agreement that delegates work to an external party, while FDE refers to the role of the person doing the work. Even when outsourcing FDE, the contract type—either "chukai" (fixed-scope contract) or "jun'inin" (quasi-mandate)—is chosen separately. For work where what to build is revised as the project progresses, a quasi-mandate is considered a better fit*3.

Q. If starting small, which tasks should be delegated first?

Tasks that are repeated daily and whose time spent can be counted are a good fit. Tasks like data entry, transcription, and aggregation may be short per instance but occur frequently, making total work time large. Such tasks make it easy to verify effects and to define how results are measured.
