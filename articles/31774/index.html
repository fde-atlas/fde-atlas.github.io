How AI participates in FDE delivery: automating the work, preserving the decisions

Original title: AI怎样参与FDE交付：自动化工作，保留决定

pafa.github.io · 2026-09-08 · 6 min read

Summary

This chapter examines how AI participates in FDE delivery through three cases: Mistral's Fortran-to-C++ migration for an unnamed European energy company, where autonomous single- and multi-agent attempts failed and humans ended up orchestrating coding, testing, and review, completing only 40,000 of 300,000 lines in the first round; Palantir's Evolve, which requires users to specify goals and validation methods upfront, with the principle that whoever generates candidates cannot also change acceptance criteria; and AWS preview terms where human experts and AI agents deliver jointly but customers retain responsibility for production readiness, deployment, and maintenance. The conclusion: automatable work can expand, but business meaning, evidence, and acceptance decisions stay with people.

Key points

  • Mistral's autonomous agent attempts failed; human orchestration succeeded.
  • First round covered 40,000 of 300,000 lines, not extrapolatable.
  • Palantir Evolve separates code changes from validation-criteria changes.
  • AWS terms keep production, deployment, and maintenance with customers.
  • As automation matures, people shift to acceptance and decision roles.

AI can help write code, find information, generate tests, and analyze errors. It can also participate in improving AI systems themselves. But between "the agent has completed the task" and "the organization can accept this change" there still lies business meaning, evidence, and decision authority.

This chapter follows three concrete paths: Mistral's legacy code migration, Palantir's constrained iteration mechanism, and AWS's delivery by human experts together with agents. They respectively illustrate how work is organized, how products submit changes, and how customers take over responsibility; they cannot be pieced together into an FDE system in which no one is involved anymore.

Mistral: after two autonomous attempts, switching to human orchestration

On September 9, 2026, Mistral described migrating a reservoir simulation program for an unnamed European energy company: rewriting a legacy program written in Fortran as a C++ implementation. Both are programming languages; what the customer truly cares about is whether the computation remains correct after the rewrite and whether the structure is easier to maintain. The customer's reservoir engineers specified the final results and key intermediate checkpoints; the team first established numerical equivalence checks between the old and new programs, then carried out the rewrite. 1

Initially, a single agent was allowed to autonomously handle each subroutine; the result was close to sentence-by-sentence translation, without fully accomplishing the structural transformation. The second attempt used multiple agents for planning, coding, testing, and review, but still stalled when encountering complex errors. In the end, humans orchestrated coding, testing, and review, domain engineers audited module architecture, and humans reviewed PRs. The first round processed 40,000 of the original 300,000 lines, starting from modules that could run independently; this cannot be extrapolated to completion of the entire codebase. 1

This case does not hide the failures behind the final results. A single agent can generate usable syntax yet fail to achieve the goal of modernization; multiple agents can divide the work but may still lack a decision-maker for handling exceptions. Adding more roles and establishing a reliable improvement path are not the same action.

"Numerical equivalence" also has clear boundaries. It shows that the old and new programs agree on the inputs and locations checked; it cannot prove that the old model's scientific assumptions are always correct in the real world. Domain experts still need to judge which inputs are representative, which differences are acceptable, and whether there are conditions the new program does not yet cover. Engineering testing and scientific judgment must connect; they cannot substitute for each other.

Below, the case insights are converted into this book's operational recommendations; undisclosed details are not attributed to Mistral. Before starting work, the team should first save a runnable baseline, then split the goal into two items: preserving behavior and improving structure. The two are checked separately: the former compares old and new results, the latter examines module boundaries and maintainability. If the program cannot even run the baseline, restore the baseline first; do not let an agent generate a new set of answers and use them to prove itself correct.

When repeated fix failures occur, pause the current module, save the failing inputs, changes, and logs, and hand them to someone with the relevant domain or engineering authority to judge. You can shrink the module, adjust the architecture, or defer that part; you cannot count unresolved modules into the completion percentage just because other modules have succeeded. At delivery, list migrated items, retained legacy systems, and unknown parts together, so the next maintainer knows the boundaries.

Palantir: after generating candidates, who changes the acceptance criteria

Palantir's implementation system has already explained how AI FDEs and Evolve organize tools, constraints, and validation. Here it is placed alongside Mistral's failure path: the former turns iteration into a platform product, while the latter records how a team moved from autonomous attempts to human orchestration. Both paths must answer one question—can those who generate candidates also change the conditions the candidates must satisfy?

The September 8 Evolve announcement requires users to first specify goals, validation methods, and constraints, then review proposals and agent activity; the documentation shows candidates compared against a baseline, not proven customer benefits. 23 Automated comparison is more convenient, but it does not automatically make tests cover real difficulties.

Following the Mistral case insight: if a rewrite fails to preserve key intermediate results the customer has specified, the coding agent must not be allowed to delete that checkpoint and then report the remaining passing tests as success. If the old test itself is wrong, evidence should be presented and confirmed by domain personnel who can adjudicate the meaning; erroneous tests can be modified, but already-confirmed scientific requirements must not yield to migration convenience. This acceptance principle is this book's recommendation, not an undisclosed operational record of that project.

Therefore, at least two kinds of changes must be separated: what the program or model changed, and what changed in the validation conditions. The former requires performance, behavior, or cost evidence; the latter requires explaining where the original check was distorted and why the modified conditions still represent customer requirements. Submitting both together can be reasonable, but there must not be only a single "all passed" score.

When subsequent inputs exceed the tested range, re-judge; an activity chart or a single baseline comparison can only provide evidence within its actual coverage.

AWS: agents stay in the customer environment—how does responsibility stay behind

The AWS FDE preview terms verified by this book on October 1, 2026 make clear that human experts and AI agents jointly provide the service, customer-customized agents may remain in the customer environment, and intellectual property boundaries for proprietary tools and customer deliverables are agreed separately. The customer remains responsible for production readiness, deployment, operation, permissions, testing, and maintenance, and the customer is also responsible for revoking access after the engagement ends. These are the specific provisions of the preview terms read, not a universal rule for all FDE contracts. 4

The distinction here is very practical: obtaining an agent that can continue running does not mean acquiring the vendor's full delivery tooling, nor does it mean the vendor bears every future production decision. At procurement, you need to know which components can be maintained independently, which still depend on the service, and when re-authorization is required. When the customer has no operations staff, the handover problem cannot be solved by "the agent will stay."

A named application can help understand this boundary. In a September 8 announcement, AWS and ADP described ADP Assist's agent built on AgentCore, supporting payroll exception correction, Q&A, and reporting under human supervision. The announcement cannot prove that all actions are executed automatically by AI, nor does it explicitly name this collaboration as FDE; this book does not adopt its efficiency figures, which lack sample explanations. 5

Take a payroll exception as a teaching exercise: an agent can collect discrepancies, propose explanations, or prepare corrections, but formally changing accounts requires verification personnel and approval authority. If a fix changes an amount, who verifies, who approves, and how duplicate actions are recovered should be determined before execution. Human supervision is not a glance after every action, but ensuring that critical actions have an appropriate decision position.

Once automation matures, people move to more useful positions

The three paths do not give the answer that "humans must forever manually check every step." Repeatable actions with clear boundaries and reliable validation can be progressively automated; when business meaning is unclear, exceptions are uncovered, or authorization is missing, the conditions must be filled in first.

Delivery records should connect goals, inputs, changes, validation, approvals, and operational responsibility into one line, and enable those taking over to find the input or change that caused an error and restore a previous state. Agents can prepare the records, verified by appropriate personnel; a high score generated by itself cannot substitute for the acceptance decision made by someone with the authority to be responsible.

To judge whether efficiency has improved, human review, customer handover, and subsequent maintenance must be counted; faster code generation alone is insufficient to show that capability has been left with the customer.
