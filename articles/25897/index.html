Time to First Value: A Plan Implementation Teams and FDEs Can Run Before Go-Live

Quivly · 2026-11-03 · 8 min read

Summary

This Quivly guide argues that implementation plans built backwards from go-live leave customers seeing activity but no results for weeks. It proposes planning around time to first value instead: define a first-value milestone at kickoff, map the critical path to it, cut wait states like credential gathering and security reviews, prove a first win with a small pilot team on live data before go-live, and hand value evidence to customer success. Weekly signals track slippage, and the handoff packet records what was achieved, who saw it, and what comes next.

Key points

  • Define the first-value milestone at kickoff, separate from go-live.
  • Sort tasks into critical path, go-live needs, and backlog.
  • Waiting, not work, is usually the biggest delay; request inputs early.
  • Prove a first win with one pilot team on live data before launch.
  • Pass value evidence to customer success instead of just task lists.

Most implementation plans are built backwards from go-live. The kickoff sets a launch date, the project plan fills the weeks in between with configuration, integration and training tasks, and value is something the customer is expected to find after launch. That ordering is the reason so many rollouts feel slow to the customer even when they are on schedule. For weeks, the buyer who signed the contract sees activity but no result.

Implementation teams and forward deployed engineers (FDEs) can change that by planning around a different date: the day the customer gets its first real win. This guide sets out a time-to-first-value plan that implementation teams run themselves. It covers how to define a first-value milestone at kickoff, how to remove the wait states and dependencies that delay it, how to prove the first win before go-live, and how to hand the evidence to customer success so the value story survives the handoff.

If you are looking for how to measure time to value across a portfolio, see our guide on how to measure time to value. This article is about delivery: what the implementation team does, week by week, to bring that first win forward.

Why Implementation Teams Should Own Time to First Value

Time to value is often treated as a customer success metric that gets reported after onboarding ends. By then, most of the delay has already happened. The decisions that set the pace are made during implementation: which integrations go first, which team is trained first, which data is migrated before anything else and which approvals the customer is asked for.

FDEs and implementation leads sit closest to those decisions. They know which tasks are truly required for the first outcome and which are there because the template included them. When they own time to first value, three things change:

  • Sequencing follows value, not convenience. Work that unlocks the first outcome moves to the front of the plan, and everything else can follow.
  • Blockers get treated as risks. A missing credential or an unanswered request becomes a threat to a named milestone, not just a late task.
  • The customer sees progress in its own terms. Status updates describe how close the customer is to a result, not how many tasks are complete.

Define the First-Value Milestone at Kickoff

The first-value milestone is the earliest point at which the customer can see the product doing the job it was bought for, using its own data and its own people. It is smaller than the full outcome in the business case, but it must be real. A configured workspace is not first value. A first report built on live customer data, used by the team that asked for it, is.

Agree the milestone in the kickoff meeting, with the customer's sponsor in the room. A useful definition answers four questions:

  • What outcome? Describe it in the customer's language, for example "the support lead can see which accounts opened urgent tickets this week without exporting anything."
  • For whom? Name the team or person who will use it first. A first win that nobody on the customer side uses does not count.
  • With what data? List the systems and fields that must be connected. This tells you which integrations are on the critical path.
  • How will we know? Define an observable signal, such as the first workflow completed in the product or the first decision made from its output.

Write the milestone into the project plan as its own line with a target date, and keep it separate from go-live. If your onboarding framework already uses checkpoints, the first-value milestone usually maps to the "first value event" stage described in our post on customer onboarding milestones. Scoping work done before kickoff makes this much easier, which is why we recommend settling it during implementation scoping.

Map the Critical Path to First Value

Once the milestone is defined, work backwards from it. List every task that must be done before the customer can see the first outcome, and mark the dependencies between them. This is a simple form of the critical path method used in project management: the longest chain of dependent tasks sets the earliest possible date.

Most implementation plans contain far more tasks than the critical path needs. Sort each task into one of three groups:

  • On the path: the first value cannot happen without it, such as connecting the one system that holds the data the first workflow needs.
  • Needed for go-live: important but not required for first value, such as rolling out single sign-on to every team or migrating historical records.
  • Nice to have: requested but not tied to either milestone. Park these in a backlog with an owner.

Share the critical path with the customer. When their project lead can see that one approval or one data export is holding up their first win, the request tends to move faster than when it sits in a long task list.

Cut Wait States and Dependencies

On most implementations, the work itself is not the slowest part. Waiting is. Tasks sit idle while someone gathers credentials, a security team reviews a questionnaire, a sample file is exported or an internal engineer finds time to review a mapping. Lean practitioners call this waiting a form of waste, and the idea from lean manufacturing applies well to delivery work.

To shrink wait states on the critical path:

  • Request inputs before kickoff. Send a short list of the credentials, files and contacts needed for the first-value milestone as soon as the deal closes.
  • Ask once, in one place. Scattered requests across email threads and chat channels get lost. Use a single request list with owners and due dates.
  • Start reviews early. Security and legal reviews often have their own queues. Start them in week one, in parallel with configuration.
  • Use a narrow first scope. Connect the one data source the first workflow needs before connecting everything. You can widen the scope after the first win.
  • Set a response window. Agree with the customer how quickly each side will answer questions on the critical path, and escalate politely when the window passes.
  • Track idle time, not just effort. In weekly reviews, note how long each critical task waited and why. Patterns across accounts show you which requests to move earlier in the template.

Prove the First Win Before Go-Live

Go-live usually means every team, every integration and every agreed configuration is in place. First value does not need any of that. The aim is to give the customer a working result for one team on one workflow as early as possible, then expand.

A practical approach is a short first-win sprint inside the implementation:

  • Pick the pilot group. Choose a small, motivated team that owns the workflow in the milestone.
  • Connect only what the workflow needs. Use live data from the systems on the critical path, even if other integrations are still in progress.
  • Run the workflow together. The FDE and the pilot team complete the workflow in a working session, not a demo. The customer does the work; the FDE removes friction.
  • Capture the result. Record what the team could do that it could not do before, how long it took and what it replaced, in the customer's words.
  • Share it with the sponsor. A short note or screen recording of the first win keeps the sponsor engaged through the rest of the rollout.

Proving value early also lowers delivery risk. If the first win does not land, you learn about a data, scope or expectation gap while there is still time to fix it, rather than after launch. Our guide to implementation risk signals covers what to watch for when a rollout starts to stall.

Keep the Plan Honest With Weekly Signals

A first-value plan only works if the team notices quickly when it slips. Review a short set of signals each week with the customer's project lead:

  • Days remaining to the first-value milestone, and whether the date has moved.
  • Open requests on the critical path, with their owner and how long each has been waiting.
  • Integration status for each system the first workflow depends on.
  • Whether the pilot team has logged in and started the workflow.
  • Tone and topics in recent calls, emails and support conversations, which often show frustration before any task is late.

When the milestone date moves, say so and say why. Customers tolerate a delay they understand far better than one they discover at the next status meeting.

Hand Value Evidence to Customer Success

The first win is the most useful thing an implementation team can pass to customer success, yet it is often lost in the handoff. The CSM inherits a list of completed tasks and a go-live date, but not the story of what the customer achieved and why it mattered to them. That pairs with a related guide on where AI removes manual work in each phase of customer onboarding.

Make value evidence a required part of the handoff. A good packet includes:

  • The first-value milestone as agreed at kickoff, and the date it was reached.
  • What the pilot team achieved, in the customer's own words where possible.
  • Who on the customer side saw the result, including the sponsor's reaction.
  • The next outcomes the customer expects, and which teams or workflows come next.
  • Open risks: deferred integrations, unanswered requests, or teams that have not adopted yet.

With this evidence, the CSM starts from a proven result instead of a blank page and can build the next success plan on it.
