What's the difference between fine-tuning and RAG? Decision criteria and how FDEs choose between them in the field

Original title: ファインチューニングとRAGの違いは？FDEが現場で選ぶ判断基準と使い分け

FDE総研 · 2026-10-07 · 10 min read

Summary

This article explains how FDEs should choose between RAG and fine-tuning when adapting LLMs to client workloads. RAG places knowledge outside the model and retrieves it per query, while fine-tuning alters model weights. RAG wins on knowledge freshness, citations, and access control; fine-tuning suits strict output formats, classification tasks, and shorter prompts. The author recommends trying prompt improvements first, then RAG, and only considering fine-tuning when behavior issues persist and training data can be maintained. The two can be combined, as in UC Berkeley's 2024 RAFT method. It also describes FDE practice: assess data location, quality, and permissions early, build evaluation sets during PoC, and pitch options to executives on update, governance, and cost rather than accuracy.

Key points

  • RAG injects retrieved knowledge into prompts; fine-tuning changes model weights.
  • Choose RAG for fresh knowledge, citations, and permission-based access control.
  • Consider fine-tuning only for stable output formats after prompt and few-shot attempts fail.
  • RAFT (UC Berkeley, 2024) shows retrieval and fine-tuning can be combined.
  • FDEs should build evaluation sets early and start with RAG for quick value.

What's the difference between fine-tuning and RAG?

RAG is a mechanism that retrieves external knowledge and passes it at answer time, while fine-tuning is a technique that additionally trains the model itself.

Fine-tuning and RAG (Retrieval-Augmented Generation) are both techniques for adapting large language models (LLMs) to business needs. However, the places they modify are completely different: RAG places knowledge outside the model, while fine-tuning changes the weights inside the model. Understanding this difference forms the foundation for how an FDE (Forward Deployed Engineer) builds proposals at customer sites.

Difference in mechanism: "passing" knowledge versus "teaching" it

RAG retrieves relevant documents for each question and puts them into the prompt, while fine-tuning changes the model's behavior with training data.

RAG is a concept proposed in 2020 by Lewis et al. at Meta AI (then Facebook AI Research) in the paper "Retrieval-Augmented Generation for Knowledge-Intensive NLP Tasks." In current implementations, the following flow is common:

  • Split internal documents and manuals into appropriately sized chunks
  • Convert them into vectors with an embedding model and register them in a vector DB or search engine
  • Retrieve documents close to the user's question
  • Put the retrieval results into the prompt and have the LLM generate an answer

Fine-tuning, on the other hand, prepares several hundred to several thousand pairs of inputs and expected outputs, and updates the model's weights through additional training. Techniques that reduce computational cost by limiting the parameters updated, such as LoRA (Low-Rank Adaptation) proposed by Hu et al. at Microsoft in 2021, have become widespread, making it more accessible than before.

Comparison of cost, update frequency, and explainability

RAG wins on ease of knowledge updates and ease of showing sources, while fine-tuning wins on output consistency and inference-time efficiency.

From a practical standpoint, many requests to "teach the model new facts" are actually problems that can be solved with RAG. A 2023 study by Ovadia et al. at Microsoft, "Fine-Tuning or Retrieval? Comparing Knowledge Injection in LLMs," also reported that RAG produced consistently better results for tasks involving incorporating new knowledge.

How should RAG and fine-tuning be used differently?

The basic rule: choose RAG when knowledge freshness and citations matter, and fine-tuning when you want to standardize output format or decision patterns.

The decision axis is simple: "Is what's missing knowledge, or behavior?" Just dividing the issues heard from customers into these two categories makes the direction of the discussion much clearer.

Conditions for choosing RAG

When the information handled changes frequently, or when answers need to show their basis, choosing RAG first is reasonable.

  • Handling information updated monthly or weekly, such as internal regulations, product specifications, and FAQs
  • Fields like finance, healthcare, and the public sector, where answer citations are needed for audits or explanations
  • Cases where viewable documents differ by department or role, requiring access control
  • Cases with a large volume of documents, where training the model on all of them is unrealistic

Access control is a particularly important issue for FDEs in the field. Knowledge embedded in a model through fine-tuning cannot be distributed differently by permission. RAG, which can apply permission filters at the retrieval stage, is a configuration that fits corporate governance requirements more easily.

Conditions for choosing fine-tuning

Fine-tuning is effective when you want to reliably enforce strict output formats, industry-specific writing styles, or classification rules.

  • Consistently outputting reports or structured data (such as JSON) in a fixed format
  • Improving accuracy of repeated tasks such as inquiry classification or entity extraction
  • Matching specialized terminology or internal document styles
  • Having the model memorize instructions currently given in long prompts, to improve inference cost and response speed

However, many cases can be sufficiently resolved by improving prompts or using a few examples (few-shot). Since fine-tuning adds burdens including training data quality management and model update operations, it is safest to treat it as an option to choose after trying other approaches.

Decision flow when in doubt

It is safest to try in the order of prompt improvement → RAG → fine-tuning, verifying effectiveness with evaluation data at each stage.

  • Separate the issues: Classify whether the cause of incorrect answers is "lack of knowledge" or "instability in format/behavior"
  • Try with prompts: Check whether clarification of instructions and few-shot examples improve results
  • If knowledge is lacking, use RAG: First measure retrieval accuracy (whether the needed documents rank highly)
  • If it's a behavior problem, consider fine-tuning: Check whether you can collect training data and maintain it continuously
  • Compare with an evaluation set: Compare before and after improvements numerically using the same evaluation data

When should the two be combined?

When you need to reference internal knowledge while simultaneously adhering to industry-specific output formats and terminology, combining them is a strong option.

RAG and fine-tuning are not mutually exclusive. You can divide the roles: knowledge is provided via RAG, while behavior is shaped through fine-tuning. RAFT (Retrieval Augmented Fine Tuning), published by Zhang et al. of UC Berkeley in 2024, is a method that trains the model to select appropriate evidence and answer even when retrieved documents contain noise. Research on combining the two is also advancing.

Typical patterns where combining works well

For operations where you want to standardize how search results are interpreted and the response format, adding light fine-tuning on top of RAG is effective.

PatternRole of RAGRole of fine-tuning
Internal help deskSearch the latest regulations and procedure manualsStandardize response templates and polite tone
Maintenance and inspection report creationReference past inspection records and equipment specificationsEnforce report formats and technical terminology
Contract review supportSearch internal standards and past clause examplesTrain rules for risk classification labeling

What to check before combining

While combining them is highly effective, it also doubles the operational burden, so you need to first confirm whether you can establish evaluation and maintenance systems.

  • Do you have evaluation data?: Can you prepare an evaluation set that allows comparing RAG alone with the combined configuration?
  • Are the update cycles aligned?: Who updates documents and retrains the model, and at what frequency?
  • Can you isolate the causes?: Can you distinguish from logs whether an incorrect answer is due to retrieval failure or generation failure?
  • Can you keep up with model updates?: When the base model is updated, do you plan on the assumption of retraining?

The last point is often overlooked. With base model generations changing rapidly today, it is not uncommon for a painstakingly fine-tuned model to be overtaken by the combination of a next-generation model and prompting. When making investment decisions, you should also consider this risk of obsolescence.

How do FDEs make decisions and implement at customer sites?

FDEs first build a minimal configuration with prompts and RAG, identify gaps using evaluation data, and only then proceed to training.

The value of an FDE lies not only in making technically correct choices, but in delivering "something that works" quickly, adapted to the customer's operations, data, and organizational constraints. AIP, offered by Palantir, takes the approach of connecting LLMs to an Ontology (a model that gives meaning to business data). This reflects the idea of grounding answers in trusted data rather than having the model memorize knowledge, which is close to a RAG-like approach.

What to determine in the initial phase

What should be determined first is not the technology selection, but the location and quality of the data, and who can judge what the "correct answer" is.

In the initial interviews, checking the following items will make later decisions less likely to waver.

From hands-on experience, projects tend to stumble not because of model selection, but because of data-side issues, such as documents existing only as scanned PDFs or different team members having different versions.

Evaluation design and moving from PoC to production

Building an evaluation set with business stakeholders during the PoC stage affects both the transition to production and the fine-tuning decision.

  • Create the evaluation set: Collect pairs of questions and expected answers from actual inquiries and business cases
  • Measure the baseline: Record accuracy and format compliance rates with a prompt-only configuration
  • Introduce and measure RAG: Evaluate retrieval recall and answer quality separately
  • Classify remaining errors: Distinguish whether they are due to lack of knowledge or errors in format/judgment
  • Decide on fine-tuning: Proceed only if behavior errors are frequent and training data can be continuously prepared

An evaluation set is not something you build once and finish. By continuously adding incorrect answers found in production, it can also serve as a regression test when changing the model or RAG configuration.

Key points when explaining to decision-makers

For executives and business owners, comparing options across the three points of "updates, governance, and cost" rather than accuracy makes it easier to reach agreement.

  • Updates: When information changes, who can reflect it and with how much effort?
  • Governance: Can answers show their evidence? Can output be differentiated by permissions?
  • Cost: Not just initial construction costs, but what about operating costs including retraining and document maintenance?

From the perspective of companies considering FDE adoption, requests like "we want to build a company-specific AI with fine-tuning" often stem from intentions such as differentiating from competitors or preserving internal knowledge. An FDE should understand those intentions, then show a path of first delivering value quickly with RAG and considering training once the data and evaluation foundations are in place. This kind of phased proposal is likely to build customer trust.
