Manufacturing and Aviation: Engineers Must Understand Production

Original title: 制造与航空：工程师必须理解生产

pafa.github.io · 2018-03-27 · 21 min read

Summary

This chapter uses Airbus manufacturing and aviation maintenance to examine how forward deployed engineers should work in industrial settings. Opening with Nabeel S. Qureshi's account of being embedded at the Airbus plant in Toulouse, it argues that coding skill alone is insufficient: engineers must decompose vague states like "ready to start work" into verifiable conditions, and distinguish information waits, decision waits, and resource waits. Cases include Skywise's Dispatch app, the Configuration Cockpit tool, easyJet's predictive maintenance partnership, a continuous chemical plant, a packaging-company proof of concept, and Siemens's industrial AI collaborations. The chapter stresses that software can shorten the first two waits but not physical supply constraints, and that published effect figures often lack independent verification.

Key points

  • An FDE's value lies in understanding how production constrains decisions, not just coding.
  • "Ready to start" must be split into verifiable conditions: arrival, inspection, allocation.
  • Configuration checking keeps a "needs manual analysis" state, acknowledging system limits.
  • Predictive maintenance matters only if alerts lead to executable inspections.
  • Software cannot eliminate physical waits like the Pratt & Whitney engine shortage.

Nabeel S. Qureshi joined Palantir in the summer of 2015 as a Forward Deployed Engineer. He later recalled living in Toulouse for a year and going into the Airbus factory four days a week. The customer's problem was ramping up A350 production: they connected work orders, missing parts, and non-conformance items into an interface that let users see other teams' progress, parts, and plans, and look up how quality issues had been handled in the past. 1

These words are not the stars of AI hype, yet they determine something more immediate: whether the next step of production can begin.

This first-hand account leaves behind two real design choices: bringing current cross-team dependencies together, and turning historical quality handling into searchable evidence. The former helps locate who is still waiting on whom; the latter helps users find existing handling experience. The account does not disclose how individual decisions were divided across features or how each was accepted, but it presents a concrete path to ramping up production: first helping the shop floor identify and clear obstacles in production.

This is also manufacturing's first test for the FDE. Being able to write code only guarantees that a system runs according to some definition; understanding production is what reveals whether the definition itself omits key conditions. This chapter first looks at Airbus manufacturing, supply, and maintenance, then compares the different work at a chemical plant, a packaging company, and Siemens, and finally returns to reuse and waiting. These belong to different stages and were not the same project completed by a single FDE team. The question running through them is: how does data acquire a meaning clear enough for the responsible people to act?

Asking Clearly What "Ready to Start Work" Means

In July 2018, Airbus introduced Skywise applications for suppliers. Among them, Dispatch is an application that helps align supply chain planning, connecting supplier production plans with Airbus demand and focusing on missing parts, deliveries, and assembly scheduling; Premium Aerotec was a pilot partner for this application. The announcement also noted that early participants were selected, with willingness to participate and digital readiness both being criteria. 2

This offers an entry point for understanding manufacturing software. A customer's requirements table and a supplier's production table, even if both are accurate, may not answer the same question. The former wants to know whether a part can be used on a given date; the latter may only record whether manufacturing can be completed on that date. Between the two lie transportation, inspection, configuration matching, and who handles exceptions when they occur. Simply placing the two dates side by side may produce a tidy chart, but it does not eliminate the real discrepancy.

Below is a teaching scenario of a factory that runs through this chapter; it is not an internal Airbus record: a factory is preparing to install a set of components, the warehouse shows they have arrived, but the crew says work cannot start because inspection is not yet complete. If an engineer writes "inventory quantity greater than zero" as the condition for starting work, the system will push the crew to do something they currently cannot do. If the crew keeps explaining and keeps ignoring the reminders, even the most advanced interface becomes an extra burden.

The solution is not to change the color of "arrived." The engineer needs to work with the warehouse, inspection, planning, and floor supervisors to break a vague promise apart: Have the parts actually arrived? Are they the correct identity? Is inspection complete? Have they been allocated to this job? Has the previous step been handed over? Each answer must also include who confirmed it and when. Only then can planners know whether to chase transportation, chase inspection, or reorder tasks.

Continuing this example: suppose inspection is complete but the data has not yet been transmitted back; the software must not immediately judge the physical part as non-conforming. These are two different problems: one requires handling the part, the other requires verifying the record. If the system merges them into the same red flag, the shop floor must re-investigate every alert. So-called data integration merely moves the investigation work from finding the spreadsheet to interpreting red flags.

Therefore, an engineering process that can be discussed and accepted should start from specific exceptions: the system detects a missing condition, states which condition is missing, and hands it to someone able to verify it; after verification, it preserves distinct conclusions such as "record outdated," "inspection incomplete," or "the part is genuinely faulty," and then the person authorized to schedule production decides the next step. Once action is taken, the result should flow back into the system so the next shift can see why the issue was closed.

This model connects software with responsibility. The program is responsible for finding differences that need handling, professionals are responsible for judging what the differences mean, and production supervisors are responsible for rearranging work within permitted conditions. The three can cooperate, but a single "smart recommendation" button must not blur their responsibilities. This is also what makes an engineer's on-site presence concrete: he needs to see how one field changes other people's work, not merely hear which field the customer wants added.

Reminders themselves carry a cost. In this teaching example, if every task waiting for inspection automatically demands immediate attention, the system merely shifts planning pressure onto the inspection staff. To respond to more reminders, they may keep switching tasks, while the truly urgent ones remain in the queue. Designers therefore need to confirm priorities with production supervisors and allow recipients to explain why they are not handling something yet. When measuring effectiveness, look at whether the wait for the whole job has shortened, not merely whether the department issuing reminders clears its own backlog faster. An improvement in a local report may simply have handed the waiting to someone else.

Why One Aircraft Has Several States

After an aircraft leaves the factory, the relationship between data and the physical object does not automatically stabilize. A part replacement or a software update can change "what this aircraft is right now." Manufacturing cares about whether assembly can continue; maintenance must also continuously confirm the equipment's actual configuration. This is another business problem, but it gives "understanding the field" a more precise technical meaning.

Airbus's configuration verification tool, Configuration Cockpit, offers a publicly documented process. The tool has been available to Skywise customers since May 2024 and was extended to non-Skywise customers in June 2025. Inputs can come from maintenance records or from onboard configuration reports, which are then checked against the reported configurations and the list of allowed parts that Airbus holds. Besides matches and errors, the results also preserve a "requires manual analysis" status. 3

There are at least three different sources of information here: the operator's maintenance records, the configuration reports generated by the aircraft, and the configuration baseline held by the manufacturer. They come from different activities, and the fact that they all describe the same aircraft does not mean they are always in sync. The system's job is precisely to make these differences visible, not to quietly pick one as the sole answer when importing data.

For non-technical readers, think of it as checking three files for a complex piece of equipment: what the ledger records, what the equipment reports, and what the rules allow. The first may need updating, the second needs correct interpretation, and the third determines whether, even if the first two agree, a problem still exists. Data consistency and status correctness are judgments at two different levels.

"Requires manual analysis" deserves particular attention. If an engineer only pursues the proportion of automated judgments, they may force cases that cannot be automatically determined into pass or fail. The former conceals unresolved problems; the latter creates unnecessary alerts. Preserving a third status amounts to acknowledging the system's capability boundary and turning that boundary into an actionable allocation of work.

However, a status alone does not mean the process is complete. From the deliverer's perspective, one should keep asking: whose work queue does it enter? What raw evidence can the handler see? Do they know which aircraft and which part the judgment applies to? If a judgment cannot be made yet, what evidence is needed next? These implementation questions need to be confirmed in the actual arrangements of each airline.

Equally important is not letting "matched" promise things it never checked. Passing configuration verification only answers the questions that verification covers; it cannot replace all maintenance judgments. Airbus's technical article explicitly emphasizes the checking of software part numbers and positions the tool as an additional safety check; the checks operators must complete have not disappeared because of it. 3

This poses a requirement for industrial FDEs that is easily underestimated: he does not need to replace every domain expert, but he must know where the system must stop and wait for an expert. A reliable application should let users know why it reached a conclusion and what it has not judged. Otherwise, floor staff will either over-trust the results or have to re-check everything from scratch, and in neither case has the software truly done its job of assistance.

Responsibility also cannot be assigned only once at go-live. After rules change, old conclusions may no longer apply; after data is re-synced, existing discrepancies may disappear. The people maintaining the system need to know when to re-check, and business owners need to know when to re-confirm. Maintenance here includes both keeping the program running and keeping the definitions it uses still valid. In production, "broken" sometimes manifests as a program smoothly executing a rule that has become outdated.

After prediction, the work has only just begun

If configuration reconciliation answers "are things consistent now," predictive maintenance attempts to answer "what might happen in the future." On March 27, 2018, easyJet announced a five-year predictive maintenance partnership with Airbus, disclosing that an earlier pilot had focused on three types of technical issues across 85 aircraft. The airline's announcement also described maintenance actions in which engineers removed components in advance. 4

The maintenance actions described in the announcement show the relationship between prediction and action: after an alert, engineers still need to assess and handle the component.

It is worth extending this process one step further. Suppose an abnormal sign in a component is detected early; the airline still needs to determine: is the alert worth acting on? When will the aircraft have a suitable maintenance window? Are the required spare parts and personnel available? After handling it, does what was actually found support the initial judgment? If these questions are not resolved together, prediction may remain stuck on a screen. This is an analysis of implementation conditions, not a reconstruction of work records easyJet has not disclosed.

This also shows why "how far in advance" is not simply a matter of the longer the better. If the lead time is too short, the organization cannot arrange things in time; if the lead time is long but uncertainty is high, it may trigger unnecessary inspections and replacements. The right choice depends on the actions that can be taken, and on the respective costs of acting wrongly and of not acting. If an FDE only listens to the data team explain model accuracy, they have not yet understood the delivery goal; they need to know how the maintenance team can actually use this prediction.

Expertise also does not emerge naturally just because data is pooled. In July 2018, component supplier Liebherr, in the partnership announcement, placed its own component knowledge alongside Skywise data analytics, stating that the pilot was used to study the in-service performance of onboard systems and to explore improved operational recommendations. 5 The value of this material lies in bringing supplier experts back into the story: whoever designs, manufactures, and services these components may hold the knowledge necessary to explain anomalies.

Cross-company collaboration is therefore not simply enlarging a data pool. If some signal changes, the algorithm can help locate it, the supplier's experts can explain the component's mechanisms, and the airline's maintenance staff know what arrangements are feasible. To connect the three, the delivery team must let questions flow with sufficient context: what kind of equipment, under what operating conditions, and what treatments were performed before. When context is missing, more data sometimes only produces more follow-up questions that need to be asked.

easyJet's announcement does not provide all false positives, false negatives, or control designs, so no net benefit for the entire fleet can be computed from it, let alone the assumption that all technical delays can be eliminated.

A teaching table can be used to show which denominators should be recorded next time. Suppose an equipment team issues twenty alerts within the same four-week observation window, already deduplicated one by one; the following numbers are entirely fictional and unrelated to easyJet.

Stage reachedCount and denominatorWhat this stage has not yet proven
Alert issued20 alerts, observed populationCannot yet say twenty failures will occur
Professionals deem an inspection worth scheduling12/20The other eight still consumed investigation time; not uniformly labeled false positives
Inspection completed within an available window9/12Three not yet completed due to window or resource constraints; cannot count as adopted

If only "six findings" is reported, readers cannot see the attrition from twenty alerts to nine inspections. The teaching record separately lists a total of eight hours of investigation and two spare parts reserved for inspection but unused, each tied up for seven days; these costs must also be counted, not just the six issues found requiring action; whether the actions were completed and what results they produced must be recorded separately. For equipment that triggered no alert but later failed, a separate record must be created before false negatives can be discussed. Completed adoption record and blank table preserves the denominator at each step and the reasons for non-completion, helping the team decide whether the next step should be changing the model, notifications, resource arrangements, or stopping the expansion of scope.

Continuously running plants cannot simply copy work-order experience

Aircraft assembly and component maintenance can usually be tracked around a single work order. A continuously running chemical plant poses a different requirement: when upstream parameters change, downstream is still producing, and judgments and actions occur within a constantly changing process.

The Guangxi Huayi Energy & Chemical case published by Supcon provides this contrast. The company describes a project connecting nine core business systems and nearly two hundred interfaces; its TPT time-series large model is used for trend prediction on units such as sulfur recovery, in coordination with planning and scheduling systems. The case also distinguishes prediction of process parameters, control parameter optimization, and cost-benefit estimation. 6 These are the vendor's statements about engineering scope and are not sufficient on their own to prove economic benefits; the public materials also do not confirm that the delivery team used the FDE title.

A time series can first be understood as measurement records such as temperature and pressure arranged over time. Predicting how these values change faces different inputs and failure modes than using language to explain an operations manual. Here one cannot treat all models as chat models, nor infer that several applications share the same action permissions just because they are all called agents.

Reasoning from an implementation perspective, a team must at least distinguish three things: what the prediction alerts operators to, what the optimization program recommends changing, and what the control system actually permits changing. An accurate prediction may not leave time to act; a recommendation that improves one local metric may also affect another process step. Specific limits, interlocks, and operating procedures should be determined by qualified production and safety teams; this book does not prescribe control parameters for real plants.

This also changes the acceptance question. The aviation maintenance teaching table asks whether alerts led to actionable inspections; a continuous plant must also be asked whether it is stable under the same load and feedstock conditions, how takeover works during anomalies, and whether local optimization has pushed costs onto other steps. If production conditions or prices changed during the same period, financial results cannot be attributed solely to the model. The value of on-site knowledge lies precisely in knowing which conditions must be compared together, not in collecting more measurement points and declaring production understood.

Packaging company: first selecting which work to validate among multiple ideas

Manufacturing companies' problems do not all occur directly on the production line. A case published by Hithink RoyalFlush on October 23, 2026, credited to Nancal Technology, states that Lemei Packaging carried out a generative AI proof of concept with Nancal Technology and Amazon Web Services: Nancal's FDE team worked with six of the client's departments to identify 13 candidate requirements; after internal client review, five scenarios entered proof of concept, and four were ultimately chosen for in-depth delivery: financial profit analysis, IT work orders, resume screening, and HR policy Q&A. 7

The implementer stated that the team completed development of the four assistants in about 40 days and ran the relevant business processes end to end in the client's intranet environment; Amazon Web Services provided architecture guidance and security issue support. The financial scenario required first confirming metric definitions such as cost, freight, and exchange rates with business staff, before letting users drill down according to those rules. 7 This is a vendor's description of a proof of concept and should not be written up as four applications already running stably over the long term; its accuracy and time-saving figures lack samples, controls, and observation periods sufficient for independent recalculation.

What is worth preserving here is not the promise that "four assistants can be built in 40 days," but having the client first choose among multiple requirements, then breaking a selected piece of work into verifiable business definitions. Confirming financial rules does not automatically resolve the fairness of resume screening or the approval permissions of work orders; even though the four assistants share one underlying platform, each must still be validated by the corresponding business owner.

Siemens: how industrial AI enters both products and its own operations

The AI competition among manufacturing companies does not happen only on the production line. Siemens both provides industrial software to customers and uses AI in its own operations; only by separating these two identities can we see clearly which capabilities are being productized and which are merely internal applications of the same company.

On January 6, 2026, Siemens announced a partnership with PepsiCo in a CES announcement: first converting some US manufacturing and warehousing facilities into digital twins, then using simulation to test new configurations. A digital twin is a digital representation used to analyze a real object; the model must reflect equipment, conveyor paths, and human activity before it is suitable for comparing changes. 8 The engineering sequence here matters: establish a performance baseline, experiment in a virtual environment, and then decide which changes are worth bringing into physical facilities. The throughput and capital expenditure improvements in the announcement were disclosed by the partners and cannot be extrapolated to all plants.

On September 15, Siemens and Salesforce announced a further partnership connecting industrial design and product information with sales and service processes. The announcement also described another project Siemens had already deployed internally: previously about 2,500 inbound leads per month went untriaged, and two AI agents helped reach out, qualify, and hand leads to sales. 9 This is an internal pre-sales process; it cannot be written up as a factory operations result, nor can it serve as proof that the full plan for the joint product has launched.

What the two scenarios share is not a single universal prompt. Factory transformation must correspond to specific equipment and physical constraints; sales handoff must correspond to customer records, requirements, and the responsible salesperson. Teams must first connect the objects and executable actions into existing tools. A model producing a configuration or a sales recommendation is only one step of the work; actual transformation or human takeover still follows.

From this, the book proposes two different feedback requirements: when simulation and physical observation disagree, the factory team should be able to adjust assumptions and the model; when a lead handoff is inappropriate, the sales team should be able to explain which condition was judged incorrectly. The public announcements do not disclose the full validation and rework rules, so these requirements cannot be presented as practices Siemens has proven item by item. And since equipment control is involved, one cannot infer from an industrial software partnership that the model has been granted independent operational authority.

Its competitive position likely comes from long-term accumulation of industrial objects, existing software, and customer engineering knowledge, then applying these capabilities to cross-departmental tasks. Owning a complete product portfolio can reduce re-integration work, but it will not make acceptance identical across customers. For FDEs, the real opportunity lies in specific interfaces and on-site exceptions; the two announcements read here do not confirm that these projects used the FDE job title, nor can the coverage of lead handling alone prove an increase in closed deals.

After leaving the site, what can be left behind

When Airbus launched Skywise in June 2017, it had already extended its internal manufacturing, engineering, and service collaboration experience to airlines and suppliers. 10 Moving from one company's problems to a platform used by many parties is a highly attractive step in the industrial FDE model: can a single on-site engagement leave behind capabilities that are used more than once?

Whether industrial software can be reused at another site depends on whether the rules still apply. Whether one part can substitute for another, and who may sign off after a process step is completed, must be confirmed together with the specific equipment and production arrangements. Configurable rules should also have an owner who can explain their purpose; they cannot merely hide inside a conditional judgment written by an on-site engineer. For general methods, see Chapter 19: Product Reuse and Chapter 21: Boundaries and Exit; here we first look at the question of time, which is especially important in industrial records.

Return to the component installation scenario. If inspection requirements change, the conditions for "ready to start work" today differ from last month's, so when reviewing old tasks you cannot directly apply today's rules. You need to recover the part's state at the time, the conditions in effect then, the person who made the judgment, and what changes occurred afterward. Otherwise, a decision that was compliant at the time may be re-flagged as an anomaly by new rules, and past mistakes may be masked by them. Preserving this correspondence adds construction work, but it lets quality and production staff verify the basis around the same task instead of arguing with screenshots from different dates.

Cross-enterprise reuse also has commercial conditions. In its quarterly report for the period ending September 2024, Palantir explained that the Airbus partnership begun in 2016 gradually evolved into the Skywise platform collaboration, while limiting its ability to independently offer the platform to certain airlines and Airbus competitors. 11 Thus, a more mature platform with broader customer coverage does not necessarily mean the vendor can enter every market without restriction. An industry partner brings access to customers, but may also define the boundaries of that access.

The materials read in this chapter do not disclose the full allocation of costs and benefits of the partnership. Business leaders can still ask about specific responsibilities: who confirms rule changes, who maintains the records, and whether cross-enterprise use is contractually restricted. These industrial conditions affect how far the collaboration can extend; one cannot assume the same arrangement applies just because the same platform has been adopted by another company.

Which kind of waiting can software eliminate

Are these requirements not also things a competent internal engineering team, manufacturing execution system team, or systems integrator should do? Why classify them as FDE work?

The honest answer: these capabilities are not exclusive to FDEs. Airbus's early manufacturing collaboration has explicit records of the individuals' FDE roles; the Lemei Packaging case was explicitly described by the implementer as using the FDE model. But the other supply chain, maintenance, and operations materials in this chapter are insufficient to prove that every application was delivered by this role. What they help us examine is a way of working, not an ever-lengthening list of credits for a single job title.

The conditions for applying this approach are that the problem spans multiple systems and roles, and existing divisions of responsibility are insufficient to keep improving the software until it is usable. In that case, bringing people who can write code close to real users may shorten the distance between discovering misunderstandings and correcting the program. But if standard systems already meet the need and the organization has stable maintenance capability, adding a long-term on-site team may not be cost-effective. The criterion should be unresolved collaboration difficulties, not whether the job title is fashionable.

Even when collaboration problems are solved, physical constraints remain. On February 19, 2026, when Airbus released its full-year results, it still explicitly noted that the shortage of Pratt & Whitney engines was affecting the production ramp-up, and that related supply commitment issues were affecting plans for the A320 family. 12 This is not a judgment on Skywise's success or failure, but it is enough to remind readers: not all waiting in aircraft manufacturing comes from invisible information.

At minimum, three kinds of waiting should be distinguished. The first: something has happened, but the relevant people do not yet know. The second: everyone knows about the problem, but there is no appropriate decision or coordination. The third: the decision has been made, but equipment, parts, capacity, or transport time still impose constraints. The first two may benefit from better software and collaboration processes; the third requires changes to real resources or physical processes. Software can help detect the third kind of waiting earlier and adjust its impact, but it cannot make an engine appear on time merely by updating a spreadsheet.

Returning to the same component installation task, the three kinds of waiting can be written into a single time record. Both columns in the table below are constructed values, meant only to illustrate the comparison method, not measured improvements at Airbus or any factory. Assuming the task's parts, inspection requirements, personnel capabilities, and available workstations are identical, the "revised process" column represents only a change intended for examination.

Work node and inputConstructed record of original processConstructed record of revised processWho confirms, what is retained
Parts arrive08:0008:00Warehouse staff confirm part identity and receiving record
Inspection completed08:4008:40Inspection staff sign off the result, forming a citable record
Production lead receives complete basis09:1008:45System records the arrival of materials; lead confirms all information needed for the task is present
Installation arrangement confirmed09:2508:50Production lead records the decision and applicable conditions
Workstation and personnel available, installation begins10:0010:00Crew confirms actual start of work, recording resource conditions at the time

In the original process, the thirty minutes from 08:40 to 09:10 are information waiting; the next fifteen minutes are decision waiting; and the thirty-five minutes after 09:25 are waiting for a workstation and personnel. In the constructed revised process, the first two segments shrink to five minutes each, yet actual start of work is still ten o'clock. Resource waiting therefore becomes longer, and total start time shows no improvement. This result is not contradictory: knowing earlier and arranging earlier can reduce lookups and ad hoc coordination, but it will not automatically produce a workstation that frees up ahead of schedule.

At actual acceptance, the time cannot all come from the application's refresh logs. The inspector's sign-off, the point at which the responsible person obtained sufficient basis, the decision record, and the crew's start-of-work acknowledgment each answer different questions. If only the moment data reached the server is recorded, there is no way to know when the information was sufficient to support scheduling; if only the moment the task was closed is recorded, there is no way to determine what was actually being waited on midway. The reason for waiting should also be verifiable by the responsible person, to avoid counting all unrecorded stages as software time.

To compare tasks before and after realistically, one must also control for task type, inspection difficulty, shift, and resource load, and record whether staffing was increased, workstations expanded, or supply plans changed during the same period. If the project only changed information handover, first check whether the corresponding times shortened; if decisions became faster due to authorization adjustments, that should be attributed to shared process improvement. If resource supply changed, list it as a separate cause. Only then can you see what the project improved, and where the next investment should go.

This distinction changes the project's goal. If the team mainly reduced lookup, verification, and handover time, it should first demonstrate the changes in those stages, then study whether they carried through to output. Just because aircraft output grew at the same time, the entire growth cannot be credited to the software. Capital investment, personnel, suppliers, and process changes may all have played a part; without corresponding evidence, the FDE's contribution can only be limited to what can be explained and verified.

It also changes where the engineer's attention goes in the field. Faced with "we need a more real-time dashboard," the question worth investigating further is: after the dashboard updates, who will make what different decision? If no one can act, the problem may lie in authorization or resources; if they can act but lack sufficient basis, the problem may lie in data definitions; if they already act but no results are recorded, the next round of forecasting and scheduling will again be built on incomplete records. Finding the step that is truly broken is more valuable than refreshing all the data faster.

From work orders at the Toulouse plant to configuration verification after aircraft enter service, the work running through it all is making the state in the software withstand the next action. Understanding production does not require an engineer to master everyone's expertise; it requires him to respect how those expertise jointly constrain a decision. The best result left in the field is that people who come later can still rely on the system to do the required work correctly, and know when they must stop and investigate.
