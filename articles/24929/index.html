The Complete Guide to Corporate Use of FDEs | Criteria for Adoption Decisions

Original title: 企業のFDE活用完全ガイド｜導入判断の基準

日本Fde協会 · 2026-07-24 · 5 min read

Summary

This guide by the Japan FDE Association helps companies decide whether to engage Forward Deployed Engineers. It distinguishes FDEs from in-house SEs (who maintain systems) and consultants (who mostly produce proposals), arguing FDEs handle everything from business analysis through implementation to operational adoption. It proposes eight practical evaluation areas—four covering building (requirements, AI agents, app design, prompts/RAG) and four covering sustained use (security, internal controls, documentation, training). It outlines a four-step engagement flow and notes smaller, person-dependent organizations often benefit most.

Key points

  • FDEs differ from in-house SEs and consultants by owning analysis, implementation, and adoption end to end.
  • Best-fit cases: unclear requirements, tools that fail to stick, or person-dependent operations.
  • Eight evaluation areas; buyers should verify vendors can address security, controls, documentation, and adoption.
  • Four-step flow: field interviews, prototype, rollout, then handover and continuous improvement.
  • Small companies benefit too; start narrow and define measurable baselines upfront.

"We introduced AI tools, but no one on the ground uses them." "Only the person who built them can operate them." — We at the Japan FDE Association receive numerous inquiries like these from corporate representatives. When considering FDE (Forward Deployed Engineer) utilization in your company, how does it differ from in-house SEs or consultants, and what criteria should you use to decide on adoption? By reading this article, you will be able to judge whether your company should leverage FDE talent from the perspective of eight practical domains. We organize this with insights from the field experience of Representative Director Maekawa and Director Mitsunaga.

Situations Where FDE Utilization Benefits Companies

An FDE is a professional who embeds themselves in the customer's workplace and provides end-to-end support, from organizing business processes to designing AI-driven improvements and ensuring operational adoption. They are not simply someone who builds tools, nor a consultant who produces documents. Their distinguishing feature is that they take responsibility for both "building" and "ensuring continued use."

Cases Where Adoption Is a Good Fit

FDE utilization tends to be most effective in the following situations. First, when business challenges are visible but requirements are not yet fully defined. Second, when AI or tools have been introduced but have not taken hold in the workplace. Third, when work is concentrated in a specific person and has become dependent on them. Conversely, if specifications are completely finalized and all that is needed is "building," conventional development outsourcing will suffice.

An Example from the Field

At one manufacturing client, production management relied on an Excel file dependent on a single employee, and operations would halt whenever that person was absent. What was needed here was not a new system, but an inventory of business processes and a redesign of operational rules. Organizing before building — talent that can maintain this order is indispensable in FDE-style support.

Clarifying the Differences from In-House SEs and Consultants

The first stumbling block in adoption decisions is the question, "How does this differ from in-house SEs or consultants?" Because the roles appear to overlap, we compare and clarify them below.

Design That Doesn't Stop at Proposals

According to a story we heard from the CEO of a SaaS company, they received a thick consultant proposal, but implementation was entirely handed off to a separate vendor and proceeded while remaining misaligned with actual conditions on the ground. FDEs do not create a gap between proposal and implementation. When Representative Director Maekawa worked as a system PM, he single-handedly handled everything from reporting to management to hands-on implementation and client negotiations, moving across departments — and this led to results. "Doing it yourself" is the core of the FDE role.

The Eight Practical Domains to Evaluate in Adoption Decisions

Superficial comparison articles tend to focus on "whether something can be built," but what truly matters in corporate FDE utilization is the unglamorous domains beyond that. Here are the eight domains we evaluate when assessing requests.

Building Capability (1–4)

(1) On-site interviews and business process organization, (2) business improvement using AI agents and LLMs, (3) design of business applications, workflows, and data integrations, (4) design and evaluation of prompts and RAG (mechanisms for referencing external data). These are the areas commonly discussed in most proposals.

Sustained-Use Capability (5–8)

(5) Information security and access/log management, (6) implementation grounded in internal controls, segregation of duties, and audit trails, (7) documentation and knowledge transfer to prevent over-reliance on individuals, (8) training, operational adoption, and continuous improvement. When making an adoption decision, always confirm whether the provider can speak to items 5–8. Who can access which data, and can AI operation logs withstand audits? Will operations continue even if a key person leaves? Whether this perspective is present determines success or failure.

The Flow from Request to Results

Here is the flow for actually starting FDE utilization, based on our own practice.

Four Steps

First, on-site interviews and problem identification. This is where you determine "the problem that truly needs solving." Second, presenting a prototype. In line with Representative Director Maekawa's creed, "show something working first," this eliminates misaligned understanding early. Third, implementation and internal rollout. Fourth, operational adoption and handover.

Avoiding Stumbles in the First Step

From our startup experience, if you skip marketing and market understanding at the start and proceed technology-first, you may see no results for six months. Conduct thorough market and on-site interviews before starting to build — following this order may seem like a detour, but it is actually the shortest path. Grasping upfront the baseline for measuring results numerically (work hours and costs of the target processes) also keeps investment decisions steady.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q. How does an FDE differ from an in-house SE or consultant?

A. In-house SEs focus on maintaining operations, and consultants focus on proposals and document creation, whereas FDEs handle everything end-to-end hands-on, from business process organization through implementation to operational adoption. For details, see "What Is an FDE? A Practitioner Explains the Differences from SEs and SIers."

Q. Is FDE utilization meaningful even for small companies?

A. Yes. In fact, the more an organization has limited staff and tends toward over-reliance on individuals, the more likely it is to see benefits from process organization and adoption support. We recommend narrowing down to specific target processes and starting small.

Q. Can we make a request without advanced AI expertise?

A. Technical knowledge is not required on the client side. Rather, what matters is being able to articulate the challenges on the ground. The FDE handles the organization and structuring.

For an overview of FDE utilization, also see "What Is an FDE", and for its role in DX promotion, see "Why DX Promotion Needs FDEs". For defining requirements, "Avoiding Failure in Requirements Definition | 5 Things FDEs Do" is a helpful reference.
