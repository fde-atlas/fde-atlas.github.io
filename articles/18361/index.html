In-house FDE Development | Turning Employees Who Can Rebuild Their Own AI Systems

Original title: 社内FDE育成｜自部署のAIの仕組みを作り直せる社員に

株式会社Aiworker · 2025-11-30 · 17 min read

Summary

AIworker, a Japanese startup founded in 2024, offers a training program that turns business-savvy employees into "internal FDEs" who build and maintain AI-powered workflows for their own departments. The standard program runs six months: three months of weekly training followed by three months of on-the-job support, using the company's existing AI tools with no new contracts required. Case results include 11 non-IT departments at a major payment company all completing working deliverables, and a holding company's HR group cutting manual secondment checks from 58 to 36 items. Pricing starts at 1.95 million yen (excluding tax) for three trainees, and the program may qualify for Japan's human resources development subsidy.

Key points

  • Trains business employees to build and fix AI workflows for their own departments
  • Six-month format: 3 months training plus 3 months on-the-job support
  • Uses existing corporate AI tools; no new tool contracts needed
  • Cases: 11 non-IT departments delivered working outputs; manual checks cut from 58 to 36
  • Pricing from 1.95 million yen for three trainees; subsidy eligibility discussed

What is happening in the field

Attending training alone does not change frontline operations. In consultations, we often hear comments like the following.

AI tools were rolled out company-wide, but only a few employees use them

Only a limited number of people can adapt the tools we provided to their own work.

We did the training, but a month later no one is using it

People only learned with generic practice exercises and got stuck when trying to apply them to their own work.

Systems built by external vendors cannot be fixed in-house when the work changes

Every time a procedure changes slightly, we have to contact the vendor that built it.

Waiting for external quotes for every revision, unable to set the timing ourselves

Even for small fixes, quoting and contract procedures come first.

What employees build gets stuck at the information systems department's review

Because what information may be entered and the approval procedures are not defined, it cannot be used in actual operations.

The people who know the exceptions and the reasons behind procedures best are the employees who have handled that work.

We will work with you to sort out which department and which task to start with, in 30 minutes.

What is an in-house FDE?

FDE stands for Forward Deployed Engineer, a term for engineers who go into the customer's field, listen to their issues, and develop AI systems on the spot. We call employees who take on this role in-house "in-house FDEs." Being able to write code is not a requirement. The in-house FDE role has three parts.

1. Listen to the field

  • Interview how the work is done, how exceptions are handled, and how decisions are made in various situations, and organize it into a form that can be handed to AI

2. Shape it with AI

  • Based on the organized procedures, build systems and AI agents that automate their own department's work themselves

3. Bring it back to the field

  • Have colleagues in the department use the built system, refine it through use, and expand it to the next tasks

The goal is to expand employees' roles. Final decisions involving quality, safety, and money remain with people even after the system is built.

Reference: 85.1% of Japanese companies said they lack sufficient personnel to drive DX, and only 16.7% of Japanese companies have already in-housed the necessary parts of system development (IPA "DX Trends 2025"). Among manufacturing sites with challenges in skill development, 62.8% cited "shortage of personnel to provide guidance" ("2026 Monodzukuri (Manufacturing) White Paper").

How to select trainees

Whether training ends as mere training or employees continue building systems often depends on who is chosen as trainees. We look at the following five criteria when selecting people.

Deep understanding of the work in question

Someone who can handle exceptions and explain why procedures are the way they are.

Can express their problems in numbers of hours

We choose people who can state specifics in their own words, such as "this aggregation takes several hours every week."

Not deterred by testing and fixing

People accustomed to redoing things repeatedly are less likely to stall midway.

Can secure 20% of working hours

The condition is that their supervisor commits in advance to the weekly lecture and assignment time.

Has colleagues to test what they build

If someone in the same department will try the prototype and give feedback, points to fix are found quickly.

Why we choose 2-3 people per department

With only one person, there is no one to consult with and work tends to stall midway. Selecting based on both supervisor recommendation and the person's own wish keeps the work aligned with the department's priorities.

How to choose the first project

We recommend work that recurs weekly or monthly, takes 30 minutes or more per instance, and has roughly fixed procedures. Start with something that has no direct impact on clients.

How it proceeds

The standard is six months: three months of development training plus three months of adoption support. At each stage we verify completion criteria, and gradually transfer responsibility for development and maintenance from us to your employees.

Months 1-3

Development training

With our support, trainees learn development procedures using their own work as material. Once a week for 12 sessions total, with up to 20 people per class.

Completion criteria: build one system using the training material and finish the final presentation

Months 4-6

Adoption support

While using it in real operations, they fix it together with us. We also advise on integration with existing systems and internal procedures.

Completion criteria: fix one or more features without our support

Month 7 and beyond

Ongoing support

Development and maintenance are handled in-house, while we support platform operations and rollout to other departments.

Annual review: results of effectiveness measurement and the next target departments

You can also apply for only the three months of development training. The activities for each phase, from month 1 through month 7 and beyond, are shown in the diagram at the top of this page.

The five types of work covered in training

  • Creating documents Drafts of reports, meeting minutes, proposals, and internal approval requests
  • Answering questions Checking internal regulations and past cases, handling routine inquiries to accounting and general affairs
  • Reading large volumes of material Extracting key points from contracts and specifications, analyzing survey trends
  • Automating manual tasks Transferring PDF documents into Excel, routine aggregations and report creation
  • Finding errors Cross-checking purchase orders against quotations, checking application forms for missing entries

The project material is decided after hearing about the trainees' work. The building sessions (sessions 7-10) are restructured to fit the tools available at your company. A chat-only environment, no-code integration tools, or code-writing AI are all fine.

If you tell us the trainee candidates and the AI tools available, we will show you a 12-session structure tailored to your company.

Training case studies

Cases where employees became able to build and fix systems themselves. Company names are withheld. For the figures, we note when they were measured and how.

An employee from a non-IT department builds a system for their own department

ChallengeEmployees from non-IT departments who learned only the basics at an in-house hackathon were unable to apply them to their own department's work. For most participants, it was their first time using the tools.
Support providedOur consultants provided weekly one-hour accompaniment sessions (two terms). We do not build things ourselves; the employees build them. The first session was spent taking inventory of their work, first separating the steps to delegate to AI from the steps where humans retain judgment. Within strict security rules, we narrowed things down to configurations that could actually be built.
ResultsWe supported 11 departments in total (6 in the first term, 5 in the second), and every department completed a working deliverable within the period. Outputs include automatic reminders for unfinished tasks, automatic task creation for cross-department requests, and an AI that answers inquiries. 120 people have taken the related training.

A format where the HR group can change the settings themselves

ChallengeAttendance, payroll, and secondment data were scattered across each company, and people verified the connections between them by hand and by eye.
Support providedWith weekly on-site presence, we built, together with employees, the sorting of attendance data, pre-checks of payroll data, and detection of unregistered secondees. Thresholds and monitoring items were handed over in a form the HR group can change themselves, and a maintenance guide is also provided.
ResultsWe automated the sorting of attendance data for about 630 employees across three companies. The number of items to check visually when confirming secondees dropped from 58 to 36 (actual data).

From chat-only usage to using AI in each person's own work

ChallengeEveryone was only using AI as a chat tool, and the way their own work was done had not changed.
Support providedWe created usage rules together and, in regular meetings, applied them to each person's own duties: creating payment requests, updating shareholder meeting materials, self-checking figures of portfolio companies, drafting reports, and checking NDA templates.
ResultsIn a follow-up survey (October 2026, 5 respondents), 4 out of 5 used it almost every day, with an average usefulness rating of 4.6 (out of 5). The target group has expanded to 25 people.

We build systems in-house using the same procedure and use them daily: updating the contract ledger for business partners, producing research reports, and checking disclosures by competitors.

How to compare against the benefits

Work hours of the target task × number of people involved × hourly wage × duration of continued use

Using this formula, you estimate the benefits first and compare them against the cost. During the training, we actually measure work hours before and after implementation, so the estimate and the results can be verified with the same yardstick.

About subsidies

Training based on your own company's work may qualify for the Human Resources Development Subsidy. Under the August 2026 revision, generic prompt training is no longer eligible, and executives are also excluded. The program is scheduled to run until March 31, 2027. Since the labor bureau determines eligibility and the subsidy amount, we will check together at the planning stage.

If you tell us the work hours and number of people for the target task, we can work out the figure to compare against the cost together.
