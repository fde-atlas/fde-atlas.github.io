FDE Project Lifecycle: Six Stages from Requirements Discovery to Delivery Retrospective

Summary

This practical article from FDEChina breaks FDE projects into six stages: Discovery, Scoping, PoC, engineering, launch/operations, and handover/retrospective. Each stage gets a goal, common pitfalls, and actionable checklists tied to verifiable artifacts like meeting notes, acceptance criteria, regression cases, and handover documents. Introduction

An FDE (Forward Deployed Engineer) delivers vague requirements into usable AI systems or software products at the customer's site. These projects rarely fail at writing code; they fail more often at stages out of order: starting work before requirements are clear, treating a passing prototype demo as completed validation, and nobody watching the data after launch. This article breaks FDE projects into six stages, giving goals, common pitfalls, and directly actionable checklists for each; for the full definition of FDE and a panoramic view of the role, see the Beginner's Guide.

Lifecycle Overview

Most FDE projects go through six stages in sequence: requirements discovery (Discovery) → scoping → prototype validation (PoC) → engineering → launch and operations → handover and retrospective. Section 6 of the Beginner's Guide gives a one-paragraph overview of these six stages; this article expands on each. The stages don't have to be strictly serial, but skipping any step usually means paying double the price later.

Stage 1: Requirements Discovery (Discovery)

Goal: Turn vague requests into a verifiable list of questions, clarifying "who, in what scenario, with what process, solving what problem."

Common pitfalls: Treating leadership briefings as requirements—vision is not process; only interviewing management, not the frontline employees who actually operate the system.

Action checklist:

  • Interviews should cover decision-makers, users, and people affected by the process, with meeting notes produced for each session;
  • Rewrite requests into decidable question formats, such as "how long does this step currently take with manual verification";
  • Before leaving, read the question list back to the business side and confirm both parties share the same understanding before adjourning.

Stage 2: Technical Scoping

Goal: Pick an entry point from the question list that is small enough to prove value, and clearly state what will NOT be done.

Common pitfalls: Overly ambitious scope, trying to cover the entire process in one step; only defining "what to do" without defining "what counts as done well," leading to endless scope creep later.

Action checklist:

  • Rank candidate scenarios by "business value × data availability × implementation difficulty," and take the top 1-2;
  • Write quantifiable acceptance criteria for the entry point (e.g., accuracy, hours saved) and explicit exclusions;
  • Agree with the customer: scope changes go through written confirmation, not verbal agreement.

Stage 3: Solution and Prototype (PoC)

Goal: Build a runnable prototype as fast as possible while validating both technical feasibility and business value—not just validating that "the demo runs."

Common pitfalls: Demoing with clean sample data that collapses when connected to real data; equating a successful demo with passed validation, leaving all edge cases to explode during the engineering stage.

Action checklist:

  • Align with the customer on 3-5 PoC pass criteria before starting work;
  • Connect the customer's real sample data as early as possible, covering at least both normal and abnormal cases;
  • During the prototype phase, record every failure path as input for the engineering stage.

Stage 4: Development and Integration (Engineering)

Goal: Turn the prototype into a production system that runs stably in the customer's environment—data pipelines, permissions, security, and integration with existing systems all need to be implemented.

Common pitfalls: Underestimating customer environment constraints: intranet deployment, approval processes, legacy system interfaces; missing evals, so any change to the model or prompts invalidates all previously validated results.

Action checklist:

  • Establish an eval baseline: turn the real cases accumulated in Stage 3 into a regression set, run it after every change;
  • Before integration, clarify customer-side constraints (network, permissions, data compliance, interface methods) and confirm them in writing;
  • Deliver in small steps: let the customer see runnable increments every 1-2 weeks, instead of holding everything until the end.

Stage 5: Evals and Launch (Launch and Operations)

Goal: Launch with eval evidence, and ensure the system is "watched and managed" in production.

Common pitfalls: Going dark after launch—no monitoring or feedback channel, so problems are only discovered through customer complaints; treating launch as the project's endpoint and no longer tracking business metrics.

Action checklist:

  • Run through the acceptance checklist before launch: confirm functionality, eval metrics, permissions and security, and rollback plans item by item;
  • Set up basic monitoring and alerting: call volume, failure rate, latency, and quality spot checks;
  • Agree on a feedback channel and response time limits, and regularly share operational data with the business side.

Stage 6: Handover and Retrospective

Goal: Hand the system over to the customer team or an internal team, so delivery experience becomes an organizational asset rather than staying in individual heads.

Common pitfalls: Handover equals dumping documents—only providing code and deployment manuals, not operational knowledge or a known-issues list; no retrospective at project end, stepping in the same pits on the next project.

Action checklist:

  • The handover checklist covers: architecture documentation, operations manual, known issues, upgrade paths, and contacts;
  • Arrange a shadowing period: the customer team operates while the FDE observes and answers questions, usually 2-4 weeks;
  • The retrospective answers three questions: what exceeded expectations, what went wrong, and which experiences can be componentized back into the product.

Common Variants

Short-cycle PoC-type projects often compress engineering to a minimum, focusing on Stages 1-3, and only decide whether to invest in productionization after passing; long-term on-site engagements are the opposite, cycling through the six stages repeatedly, advancing with small versions each cycle. Whichever variant, don't skip Discovery or the retrospective—the former determines direction, the latter determines growth.

Summary and Next Steps

The value of the six stages lies not in the process itself, but in leaving inspectable deliverables at every step.
