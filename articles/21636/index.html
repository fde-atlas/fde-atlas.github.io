Summary

Written by Shohei Tokumitsu, CEO of DeFactory, this article explains Japan's 'quasi-mandate contract with completion of results', codified in revised Civil Code Article 648-2 (effective April 2020). Positioned between traditional contracting (ukeoi) and time-based quasi-mandate contracts, it ties payment to deliverables while imposing no strict completion obligation—only a duty of professional care—and allows proportional compensation on early termination. The author compares the three contract types, argues this form suits agile development and AI PoC projects with uncertain requirements, and stresses that vague deliverable definitions cause acceptance disputes. It also links the model to FDE work and freelance engagement strategies.

Key points

  • Codified in revised Civil Code Article 648-2 (April 2020), payment is tied to deliverables, not hours.
  • Unlike ukeoi contracts, no completion obligation—only a duty of professional care applies.
  • On early termination, contractors can claim proportional compensation for benefits the client received.
  • Best suited to agile development and AI PoC projects where requirements are uncertain.
  • Contracts must specify deliverables, acceptance criteria, non-functional requirements, and termination settlement to avoid disputes.

When considering outsourcing contracts for system development or AI development, you increasingly encounter an unfamiliar contract type called the "quasi-mandate contract with completion of results."

A quasi-mandate contract with completion of results is an outsourcing contract in which the client agrees to pay compensation for the results obtained as a consequence of the contractor performing the delegated tasks.

This contract type was clarified in statutory text by the revised Civil Code enacted in April 2020, providing legal grounds for the previously gray-area practice of "paying compensation for deliverables under a quasi-mandate contract."

In this article, drawing on DeFactory's extensive experience supporting contracts for highly skilled IT professionals, we explain-for both system development procurement staff and freelance engineers taking on orders-the differences from fixed-price (ukeoi) contracts and performance-ratio quasi-mandate contracts, points to note when drafting contracts, and how this contract type should be used in AI development and agile development settings.

In a nutshell, the quasi-mandate contract with completion of results is positioned as a contract type that sits between fixed-price contracts and performance-ratio quasi-mandate contracts: "less strict than a fixed-price contract, but pays compensation for results."

In system development, its adoption has been spreading rapidly in recent years as a risk hedge for projects with significant requirement fluctuations.

First, let us clarify the legal definition and the differences from other contracts that are often confused with it.

The quasi-mandate contract with completion of results is defined as "a quasi-mandate contract in which the mandator and the mandatary agree in advance that compensation will be paid for the results obtained through the performance of the delegated tasks."

This contract type was codified in Article 648-2 of the revised Civil Code, effective April 1, 2020.

Under the previous Civil Code, the principle was that mandate contracts were compensated for the "provision of services" itself, which created a gap with business practice of tying compensation to deliverables.

With the revision establishing the "completion of results" type in statutory text, contracts used in system development and consulting practice-"quasi-mandate but paid on a deliverable basis"-now have clear legal grounds.

There are three main points regarding compensation payment under a quasi-mandate contract with completion of results.

  • Trigger for compensation: In principle, compensation is paid upon delivery of the "results" obtained through performance of the delegated tasks (Article 648-2, Paragraph 1 of the Civil Code)
  • No obligation to complete the work: Unlike fixed-price contracts, the mandatary has no obligation to complete the results; what matters is whether the work was performed with due professional care
  • Proportional compensation claim upon early termination: Even if the contract is terminated before delivery of the results, if the mandator receives benefit from the portion already performed, compensation proportional to that portion can be claimed (Article 648-2, Paragraph 2 of the Civil Code, applying Article 634 mutatis mutandis)

The biggest difference between a quasi-mandate contract with completion of results and a fixed-price contract lies in the presence or absence of the "obligation to complete the work."

In a fixed-price contract, the contractor bears the obligation to complete the work, whereas under a quasi-mandate contract with completion of results, even if the deliverables are incomplete, it does not immediately constitute breach of contract as long as due professional care was exercised.

As this table shows, the presence or absence of liability for non-conformity (formerly warranty against defects) is the most significant practical dividing point.

Under a fixed-price contract, if the deliverables contain defects that do not conform to the contract, the client can demand cure, price reduction, damages, or contract termination.

On the other hand, under a quasi-mandate contract with completion of results, even if the deliverables have defects, the mandatary is not immediately held liable as long as they exercised the duty of care required of a professional (due professional care).

If a contract is drafted without understanding this difference, the client side often ends up in a situation where "we intended a fixed-price contract, but we cannot legally take a strong stance against defects in the deliverables."

The biggest difference between a quasi-mandate contract with completion of results and SES or worker dispatch contracts lies in where the authority to direct and command resides.

Under quasi-mandate contracts in general, including quasi-mandate contracts with completion of results, the client (mandator) cannot directly direct and command the mandatary.

  • Worker Dispatch Contract: This is a contract format in which the client company is legally permitted to give direct work instructions to dispatched workers. A worker dispatch contract is concluded between the dispatching company (staffing agency) and the client, and various obligations under the Worker Dispatch Act arise (such as maintaining a dispatch management ledger and managing the "conflict date").
  • SES Contract (a form of quasi-mandate contract): As explained in What's the Difference Between SES and SIer? Characteristics, Pros and Cons, SES is a way of working based on a quasi-mandate contract, in which engineers work on-site at clients and receive compensation based on hours worked. The right to direct work lies with the contractor (usually the engineer's affiliated company), and if the client gives direct work instructions, there is a risk of being deemed "disguised subcontracting."
  • Deliverable-Based Quasi-Mandate Contract: Like SES, the right to direct work lies with the contractor, but the difference is that compensation is calculated based on the completeness of deliverables rather than hours worked.

What deserves particular attention in practice is the case where, even under a deliverable-based quasi-mandate contract, the client's staff give detailed instructions to the contractor (such as freelance engineers) about daily work schedules and procedures, effectively managing them as if they were the client's own employees.

This can be judged as disguised subcontracting regardless of the contract format and may become subject to corrective guidance from the Labor Bureau.

When choosing a deliverable-based quasi-mandate contract, it is essential to agree on what will be delivered, by when, and at what level, on a deliverable basis, and to operate the contract in a way that avoids excessive intervention in daily work processes.

The deliverable-based quasi-mandate contract is a contract format that meets the client's need to "proceed more flexibly than with a fixed-price contract, while still securing some accountability for results."

On the other hand, if a contract is concluded while the definition of deliverables remains vague, it carries the risk of leading directly to acceptance disputes.

The reason the deliverable-based quasi-mandate contract is recommended is that it allows the risk of requirement changes to be shared between the client and the contractor.

Benefits for the Client

  • Unlike time-and-materials contracts (billed by hours worked), compensation is tied to deliverables, making it easier to forecast budgets
  • Since there is no strict liability for non-conformity as in fixed-price contracts, the contractor is more likely to flexibly accommodate requirement changes and specification adjustments
  • It is easier to place orders even with excellent freelance engineers or small teams, while requiring commitment to results

Benefits for the Contractor

  • Since there is no "obligation to complete the work" as in fixed-price contracts, the contractor does not have to unilaterally bear the risk of project delays caused by external factors (such as delays in the client's decision-making or frequent specification changes)
  • As long as the contractor has exercised due professional care, they will not immediately be held liable for damages even if there are certain defects in the deliverables
  • Even if the contract is terminated midway, the right to claim compensation corresponding to the benefit of the work already performed is guaranteed under the Civil Code

Especially in projects such as agile development or AI development, where it is difficult to fully finalize the specifications of the final deliverable at the start, the deliverable-based quasi-mandate contract is more suited to reality than a fixed-price contract.

The most common cause of disputes in deliverable-based quasi-mandate contracts is that the definition of "deliverables" is not concretely specified in the contract document.

Common failure patterns seen in practice include the following.

  • Scope of deliverables agreed only verbally: Work begins based on a vague agreement such as "build something that works," and the client expected "a user-friendly UI" while the contractor considered the deliverable complete with "backend API implementation completed"—a mismatch in understanding
  • Omission of non-functional requirements: Even if functional requirements (what the system can do) are agreed upon, non-functional requirements such as performance, security, and availability are left undefined, leading to disputes at the acceptance stage such as "this cannot be deployed to production"
  • Scope creep: Unlike fixed-price contracts, deliverable-based quasi-mandate contracts do not impose a strict completion obligation, so the client keeps adding requests like "since we're at it, let's also do this," and the originally agreed scope of deliverables gradually expands

A quasi-mandate contract is, by nature, aimed at the performance of the work itself and does not legally guarantee the completion of deliverables.

If the client places an order with a "fixed-price contract mindset" without properly understanding this characteristic, discrepancies in understanding such as "this is not what I expected" tend to surface at the acceptance stage.

Even in DeFactory's support for companies' IT talent utilization, consultations about disputes caused by mismatches in contract formats are not uncommon.

There are five main conditions required to prevent disputes in deliverable-based quasi-mandate contracts.

  • Concrete definition of deliverables: Clearly specify, at the level of an appendix or specification document, the feature list, number of screens, supported environments, and delivery format of deliverables (source code, documentation, deployed environment, etc.)
  • Explicit acceptance criteria and acceptance period: Define what constitutes "completion of the deliverable" (e.g., passing test items, criteria for conducting acceptance testing) and the period for acceptance (e.g., within 10 business days after delivery)
  • Concretizing the standard of due professional care: Reinforce through special provisions the benchmark for "the technical standard normally expected of a professional" (e.g., versions of languages and frameworks used, compliance with coding standards)
  • Compensation settlement method upon mid-term termination: Based on Article 648-2, Paragraph 2 of the Civil Code, agree in advance on the method for prorating compensation for partial deliverables
  • Attribution of intellectual property rights and defect-remedy arrangements similar to non-conformity liability: Even in the absence of statutory liability for non-conformity, it is common in practice to include, as a voluntary provision, free correction support for a certain period (a bug-fix special provision)

These should not simply be copied from a template of a service agreement; they need to be individually tailored as special provisions according to the characteristics of the project.

In particular, a contract with a vague definition of deliverables will sow the seeds of future disputes, regardless of whether you are the client or the contractor.

The deliverable-based quasi-mandate contract demonstrates its true value in cutting-edge projects with high requirements uncertainty.

How to make effective use of this contract format in agile development and AI development (PoC) is an important decision point for those in charge of procurement.

The deliverable-based quasi-mandate contract is best suited for "projects where the specifications of the final output cannot be finalized at the start, but deliverables can be defined at certain milestones."

  • Cases suited to fixed-price contracts: Contracted development where requirements definition is complete and the possibility of specification changes is low (e.g., replacement of business systems)
  • Cases suited to time-and-materials quasi-mandate contracts: Work such as operations and maintenance, technical support, and SES on-site work, where the hours worked themselves have value
  • Cases suited to deliverable-completion quasi-mandate contracts: Agile development where deliverables per sprint (working prototypes, verified technical elements) can be defined, and PoC (Proof of Concept) for AI development where final accuracy or feasibility cannot be predicted in advance

In AI development PoCs, the very purpose is to verify "whether the model reaches the expected accuracy," so imposing a "completion obligation" under a fixed-price contract does not fit the reality.

On the other hand, with time-based contracts that pay only for hours worked, it is difficult for the client to hold the contractor accountable for results.

The deliverable-completion quasi-mandate contract fills the gap between these two, and can be said to have high affinity with AI and data-related projects.

As explained in detail in What is an FDE (Forward Deployed Engineer)? Roles, careers, and how to go independent as a freelancer, explained by professionals, FDEs work deeply on-site with customers and handle everything from implementing AI and data infrastructure to integrating it into business operations, end to end.

The reason FDEs and deliverable-completion quasi-mandate contracts work well together is that both prioritize "commitment to results" over "excessive process management."

Most projects handled by FDEs have the following characteristics.

  • The client's business challenges themselves are not fully articulated at the start of the project
  • The process assumes that requirements are discovered and adjusted while implementing
  • From technical verification (PoC) to production implementation, the granularity of deliverables changes with each phase

Applying a fixed-price contract to such highly uncertain projects would require "freezing specifications" upfront, potentially undermining the agility that FDEs are supposed to deliver.

With a deliverable-completion quasi-mandate contract, deliverables (verification results, implemented features, integrated systems, etc.) can be defined phase by phase, allowing both the client and the FDE to operate the contract with mutual understanding.

As mentioned in Transitioning to an FDE role! Job market trends and required skills, the market value of FDE talent is rising year by year, and the ability to design contract structures is becoming one of the skills required of companies that hire FDEs.

To prevent acceptance inspection disputes in deliverable-completion quasi-mandate contracts, the necessary condition is to document not only functional requirements but also non-functional requirements and code quality in advance.

  • Documenting non-functional requirements: Include measurable criteria in the deliverable definitions, such as target response times, expected concurrent access counts, and recovery time objectives (RTO) in the event of failure
  • Agreeing on code quality standards: Establish the quality level of delivered code in advance, including whether static analysis tools are used, test coverage targets, and code review processes
  • Creating acceptance criteria documents: Clearly document in bullet points "what must be satisfied for a deliverable to be accepted" for each sprint or milestone, and incorporate a process in which both the client and contractor sign and confirm

In practice, these documents are attached separately from the main contract as "work specifications" or "deliverable definition documents," and linked in the contract with wording such as "the deliverables under this contract shall be as defined in Appendix XX."

If you assume the scope of deliverables has been confirmed based only on verbal or chat exchanges, disputes over "who said what" tend to arise at the acceptance stage.

The deliverable-completion quasi-mandate contract is also an important option for companies leveraging highly skilled freelance talent.

Let's look at strategies for using external partners whose compensation is directly tied to results, unlike lump-sum orders to major SIers.

Companies choose professional freelancers and external partners directly instead of major SIers because, without going through multi-layered subcontracting structures, decision-making and implementation are faster, and the parties have a stronger sense of ownership over results.

  • Simplified commercial flow: In multi-layered subcontracting structures via major SIers, requirements pass through multiple rounds of telephone game before reaching on-site engineers, whereas direct orders to freelancers involve less communication loss
  • Direct connection to realistic technology selection criteria: Without going through the SIer's organizational decision-making processes, implementers can directly align technology selection validity with the client
  • Visible commitment to results: The contract form itself, a deliverable-completion quasi-mandate contract, sends the message that "results, not hours worked, are what count," and the more talented the person, the more they tend to prefer this contract form

On the other hand, direct orders to freelancers and external partners increase the client's operational burden, such as drafting and managing contracts, tax processing, and vetting talent.

To reduce this burden, a growing number of companies use contract support through talent platforms such as DeFactory.

There are three main metrics needed when evaluating freelance talent under a deliverable-completion quasi-mandate contract.

  • Completeness of deliverables: To what level the agreed acceptance criteria were met
  • Adherence to deadlines and quality of communication: Transparency of progress, speed of reporting when issues arise, and appropriateness of responses
  • Validity of technical decisions: Not just building what was instructed, but whether appropriate proposals on technology selection and design decisions were made to the client

Regarding compensation, the practical point is to design it as "result-based pricing" that takes into account the difficulty of the deliverables and the rarity of the skill set, rather than simply importing hourly or monthly rates as in time-based (effort-billed) contracts.

As explained in Salary and rate benchmarks for freelance IT consultants, rates for freelancers with advanced skills are trending upward year by year, and those who deliver strong results under deliverable-completion contracts tend to earn compensation above simple effort-based rate levels.

The condition for freelance talent to be highly valued under deliverable-completion quasi-mandate contracts lies not merely in implementation skills, but in "the ability to define deliverables with the client and see them through to completion."

  • Requirements-organizing ability to concretize the outline of deliverables from ambiguous requirements and reach agreement with the client
  • Self-driven ability to complete deliverables, including non-functional requirements and code quality, to a certain standard without additional detailed instructions
  • Negotiation ability to make proposals that account for risk, even for projects with high uncertainty (such as AI development PoCs)

Freelance talent equipped with this "ability to define and complete deliverables" has sufficient market value to stand on their own even in high-rate projects paying 1.2 million yen or more per month.

As mentioned in Three Conditions and Rate Benchmarks Needed for Freelance Engineers to Exceed ¥800,000 a Month, what pushes up the rate ceiling is not long working hours, but trust in results and reproducibility.

The results-completion quasi-mandate contract is a unique contract form whose legal basis was clarified by the Civil Code reform: "compensation is paid for results, but there is no obligation to complete the work."

While it lacks the strict liability for non-conformity of a contract for work, it has the effect of encouraging both client and vendor to focus on results more than a performance-ratio quasi-mandate contract, making it highly compatible with agile development and AI development PoCs, where requirements are highly uncertain.

However, to fully realize its benefits, it is essential to work out in detail in the contract elements such as the definition of deliverables, acceptance criteria, non-functional requirements, and the method of compensation settlement upon early termination.

If the contract proceeds while ambiguous, both clients and vendors face the risk of acceptance disputes and scope creep.

When advancing projects with advanced AI/IT talent such as FDEs, correctly understanding the option of results-completion quasi-mandate contracts and designing contracts suited to your project's characteristics is the shortcut to success.
