What an FDE Is and Isn't — The Last Mile

Original title: FDE 是什么，不是什么 — 最后一公里

Agentic AI Wiki · 2026-10-06 · 17 min read

Summary

The article defines the FDE role through an ownership test: a real FDE owns the customer outcome across discovery, scoping, building, production launch, and handoff, and writes production code as the means. Drawing on job posts from OpenAI, Anthropic, Palantir, and Decagon, it argues the title covers several distinct jobs—an analysis of roughly 1,000 FDE postings found only 60% were build-focused, with 30% rebranded sales engineering and 10% internal tooling. It contrasts FDEs with consultants, contractors, and architects, addresses criticisms that the role is rebranded AI engineering or IT outsourcing, and supplies a ten-line checklist plus interview questions for evaluating real postings, demonstrated on a Harvey job post.

Key points

  • A true FDE owns discovery through handoff and writes production code.
  • FDEs own technical outcomes, not commercial relationships—that belongs to sales.
  • Field learnings must feed back into the product, separating FDEs from contractors.
  • Of ~1,000 FDE postings analyzed, only 60% were genuinely build-focused roles.
  • A ten-line checklist and one interview question help verify real FDE jobs.

What an FDE is, and what it is not

Take a job with the FDE title, and you may spend your days doing demos, doing configurations, or acting as a consultant, because the title now covers several different jobs. There is one test that separates them: does the role own the customer's outcome from discovery through handoff, and write the code to achieve it with its own hands? Remove any one of those stages, and no matter what the job posting calls it, it is a different job. The comparison table and checklist at the end of this chapter let you run this test on a real job posting before you apply.

The definition, from the companies doing the hiring

Start with the employers, because they are the only ones who have written the job down in black and white. What follows can be read as first-hand evidence of what employers ask for; as for how the work is actually done, it counts only as T4 evidence: a job posting describes the job a company hopes to hire someone to do.

OpenAI's San Francisco posting, published on September 11, 2026, is the clearest single posting. The FDE "leads complex, end-to-end deployments of frontier models in production environments with our most strategic customers," and the hire "will own discovery, technical scoping, system design, building, and production launch." Success is measured by "adoption in production, measurable workflow impact, and eval-driven feedback that changes the product and model roadmap" (OpenAI, FDE – San Francisco; T4). Read that list as a sequence, from finding the problem all the way to running the system. Not one of the three success criteria is "shipped a feature" or "closed a deal"; all three only hold once the customer is actually using the system.

Anthropic's Staff-level FDE posting, published on October 14, 2026, says the same thing in one sentence: "Customers bring you in to lead, not just implement: you decide what should be built, then make sure it gets built." The same posting requires the FDE to "deliver production systems that customers can operate themselves" and to "hand over systems that customers can run" (Anthropic, Staff Forward Deployed Engineer; T4). That gives you both ends of the job. At the front end, the FDE decides what to build. At the back end, the customer can run it without the FDE.

The most useful sentence in these postings is a negative one. OpenAI's healthcare FDE posting, published on August 6, 2026, requires the hire to "own the technical solution end-to-end, from customer discovery and workflow scoping, through architecture, hands-on implementation, evals, production deployment, adoption, and handoff." It then adds: "This role owns the technical solution; it does not require ownership of commercial or executive relationships" (OpenAI, FDE Healthcare – Seattle; T4).

This chapter uses that sentence to draw its line. Owning the outcome is not the same as owning the customer relationship. Account managers own the commercial relationship: contracts, renewals, and pricing. The FDE owns whether the thing works inside the customer's business. The two meet at the sponsor; they can be held by different people, and the FDE is no less an FDE for it. What the FDE cannot hand off is any stage between the problem and the running system.

So, in this book's terms, the definition is as follows. An FDE owns the customer's outcome across the full arc of discovery, scoping, building, launch, and handoff, and writes code as the means of achieving it. Remove the front end, and you get someone who builds to order. Remove the back end, and you get someone who leaves before the thing works. Remove the code, and you get a consultant. These are all real jobs, often well paid. But none of them is this job.

Five stages plus a loop: what "owning" means at each stage

"Owning" is the most abused word in job postings, so pin it down. At each stage, owning means holding a decision, not attending the meeting where someone else makes it.

  • Discovery. You decide what the problem is. The customer's request is a hypothesis, and the FDE is the person who tests it against the real workflow. If the problem arrives at your desk already decided, written on a ticket, you do not own discovery. For how to do it, see the "Discovery" chapter.
  • Scope. You agree with the signatory on the success metric, its threshold, and the "not now" list. If the number is set by someone else and you are asked to hit it, you do not own scope. See "Scoping and the First Deliverable".
  • Build. You write production code in the customer's environment and make the architectural decisions. Designing it and handing it to a partner or the customer's team to build is a different job, covered below.
  • Launch. You stay until it runs on real traffic against the agreed threshold, has passed security review, and has passed the eval ramp. Leaving at the demo is the easiest way to keep the title while no longer owning the outcome.
  • Handoff. You decide when the customer's team can run it without you, and you have to prove it. "Launch and Handoff" calls this an acceptance test passed by the customer's operators in your absence.

Then there is the loop. What the FDE learns in the field flows back into the product. OpenAI requires its FDEs to "distill proven patterns into tools, playbooks, or building blocks that others can use" (San Francisco posting, T4). Decagon's posting for Head of Strategic Deployment Engineering, published on July 31, 2026, makes the role responsible for "deciding what Decagon should build, delivering it, and productizing it so that every customer benefits, not just this one" (Decagon; T4). This loop is not a sixth stage of delivery. It is what makes the delivery worth the vendor's money, and the "Feeding Back into the Product" chapter argues that it is precisely what separates the FDE from the outsourced engineer. Keep it on the list.

Why insist on writing code? Because code is where decisions get proven right or wrong. A consultant can recommend an advice-only (non-executing) route, a deterministic rule outside the model, and a read-only identity. The FDE has to make those choices survive the customer's data, infrastructure, and reviewers, and in the process find out which choices are wrong. Sarah Khalid, Director of FDEs at Salesforce, calls writing code the "ticket to entry," and her VP, Ruth Hickin, describes the FDE as "a combination of hacker, customer value, and professional services person" (Salesforce, March 2026; T4, a company describing its own team). Code does not define the role, but without it you do not get on the field. Even OpenAI's San Francisco posting is less absolute than its overview: one line requires the FDE to "build full-stack systems," while another says "get hands-on in the code when progress or clarity depends on it." The test is not how many hours you spend writing code, but whether, at the critical moments, the production code is written by you or by someone else following your design.

One claim has spread further than any other: that the FDE is a "startup CTO." Palantir's Forward Deployed Software Engineer posting says: "As an FDSE, your role is similar to that of a startup CTO: you will work on small teams with almost no oversight, owning the execution of high-stakes projects end-to-end" (Palantir, FDSE – US Government; T4). Pragmatic Engineer quoted a version of it (Orosz, August 2025; T3), and Decagon's posting has its lead operate "like a founding CTO for every deal." Treat this claim the way this book treats echo numbers: a company's self-description, repeated until it sounds like a finding. It captures the job's span of ownership well, but it does little to guide your day-to-day. A startup CTO chooses the market. The FDE works inside someone else's company, under someone else's constraints, and plans to leave.

One title, several jobs

The title alone can no longer tell you what kind of job you are looking at. There are three lines of evidence for this, but each is weaker than it appears.

Job postings fall into three types. Bloomberry analyzed about 1,000 FDE job postings, with data provided by the recruiting-data firm Revealera, and sorted them into three categories (Bloomberry, November 2025, updated January 2026; T2):

  • "Builder FDEs," 60%: software engineers embedded with customers who build, deploy, and maintain systems in production, spending "70% to 90% of their time writing code."
  • "Sales Engineer+," 30%: "a Solutions Engineer or Sales Engineer role under a different name," which hands off work to an implementation team and is flagged as "carrying a sales quota" (the article is inconsistent on this point, saying elsewhere that none of these roles carry a sales quota).
  • "Internal tool builders," 10%: "a GTM engineer or RevOps role that should not be called 'Forward Deployed Engineer' at all."

This conclusion should be treated with caution: it is one analyst's own classification, the article does not state its classification rules, there was no second reviewer, and no consistency check was performed. The taxonomy is plausible, but it is not a measurement. What it can really establish is this: among roles carrying this title, a substantial share drops the building, the go-live phase, or the external customer entirely.

The same company uses "engineer" for different jobs. Anthropic's "Applied AI Engineer (Enterprise)" posting, published in London on September 28, 2026, requires taking enterprise customers "from the first conversation, through technical evaluation, value validation, and procurement, all the way to the decision to build." It continues: "Once they have made that decision, you will help their systems integration partner get ready to deliver, agree on the target architecture and what good looks like, and stay closely involved as the customer's technical advisor on the Claude side throughout the deployment, while the partner does the building" (Anthropic, Applied AI Engineer (Enterprise); T4). This is an honest description of a different job: by this chapter's test, the building belongs to someone else. The problem lies with postings that blur the boundary, not with postings like this one that draw it clearly.

Job families tell you what the employer thinks it is buying. The Chinese market makes this unmistakable, because large employers place this role in different places. Ifanr's APPSO column (republished by 36Kr on August 29, 2026) reported that Tencent's three Forward Deployed Engineer (FDE) roles sit under the product job family and require three to five years of experience. ByteDance names the role "AI FDE Engineer," with no Chinese translation, places it under R&D, and spans Feishu, Volcano Engine, and data platforms. But ByteDance's FDE internship role sits under "Sales — Sales Support" (Ifanr/APPSO, via 36Kr; T3, a journalist's reading of job boards). One title, three job families, sometimes within the same company. To judge whose metrics you will be measured by, the department a posting belongs to is a more reliable signal than the title. Chinese compensation and the broader market situation are covered in Part IV, the chapter on where FDEs work.

Employers also use other titles for the same job: Applied AI Engineer, agent deployment engineer, Deployment Strategist, Forward Deployed Financial Engineer. Some companies give a separate name to the non-coding counterpart role: on Palantir's careers page, checked October 17, 2026, Deployment Strategist roles appear alongside Forward Deployed Software Engineer roles. The error runs in both directions. Some roles with the FDE title are not FDE work, and some FDE work does not carry that name. Searching job boards by title will mislead you in both directions.

None of this tells you how people in these roles actually spend their weeks. No study has followed FDEs through successive deliveries and recorded which phases they own. The available evidence is only what employers have written down: enough to show that titles are unreliable, not enough to show how often a job matches its posting.

Adjacent roles, viewed by what they own

The clearest way to see the FDE is to set it against the roles on either side of it. Alan Descoins, CEO of the consulting firm Tryolabs, offers a two-sentence test. "A consultant does one round of requirements discovery, hands you a strategy, and then leaves before a single line of code is written." "An outsourced engineer builds strictly to the ticket, but will not tell you the ticket is wrong, and will not stay to see whether anyone uses it" (Tryolabs, June 2026; T4, a company describing its own field). The consultant owns the front end, not the back end. The outsourced engineer owns the build, not the front end, and leaves before the back end. The FDE must own both.

The comparison table below extends this test to the five roles most often confused with the FDE. It is this book's synthesis based on the postings and sources cited in this chapter, not a measured finding. Read each row as the typical shape of that job. Any specific posting may differ, which is exactly what the checklist in Section 1.7 is for.

To use this table to read a job posting, find the last phase to which the posting assigns the hire's responsibilities, and the verbs it uses there. If responsibilities stop at "technical win" or signing, that is a Sales Engineer's job. If they stop at "architecture" or "advising," that is an Architect's job. If they stop at a recommendation or a roadmap, that is a consultant's job. If the system never leaves the employer's own product, that is an Applied AI Engineer's job. Only postings whose responsibilities run all the way through "go-live" to "handover" describe the first row.

Two things follow from this table. First, every adjacent role is legitimate work. The FDE is not more senior; it carries accountability for one thing no other role owns: whether the system works inside the customer's business after the vendor leaves.

Second, the Applied AI Engineer is closest to the FDE in skills and farthest from it in situation. The technical work is nearly identical. For that half of the job, this wiki's agent evals page already covers it. What the Applied AI Engineer does not have to do is find problems inside another company's politics, work on infrastructure they do not control, and design their own exit. Those three things are what most of this book is about.

The skeptics are partly right

There are three serious criticisms of this role. The ownership test can account for each of them rather than dismissing them.

"This is just AI engineering with a new label." Paden Gayle, whose O'Reilly book on the role is in early-release form, argues that "much of what is labeled FDE is really AI engineering under a different name." He draws the boundary much as this chapter does: in his description, "an engineer owns requirements discovery, scoping, building, deployment, validation, and the customer relationship" (O'Reilly live event page; T4, an author describing his own course). He is right. Most of what circulates under the FDE name teaches the build, which is the one phase the FDE shares with all AI engineers; this is exactly why this book links the technical stack to the wiki and spends its pages on either side of the build. There is one point of disagreement: Gayle places the customer relationship inside the role. OpenAI's healthcare posting explicitly excludes commercial relationships. This book sides with OpenAI: the FDE must master the working relationship with the sponsor, but the deal itself belongs to sales.

"This is just IT outsourcing with a nicer title." In March 2026, Gergely Orosz reported that he had spoken with engineers who took FDE roles and came away disappointed. One of them said: "This FDE job is classic IT outsourcing thinking," and "I quit after four weeks." Orosz's summary of such complaints was that these FDEs did little "software engineering" beyond integrating what product teams had already built, and that the work "doesn't feed back into the platform" (The Pragmatic Engineer, March 2026; T4, two anecdotes relayed via a newsletter). That's two people, not a survey. But the complaint is precise, and the ownership test predicts it exactly. A role with no product loop and no say in the problem is an outsourced engineer's job. What these engineers were disappointed by was not the FDE job; what they got was a job stripped of the stages that make it an FDE job. Orosz's earlier article had already drawn the line in the same place: "The distinction comes down to the fact that FDEs work with customers while also contributing to the products the company sells" (Pragmatic Engineer, August 2025; T3).

The same article, via The Wall Street Journal, quoted Patrick Kellenberger of Betts Recruiting: "Everyone wants them, but maybe only 10% of people in the market want to do this role" (T4, a recruiter's estimate, twice relayed). Treat the 10% as an impression, not a number. But the argument holds: many engineers who could do the job don't want the travel, the uncertainty, or the customer-facing work, and they are right to say no.

"It's sliding into consulting." In 2026, OpenAI and Anthropic each set up a separate deployment company backed by private equity money. The least charitable reading is: services revenue was too tempting, so the vendors built consulting firms with better branding. The ownership test offers a narrower, verifiable reading. A deployment company can still do FDE-style delivery, provided the on-site work keeps feeding back into a product. Once it stops, the engineers inside are consultants who write code, and the disappointed hires described above are describing exactly what that job feels like. "Origins and economics" gives the dated facts and both readings. For job seekers, the practical takeaway is short: if the offer comes from a deployment company, line 6 of the checklist below is the first line to ask about.

Where the skeptics go wrong is in the conclusion some draw from this: that the role is hype and will eventually collapse back into its adjacent roles. The distance between a capable model and a customer outcome is real, made up of misread problems, unreachable data, unplanned reviews, unagreed numbers, and unowned systems, and someone has to own it. What the skeptics demonstrate is that many jobs carrying this title do not take on that ownership. They do not demonstrate that the distance doesn't exist.

A synthetic case: who drops what

Part II follows one delivery, at a regional insurance company, from the first sales call through the retrospective. Below, that delivery is replayed into the hands of each adjacent role, to see what each kind of dropping costs.

What if each adjacent role had handled this insurance company? Sales promised a three-month free proof of concept for "a customer service chatbot." A sales engineer would make the chatbot demo look great, move on to the next deal after signing, and no one would discover that the need behind the request was actually "claims are too slow." A consultant might discover that: adjusters spend roughly a third to half of each day sorting claims documents. The insurer would get a strategy report recommending document triage, but no system. An outsourced engineer, handed that report as a ticket, would build a triage feature that writes directly into the claims system, because no one told them that write-back is the slowest door in the building and that claimant-uploaded documents are an injection path. A solutions architect would draw the correct advisory-only (non-executing) design and hand it to an integration partner, and in the weeks when accuracy sat in the low 80s, below threshold, no one would be accountable. What actually happened in Part II: one FDE, plus a part-time lead and an account manager, owned all of it. Together, the FDE and the lead converted the free proof of concept into a 30-day paid pilot with a single KPI (claims cycle time). The FDE then found the real problem on site, scoped it to auto insurance first-notice-of-loss triage, built it in advisory-only (non-executing) form inside the insurer's environment, walked it through the eval ramp, and ran a handover drill in which the insurer's operations team restored the system using only the runbook. The pattern this dramatizes is Tryolabs' warning that when "requirements gathering, building, and adoption are ultimately owned by three different people," "the tool sits unused," and OpenAI's writing of the full sequence from requirements gathering to handover into a single job.

The FDE is not a one-person show. The account manager owns the contract, the lead negotiates terms, and the claims director owns the threshold. Owning the outcome does not mean doing every task. It means that between the problem and the running system, no stage is unowned. Every chapter of Part II, from "Before the delivery starts" to "When the delivery fails", is about a place that would otherwise fall through.

The template: "Is this an FDE job?"

Run a real job posting through it before you apply, and again when you get an offer. It reads the posting the way this chapter does: by stage, with each answer quoting the original text. Each line is marked Y (the posting explicitly says so), N (the posting says the opposite), or ? (the posting doesn't mention it). A "?" is not disqualifying. It is a question to ask in the first-round interview.

IS THIS AN FDE JOB?                 post: ____________  date: ______
Quote the post for every answer. Y = says so, N = says not, ? = silent

OWNERSHIP, DISCOVERY TO HANDOFF                          Y / N / ?
 1 Discovery: does the role find the problem, or get it   [ ]
   handed over on a ticket?  quote: ________________________
 2 Scope: does it agree scope and success criteria with   [ ]
   the customer?             quote: ________________________
 3 Build: does it write production code itself, or        [ ]
   design for a partner to build?  quote: __________________
 4 Production: does it stay until the system runs on      [ ]
   real traffic?             quote: ________________________
 5 Handoff: is "the customer runs it without you" named   [ ]
   as the end state?         quote: ________________________
 6 Loop: does field work feed the product, by a named     [ ]
   channel?                  quote: ________________________

SIGNALS THAT SHIFT THE READING
 7 Pay shape: base, or a base/variable split? ____________
 8 Team or job family: product / R&D / GTM / sales? _______
 9 Code: required, or "a plus"? __________________________
10 Who else is named alongside (AE, CSM, partner, PM)? ____

READING (take the first rule that matches)
 Mostly ? ............................ ask lines 1, 3 and 5 first
 N on 4-5 and a variable split ....... sales engineer, rebranded
 N on 3, or line 9 says "a plus" ..... strategist or architect:
                                       owns the problem, not the code
 N on 1-2 (problem arrives decided) .. contractor or delivery engineer
 N on 6 .............................. services; ask what happens to
                                       what you learn in the field
 1-6 all Y and code required ......... an FDE job, whatever the title

IF A LINE IS ?, ASK
 1 "Who decided what to build on your last engagement?"
 3 "Whose name is on the production commits?"
 4 "Where were you when it went live, and for how long?"
 5 "Who was running it three months after you left?"
 6 "Name one product change that came from the field."

Harvey's posting lists target total compensation of $254,600–$350,000, split "75/25," plus equity; the posting does not specify how the variable portion is paid out. For comparison, OpenAI's San Francisco FDE posting and its Seattle healthcare FDE posting each list $185,000–$300,000 plus equity, with travel "up to 50%." All three postings were read from the employers' own job-page data sources on October 17, 2026.

This verdict is not a criticism. A former dealmaking professional who builds due diligence workflows for an M&A fund inside Harvey's product, and takes that fund from pilot to daily use, is owning a real outcome. What the checklist reveals is the means. Code is a "plus," so this role sits in the same position as Palantir's Deployment Strategist: owning the problem and adoption, standing next to the engineers rather than being one of them. If you want to write production systems, this posting is not for you. If you are a dealmaking professional who wants to turn deal work into product, it may be exactly right. The checklist doesn't make the choice for you; it just doesn't let the title make it for you. Positioning yourself against the four types of behavior this job values is the subject of "The skills map".

Two cautions when using it. First, a job posting is an expectation, not a contract. Even if the paper says Y, still ask the corresponding questions in the interview, because the answer about the last real delivery is better evidence than the posting. Ask the person who would be your lead, not the recruiter: the lead was there. Second, a posting that is mostly "?" usually means no one has thought it through yet. That gives you room to shape the job, and it also means it could slide toward whichever adjacent role the quarter's metrics favor.

Ask one question in every FDE interview: "Who is running the thing you last shipped, three months after you left?" If the answer is "the customer's team," the role owns outcomes. If it's "us," it's still service delivery. If it's "nobody can say for sure," it never made it to production. The title won't tell you which of the three you're joining. This answer will.
