The Mom Test Reading Notes: How Forward Deployed Engineers Should Conduct Customer Interviews

Original title: The Mom Test 读书笔记：FDE 怎样做客户访谈

FDE Times · 2026-10-08 · 4 min read

Summary

This reading-notes article applies Rob Fitzpatrick's The Mom Test to how FDEs should run customer discovery interviews. It argues that compliments are bad data: engineers should ask about specific past events rather than opinions about future usage, and should treat vague answers, future promises, and hypotheticals as fluff. The piece contrasts bad and good question examples, warns that pitching turns customers into polite reviewers, and stresses that the only real failure is leaving without asking for a concrete commitment, such as data access, a paired engineer, or a scheduled pilot. It recommends the book for engineers with two to eight years of experience and suggests reading it in three passes.

Key points

  • The Mom Test's three rules: discuss their life, ask about past specifics, talk less.
  • Compliments are fool's gold; only concrete commitments validate demand.
  • Pitching early turns customers from storytellers into polite reviewers.
  • A clear rejection is a win; the only failure is asking for nothing.
  • Best for engineers with two to eight years of experience facing customers.

Key Takeaways

  • The Mom Test by Rob Fitzpatrick is a short, practical handbook that offers no new theory—only guidance on how to talk to customers when everyone is lying to you.
  • Three rules: talk about their life, not your idea; ask about specific things that happened in the past; talk less, listen more.
  • Praise is bad data. Only concrete commitments, and giving customers a clear chance to say "no," can prove that customers truly need what you're building.

Walking out of a customer meeting with a notebook full of "this idea is amazing" and "we'd definitely use this"—in Rob Fitzpatrick's view, that's not success, it's bad data. Michael Lynch, in his book review, quotes Fitzpatrick: praise is like fool's gold—shiny, distracting, and worthless if you want to learn something from customers.

For Forward Deployed Engineers (FDEs), this cuts straight to the hardest part of the job. Before deployment, you sit down with customers to figure out what they truly need; everything you build afterward rests on those answers. If the answers are just polite praise, your deployment is built on sand.

A Book That Refuses to Do Theory

The subtitle of The Mom Test states its goal plainly: how to talk to customers and determine whether your business idea is sound when everyone is lying to you. The official site, momtestbook.com, positions it as a short, practical guide to learning more from customer conversations and selling more, and bluntly states that the world doesn't need another framework or theory.

That's exactly why it suits engineers. It doesn't ask you to learn a new model—only to fix the way you ask questions. Yevgeniy Brikman summarizes Fitzpatrick's rules into three: talk about their life, not your idea; ask about specific things that happened in the past, not generalities or opinions about the future; talk less, listen more.

For FDEs, the second rule is the most valuable. Ask "Do you think this feature would be useful?" and everyone nods, because nodding costs nothing. Ask "Last time you hit this situation, how did you handle it and how long did it take?" and the customer has to tell a real story. Only in real stories do you find workarounds, dirty data, and the person who is actually responsible.

Questions That Only Earn Praise

  • Do you think a dashboard with automated alerts would help your team?
  • If you had this tool, would you use it every day?
  • What did you think of the demo just now?

Questions That Uncover the Truth

  • Last time the data pipeline broke, how did you find out, and who fixed it?
  • What did you do last week to keep it from happening again?
  • Which tools have you already tried, and why did you stop using them?

The Moment You Pitch, Customers Stop Talking

Fitzpatrick points out a simple psychological mechanism: the moment you start talking about your idea, the customer stops talking about their problems. For FDEs, this temptation arrives early. You know the product, you spot the solution at a glance, and you can't resist demoing it. But every extra minute of demoing is another minute in which the customer shifts from storyteller to reviewer—and polite reviewers only give praise.

The book calls these empty responses "fluff" and divides them into three types: generalities, promises about the future, and "might" hypotheticals. For FDEs, recognizing these three types of fluff is central to customer discovery and should be practiced as a listening skill. When a customer says "we run into this problem all the time," you don't yet have data; only when you follow up with "when was the last time?" does the data arrive.

The Only Measure: Are They Willing to Risk Saying "No"?

The hardest-hitting part of the book is about how to end a conversation. Sachin Rekhi, summarizing Fitzpatrick's view, emphasizes that the next step must be asking for a very specific commitment; only after you've given a potential customer a clear chance to turn you down do they count as a genuine sales lead. Lynch adds a line worth taping to your monitor: the only way to fail is to not ask for any commitment.

Read closely, this flips the usual intuition. If the only failure is not asking, then an explicit rejection isn't a failure—it's something you can take away. The scarier outcome is leaving with a smile and nothing you can verify.

In an FDE context, a commitment can be access to real data, a customer assigning an engineer to work alongside you, or a pilot day put on the calendar.

If the customer praises you enthusiastically but dodges every one of these requests, you've already gotten the most important information from the meeting.

Who Should Read It, and in What Order

This book suits engineers with two to eight years of experience who are shifting from purely taking tickets to facing customers directly. If you're preparing for an FDE interview, read it before practicing case questions: when a question describes a customer who wants X, your first reaction should be to ask "what happened that made them want X," not to immediately start designing X.

The book is short, and the best way to use it is three passes, each with a different purpose. First pass: read it through, just to absorb the three rules Brikman summarized. Second pass: reopen the chapter on fluff with notes from your most recent requirements interview at hand, and mark every customer response that falls into one of the three fluff categories; you'll be surprised how many you mark.

The third pass isn't really reading. Before every formal call, return to the chapter on commitments and decide in advance what commitment you'll ask the customer for.

Don't put "read The Mom Test" on your resume. Write about a project: after discovering that the customer's initial requirement was just fluff, you changed what you were building.

The next time a customer praises you, take it as a signal that you've been talking too much, then ask them: what happened last Tuesday?
