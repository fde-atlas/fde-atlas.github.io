What Does an FDE Do? A Detailed Explanation in 6 Phases

Original title: FDEの仕事内容とは？6フェーズで具体的に解説

株式会社Sai · 2026-07-25 · 8 min read

Summary

This article by Japanese company Sai breaks down a Forward Deployed Engineer (FDE) project into six phases: observation, structuring the problem, minimal implementation, on-site testing, adoption, and handover. It argues FDEs start by embedding in the workplace rather than waiting for requirements documents, since described work always diverges from actual work. The first target should be a small but genuinely painful problem, because early success builds trust for larger changes. In practice, phases 3–5 loop repeatedly rather than proceeding linearly like waterfall. The author maps this framework to job postings from Palantir, OpenAI (Tokyo), and Anthropic's plan with DXC to train tens of thousands of Claude-certified FDEs, and closes with Sai's own deliverables per phase.

Key points

  • FDE projects follow six phases: observe, structure, build minimally, test on-site, embed, hand over.
  • Phases 3–5 are iterated in small loops, not run as a linear waterfall process.
  • First targets should be small but painful problems; early wins create trust for bigger changes.
  • Fast fixes of small frictions during on-site testing prevent 'delivered but unused' systems.
  • Adoption goals must be measurable; handover means the client team can maintain and improve alone.

Key Takeaways

  • An FDE project proceeds through six phases: "observe → structure the problems → minimal implementation → on-site testing → adoption → handover," and in practice phases 3 through 5 are cycled through many times in small iterations.
  • FDEs don't wait for a requirements document; they start with on-site observation. This is because the work described in interviews and the actual work are always misaligned.
  • It is effective to narrow the initial target to "a small but reliably painful problem." The first success creates trust, and trust enables the next change.
  • During on-site testing, small frictions such as "input is cumbersome" are discovered on the spot and fixed at short intervals. This rapid improvement loop is the deciding factor between a system that gets used and one that doesn't.
  • For adoption, set a measurable goal such as "processing time is cut in half," and for handover, work on improvements together with internal staff to build a self-sustaining state before graduating.

"I understand that FDEs embed themselves in the field and build things to completion. But what do they actually do?" — This article explains how an FDE (Forward Deployed Engineer) project actually progresses, divided into six phases.

For companies considering an engagement, this serves as a "preview of what will happen"; for engineers interested in the FDE way of working, it serves as "the reality of the job."

Phase 1: Observation — It All Starts with "Being" in the Field

The first day of the project starts not in a meeting room for interviews, but in the field. Accompany sales staff. Watch administrative work being done next to you. Look at the actual data. Get added to Slack channels and email threads.

This is because the work described in interviews and the actual work are always misaligned. Compared to the transcription task the person described as "this is really tough," the double-checking they do unconsciously consumes far more time — observation reveals things like this.

The deliverables of this phase are a map of the workflow and a "map of pain points." It takes anywhere from a few days to about two weeks.

Phase 2: Structuring the Problems — Agreeing on "the Problems to Solve"

The problems identified through observation are organized by impact × difficulty of solving, and where to start is agreed upon with management and the field.

What matters here is narrowing the initial target to "a small but reliably painful problem." Going straight for something big like overhauling a core system falls into the typical pattern of stalling at the PoC stage. The first success creates trust, and trust enables the next change — this order cannot be changed.

Phase 3: Minimal Implementation — Delivering Something "That Works" in a Few Weeks

For the agreed-upon problem, a minimal working system is built in a few weeks. Not something perfect, but the smallest unit that lets the field experience "this makes things easier."

Supporting this speed is a development style premised on generative AI and AI agents. Building business tools that once took months can now be done in weeks. The fact that FDEs can now succeed with small teams is inseparable from the practical application of AI agents.

Phase 4: On-Site Testing — Eliminating the Reasons It Doesn't Get Used

What was built is placed in the field and people actually use it. This is where FDEs show their true worth.

The reasons a system doesn't get used are not necessarily a lack of features. "Input is cumbersome," "I'm more used to the Excel we've always used," "I didn't even know it existed" — an FDE on site discovers these small frictions on the spot and fixes them at short intervals. This rapid improvement loop is the deciding factor between a delivered system that gets used and one that doesn't.

Phase 5: Adoption — Staying Until It Becomes Part of the Workflow

Stay until the system is integrated into daily work and people "can't go back to life without it." Preparing documentation, sharing usage in morning meetings, providing individual support to those not using it well. It's unglamorous, but it's the last mile that turns investment into results.

The definition of adoption is decided at the start of the project. By setting a measurable goal, such as "processing time for the target work is cut in half" or "everyone uses it three or more times a week," a vague sense of "done" is prevented.

Phase 6: Handover — Building a Self-Sustaining State, Then Moving On

Finally, a state is created in which the customer's team alone can run operations and make improvements. This means not just leaving documentation behind, but carrying out improvements together with internal staff so they gain the experience of "being able to fix things themselves."

The goal of FDE support is not to "stay forever." They graduate by connecting to internalization (building a self-sustaining organization), and then team up again on harder next problems or new themes — this cycle is the healthy form.

These Six Phases Overlap with Job Descriptions at Overseas AI Companies

The six-phase breakdown is not a framework SAI invented on its own. Palantir, the US company that created the FDE role, describes the role on its careers page as one it pioneered itself, a way of working in which engineers are assigned directly to customers to tackle their most pressing problems. The job posting describes the role as working alongside customers to quickly understand problems and design and build solutions (Palantir official job posting, Forward Deployed Software Engineer, verified September 8, 2026).

OpenAI's FDE job posting for its Tokyo office describes the scope of responsibility as spanning from problem discovery to assessing technical requirements, system design, building, and production deployment. The measures of success it cites are adoption into production operations and business impact (OpenAI official job posting, Forward Deployed Engineer - Tokyo, verified September 8, 2026). This is almost exactly the same order as phases 1 through 5 in this article.

The spread of the role is not limited to the inside of AI companies. In an announcement on June 11, 2026, Anthropic stated that IT services giant DXC will train tens of thousands of Claude-certified FDEs. The same announcement describes FDEs as "engineers embedded directly inside customer organizations" (Anthropic official announcement, June 11, 2026). Why this role is growing worldwide is covered in Why Are FDEs Increasing?

Note that the job postings above change in content depending on hiring status. The dates are those verified at the time this article was written.

Frequently Asked Questions About FDE Project Progression

Q. How does this differ from waterfall development?

Hearing "6 phases" makes it sound like a linear process, but in practice you loop through phases 3 to 5 many times in small cycles. Rather than fixing all requirements before building (waterfall), the idea is to move closer to the right answer by alternating between observation and implementation. That's why the project can move forward even when requirements are vague or change along the way.

Q. How long does each phase take?

It depends on the scope of the target operations and the number of people involved. As a rough guide, observation takes from a few days to two weeks, and minimal implementation takes a few weeks. Rather than fixing the overall timeline upfront, it's easier to judge progress if you decide at the start how adoption in phase 5 will be measured.

Q. Can we ask you to start from phase 1 even without a requirements document?

Yes. FDE-style support starts with identifying "what should be built," so not having a requirements document is one of the assumptions. Conversely, for large-scale development where requirements are fully fixed, or for problems that off-the-shelf SaaS can solve, traditional contracting is a better fit.

Q. Can the six phases be run fully remotely?

Yes. We can support on-site presence, frequent visits, or fully remote arrangements. However, phase 1, which centers on observing operations, involves many tasks you can only notice by watching alongside the team, so we recommend an approach that combines site visits.

Q. Can we start with just one operation in one department?

Yes. Starting with the challenges of a single operation and a single team, then expanding the scope while checking results, is the approach least likely to fail. The reason for narrowing the initial target is as described in phase 2: the first success makes the next change easier to drive.

SAI's perspective

If you read the 6 phases as a schedule, phases 1 and 2 look like "a period where nothing has been built yet." That's exactly why you need to decide in advance what you will receive from these two phases.

In our FDE-accompanied development at SAI Inc., we define the deliverables of each phase as follows.

  • Observation and structuring of issues: bottlenecks identified by examining on-site operations and data, plus agreement on which to solve first
  • Minimal implementation: one of a business system, embedding generative AI or AI agents into operations, or a data platform/dashboard—something usable on-site within a few weeks
  • On-site testing and adoption: measurement results based on adoption metrics decided at the start, such as processing time and usage rate
  • Handover: a track record of improvements built together with your team members, plus documentation and a structure that lets operations run on your own

For marketing automation for an advertising agency, we started from observation and went all the way to systematizing production and operations work; for a BCP support company case, we focused support on the adoption and handover phases.
