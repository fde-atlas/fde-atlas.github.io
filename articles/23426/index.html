How to prepare for an FDE interview, and are paid courses worth it?

Original title: FDE 面试怎么准备，付费课程值不值

FDE Times · 2026-11-17 · 4 min read

Summary

This article compares three FDE interview prep products: Educative's subscription course library, Exponent's self-study plus coaching model, and Interview Kickstart's 23-week bootcamp. It argues most knowledge and frameworks can be self-taught, and the only part that truly requires another person is the customer simulation round, where an interviewer plays a difficult enterprise buyer. The author recommends free practice first—having a colleague role-play a buyer for timed, recorded sessions—then paying only for specific weaknesses, noting FDE candidates face less competition than course ads suggest.

Key points

  • FDE job postings on Indeed grew more than tenfold from 2024 to 2025.
  • Educative, Exponent, and Interview Kickstart sell different products: course library, coaching, and a 23-week bootcamp.
  • Interview Kickstart demands 10-14 hours weekly, totaling roughly 230-322 hours.
  • The customer simulation round needs a real person pushing back, which a colleague can provide for free.
  • Practice three rounds with real people before paying, then buy only for your weakest gap.

Courses often run 138 lessons over 23 weeks, but what's truly scarce is a real person willing to play a difficult customer—and you don't necessarily need to pay a coach for that.

Key takeaways

  • Educative, Exponent, and Interview Kickstart sell three different things: a course library, one-on-one coaching, and a 23-week bootcamp.
  • Most of the knowledge and answer frameworks can be self-taught, but the customer simulation round requires a real person who keeps pushing back on you.
  • Before buying a coach, practice with a friend against the clock, and only pay for the part you still can't do yourself.

One FDE course on Educative has 17 modules and 138 lessons, plus 5 AI mock interviews scored against a rubric. Interview Kickstart's offering runs for 23 weeks.

Exponent first sells a self-paced course, then invites you to book a coach. Three companies with vastly different levels of commitment, all targeting the same buyers.

The reason isn't hard to understand. According to The Pragmatic Engineer, FDE job postings on Indeed grew more than tenfold in 2025 compared to 2024. When demand rises that fast, course sellers naturally follow.

Still, if you plan to spend money, ask a narrower question: which part of this course can't you do yourself? The answer is far thinner than the course itself.

Three sellers, three products

Calling them all "interview prep" is misleading, because the three companies sell different things. Educative sells a course library. Its general FDE course is only available to subscribers, priced at $59 per month, and includes targeted preparation for the interview processes at Anthropic, OpenAI, Google, and Palantir.

It also sells a separate Google FDE interview course: 9 modules, 48 lessons, about 12 hours, authored by Educative co-founder and CEO Fahim ul Haq. This course also requires a subscription. So the real cost depends on how many months you study, not the price of a single course.

Exponent, now moved to the aced.io domain, pairs self-paced courses with one-on-one coaching from practitioners at FAANG+ companies. The model is stated plainly: learn with the self-paced course first, then book a coach for live practice. The coaching page doesn't list prices.

Interview Kickstart goes the heaviest: 23 weeks across four phases, taught live in a class format, designed so you can keep your day job while studying. The course page also doesn't list prices, but it does state the workload: about 4-6 hours of live classes per week, plus 6-8 hours of assignments, projects, and self-study.

Do the math. Ten to fourteen hours a week for 23 weeks adds up to roughly 230 to 322 hours. That's before tuition—it's just the time you must invest. For someone with a job, time may be harder to find than money.

No amount of reading replaces someone who pushes back on you

To judge which part is worth paying for, look at the interview itself. According to Educative, the FDE interview process includes a customer simulation round, where the interviewer plays an enterprise buyer. 138 lessons can't train the reaction you need: a buyer who interrupts you mid-sentence to press on data security.

Exponent's blog guide on the problem decomposition round makes the same point. This round lasts 45-60 minutes, and the guide argues that practicing alone can't recreate the ambiguity of a real conversation. You need someone who knows how to make things difficult for you.

Interestingly, the same guide offers a free way to practice: find a friend to play the interviewer, run it for a timed 45-60 minutes, have them keep asking questions, and record the whole thing.

In other words, even the company selling coaching admits the core ingredient is someone who pushes back on you—not necessarily a paid coach.

Run your own mock interview

Suppose you find a colleague who has worked with customers. The scenario: a logistics company wants to use an LLM to read supplier invoices. Your colleague plays the head of operations and only reveals information when you ask the right questions.

By minute 10, you're already considering a retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) architecture. The "buyer" asks: "If an invoice is wrong, who's responsible?" If you get stuck, the recording will capture it clearly. That's the most valuable data from the entire exercise, and it costs nothing.

The practice should end with a proposal. Interview Kickstart's free blog offers a standard specific enough to self-assess against: a good proposal must be clear enough to evaluate and small enough to deliver.

In the invoice example, "automate the entire finance function" fails; "extract five fields from the invoices of the top three suppliers and measure accuracy on 200 samples" passes.

So what's worth paying for?

Map each company's product to the layers of practice, and the boundaries become quite clear.

Practice layerWho sells itHow to do it yourself
Knowledge and answer frameworksEducative course library, Exponent self-paced courseFree blogs, technical documentation, writing proposals yourself
Scoring against a rubricEducative's 5 AI mock interviewsWrite your own rubric and review the recordings
A real person pushing backExponent one-on-one coaches, Interview Kickstart live classesA colleague playing the buyer, timed
Learning disciplineInterview Kickstart's 23-week class scheduleSet your own plan, plus a practice partner

The first row barely needs buying. The third row may be worth paying for, but only if you can't find anyone to play a difficult enough customer, or you need someone who has been an interviewer at your target company.

The last row tests you, not the course. If you know you'll quit halfway through self-study, a fixed class schedule may be worth the money. If not, those 230 hours are better invested in a real project with real data.

Don't let the course market set your pace

There's another reason not to rush. The Pragmatic Engineer quotes a recruiter saying that every company wants to hire FDEs, but perhaps only about 10% of people in the market are willing to do the job. With so few candidates, your competition is smaller than course ads make it feel.

That's not an excuse for sloppy preparation. It means what sets you apart is evidence of working with customers, not a certificate for completing 17 modules.

The practical advice: before buying anything, practice the problem decomposition round and the customer simulation round with a real person at least three times. By then you'll know where you're weak, and you can pay precisely for that gap.

Put a short proposal on your resume that a customer or internal team actually adopted, with the metrics and delivery time stated.

Read job postings carefully too. Educative lists the customer simulation round as part of the FDE interview, so if a job description emphasizes direct work with customers, prepare for the role-play round. For that round you need someone to challenge you, and that person can easily be the colleague sitting next to you.

As long as job postings keep growing, the course market will keep expanding. But what determines success in the customer simulation round is still how many times you've been pressed with questions, not how much money you spent.
