AI agent evals with a baseline: Intercom Fin example

FDE Times · 2026-10-24 · 6 min read

Summary

The article starts from Intercom's job ad for a Forward Deployed Software Engineer in Dublin, which promises measurable business outcomes for its customer-service AI agent Fin but names no target. The author argues that defining what to measure therefore falls to the FDE. Public figures—51% resolution out of the box (Anthropic case study) and 56% average in the first 30 days (AWS)—are averages, not promises to a specific customer. Intercom tests every change to Fin against a baseline, and good forward deployed engineers bring the same habit to each customer they work with.

In brief

  • Intercom's FDE job ad promises measurable business outcomes but names no target, so defining the measurement falls to the FDE.
  • Fin's public figures are 51% out of the box and an average of 56% in the first 30 days, but neither is the number for the customer you are working with.
  • The honest answer is an eval set built from the customer's own conversations, with a baseline and a primary metric fixed before the first run.

Intercom’s job ad for a Forward Deployed Software Engineer in Dublin makes a big promise: to ensure that Fin, the company’s customer-service AI agent, delivers real, measurable business outcomes. Read to the end of the page and you will not find a single target.

That gap can be read as the job’s first test. When nobody says “you must hit this number”, the FDE has to decide what to measure, how to measure it and what to compare it against.

The situation arrives early.

The ad describes working closely with Fin’s strategic customers to understand their business problems and technical requirements, and on a customer site the question you are almost certain to hear is: “For us, what percentage will Fin resolve?” This guide shows how to answer it with an eval set rather than a promise.

The public number is not the customer’s number

There are two public figures to hold on to. Anthropic’s case study says Fin reaches a 51% resolution rate out of the box, before any customisation. AWS’s case study says customers reach an average of 56% within their first 30 days.

The figures differ because they were measured at different points, by different sources. Both are averages, so neither tells you anything about the retailer or insurer sitting across the table.

So if you treat “56%” as a guarantee, a customer whose real number comes in lower will remember that line longer than any demo. The professional answer is: “We will measure it on your own conversations, then report the number.”

How does Intercom measure itself?

The method already exists, and it comes from Intercom itself. According to Anthropic’s case study, every change to Fin, from small tweaks to major updates, is tested rigorously against the baseline running in production. The tests look at several metrics at once: answer accuracy, resolution rate, customer satisfaction and response quality.

Intercom calls this eval-driven delivery, and says it is the same discipline its AI research team uses to build and refine the product. Its Evals product tests Fin’s behaviour with simulated scenarios built from customers’ own conversations.

Intercom’s definition is compact: an Eval is a named group of Simulations, meaning multi-turn test conversations. Pedro Tabacof, an ML scientist working on Fin, writes that if you are serious about AI, you have to be serious about evals.

Example: a 30-case eval for “where is my order?”

Imagine the customer is a retailer, and the agent must answer order questions through an internal API. Before touching the prompt or the code, you do what Tabacof advises for A/B tests: fix a primary metric before you start.

Here the primary metric is the share of conversations resolved without handing over to a human agent. Answer accuracy is a secondary metric that must not fall: if the resolution rate rises while answers get less accurate, the change is rejected.

You take 30 order conversations, label the expected outcome for each, then write a small runner:

import json

# Each line: one multi-turn test conversation + the expected outcome
# {"id": "o-07", "turns": ["Where is my order #A-1029?"], "expect": "resolved"}
CASES = [json.loads(line) for line in open("evals/orders.jsonl")]

def run(agent, cases):
    results = {}
    for c in cases:
        out = agent.converse(c["turns"])
        results[c["id"]] = {"ok": out.outcome == c["expect"],
                            "reason": out.failure_reason}
    return results

def rate(results):
    return sum(r["ok"] for r in results.values()) / len(results)

baseline = json.load(open("evals/baseline.json"))  # the previous run
current = run(agent, CASES)
regressions = [k for k, r in current.items()
               if baseline[k]["ok"] and not r["ok"]]
print(f"{rate(baseline):.0%} -> {rate(current):.0%}, regressions: {regressions}")

The most important line is

regressions

. A higher overall rate can still hide a few cases that used to pass and now fail, and those are what the customer will notice first.

Read the failures before you fix anything

Suppose the first run resolves 18 of 30 cases, or 60%. That result becomes the baseline. The most valuable thing to do now is not to edit the prompt but to read the 12 failures and group them by cause.

In this example, seven fail because users type order numbers like “#A-1029” while the API only accepts “A1029”. Two fail because the API times out, and three ask about something outside the scope of orders.

The largest group shows what to fix first, and the fix is a single line that normalises the order number at the top of the lookup function:

order_id = re.sub(r"[^A-Za-z0-9]", "", raw_order_id)  # "#A-1029" -> "A1029"

The next run reaches 25 of 30, or 83%, and the regression list is empty. The remaining five belong to the other two groups, already sorted into work for the next round.

Doing it again at a real customer

With a real customer, the sequence stays the same. Use their real conversations as the source, just as Intercom’s Evals builds scenarios from customer conversations, and label them with someone on the customer’s side who knows the business, because you cannot know on your own which answer about their refund policy is correct.

Then write the primary and secondary metrics into a document the customer also reads, before the first run. Every change, even a single line of prompt, is rerun against the baseline, as Intercom does with Fin itself.

This order also settles who sets the target. When the customer has agreed the primary metric from the start, the final number is a result both sides recognise, not a score the FDE awarded itself.

Mistakes to avoid

The first mistake is choosing the metric after seeing the results. Running first and then picking the best-looking number to report is the fastest way to lose trust, and it is exactly what the advice to fix the metric up front is meant to prevent.

The second is looking only at resolution rate. Intercom measures accuracy, satisfaction and response quality as well, because an agent that “resolves” a conversation with a wrong answer is worse than one that hands it to a human.

The third is an eval set made up only of easy cases you invented yourself. Cases like “#A-1029” only appear in real conversations, and they are often the largest group of failures.

How does this skill show up in a CV and an interview?

When reading FDE job ads, watch for phrases like “measurable outcomes” that come with no number attached. When you see one, have a concrete story ready about a time you set the measurement yourself: which metric you chose, why, and what happened.

At Staff level, Intercom expects someone who sets direction, owns outcomes and works directly with customers and the Go-To-Market team. An eval set with a baseline lets you report results to the sales team in numbers, rather than in impressions after a demo.

On a CV, a good bullet has three parts: the system you connected, how you measured it, and the before and after numbers. Following the example above: “Built a 30-conversation eval set for an order-lookup agent; self-service resolution rose from 60% to 83% (18→25/30) with no regressions.”

This week’s exercise

Pick a public API with per-user data, such as orders, tickets or appointments, and write a simple agent that calls it. Write 30 test conversations, fix a primary metric, run it once as a baseline and record the cause of every failure.

What you bring to the interview is not the repo. It is the sentence “7 of the 12 failures came from how users typed the order number”, and only someone who has done the measuring can say it.
