Summary

Written by Shohei Tokumitsu, CEO of DeFactory, this guide compares five ways to obtain engineering capacity—SES, IT consulting, SIer, full-time hiring, and FDE—across ten dimensions including value delivered, contract type, cost structure, and typical failure modes. It cites 2026 price ranges (FDE at ¥1.1–1.8M per person-month, averaging ¥1.35M; IT consultants ¥2–3M; FDE-level hires at ¥10–25M annual salary plus fees). A matrix maps eight problem areas to recommended options, with FDE favored for AI adoption and DX. The core argument is to evaluate total cost of ownership, including lead time and opportunity cost, rather than monthly rates. It concludes that combining forms by project phase is standard practice, with FDE often serving as a bridge toward in-house capability.

  • TCO including lead-time opportunity cost matters more than monthly rates.

"We don't have enough development resources," "AI adoption isn't moving forward," "We want to drive DX but don't know what to ask for"—when you face challenges like these, your options for engagement models, both external and internal, can be organized into five categories: SES, IT consulting, SIer, full-time hiring, and FDE.

While all of these may look the same in the sense of "borrowing engineering capabilities," their delivered value, contract types, cost structures, and failure patterns are completely different. Making the wrong choice can mean the contract period passes by without results that justify the cost.

In this article, from the practitioner's perspective of DeFactory, which has provided FDE-type support, we thoroughly compare the five engagement models and explain, end to end, how to choose the one that fits your company's challenges.

Shohei Tokumitsu

Representative Director of DeFactory. Based on his experience leading multiple 0-to-1 new business developments in the software development domain, both in-house and at other companies, and driving business growth, he currently works as an FDE field consultant/delivery lead and product manager (PdM), as well as handling recruiting (FDE, upstream SE, PM, PMO, CPO, CTO, consulting).

1. Why Choosing the Wrong Engagement Model Is So Costly

The reason a poor choice of engagement model is so costly is that it's not just a temporary failure of "picking an unsuitable vendor."

  • Since contract periods run in units of six months to a year, the time lost before noticing a mismatch is significant
  • When switching, onboarding costs for the new vendor are incurred all over again
  • In particular, with SIers and full-time hiring, the psychological hurdles of changing contracts or resigning are high, making course correction slow

In other words, choosing an engagement model is not only the "entry point"—it is a decision that determines the very nature of the project for the following six months to a year. That is precisely why it is worth spending time on this choice before getting started.

3. Detailed Explanation of Each Engagement Model

3-1. FDE (Forward Deployed Engineer)

What it is

A hybrid role that embeds into the client company's operations and handles everything end to end, from problem discovery to data platform construction, AI implementation, and embedding into business operations. For details, see "What is an FDE? Adoption Strategies for Forward Deployed Engineers, the New AI-Era Role, for Enterprises."

Companies it suits

  • Companies whose PoC succeeded but production rollout is stalled
  • Companies that adopted AI tools but haven't embedded them into operations
  • Companies without a CTO or technical lead that need someone they can entrust with technical decisions

Cost structure breakdown

Monthly rates are ¥1.1-1.8 million (average ¥1.35 million). This is the actual average from seven FDE contracts closed from late 2025 through 2026. The breakdown roughly divides into "problem definition and requirements gathering (weighted in months 1-2)," "implementation (months 3-6)," and "embedding support and handover (second half)." Since ramp-up takes 3-4 months, short-term contracts tend to offer poor cost-effectiveness.

Common failure patterns

  • Giving detailed task instructions as if it were an SES arrangement, squandering the FDE's problem-discovery capabilities
  • Depending on a single FDE, so knowledge disappears the moment they leave
  • Proceeding to contract without clearly defined success metrics (KPIs)

(For details: 6 Principles for Evaluating FDE Consultants / 4 Principles for FDE KPI Design *coming soon)

3-2. SES (On-site Dispatch)

What it is

A contract type where engineers are stationed at the client site and compensated for working hours within the instructed scope. Problem definition and support for embedding into operations are out of scope.

Companies it suits

  • Companies whose requirements are already fixed and that want to quickly fill development "headcount"
  • Companies that want engineers to work within their command structure as part of the existing team

Cost structure breakdown

Actual market rate data published in our internal article "SES Engineer Rates and Pass-Through Ratios" (as of 2026, monthly per-person rates).

RoleTypical monthly rate

The above is compensation for working hours, with no variation based on results. While additional costs are unlikely to occur, the client is expected to separately bear the workload involved in requirements definition and issue analysis.

Common failure patterns

  • The client has not prepared the requirements definition of "what should be built" and leaves it entirely to the SES provider, causing the project to stall
  • Consuming working hours becomes the goal itself, and the connection to business outcomes becomes invisible
  • Multiple SES companies and individuals are mixed together, making responsibilities unclear

3-3. IT Consulting

What it is

A role that supports corporate IT strategy and system implementation from the standpoint of analysis and strategic recommendations. Implementation is generally handed off to a separate vendor.

Companies it suits

  • Companies that have not yet determined "where to start" and need recommendations at the business strategy level
  • Companies that want to objectively compare and evaluate multiple options

Cost structure breakdown

¥2,000,000–3,000,000 per month is mainly compensation for the workload involved in research, analysis, and documentation. Once the implementation phase begins, separate engagements with an SIer, SES, or FDE become necessary, so the total cost is the cost of the recommendations plus the implementation cost, which is an important point to note.

Common failure patterns

  • The recommendations and reports become "pie in the sky," and the handoff to a separate vendor in the implementation phase does not go smoothly
  • The strategy is high quality, but it does not go deep enough into on-the-ground operational details, causing rework during implementation

3-4. SIer (System Integrator)

What it is

A company or business model that takes on a client company's system development and implementation in one package, from requirements definition through design, implementation, and operation/maintenance.

Companies it suits

  • Companies that want to entrust large-scale, complex core systems in one package, from requirements definition onward
  • Companies with clear specifications and no expectation of major changes during the development period

Cost structure breakdown

Tens of millions of yen per project. Because these are fixed-price contracts, no additional costs arise as long as the work stays within the specifications agreed upon at the time of contract, but each specification change requires a new estimate and contract amendment.

Common failure patterns

  • Requirements change during the development period, and each change incurs additional costs and contract amendments
  • Multi-layered subcontracting structures make communication with the actual development site slow
  • Post-completion maintenance and modification costs exceed initial expectations

3-5. Full-Time Hiring

What it is

An option in which you hire someone as an employee under an employment contract and build up technical capability and knowledge internally over the long term.

Companies it suits

  • Companies that want to retain a specific technical domain as a core internal capability over the long term
  • Companies whose business direction is already established and for whom the value of a dedicated specialist is clear

Cost structure breakdown

In addition to an annual salary of ¥10,000,000–25,000,000 (for FDE-equivalent talent), if hired through a recruitment agency there is a referral fee of roughly 30–35% of the annual salary, and the productivity loss during onboarding (several months until full productivity) also counts as a real cost.

Common failure patterns

  • Hiring competition is fierce, and it takes six months to over a year from building a candidate pool to making a hire
  • Even after hiring, onboarding takes longer than expected, and the person does not become immediately productive
  • Hiring just one person leaves knowledge concentrated in a single individual, and that knowledge is lost when they leave

5. In-Depth Cost Comparison: The True Cost Seen Through TCO (Total Cost of Ownership)

If you compare only monthly fees, SES appears to be the cheapest. However, what you should confirm before placing an order is not the "monthly fee" but the total cost required to achieve results (TCO: Total Cost of Ownership).

Elements to include in TCO

  • Direct monthly and annual costs
  • Productivity loss during the onboarding period (ramp-up period × man-hours of internal staff involved)
  • Internal man-hours when requirements definition and issue analysis are done in-house
  • Re-onboarding costs when contract switching or additional orders occur
  • (For full-time hires) The cost and duration of the recruiting process itself
  • Opportunity loss caused by lead time before work begins (detailed in 5-1)

5-1. Hidden Costs: Lead Time Before Work Begins and Opportunity Loss

Among TCO components, the most commonly overlooked is the cost generated by "the period from deciding to place an order until problem-solving actually begins." During this period, the issue remains unaddressed, and the corresponding business impact (man-hours that could have been saved, revenue that could have been gained, etc.) continues to be lost. This never appears on an invoice, yet it can effectively be the largest cost factor.

How to Think About Opportunity Loss (Formula Based on Actual Figures)

While it is difficult to calculate opportunity loss precisely, using actual figures published by DeFactory allows you to grasp the "order of magnitude" without making arbitrary assumptions.

Opportunity Loss = Lead Time Until Work Begins (months) × The Solution Cost That Would Have Been Spent During That Period (= FDE actual unit price of 1.1-1.8 million yen/month, average 1.35 million yen)

This approach treats "the cost that would have been invested in personnel to solve the issue" as the lower bound of value lost during the idle period. Furthermore, considering the results published in the "What is an FDE?" article (2.5x work efficiency and 1/3 development lead time), a delayed start means not merely saved costs, but that these efficiency gains themselves are postponed without being realized.

For example, if full-time hiring takes a 9-month lead time (6-9 months of recruiting plus onboarding), applying the FDE actual unit price (average 1.35 million yen/month) as the lower bound of opportunity loss yields roughly 12.15 million yen worth of value-creation opportunities postponed, in addition to direct hiring costs. This is the perspective of "leaving unaddressed for 9 months the value an FDE could have generated, throughout the entire recruiting period," and serves as a rough lower bound for opportunity loss rather than a reliable monetary forecast.

With this in mind, even if "full-time hiring doesn't seem expensive when looking only at annual salary plus recruiting costs," the delay before work begins itself becomes a substantial additional cost. Conversely, engagement types with short lead times, such as FDE or SES, may appear to have high monthly rates, but are often advantageous in TCO terms when opportunity loss is included.

*The above is an approach using the FDE actual unit price as a rough lower bound for opportunity loss; actual business impact varies greatly depending on the project, company, and nature of the issue.

6. Failure Patterns That Occur When You Choose the Wrong Engagement Model

  • Leaving problem definition to SES and stalling progress: Since SES is premised on implementation within the scope of instructions, ordering without a defined "what to build" leads to unfortunate outcomes for both parties
  • IT consulting recommendations not leading to implementation: Because strategy and implementation are handled by separate parties, neglecting handover design leaves only a report behind and the project stalls
  • Costs ballooning with every requirement change after ordering from an SIer: Due to the nature of fixed-price contracts, specification changes directly translate into additional costs. Be cautious about ordering from an SIer before requirements are fully finalized
  • Full-time hiring taking too long and missing business opportunities: It is not uncommon for the hiring process itself to take six months to a year, and the opportunity cost during that period is often overlooked
  • Ordering FDE services with the same mindset as SES: Giving detailed work instructions prevents FDEs from demonstrating their true value (problem discovery capability)

7. Self-Diagnosis: Check Which Engagement Model Fits Your Company

Please answer the following questions with Yes/No.

Q1. Do you want to entrust everything end-to-end up to "production deployment and on-site adoption"? → Yes: FDE / full-time hiring are candidates. No: Go to Q2

Q2. Is the problem itself not yet articulated, and do you need recommendations at the business strategy level? → Yes: IT consulting is a candidate. No: Go to Q3

Q3. Is what you need simply "development headcount," with requirements already finalized? → Yes: SES is a candidate. No: Go to Q4

Q4. Do you want to build a large-scale, complex system all at once and entrust maintenance as well? → Yes: SIer is a candidate. No: Go to Q5

Q5. If you chose "entrust up to production deployment and on-site adoption" in Q1: Do you want to keep this function/area as your company's core capability over the long term? → Yes: Full-time hiring (or transition to in-house development after FDE accompaniment). No: FDE

8. Combined Strategy: Mixing by Phase and a Roadmap for Transitioning to In-House Development

In practice, rather than "choosing just one," it is common to combine options according to the phase.

  • Phase 0 (early stage with unclear problems): Organize strategy with IT consulting
  • Phase 1 (filling the implementation gap): FDE accompanies you through production deployment and adoption
  • Phase 1.5-2 (standardized maintenance and operations phase): Supplement capacity with SES
  • Phase 3 (when areas to keep as core functions long term become clear): Switch to full-time hiring, with the FDE shifting to an advisory support role

Many companies that adopt FDEs ultimately aim for this "transition to full-time hiring (in-house development)." The most accurate understanding is that an FDE is not the goal, but an option that bridges the transition period until in-house development is achieved.

9. Frequently Asked Questions (Q&A)

9-1. Which engagement model is the cheapest to start with?

Looking only at monthly costs, SES is the cheapest. However, since it assumes your company handles requirements definition and problem analysis, we recommend comparing based on TCO including those labor hours.

9-2. Can I use multiple engagement models at the same time?

There is no problem with that. In fact, using different models by phase or combining them is common in practice (see Chapter 8).

9-3. Which should I consider first: FDE or full-time hiring?

If the problem is already clear and you want to start immediately, FDE is a candidate; if you can afford to spend time on hiring, full-time hiring is a candidate. Many companies proceed with hiring activities in parallel while working alongside an FDE.

9-4. After requesting strategy work from an IT consultant, should I ask the same company for implementation?

It is not mandatory, but to avoid handover losses, we recommend checking in advance whether they can handle implementation consistently, or whether their handover design is solid.

9-5. Can I use an SIer and an FDE together?

Yes. Some companies use them in a division of roles: SIer for large-scale renewals, and FDE for resolving implementation gaps or partial AI utilization.

9-6. Can I change the engagement model midway?

It depends on the contract type. Quasi-mandate contracts (FDE, SES, IT consulting) can be revised relatively flexibly, but fixed-price contracts (SIer) and employment contracts (full-time employees) involve high change costs.

Summary

  • Choosing the wrong engagement model is a weighty decision that directly leads to time losses on the scale of six months to a year
  • The five engagement models (FDE, SES, IT consulting, SIer, full-time hiring) each differ in the value they provide, contract type, cost structure, and failure patterns
  • It is important to compare based on TCO (total cost of ownership), not just monthly costs
  • In practice, rather than narrowing down to a single option, combining and transitioning according to phase is common
