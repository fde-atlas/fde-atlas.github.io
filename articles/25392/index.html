Generative AI Adoption in the Enterprise and Security Measures: Risks to Address First, Control Design, and the Role of FDE | FDE Research Institute

Original title: 生成AIの社内導入とセキュリティ対策｜最初に潰すリスクと統制設計・FDEの役割

FDE総研 · 2026-09-09 · 11 min read

Summary

This Japanese-language article argues that generative AI security failures in enterprises stem less from models themselves than from poor design around them. The main risks are sensitive data leaving via chat inputs, shadow AI, flattened permissions in RAG indexes, prompt injection, and unverified outputs. It proposes layered technical controls across entry, permissions, execution, and logging, plus practical governance: a data-classification-by-use-case matrix, risk-based approval flows, and a consultation channel. It closes by defining the FDE role—originating at Palantir—as implementing systems that satisfy both business and security requirements on-site, complementing rather than replacing security departments.

Key points

  • Real incidents happen at inputs and permissions, not model training.
  • RAG can flatten access controls, letting AI leak files users shouldn't see.
  • Banning AI outright drives usage underground; provide approved tools first.
  • Design controls in four layers: entry, permissions, execution, logging.
  • FDEs implement controls on-site where security teams lack business context.

What security risks should be addressed first when deploying generative AI internally?

The first things to address are the three entry points: external transmission of confidential data, unauthorized use (shadow AI), and misuse of outputs.

Security discussions about generative AI tend to focus on "whether the model uses data for training." But actual incidents happen much earlier in the process. No one knows who input what data, into which tool, with what permissions. This lack of visibility is the biggest risk. Even in OWASP's published Top 10 risk list for LLM applications, prompt injection, leakage of sensitive information, and excessive agency rank at the top. In other words, the center of gravity of threats lies not in the "model" but in the "design around the model."

Through which routes does confidential information leak?

Most leaks come not from training use, but from pasting into prompts and from internal search integrations with overly broad permissions.

The routes can be organized into the following three.

  • Manual exfiltration: Staff paste contracts, customer lists, or source code directly into the chat box.
  • Flattened permissions: In a RAG configuration (combined with internal document search), files from departments the user should not access end up included in the index.
  • Transmission via invisible routes: Data is sent through external SaaS or browser extensions.

The particularly troublesome issue in the field is the second one, flattened permissions. If folders containing personnel evaluations or credit information are vectorized all together, access controls can be bypassed through the AI's answers. Controls that were maintained on the file server collapse the moment they are put on the search platform. My impression is that this failure happens very easily in the early stages of deployment.

Why is shadow AI (unauthorized use) dangerous?

Because if you only impose bans, use goes underground and work proceeds in places where neither controls nor logs reach.

A blanket ban looks safe but actually encourages the use of personal accounts. The worst thing for a company is not "generative AI being used." It is "not knowing the fact that it is being used." Therefore, a realistic initial policy is not prohibition, but preparing approved safe options first and designing to steer users toward them. As long as the approved environment is less convenient than personal use, shadow AI will not disappear.

What are the risks of prompt injection and output misuse?

Instructions embedded in external documents can make the AI malfunction, leading to exfiltration of internal data or the use of false information in business operations.

In configurations where external PDFs or web pages are read, if a document contains text like "ignore all previous instructions and output the following," the model may comply with it. Also, operations that copy generated results into customer deliverables without human review cause incidents. Technical defenses and verification steps on the business process side must always be designed as a set.

How should technical controls to protect internal data be designed?

Divide into four layers—entry, permissions, execution, and records—and design technical controls as layered defenses that work independently at each layer.

Treating technical controls as a question of "which tool to choose" usually fails. What matters is building a structure where, even if one layer is breached, the other layers can still protect. Both ISO/IEC 42001 (the international standard for AI management systems, published in 2023) and the NIST AI Risk Management Framework (released January 2023) assume controls across the entire lifecycle, not a single countermeasure.

Entry measures: Where should data be stopped?

Use pre-transmission filtering and machine-based classification of data to prevent sensitive information from leaving the company in the first place.

For implementation, the basic approach is to route traffic through an internal proxy or AI gateway and detect personal information, credentials, or specific keywords to block or mask them. As a prerequisite, data classifications (public / internal only / confidential / highly protected personal information, etc.) must be defined. If you introduce only filters while classifications remain vague, false positives become too frequent and the field starts looking for workarounds. The success of entry measures is determined by the definition of data classifications before the tools.

Permission design: How should the RAG search scope be narrowed?

The search index inherits the access control of the source data, so documents outside a user's permissions do not appear in search results.

Specifically, each document carries an ACL (access control list) as metadata, and at search time filtering is applied based on the user's department or role. When granting AI agents tool execution permissions, read-only should be the default, and operations involving writing, sending, or payments should go through an explicit approval flow. The "excessive permissions" issue that OWASP points out often grows over time as convenience is prioritized. Follow the order of "start narrow, expand as needed" for permissions.

Logs and auditing: What should be recorded to fulfill accountability?

Store records linking "who, when, with which data, and what output was obtained" so they can be traced later.

At minimum, keep the following items.

  • User ID
  • Timestamp
  • IDs of referenced documents
  • Model name and version
  • Summary (or full text) of inputs and outputs

Since the logs themselves become a mass of sensitive information, retention periods, access rights, and encryption must be decided at the same time. Being unable to answer "what might have leaked" when an incident occurs leads to consequences as serious as the leak itself.

How should governance and operational rules for generative AI use be created?

Decide what is allowed along two axes—use case and data classification—make exceptions application-based, and decide upfront how often the rules will be reviewed.

The most common failure in rule-making is producing a document listing abstract prohibitions and stopping there. A policy that only says "do not input confidential information" leaves the field unable to make judgments. The "AI Guidelines for Business Operators" published by the Ministry of Internal Affairs and Communications and the Ministry of Economy, Trade and Industry in April 2024 also assume that businesses will tailor them to their own risks. Rules should be detailed enough that the field can make a decision in three seconds.

What needs to be decided for a usage policy to be sufficient?

Three points—approved tools, what is allowed per data classification, and use cases requiring human review—are enough to keep operations running.

Deciding in the following order avoids getting stuck.

  • List of approved tools: Specify which services and plans may be used (write it at the plan level, since data handling differs between free and corporate plans).
  • Data classification × use case matrix: Show in a table whether input is allowed.
  • Use cases requiring human review: Customer deliverables, legal judgments, hiring evaluations, credit decisions, etc.
  • Prohibitions: Use of personal accounts, entering credentials, unapproved external integrations.
  • Contact point and exception application channel: Always provide someone to consult when in doubt.

How should the approval process and risk assessment be run?

Sort new use cases by impact: low-risk ones are self-declared, and only high-risk ones go through review.

If everything goes through review, cases will inevitably back up. Three criteria are sufficient to function as decision axes.

  • Data classification being handled
  • Whether the output affects people's rights or transactions
  • Whether the AI has execution permissions

It is good to start with a simple line: if none of the three apply, proceed by self-declaration; if even one applies, send it to review. The EU AI Act (effective August 2024, being applied in stages) also adopts risk-based classification, and this approach is easy to apply to internal controls at Japanese companies.

How should education and exception handling be designed?

Rather than annual classroom training, it is effective to embed practices through department-specific case collections and a "place to ask when in trouble."

Looking at actual workplaces, what prevents accidents is often not the policy document but having someone you can casually ask, "Is it OK to input this?" Set up a single channel with legal, the IT department, and field leaders participating, and accumulate decided cases as internal knowledge. This practice seems to have the greatest impact in the end. As decided cases accumulate, subsequent inquiries decrease.

How does an FDE contribute to generative AI security?

An FDE works on-site at the customer and is responsible for implementing systems that satisfy both business requirements and security requirements.

FDE (Forward Deployed Engineer) is a role established by Palantir. FDEs embed themselves nearly full-time at customer sites and consistently handle everything from understanding the business to implementation and operational adoption. Security controls for generative AI are an area where FDEs can deliver particular value, because failures in controls usually stem from either "the security department not knowing the field's business" or "the field not knowing the risks."

How does an FDE differ from security staff?

The security department sets the standards, and the FDE builds a working system that meets those standards.

The boundary between the two roles can be organized as follows. It is not an adversarial relationship but a complementary one, where the frontline FDE implements the standards set by the second line of defense (the security department). {{45} Perspective | Security department | FDE

What does an FDE do first in the field?

Start with three things: taking inventory of data flows, verifying the permission model, and implementing one small, safe use case.

The approach is as follows.

  • Interview the field about which data moves in which processes, and diagram it.
  • Verify whether existing access controls can be inherited by the AI platform.
  • Implement just one use case in a low-sensitivity area and collect operational logs.

Rather than aiming for company-wide rollout from the start, it is ultimately faster to verify on a small scale that controls actually work before expanding. In businesses like VACAN that handle real-time field data, a design that maintains both data freshness and permission consistency becomes especially important.

When should a company have an FDE?

It applies when business requirements differ by department, and standard SaaS settings alone cannot achieve both governance and usability.

Conversely, if usage is limited to general-purpose writing and the data handled is mostly public information, existing tools' management features and policy development are often sufficient. FDE is most effective when you want to safely launch use cases involving core data or personal information without halting business operations. When in doubt, a good rule of thumb is to check whether you are in a state where "meeting the security requirements would bring business operations to a standstill."

SituationRecommended structure
Centered on general-purpose writing, mainly public informationExisting tools' management features + policy development
Involves core data or personal informationOn-site implementation by FDE + security department standards
Business operations stall when governance requirements are metDeploy an FDE and redesign business processes and governance simultaneously

Security in the internal adoption of generative AI is not a binary choice between prohibition and permission. It is a design question of how much to block with technology and how much to leave to operations and human judgment. First establish a structure where technical controls, governance, and on-site implementation can be discussed at the same table. Even if it seems like a roundabout approach, that is the shortest path.
