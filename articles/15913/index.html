FDE is the criterion for distinguishing real AI implementation consultants from fake ones, not a job title on a resume.

Summary

This 91APP article argues that the FDE title has become a marketing label, and offers a checklist for telling real forward deployed engineers from rebranded consultants. Citing Indeed data (643 to 5,330 job postings in a year, up 729%) and investments by AWS ($1B) and Microsoft ($2.5B, 6,000 engineers), it contrasts this with an MIT NANDA finding that 95% of enterprise GenAI pilots show no measurable P&L impact. Everyone in the market wants to call themselves an AI implementation companion, but what separates a real FDE from a fake one? This piece breaks down how a genuine Forward Deployed Engineer actually works, and gives brand owners a checklist they can take straight to vendors—asking about on-site presence mechanisms, the depth of requirements translation, evidence from production environments, and experience feedback loops—to find out who is truly willing to stay until the system is stable.

The number of "Forward Deployed Engineer (FDE)" job postings on Indeed surged from 643 in April 2025 to 5,330 in April 2026 within a year, a 729% annual increase. Over the same period, AWS announced a $1 billion investment to build a dedicated Forward Deployed Engineer team, and Microsoft followed with $2.5 billion and 6,000 engineers (according to TechCrunch's July 2026 report).

This is a rare double down on talent and capital. Yet at the same time, MIT NANDA's 2025 report "The GenAI Divide: State of AI in Business" pointed out that 95% of enterprise generative AI pilot projects ultimately had no measurable impact on the P&L (Fortune coverage). The two events are not necessarily cause and effect, but their coincidence is itself worth noting: the money has been spent and the positions opened, yet the market's hunger for "implementation capability" has not translated into most AI projects delivering real results.

This gap leaves an opening: when every vendor wants to write "we have FDEs accompanying you" in their proposals, how should brand owners tell whether the other side is truly willing to stay until the system is stable, or has merely given the word "consultant" a new coat of paint?

We recently discussed this topic with marketing executives at several retail brands, and the complaints were almost identical: at contract signing they heard "we will accompany you through implementation the whole way," but at project close they received a slide deck and a system no one maintains. This scenario is familiar to us—CDP or AI implementation projects stuck in the predicament of "the consultant disappears after handing over the report" have long been a pain point for retail brands. This is the final satellite piece in the "Forward Deployed Engineer" series; earlier pieces covered the truth about requirements translation, why implementation speed is not the key to success, what the rebranded-consultant controversy is about, and how technology can actually complete the last mile. This piece is more practical: a checklist you can take directly to vendors.

Broken down, the FDE job is actually four layers

To explain the difference between real and fake FDEs, this article breaks the job into four layers. Market discussions of FDEs often stop at the surface-level action of "an engineer dispatched to the client's site," but what truly determines success or failure is whether these four layers are fully traversed.

  • Requirements translation. What clients say they need is often not the real problem but a symptom of it. A real FDE will probe repeatedly and use data and evidence to challenge the client's original assumptions, while a rebranded consultant copies the client's words verbatim into the spec sheet.
  • Rapid prototyping. A real FDE uses a minimum viable version to first validate whether the direction is right, running through a real workflow to show the client. Rebranded consultants often skip this layer entirely, drawing up a complete architecture diagram first, only to discover it doesn't work when it's time to actually connect the data.
  • System deployment and operations. This layer makes it easiest to tell real from fake: did the system actually connect to the client's production environment, withstand real traffic, and pass permission and compliance checks, or does it forever remain at the "runs smoothly in the demo environment" stage?
  • Experience feedback loop. Did past projects distill what was learned into nourishment that makes the next delivery faster and more accurate, or does every project start from zero, with a new batch of consultants and all previously accumulated judgment reset to zero?

A real FDE is someone who completes all four layers, repeatedly. Rebranded consultants usually manage only the surface work of the first and second layers; the third and fourth are either not done or beyond them. Watch out for one easily confused situation: long-term on-site presence caused by a product riddled with bugs and requiring daily firefighting is a completely different thing from proactively completing these four layers and continuously optimizing—worth judging separately when assessing real versus fake.

Palantir puts it bluntly: the copies are all discount versions

The Forward Deployed Engineer role is not new; Palantir has used this model for over a decade, dispatching engineers directly into government agencies and enterprises and staying with them until the system works. The current AI boom has reignited interest in the role, and has also prompted a flood of companies to slap the FDE label on their consulting services.

Ted Mabrey, Palantir's global head of commercial, was blunt on his own Substack: tech companies trying to replicate Palantir's model have mostly failed, and the copies on the market are uniformly "discount versions" (LeadDev coverage). The logic behind this statement is simple: the real FDE model requires a company willing to bear the long-term labor costs of on-site presence in exchange for the client's trust that problems will truly be solved, whereas the discount version merely swaps the consultant's title while the internal delivery methods, evaluation methods, and contract terms remain completely unchanged.

Chris Slovak, Unframe's global CTO, raises a challenge from another angle: companies want to build automated systems to reduce reliance on labor while also spending heavily on this expensive, hard-to-find group of people to build those systems. Whether this model is sustainable in the long run has no industry consensus. This contradiction is itself a useful line of questioning—vendors who describe integration work as effortless, as if there were no trade-offs, usually have never squarely addressed this contradiction.

AWS and Microsoft are not betting on the same thing

At the end of June 2026, AWS announced a $1 billion investment to establish a dedicated forward deployed engineering unit, embedding engineers directly inside enterprise customers to accelerate AI implementation (TechCrunch coverage). Two days later, Microsoft followed suit but took a somewhat different path: the unit it established is called "Microsoft Frontier Company," with a $2.5 billion investment and 6,000 engineers. Judson Althoff, CEO of Microsoft's commercial business, emphasized that this scale goes beyond the usual definition of "forward deployed engineering"—the goal is the industry's largest, most capable, outcome-oriented engineering organization.

What is notable about this contrast is that neither company chose the cheapest approach—simply reprinting existing consultants' business cards with FDE on them. They chose genuine long-term investment, betting that enterprise customers will pay for "actually being able to implement," not for a fashionable job title. At the same time, OpenAI also brought in private equity partners to establish a company focused on enterprise deployment, and Anthropic formed a joint venture with financial institutions to do something similar (TechCrunch's July 2026 report). These companies made the same judgment almost simultaneously: the bottleneck in AI implementation is not model capability, but whether engineers can be kept by the client's side to solve the problem all the way through.

Worth noting is that AWS's official positioning of this team is not for engineers to stay with clients permanently. AWS's official blog states clearly that delivered systems should move toward "self-sufficiency, not continued dependence". This detail actually reinforces this article's criterion: real long-term investment buys engineers' willingness to stay until the system is stable and knowledge has genuinely been handed over to the client's team—it has no direct relationship with how long they stay. Refusing to ever leave the client's side may instead indicate a product highly dependent on labor, with knowledge never transferred out.

For Taiwanese brands, this playbook is actually nothing new. MediaTek's turnkey solutions business over the years has relied precisely on the FAE (Field Application Engineer) role that Taiwan's hardware industry has long had, staying with customers all the way until the solution can actually go into mass production and ship (Business Next report). The term FDE may look like a buzzword invented by Silicon Valley startups, but Taiwan's supply chain has always understood what it means to see things through to the end.

Why rebranded consultants fail: the incentive structures are fundamentally different

When you put rebranded consultants and real FDEs side by side in the same proposal, they look like the same kind of service, but the underlying business models are often different. Traditional consulting engagements are typically priced per project; once the deliverables are done and the acceptance form is signed, the deal is over, and consulting firms have little incentive to stay on for ongoing maintenance. The true FDE model works the other way around: the company is willing to bear the fixed costs of being on-site, in exchange for the client's long-term renewals and trust. The math is calculated over a relationship spanning several years, not the amount on a single contract. Of course, traditional consultants can also sign long-term contracts and price based on outcomes; the contract's name was never the only criterion. What really matters is whether the other party has actually walked through the four layers described earlier, not what service items are written in the proposal.

This incentive gap echoes the most important finding in the MIT NANDA report. After observing more than 300 enterprise AI adoption cases, the report's authors pointed out that the core reason most pilot projects fail is that these systems cannot retain feedback, adapt to context, and keep learning over time, while infrastructure, regulation, or talent are merely secondary factors. That report is about whether the system itself has a learning mechanism; what we want to point out is that this mechanism usually also requires someone to continuously maintain and adjust it alongside. A one-time delivery arrangement where the vendor leaves once acceptance is complete can hardly sustain this kind of long-term adjustment work. This state of quietly failing with no one around to troubleshoot is the same phenomenon as automation pipelines silently breaking. The rebranded consultant model does indeed struggle to sustain this on an incentive level, but as long as a consulting firm genuinely completes the system deployment and operations layers and leaves behind a mechanism for continuous adjustment, it can achieve the same result.

The reason real FDEs seem expensive and hard to find is not market hype, but that the four layers of work—requirement translation, system integration, production environment operations, and experience feedback—inherently require sustained staffing. Consultants who avoid this cost reality and tell brands that "adoption is easy" usually intend to close the case and leave before the third layer.

New problems and new approaches discovered in this process also feed back into the iteration direction of the CDMP product itself, and the accumulated judgment does not reset to zero just because the client changes.

Ask the right questions, and you will find out who can truly stay the course

When evaluating an AI adoption proposal, it is meaningless to just look at whether FDE is written on the vendor's résumé; the key is to use specific questions to force out answers. The following sets of questions correspond to the four layers broken down earlier and can be copied directly to ask vendors.

  • Ask about the embedding mechanism and handover plan. Does the contract clearly specify post-delivery ongoing service terms, on-site frequency, how often performance is reviewed, and how knowledge and systems will be handed over to your team—or is there only a one-time acceptance clause with no handover plan? A partner genuinely willing to stay the course will proactively write review cycles and handover plans into the contract, rather than glossing over them vaguely only when you press, and will not treat "always staying by your side" as the sole success metric.
  • Ask about the depth of requirement translation. Ask them to describe their previous engagement: how did the requirements the client originally stated differ from the problem that was actually solved in the end? If they cannot articulate the difference, or say "we just do whatever the client says," it means they have never really done the requirement translation layer.
  • Ask for evidence from the production environment. Do not just look at demos; directly ask how long the system has been running since launch, what problems it has encountered, and how they were fixed. Those willing to talk about the pitfalls they have hit and able to describe actual operational details are usually the ones who have genuinely deployed things into a production environment.
  • Ask about the experience feedback mechanism. Ask whether what they have learned has become part of their methodology, tools, or product features, or whether every project is an isolated event where the accumulated judgment disappears as soon as the personnel change. This is the key to distinguishing whether they have made a long-term investment in this capability or are just taking on projects for one-off revenue.
  • Ask about failure cases. Ask them to describe a project that did not succeed or had to change course midway, and to explain why and what lessons were learned. Those who can give specific details and describe how they adjusted are usually more credible than a long list of success stories; being unable to articulate any lessons learned is the real red flag.

These five sets of questions do not require the other party to give a perfect answer on the spot; the point is to see whether they are willing to answer honestly or just dodge around the questions. A partner willing to clearly state what they cannot do is usually more trustworthy than one who can only say pretty things. You can also ask, when the system breaks, whose name is on the approval line; a vendor who cannot answer this has never truly been accountable for post-launch results.

Labels can be bought; staying the course cannot

The fact that AWS and Microsoft are willing to spend billions of dollars to build long-term embedded engineering teams shows one thing: this market already has enough money and talent to prove that forward deployed engineering is worth investing in. But what money cannot buy is the commitment to stay until the system is stable and responsibilities are clearly handed over. A company can change its consultants' job titles to FDE overnight, but it cannot change whether the contract specifies ongoing services and a handover plan, nor can it change whether they are willing to talk about where their last project failed.

The checklist in a brand's hands was never about asking for the other party's job title; it asks whether they can sustain long-term embedding, and whether they know when to let go. Ask the right questions, and the answers will naturally tell you whether the person in front of you is truly prepared to stay with you until the system is stable, or is planning to finish this one deal and leave.

The FDE questions brands ask most often

Q1: What is an FDE (Forward Deployed Engineer)? How is it different from a regular consultant? A1: An FDE is an engineer dispatched into the client's organization who personally participates in system building, integration, and post-launch operations. The scope of work covers requirement translation, prototype validation, production deployment, and long-term optimization. The biggest difference from traditional consultants is that FDEs remain accountable for post-delivery results; delivery does not mean the case is closed.

Q2: Why are so many companies scrambling for FDEs lately, even investing heavily? A2: Because companies have generally found that the real bottleneck for AI projects is deployment and integration, not just model capability itself. The MIT NANDA report pointed out that most generative AI pilot projects have no measurable impact on profit and loss. This has led companies like AWS, Microsoft, OpenAI, and Anthropic to invest heavily, racing to fill the talent gap in making AI truly land in production.

Q3: Small brands have limited budgets and cannot afford a dedicated FDE. What should they do? A3: You do not necessarily need to hire a full-time dedicated engineer; the key is to confirm whether the consulting firm or platform you work with offers a similar long-term partnership mechanism, such as clearly defined service response times, regular performance reviews, and system health checks, rather than a one-time delivery that ends once the contract is signed.

Q4: Where are rebranded consultants most likely to be exposed compared to real FDEs? A4: They are most easily exposed at the system deployment and operations layer. Rebranded consultants can usually only handle early-stage interviews and planning; once you ask about the actual operational details after the system goes live, the problems encountered, and how they were resolved, they start to become vague.

Q5: After introducing an AI system, how long until you can verify whether it has "truly landed"? A5: There is no fixed number of days, but at minimum you should go through one complete business cycle—for example, one promotional campaign or one membership lifecycle loop for a retail brand—as the starting point for observation, rather than just looking at the demo effect on launch day. The number of days is only the minimum threshold; what you really need to watch is whether the system keeps running stably and whether it adjusts its judgments based on new data.

Brands can take this checklist directly and use it to evaluate any implementation partner, including us.
