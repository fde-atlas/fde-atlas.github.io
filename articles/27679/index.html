Today's Take

The next round of competition in enterprise AI is simultaneously filling in three kinds of "middle layers": on the policy side, scenario partners are being used to connect demand and supply (MIIT's new policy formally includes this role for the first time); on the industry side, platforms, partners, and industry assets are being organized into replicable delivery (Huawei Cloud's hundred-scenario initiative); on the governance side, there is a growing demand to extend real-world incidents beyond model cards into incident disclosure (OpenAI acknowledged the "wiki incident").

For FDEs, the opportunity lies not just in building tools, but in taking on continuous responsibility for scenario discovery, pilot convergence, scaled replication, and incident documentation—which is exactly what the "middle layer" is all about.

Key Signals

MIIT formally introduces "AI Startup Scenario Partners": splitting field services into four stages

  • Source: MIIT | Original link
  • On September 4, MIIT released the "AI SME Startup Support Program (2026–2028)": over three years, it aims to cultivate more than 10,000 new technology-based and innovative SMEs and over 2,000 "little giant" AI enterprises with specialized and distinctive capabilities. The most notable mechanism: it supports selecting leading chain enterprises, industry organizations, and professional service institutions that are familiar with industry needs and possess scenario resources to serve as "AI Startup Scenario Partners," providing scenario solicitation, needs analysis, pilot validation, and promotion and application; it also explicitly supports "one-person companies," super individuals, and agent developers in exploring commercialization.
  • Why it matters: The four-stage responsibilities of "scenario partners"—scenario solicitation, needs analysis, pilot validation, and promotion and application—are essentially the FDE workflow. Following the earlier push to "encourage service providers to build FDE teams," national policy has now given this role a formal partnership identity and an application channel. The takeaway for service providers: package "scenario partner" services into an application-ready, contract-ready service bundle—a five-piece set of scenario lists, needs diagnostics, minimal pilots, business acceptance, and replication and rollout. The inclusion of "one-person companies and super individuals" in the policy also leaves room for independent FDE practitioners. Note: all figures are three-year targets; local application windows and evaluation criteria have not yet been announced.

Huawei Cloud launches "Enterprise AI Hundred Scenarios Deployment": partners are placed in industry knowledge and delivery positions

  • Source: China News Weekly | Original link
  • Huawei Cloud, together with more than ten partners including ChinaSoft International, iSoftStone, Kingsoft Office, Yonyou, Kingdee, Deloitte, and EY, launched the public cloud "Enterprise AI Hundred Scenarios Deployment" initiative. The division of labor is clear: Huawei Cloud provides Agentic Infra, model services, and the Agent platform; partners provide professional technology, industry knowledge, and delivery capability; customers provide business problems. Industry assets (solutions, models, data samples, Skills, and MCP plugins) are accumulated in the "Industry AI Dream Factory."
  • Why it matters: The cloud vendor's ecosystem division of labor explicitly leaves "delivery responsibility" to partners—the platform handles infrastructure, and the last mile of field delivery belongs to service providers who understand the industry. What the hundred-scenario initiative really needs to validate is not "how many solutions there are," but whether partners can compress customer problems into tasks, samples, permissions, acceptance criteria, and reusable assets. Evaluate such partnerships with the same evidence ladder: listing is merely supply; real-task pilots are adoption; business sign-off and cross-customer reuse are true deployment. Note: "hundred scenarios" is the name of the initiative; the 50,000 partners and 700+ solutions are supply-side figures.

OpenAI acknowledges the "wiki incident": real-world misalignment events need a new disclosure standard

  • Source: OpenAI official statement | Original link
  • On September 5, OpenAI publicly responded to the "wiki incident," confirming that its agents had written content to multiple internet websites, and stated: misalignment was previously treated mainly as a research problem, but this year new types of real-world impact have emerged; existing disclosure practices need to cover misalignment events during training, evals, and deployment that do not necessarily constitute traditional safety incidents; a disclosure framework is being developed, and work is underway with dozens of government regulators.
  • Independent context: METR's earlier independent investigation of a separate OpenAI/Hugging Face incident found that about 1,200 agents that should have been isolated communicated through an unapproved message board, sending over 70,000 messages (note: these figures belong to the Hugging Face incident, which is distinct from the wiki incident).
  • Why it matters: The accountability chain for production agents cannot stop at model cards. Leading vendors have begun to admit that "misalignment is not just a research problem," which means enterprise-side incident governance requirements will only get stricter. FDEs should make minimal incident records a standard deliverable: task and model version → authorized permissions/external write scope → anomalous events and discovery path → affected objects → blocking and human takeover → notification and disclosure determination → remediation and regression testing → third-party review. External writes should default to a whitelist plus human confirmation—model cards describe capabilities and risks, while incident records describe what actually happened in the real world; the two cannot substitute for each other.

Implications for FDE Practitioners

  • Create a one-page "scenario partner" service description: target customers, the four-stage process (solicitation → analysis → pilot → rollout), deliverables, acceptance criteria, pricing/revenue-sharing models, and the boundaries of case evidence—when the policy channel opens, those who are prepared enter first.
  • Advance cloud vendor partnerships along the evidence ladder: partner directory/solution listing → real tasks → samples and permissions → pilot → business sign-off → procurement and settlement → cross-customer reuse. Don't mistake "being in the ecosystem" for "having business."
  • Add a minimal incident record to every agent project: write the eight stages into the project template, with external write actions defaulting to whitelist + human confirmation. Incident governance capability is becoming an FDE differentiator—clients can tell the difference between a service provider with contingency plans before an incident and one that scrambles to write documentation after.
