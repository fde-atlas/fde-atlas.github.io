Why PoCs Stall and How to Fix It: 7 Keys to Moving into Production

Original title: PoC止まりの原因と対策｜本番化への7つのポイント

株式会社Sai · 2026-07-05 · 8 min read

Summary

This article by Japan's Sai examines why AI proofs of concept stall before production. It argues the causes are mostly organizational and design flaws—vague goals, missing frontline involvement, demo-only builds, lack of business metrics, and separation between builders, users, and decision-makers—rather than technical failure. It proposes seven measures, including agreeing on production criteria and budgets before starting, designing evaluations around business metrics like hours saved, and deploying in a limited scope early. It cites Palantir's AIP bootcamps and OpenAI's Tokyo FDE job posting as examples of building directly on customer data so pilots lead into production.

Key points

  • Most PoC failures stem from process and structure, not engineering skill.
  • Define production criteria, budget, and team before starting a PoC.
  • Measure results in business metrics, not model accuracy alone.
  • Deploy in one department or workflow first, then expand.
  • Palantir and OpenAI use FDE models that bridge pilot and production.

"The PoC went well. But nothing happens after that."—This is a common concern heard from many companies since the generative AI boom began. A PoC (Proof of Concept) is a small-scale proof-of-concept experiment to test whether a new mechanism works, yet many such experiments end without ever reaching production operation.

Regarding this state, known as "PoC stall," we break down why it happens from a structural perspective and present the steps to carry a project all the way through to production operation.

The 5 Major Causes of PoC Stall

1. The experiment starts without clarity on "what it is meant to verify"

This is the case where the purpose of the PoC is simply "let's try AI for now." If the hypothesis to be validated (e.g., can we automate 50% of first-tier inquiry handling?) is not defined, the project ends with "looks promising, but so what?" and leads to no next decision.

2. The frontline is not involved

This is the pattern where the PoC is run solely by the IT department or DX promotion office, without the involvement of the people who actually perform the work. Even if it succeeds technically, it later turns out that "it doesn't fit the actual workflow on the ground" or "it can't handle edge cases," and the rollout stalls.

3. A "demo-only" build that ignores production operation

This is the case where the PoC is polished into a clean demo, with no consideration given to the data integration, access control, security, and operational framework required for production. When it comes time to go to production, it means "rebuilding almost from scratch," and the project hits the wall of additional budget.

4. There is no yardstick for measuring cost-effectiveness

If the results are presented only as technical metrics like "we achieved 90% accuracy" without being translated into business metrics (hours saved, response time, contribution to revenue), executives cannot make an investment decision.

The absence of such a yardstick is not limited to PoCs. According to a survey by IPA, the proportion of companies working on DX that answered they had "set" outcome metrics was 23.9% in fiscal 2025—less than one in four companies. Nearly 30% also answered that DX outcomes were "unknown" (IPA "DX Trends 2026" (PDF), published July 30, 2026 / survey conducted mid-April to mid-June 2026, 1,799 valid responses, confirmed November 5, 2026).

5. "The builders," "the users," and "the deciders" are separated

The vendor builds it, the frontline uses it, and management decides it—without a common language among these three parties, the results of a PoC land nowhere.

7 Tips for Connecting PoCs to Production Operation

① Decide the "conditions for going to production" before the PoC

Before starting, agree on criteria such as "if we exceed this number, we proceed to production," along with the rough budget and staffing for that scenario. Starting the discussion after the PoC ends is too late.

② Design the validation in "business language"

Not "model accuracy," but "will X hours of monthly work become Y hours?" When hypotheses are framed in business metrics, the results can be used directly as input for investment decisions.

③ Involve the frontline ace from the very beginning

Have the person who knows the work best join the PoC team, even if only one hour per week. This is the most cost-effective investment, serving both to surface edge cases and to secure an "internal champion" after rollout.

④ Identify production constraints first

Usable data, security requirements, integration with existing systems, operations staff—list the constraints you will face in production first, and build them into the PoC design. The iron rule is "never experiment with technology that can't be used in production."

⑤ Put it into production on a small scale

Rather than a large production rollout after a perfect PoC, it is more successful to start limited real-world operation in a restricted scope (one department, one business process) and expand from there. Overseas, approaches have emerged that compress validation itself into units of days and run it on real data. Since 2023, US-based Palantir has incorporated AIP bootcamps into the early stages of customer acquisition. Its annual report for the fiscal year ended December 2024 describes this as an effort to deliver actual workflows on customers' real data within days (Palantir Technologies Form 10-K (fiscal year ended December 2024)). The company positions this as a short-term pilot, and also notes that it does not necessarily lead directly to revenue.

⑥ Develop operations personnel in parallel

One barrier to production is the "who maintains it after it's built?" problem. Have internal staff get hands-on during the PoC period to lay the foundation for in-house development.

⑦ Report the results in management's language

The final report should be about "return on investment, risks, and proposed next decisions," not "accuracy and technical architecture." The true deliverable of a PoC is not a working prototype but the decision-making material needed to move forward.

3 Steps to Get a Stalled PoC Moving

If you are dealing with a PoC that has already stalled—rather than one you are about to start—reset it in the following order.

  • Articulate why it stalled: Identify as a team which of the 5 major causes above applies. The true nature of "no updates, for some reason" is usually the absence of production criteria or a lack of a cost-effectiveness yardstick
  • Define the conditions for production, even retroactively: Decide now: "if we hit this number, we proceed; if we hit that number, we withdraw." Deciding to withdraw is also a legitimate PoC outcome—leaving it in limbo is the costliest option
  • Carve out a scope that can go into production on a small scale: Without waiting for a company-wide rollout decision, break it down into units that can start real operation in one department or one business process. Once things start moving, the stalled discussions move forward too

For PoCs that have been "on hold" for more than 3 months, we recommend deciding one way or the other: reboot them with these 3 steps, or clearly decide to withdraw.

PoC FAQ

Q. What is the difference between a PoC, a proof-of-concept experiment, and a trial?

PoC (Proof of Concept) is translated into Japanese as "概念実証" (concept verification) and is used almost synonymously with demonstration experiments. A trial usually refers to the stage of testing a finished product, which differs from a PoC in what is being verified: a PoC confirms the feasibility of a mechanism that has not yet been built.

Q. How long should a PoC last?

It depends on the number of hypotheses being tested, but it is healthy to cap it at a few weeks, or at most within three months. A PoC without a deadline tends to become an "endless experiment," and the length of the period itself becomes a cause of PoC fatigue.

Q. What is "PoC fatigue"?

It refers to a state where, despite repeated PoCs, nothing ever reaches production, and both frontline teams and management lose their enthusiasm for verification. The cause is not the failure of experiments but the absence of "design after the experiment." Before running more PoCs, the priority is to establish the conditions for productionization and the decision-making process.

Learning from overseas companies: the solution of "building in the field from day one"

The FDE (Forward Deployed Engineer) model adopted by US companies Palantir and OpenAI is one answer to the problem of PoCs going nowhere. Engineers embed themselves at the customer's site, build systems with real data and real operations, and stay alongside the client until operations take hold — an approach that does not separate "experimentation" from "production."

The same philosophy appears in the job posting for FDEs at OpenAI's Tokyo office. It states that the person in charge holds technical delivery responsibility from the first prototype through stable operation, and that success is measured by adoption in production operations and business impact (OpenAI official job posting, Forward Deployed Engineer - Tokyo, confirmed September 9, 2026).

Walls arise when you try to "transplant" something built in the lab into the field. If you build in the field from the start, the PoC becomes the first step of production itself.

SAI's perspective: a PoC is not a "goal" but a "waypoint"

We at SAI Inc. focus on go-to-market support and FDE-accompanied development. Our published case studies include website development for an advertising agency and automation of social media and ad operations, and embedding AI usage among staff at a BCP support company. In both cases, we went beyond simply deploying tools and carried the work through to integration into daily operations. From this experience, we believe the cause of stalled PoCs lies not in technology but in design and organizational structure.

At SAI, we define the "conditions for productionization" together with you from the PoC planning stage, engineers enter the field and verify with real data, and we stay alongside you through operational adoption and in-house development.
