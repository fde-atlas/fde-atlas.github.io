Operations and Maintenance of AI Systems | Who Does What After Delivery

Original title: AIシステムの運用保守｜納品のあとに誰が何をするか

FDE Consulting · 2026-11-11 · 8 min read

Summary

The article argues that generative AI systems differ from ordinary business systems in maintenance: even if the client changes nothing, model deprecations, API changes, and updates to referenced documents force behavioral changes. The author lists four post-delivery changes, assigns tasks among business owners, IT staff, and vendors, and stresses that judging whether answers are business-correct cannot be outsourced. Concrete examples include OpenAI retiring GPT-4o-family models on January 14, 2027, and Anthropic ending Claude Sonnet 4.5 on November 30, 2026. The piece also lists five items to fix in maintenance contracts and four questions to ask when comparing quotes, suggesting an FDE can bridge business and technology when no internal owner exists.

Key points

  • AI systems change behavior even without client-side modifications, unlike normal systems.
  • OpenAI gives at least 6 months' notice for GA model retirement; Anthropic at least 60 days.
  • Answer-correctness judgment must stay in-house regardless of outsourcing maintenance.
  • Contracts should cover scope, re-running evals, incident response, workload limits, and handover.
  • An FDE can fill the gap between business owners and technical staff in operations.

Many companies have an AI system built by an outside company and accept delivery, then go into production without deciding who will handle ongoing operation and maintenance. In my view, ordinary business systems keep running if you leave them alone. Systems that incorporate generative AI change behavior even if your company changes nothing, due to model replacements by the provider or API changes.

Below, I first organize how the operation and maintenance of AI systems differs from ordinary systems, then cover in order: the changes that occur after delivery, the division of responsibilities between the ordering party and the contractor, the items to settle in the maintenance contract, and the points to verify in the estimate.

The overall process from request to operation is described in The AI Development Process, and contracts for outsourcing development in AI Development Contracts. This article deals only with what happens after delivery.

How does the operation and maintenance of AI systems differ from ordinary systems?

There is already an established framework for what to decide in the operation and maintenance of ordinary systems. The "Non-Functional Requirements Grades" published by IPA (Information-technology Promotion Agency) in April 2010 divides non-functional requirements into six major categories—availability, performance/scalability, operability/maintainability, migration, security, and system environment/ecology—so that the ordering party and developers share a common understanding. In my view, the operability/maintainability category can still be used as-is for AI systems.

What falls short in AI systems lies outside this framework. For machine learning systems, D. Sculley et al.'s "Hidden Technical Debt in Machine Learning Systems," presented at NIPS 2015, points out that even if results come quickly, real production systems incur continuously large maintenance costs. The situation is similar with generative AI. I have summarized the differences in a table.

PerspectiveOrdinary business systemSystem incorporating generative AI
What triggers behavior changeWhen your company makes modificationsIn addition to your own modifications, model replacements by the provider, API changes, and updates to referenced documents
How to verify it works correctlyWhether tests pass and no errors occurEven with no errors, verify via evaluation tests whether answer quality has degraded
When maintenance work arisesWhen bug reports or modification requests come inOn the provider's schedule, with a deadline
People needed for judgmentMainly engineersEngineers plus business staff who can judge whether answers are correct for the business

Of the right-hand column of the table, I believe the third row is the easiest for the ordering party to overlook. With ordinary systems, maintenance work can be postponed at your own convenience, but model end-of-life has a deadline.

Four changes that occur after delivery

1. Model end-of-life

Both OpenAI and Anthropic state in their official documentation that they periodically deprecate old models whenever they release new ones. The notice periods differ by provider. OpenAI states that, except when accelerated for safety or compliance reasons, generally available models are announced at least 6 months in advance, and preview models may be deprecated with as little as about 2 weeks' notice. Anthropic states it notifies customers using a published model at least 60 days before ending its availability.

There are actual examples. On October 14, 2026, OpenAI notified developers using some GPT-4-series models, including GPT-4o, that they would be removed from the API on January 14, 2027. Anthropic plans to end availability of Claude Sonnet 4.5 on November 30, 2026. Anthropic's documentation states that requests to models after their end-of-life date will fail. On Microsoft's cloud, the standard lifecycle for generally available models is 18 months.

In my view, two things should be decided before delivery: not choosing preview models for production use, and deciding who will monitor the end-of-life schedules of the models in use.

2. API specification changes

Not only models but the APIs themselves change. On August 26, 2025, OpenAI notified developers using the Assistants API that it would be removed from the API one year later, on August 26, 2026. The successors are the Responses API and Conversations API. With API version 2026-10-21, Anthropic removed parameters such as temperature that control output variability, returning a 400 error if specified.

With such changes, code fixes become necessary even without changing models. Before starting maintenance, make a list of which APIs and versions the delivered code calls.

3. Changes to referenced documents and business operations

In systems that answer by referencing internal regulations or product materials, the referenced documents must be replaced whenever regulations are revised or products are added. Someone is needed to verify whether the answers have changed as a result. Engineers can perform the document replacement, but only business staff can decide which documents should be treated as current.

4. Changes in usage

Once in production, questions arise that were not anticipated at development time. The "Guidelines for Secure AI System Development" created by the UK's NCSC with the US's CISA and others also lists, for the operation and maintenance phase, monitoring system behavior and supporting users so they can evaluate behavior changes caused by model or other changes. The core of operations becomes keeping records of inputs and outputs, periodically reviewing questions that could not be answered, and adding them to the evaluation tests. How to build the recording and evaluation mechanisms is described in What is LLMOps?.

Who does what in operation and maintenance

Dividing who does what for the four changes yields the following. The table is my own organization; the split between the two right-hand columns changes depending on whether the contractor takes on maintenance.

TaskBusiness staffInformation systemsContractor (if maintenance is outsourced)
Monitoring end-of-life and API change noticesResponsibleResponsible
Switching to new models or APIsDecide the switchover datePerform the work
Verifying answers after switchoverDecide whether to accept the resultsSchedule the workRun evaluations and produce results
Replacing referenced documentsDecide which documents are currentVerify permissionsPerform the replacement
Reviewing unanswered questionsWrite correct answersAdd to evaluation tests
First response to incidentsNotify usersServe as the point of contactInvestigate causes and fix
Checking usage feesReview monthlyInvestigate reasons for increases

The reason the column for the business owner in the table is left blank is that the task of judging whether answers are correct cannot be outsourced anywhere. Even if you outsource maintenance, the work in this column remains. In my view, when deciding on the operation and maintenance structure, it is better to decide who among the business owners will take on this column before choosing a vendor.

What to Decide in the Maintenance Contract

If the development contract is a fixed-price (contract-for-work) type, and the deliverables do not match the contract content, you must notify the vendor within one year of becoming aware of the nonconformity, or you will lose the right to demand remedies such as completion or a reduction in compensation (Article 637 of the Civil Code). This is a safeguard against defects in what was built, and does not cover work when the provider's model changes. Model switching and document replacement need to be separately agreed upon as part of the operation and maintenance contract.

ItemWhat to DecideIf Left Blank
Scope of maintenanceWhich of the following are included: fixing defects, switching models, responding to API changes, replacing documentsA separate estimate will be needed every time an end-of-service notice arrives
Re-running evaluationsAfter switching or replacement, which evaluation questions to run and who accepts the resultsNo one notices that the answers have changed
Handling of failuresContact point, hours of acceptance, target time to recoveryNo one can act when it goes down at night or on a holiday
Workload and costsMonthly workload cap, how overages are handled, who pays usage feesDisputes arise in months when switching work overlaps
HandoverWhat is handed over when maintenance ends (code, prompts, evaluation questions, procedure manuals)You become unable to change vendors

In my view, the second row of the table is the easiest to leave blank. If you make it a premise that the evaluation questions created during development continue to be used in maintenance, you avoid having to redefine the pass threshold at every model switch. If the evaluation questions are not included in the development deliverables, have them handed over before signing the maintenance contract.

What to Verify in Operation and Maintenance Estimates

Before comparing prices, asking the vendor the following four questions makes it easier to compare the contents of the estimates.

  • Who will check, and how often, the list of models and APIs in use and their end-of-service schedules
  • The procedure for switching models and how far the evaluation questions will be run
  • The boundary between work included in the monthly fee and work not included
  • The list of items to be handed over when maintenance ends

The overall approach to estimates is explained in Breakdown of AI Implementation Costs. I believe that estimating operation and maintenance costs based on how many times per year the above work occurs is closer to reality than setting it as a percentage of development costs.

When No One Can Oversee Operation and Maintenance

In the responsibility table above, if your organization has no one to bridge the gap between the person judging answer correctness and the person doing the technical work, then even when a notice arrives, no one will act and the deadline will simply pass. The company you contracted for development may not handle maintenance either.

The role of getting involved on-site, building the bridge between the business and the AI, and taking responsibility for operations even after going live is called an FDE (Forward Deployed Engineer). What kind of role this is is described in What Is an FDE. How to compare potential vendors is summarized in How to Choose an FDE Support Company.
