How to Streamline Business Operations with AI: Which Tasks to Start With

Original title: AIで業務効率化を進める手順｜どの業務から手をつけるか

FDE Consulting · 2026-10-27 · 8 min read

Summary

This Japanese-language article uses Japan's Ministry of Internal Affairs and Communications 2026 (Reiwa 8) Information and Communications White Paper to argue that while 86.4% of Japanese firms use generative AI, adoption is concentrated in tasks like meeting minutes and email drafting, and 27% report no organizational initiatives. The author proposes a two-stage approach: first spread safe individual use of general AI tools, then redesign business processes around human-AI collaboration. He offers five criteria for choosing the first tasks (high volume, text-based, human-verifiable, semi-standardized, measurable) and a six-step implementation procedure. Stage-two bottlenecks are data readiness, access scoping, and accountability rather than model performance, which is where external help, including FDE-type roles, matters most.

Key points

  • 86.4% of Japanese firms use generative AI, but 27% have no organizational initiatives.
  • Two stages: individual productivity first, then redesigning whole business processes.
  • First tasks should be frequent, text-based, human-verifiable, and measurable.
  • Six steps: inventory tasks, select candidates, set rules, pilot templates, measure, redesign.
  • Stage-two blockers are data readiness, data access scope, and accountability, not AI capability.

Many companies want to improve work efficiency with AI. However, in many cases, after distributing tools, they stall with questions like "What work should we use it for?" or "We can't tell if it had any effect." In my view, most of the reasons they stall lie in not deciding in advance which tasks to start with and how far to go.

This article summarizes the steps for those driving work efficiency with AI. The data comes from the Ministry of Internal Affairs and Communications' 2026 Information and Communications White Paper. How to map out workflows is covered in AI × Human Workflow Design Techniques, and why deployed AI goes unused is covered in Why AI Implementations Fail, so here I will focus on "how to choose the first tasks" and "the order in which to proceed."

What Japanese companies are using AI for

According to the 2026 Information and Communications White Paper, 86.4% of Japanese companies said they use generative AI for some kind of work. This is a significant increase from the fiscal 2024 survey.

Usage is skewed. By type of work, use in "meeting minutes and email drafting assistance" was highest at about 70%, followed by "sales" at about 60% and "internal help desk" at about 50%. The proportion of companies that felt implementation effects was also about 70% for "meeting minutes and email drafting assistance," notably higher than other categories.

What they expect is also efficiency. In a question about possible impacts of using generative AI, Japan's top answer was "improving work efficiency through shorter work time" at 61.6%, far above "improving the quality of products and services" at 32.2% and "resolving staff shortages" at 26.8%. The white paper notes that while generative AI is viewed in Japan in the context of work efficiency, the other three countries may be taking a step further in how they view it.

On the other hand, this does not necessarily mean companies are tackling it as an organization. 27.0% of Japanese companies answered that there are "no organizational initiatives" for business transformation through generative AI, notably higher than the US, Germany, and China, with a particularly strong tendency among small and medium-sized enterprises.

In my view, these results can be read as "many people have started using it, but the company's workflows have not yet changed." Advancing work efficiency means closing this gap.

Work efficiency has two stages

The white paper presents the path to results from AI implementation as stages. First, as an organization, spread the use of easily adopted general-purpose AI in employees' individual tasks to deepen internal understanding of AI's characteristics and the tasks it can handle. Then, decide the form of collaboration between humans and AI, and transform entire business processes with AI as the premise—that is the order.

These two stages differ in what must be decided and the effort involved. I think of them as follows.

StageWhat to doMain costsWhat to decide internally
1. Speed up individual tasksCreate an environment where general-purpose AI tools can be used safely and spread know-how on how to use themTool fees, rule-making, sharing usage methodsWhat information may and may not be used, who to consult
2. Redesign workflowsDecide the division of roles between humans and AI and connect to internal systems and dataDesign and implementation effort, data preparation, operationsWhich tasks to change, who is accountable for results

The table is my own organization based on the white paper's stages. Stage 1 alone reduces task time. However, I believe company metrics move significantly only after entering Stage 2. Stage 1 is also preparation for Stage 2: when employees know what AI can and cannot do, discussions about which tasks to choose in Stage 2 move faster.

Why companies tend to stall at Stage 1

The white paper points out that even when general-purpose AI tools are introduced, only some proactive employees may use them, failing to lead to company-wide adoption and results. The leading companies interviewed spread adoption across the whole company through support measures such as collecting and sharing usage examples and holding regular workshops.

An example introduced in the white paper is Nissin Foods Group. By selecting tasks where generative AI could be expected to have an effect for each department, creating prompt templates, and gradually expanding initiatives to measure effects, the company's internal generative AI usage rate has reportedly recently exceeded 70%. What is instructive is that rather than just distributing tools, they put effort into selecting tasks, preparing templates, and measuring effects.

Five perspectives for choosing the first tasks

Which tasks you start with determines how smoothly things progress afterward. In my view, it is good to compare candidates against the following five criteria.

PerspectiveTypical examplesPoor fits
High frequency and volumeDaily emails, weekly meeting minutes, first responses to inquiriesPlanning done a few times a year
Text-based input and outputDocument drafting, summarization, classification, transcriptionTasks requiring physical inspection
Errors can be verified by humansDrafts that the person in charge reads before sendingResponses that go directly to customers
Procedures are somewhat standardizedRoutine applications, reports in fixed formatsNegotiations done differently every time
Effects can be measured numericallyProcessing time per item, number of redosWork where you can only say "it somehow got easier"

The examples in the table are for illustration and do not apply to every company. Few tasks meet all five criteria, so in my view, it is best to make tasks satisfying the top three the initial candidates. I believe the white paper's concentration of adoption and perceived effects in meeting minutes and email drafting assistance is because these tasks fit those three criteria well.

Conversely, tasks better avoided at the start are those where errors go directly outside and those with heavy responsibility for judgment. The white paper also notes that generative AI has phenomena such as hallucination, producing factually incorrect information, and sycophancy, excessively agreeing with users. Handle such tasks in Stage 2, after designing a process where humans verify the output.

Six steps for advancing work efficiency with AI

1. List the tasks

For each department, list the recurring tasks. Write not just the names, but also the time each takes and how many times per month it occurs. If you don't measure the time here, you won't be able to compare effects later.

2. Select candidates using the five perspectives

Compare the listed tasks against the five perspectives above and narrow the initial candidates down to one to three. Choosing too many at the start increases the effort of measuring effects and makes it unclear which one worked.

3. Decide what information may be used and the environment

If employees use AI at their own discretion, confidential information they input could leak outside. The white paper also states that by preparing an internal usage environment with measures against information leaks and establishing and communicating rules and guidelines for use, AI can be used safely for individual tasks. I summarized how to create rules in How to Create Internal Generative AI Usage Policies.

4. Create templates and start small

For each task you selected, prepare an example input and a template of instructions for the AI. Have a few people use it for a few weeks, and compare time and the number of retries using the same measurement as in Step 1. For tasks that did not go well, either fix the template or drop them from the candidates.

5. Record the results as numbers and spread them

For tasks that produced results, share the template and the numbers together within the company. Having numbers makes it easier for other departments to think about how it applies to their own work. How to compare costs and benefits is described in How to calculate the ROI of AI investment.

6. Redesign the entire workflow

Once you see results in individual tasks, move on to Stage 2. The white paper states that you need to identify the characteristics of the business area and the scope of responsibility that humans should bear, consider "which tasks should use AI as an aid to human thinking and judgment, and which tasks should be executed autonomously by AI," and design the entire business process. If you connect AI with internal systems and data, it becomes an organizational effort including investment decisions.

Where Stage 2 tends to get stuck

In my view, at Stage 2, things tend to get stuck on data and role division rather than AI performance.

The first is data. The white paper cites the observation by Professor Ichiro Sato of the National Institute of Informatics that whether you have data suitable for AI, in other words whether it is "AI-Ready," is the first step to success. If the documents you want the AI to read are still on paper or scattered among individual staff members, you need to organize them first.

The second is the scope of access. When you connect internal data to AI, there is a risk that the AI will answer with information that should not be seen depending on department or position. According to the white paper, even leading companies take measures such as narrowing the range of data the AI can reference.

The third is who is responsible for the results. If the AI creates a draft and a person checks and sends it, responsibility stays with the person. If you make the AI carry the process through on its own, decide in advance where a person can stop it. In my view, unless you draw this line together with the manager on the ground, Stage 2 work will never be used in production.

How to think about outsourcing

Stage 1 centers on selecting tools, making rules, and sharing how to use them. In my view, this can be done in-house. Stage 2 involves redrawing the workflow, connecting systems, and iterating until it is actually used. This is where, I believe, getting outside help makes the most sense.

When choosing whom to hire, rather than judging by the quality of proposals, check whether they will get into the field, redesign the workflow, and stay with you until it is used in production.
