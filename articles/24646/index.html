How to Run a Requirements Discovery Workshop: A Practical Guide for FDEs

Original title: 需求发现工作坊怎么做：FDE 实战指南

FDE Times · 2026-10-22 · 6 min read

Summary

This guide walks through how forward deployed engineers (FDEs) should run a discovery workshop, using a fictional logistics company's invoice-complaint process as a worked example. It argues that most deployment outcomes are decided in this phase, whose goal is a mutually agreed problem statement and definition of success rather than a wish list. Key practices: define one core question and a tight attendee list beforehand; map the process from start and end points inward; score roughly ten candidate tasks by value and effort and pick only one; ask early whether customer data can leave the internal network; and treat everything from the workshop as unverified assumptions to be tested with real data.

Key points

  • Deployment success is mostly decided in the discovery phase, which should produce an agreed problem statement and success definition.
  • Before the workshop, set one core question and keep attendance under ten, including frontline staff who do the actual work.
  • Map the process from both endpoints inward, noting who does each step, which systems, data, and where cases stall.
  • Score candidate tasks on frequency, repetition, coverage, and effort, then commit to only one.
  • Workshop outputs are assumptions; rank them by impact and uncertainty and validate with real customer work.

Key Takeaways

  • Most deployments succeed or fail based on the discovery phase; this phase should produce a problem statement and definition of success that both sides agree on.
  • Map the process from its start and end points toward the middle, then score each task by value and effort, and pick only one.
  • At the end of the workshop you'll only have hypotheses: rank them by impact and uncertainty, then test them against the customer's real work.

Nine in the morning, the customer's office. Twelve people sit in the conference room. The head of operations opens with: "We want to bring AI to the entire complaints department." The IT lead sits at the end of the table, arms crossed. The three employees who handle complaints every day haven't been asked a single question. You have ninety minutes.

For a Forward Deployed Engineer (FDE), this scenario is all too familiar, and how this meeting goes largely determines what follows. Scaler's FDE handbook puts it bluntly: most deployments succeed or fail depending on the discovery phase.

The goal is a problem statement and definition of success that both sides agree on, not a wish list.

Below is a complete case study told from start to finish. The case involves a fictional logistics company and its process for handling invoice complaints.

Before the meeting: one question, two markers, ten people or fewer

Koji's guide on how to run a discovery workshop recommends writing down the one question this meeting must answer. For this logistics company, the question shouldn't be "How can AI help customer service?" A better question is: "In the invoice complaint process, which step should be automated first, and how will success be measured?"

Triaster, a company specializing in process mapping, recommends defining the scope before the meeting and opening by clearly stating the start and end points of that scope. In this case, the process starts when a complaint email arrives in the shared inbox and ends when a credit note is issued or the complaint is rejected with a reason.

The attendee list matters just as much. Triaster believes that subject matter experts—people who actually do the work—must be present. Keep the headcount to ten or fewer, include someone who understands how the process connects with other departments, and secure the process owner's support.

Scaler's view is the same: don't just talk to management; sit down with the people who will actually use the system. So with twelve people in the room, the first task is trimming the list.

Keep the process owner, the three complaint handlers, the finance person who issues credit notes, the IT lead, and the warehouse manager. The head of operations may only need to join for the last fifteen minutes, which is exactly when priorities get set.

Map from both ends toward the middle

Once two markers are on the whiteboard, ask one of the handlers to walk through a real complaint they personally handled last week.

Write each step on a sticky note: receive email, categorize, look up the invoice in the ERP, cross-check against proof of delivery, request approval, issue credit note.

Under each sticky note, record four things: who does it, which system is used, what data is used, and where cases typically get stuck.

Some questions no one in the room can answer, such as which system holds the proof of delivery. Don't stop to chase every detail down. PostHog's FDE handbook recommends using the customer's real work to surface real problems, rather than trying to model everything upfront.

Mark the sticky note with a "?", assign someone to verify it, and move on.

Ten plausible tasks, pick only one

By the end of the mapping exercise, the whiteboard usually holds a dozen or more ideas that all sound plausible. PostHog believes an FDE's core judgment shows precisely here: knowing which one of ten plausible tasks to do first, and which ones not to do at all.

OpenAI Academy's prioritization matrix asks two questions for each workflow: if it succeeds, how much value does it deliver? How much effort and complexity does implementation require?

For estimating value, it offers three strong signals: frequency, repetitiveness, and coverage. Below is an illustrative scoring for the logistics case, on a 1-5 scale:

Candidate workflowFrequencyRepetitivenessCoverageEffortDecision
Categorizing complaint emails5432Do first
Matching invoices with proof of delivery4534Later, once the data is clarified
Monthly complaint report for the CFO1412Don't do
Predicting which customers will complain2225Don't do

This table isn't a formula. Its value lies in forcing everyone present to explain why they chose one item over another. The CFO report is easy to build, but it runs once a month and has a single reader. Complaint prediction sounds the most exciting, but it requires the most effort and its value is unclear.

This is when someone always asks: "Since we're already doing this, could we also do the prediction part while we're at it?" PostHog's advice is to push back on every "could we also...". Write the idea into the "later" column on the whiteboard so the person who raised it sees it wasn't discarded, but don't let it slip into today's scope.

Questions you can't skip

The first question goes to the IT lead, arms still crossed. Scaler stresses that if customer data isn't allowed to leave the internal network, you must know this before designing anything. Ask directly: Can complaint emails and ERP data be sent to an external model? Who signs off on approval?

The second question comes from PostHog's scoping checklist: does the described problem point to a deeper pattern? Suppose that during the walkthrough, one handler mentions that most complaints come from the same warehouse.

The root cause might be a problem with how that warehouse scans proof of delivery, and an agent that reads email would only be cleaning up the consequences. Since the warehouse manager is in the room, you can ask on the spot whether this hypothesis is worth checking.

The third question returns to the goal Scaler set: what does success look like? Before leaving the room, make sure you get a sentence both sides nod to, such as "the time from receiving an email to a complaint entering the correct queue drops from X to Y," where X is measured with real data over the coming week.

After the meeting: everything on the whiteboard is still a hypothesis

Koji is clear on this: a workshop doesn't produce evidence. The sticky notes, the scores, even the statement "most complaints come from the same warehouse"—all of it is hypothesis.

Koji also recommends ranking hypotheses by impact and uncertainty. In the logistics case, "complaint emails are clear enough to be categorized automatically" has high impact, because the first task rests entirely on it, and no one has measured it. "Most complaints come from the same warehouse" is equally high-impact and equally uncertain.

So the first thing to do after the meeting is test both hypotheses: take a sample of real complaints, say a week's worth of emails, run the categorization step, and count complaints by warehouse. This is exactly the approach PostHog recommends: use real work to surface problems.

Send out meeting notes within a day, including the core question, the process map, the scoring table, the "later" column, the answers about data, and the definition of success.

With the customer's name removed, this document can also be brought to interviews. On a resume, "ran a discovery workshop, narrowed the scope from ten ideas to one, and measured the outcome against agreed metrics" is far more convincing than "strong communication skills."

Mistakes that turn a workshop into a pointless meeting

One mistake is not defining a start and end point, resulting in mapping the entire company—ninety minutes pass without finding a single step worth automating.

Another mistake is making commitments in the meeting before asking about data restrictions. A single email from the security team can sink such commitments. The last mistake is treating the consensus reached in the meeting as verified fact and starting to build on it directly.

A good workshop doesn't end with applause. Its closing markers are: you have a task you can start on Monday morning, a number to measure, and a list of items you've already politely declined with "not for now."
