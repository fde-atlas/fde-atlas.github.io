Summary

Chapter 1 of this book traces the rise of the Forward Deployed Engineer (FDE). It opens with an MIT report finding that roughly 95% of enterprise AI spending produced no measurable return, with failures rooted in deployment and organizational fit rather than model quality. The author recounts Palantir's origins: unable to interview intelligence analysts, founders sent engineers on-site, treating customization as product discovery—a model that grew until FDEs outnumbered traditional engineers and underpinned Palantir's later financial success. The chapter defines the FDE as an engineer embedded at the customer, bridging the gap between what a product can do and what the customer needs, and distinguishes the role from pre-sales, outsourcing, and consulting. Generative AI made deployment the new battleground, driving OpenAI, Anthropic, and others to expand FDE teams. It closes with responsibilities, required traits, outcome-based pricing, hiring practices, and a five-layer toolkit.

Key points

  • MIT report: ~95% of enterprise AI spending shows no measurable financial return.
  • Palantir invented on-site engineering because analysts couldn't be interviewed; customization became product discovery.
  • FDE defined as an engineer embedded at customers, bridging product capability and real needs.
  • Generative AI made deployment capability decisive; OpenAI, Anthropic, Salesforce expanded FDE hiring.
  • FDE work is judged by outcomes: result-linked pricing, pre-agreed success metrics, changed customer behavior.

Forward Deployed Engineer: The Secret to Delivering Customer Value in the Age of AI

Chapter 1: The Rise of the FDE

"The biggest challenge of building software for spies is: I don't know any spies." — Bob McGrew, early Palantir executive and former Chief Research Officer at OpenAI

1.1 First, a Story About a Dead Nine Million Dollars

The story always begins the same way. In the conference room of a large enterprise, a vendor's demo has just ended. The large language model answers every question fluently, the data dashboards dazzle, and even the most demanding executives can find no fault. The CEO decides on the spot: sign. A contract worth millions of dollars, handshakes, photos, a press release.

Nine months later, the project is dead.

Not a dramatic death—a silent one. The system is still running, the servers are still on, but not a single business unit is actually using it. The vendor delivered every feature in the contract, and the enterprise paid every invoice. The only thing that never arrived was "value."

If you think this was just bad luck, MIT's famous 2025 report will tell you: this is the norm. They interviewed 52 organizations, collected 153 executive surveys, and reviewed more than 300 public enterprise AI projects, reaching a single conclusion: of the 30 to 40 billion dollars burned, 95% produced no measurable financial return.

95%. In other words, the main theme of enterprise AI adoption is not success, but failure on an institutional scale.

What's more interesting is how they fail. The report specifically notes: the problem is not the models. The models that dazzled everyone in demos remain just as smart in production—they simply "don't learn from feedback, don't retain context, and don't integrate into workflows." They look like products but behave like exhibits. Stanford's same-year landscape data corroborates this: 88% of organizations are using AI, yet only a single-digit percentage of agent applications actually make it into production. McKinsey delivered the final blow: only 6% of enterprises can say AI contributes more than 5% to their profits.

In the days after the report was published, a complaint from a manufacturing COO spread through the industry: "Online they say everything has changed, but back on our shop floor, nothing has moved." (Source: Appendix C) This stings more than any statistic—his company doesn't lack budget or tools; what it lacks is someone to embed the tools into the real workflows of the shop floor.

Around the same time, the report's lead author told a contrasting story. Some startups run by nineteen- and twenty-year-olds reached twenty million dollars in annual revenue with generative AI. Their playbook was the exact opposite of established enterprises: pick a single pain point, drive through it, and bind tightly to the customers who would actually use their product. Older companies try to swallow everything in one bite; the young ones take a small bite, chew through it, then take the next. (Source: Appendix C)

Remember this contrast. It is essentially the trailer for Chapter 2 of this book.

In fact, this chasm wasn't dug in the AI era. China's enterprise software industry has been lying in it for over a decade: big companies demand customization, vendors lose money on every deal, the delivered code is abandoned once the team leaves, and everyone ends up reduced to being "the client's outsourcing shop." One practitioner put it harshly: customization is the natural enemy of SaaS, and this curse can only dissolve when the market matures. The American version is more presentable, but the script is similar: sales signs the deal, implementation moves in, and six months later a "feature-complete but unloved" system is delivered, followed by endless wrangling.

At the root is the same wall: the place where software is built and the place where value is created are not the same place. On one side of the wall, requirements are relayed through tickets, meeting minutes, and weekly reports, losing fidelity with every handoff; on the other side, the customer's real workflows hide in spreadsheets no one documents, in conventions passed down by word of mouth, in the tacit knowledge of "you have to ask Old Wang about this." The software industry has invented countless ladders to climb over this wall—requirements documents, user research, implementation methodologies, customer success systems—but the wall remains.

Until one company decided: stop climbing the wall—send the people over.

1.2 Palantir's Victory

In 2003, Silicon Valley was just climbing out of the rubble of the dot-com bust. Peter Thiel and a few Stanford-educated young people founded a company named after The Lord of the Rings—Palantir, the seeing-stone, a mythical crystal that could see far away. What they set out to do sounded like science fiction: build data analysis software for US intelligence agencies, connecting fragments scattered across countless classified databases into a coherent picture to help analysts catch terrorists.

This business had a premise that would drive any product manager to a breakdown on the spot. Years later, early executive Bob McGrew recounted the episode vividly on a podcast:

"Our goal when we started was to build software for the intelligence community—to put it plainly, software for spies. And one challenge of building software for spies is: I don't know any spies, and you probably don't either. Even if you happen to find a spy and ask them, 'How do you actually do your work?', they usually won't tell you."

No user interviews, no requirements documents, no usability testing. The first lesson of internet startup methodology was rendered completely useless here. Co-founder Stephen Cohen's solution was adorably crude: build a demo prototype, show it to people at the intelligence agencies, and ask what they thought. The response was blunt: "This is terrible—it has nothing to do with what we do." Cohen didn't retreat; he followed up: "Then how would you want it to be different?" Then he pulled out a notebook, wrote everything down point by point, went back to revise, and came back with the new version.

This clumsy loop was the embryo of the FDE. It contained two intuitions later proven priceless: first, customers in complex domains don't know what they want until they see something that works; second, the fastest way to learn what customers want is to put the people who build things next to the people who use them.

The person who upgraded this intuition into company strategy was employee number 13, Shyam Sankar. As Palantir moved from its first customer to its second and third, the team discovered a counterintuitive fact: what each customer needed differed in subtle but critical ways. The standard solution is to extract commonalities, build a general-purpose product, and say no to differences. But Palantir's customers were the CIA, the FBI, US troops on the battlefield—saying no meant losing the deal. Sankar did the opposite: build a platform flexible enough to customize, then station engineers on-site at customers to finish the last mile.

His most crucial move was changing the accounting of this practice. In the software industry's ledger, "customization for a single customer" is called services—the enemy of margins. Sankar flipped it: on-site customization is not a cost; it is product discovery. Every pitfall engineers stepped into at a customer site became a signpost for the platform's next evolution.

Sankar himself was the first Forward Deployed Engineer, and his earliest field deployment is practically the origin story of the role. Around 2007, the biggest source of US casualties in Iraq was roadside bombs. The interagency task force established to combat them allowed Sankar to bring a small team and a "still very rough" product into a classified information room for two weeks of joint work. A classified information room is a physically isolated secure space where even speakerphones are banned. Sankar came up with a barbaric workaround: he strapped a phone to his head with an elastic band to free both hands for coding—one ear listening to analysts' feedback, the other listening to colleagues back at Silicon Valley headquarters. Nineteen-hour days for two weeks: demoing, ingesting data, collecting feedback, fixing things on the spot. At the end, the analysts said: this thing is useful. Sankar himself was wrecked, calling CEO Karp: "This isn't sustainable—we're done for." Karp's answer became company culture: turn this "unsustainability" into an institution. (Source: Appendix C)

Years later, colleagues recalled that even Sankar's criticism carried no emotion. Once, while he was chatting happily with colleague Mebri at an airport café, the man suddenly received a sternly worded critique email from him—written and sent on the spot, from the seat across the table. "There was no personal attack in it, only one subtext: to win, I owe you this truth." (Source: Appendix C)

This playbook was soon tested in blood on the battlefield. Palantir engineers embedded on-site discovered that soldiers didn't need fancy intelligence dashboards at all—they just wanted a small tool to mark "this road is suspicious" on a map, because roadside bombs were the biggest killer of patrols. An engineer threw together a crude map tool on the spot: soldiers could tap to flag dangerous road segments, visible in real time to the whole unit. The tool saved lives and was later hardened into a standard feature of the platform. Note this: it could never have been born in any headquarters conference room—only in that moment when engineers and soldiers looked at the same stretch of road together.

On the commercial side, there was first a death. Palantir's first enterprise product was called Metropolis, and the market response was dismal—only a handful of financial companies were barely using it. The second attempt, Foundry, finally got things going, and the turning point was Airbus: at the Toulouse factory, a fuel pump fault on the A380 kept recurring, and Airbus's own engineers spent two years without a clue. Palantir's people came in, hooked the sensor data into the platform, and cracked the case in two weeks—during climb, fuel sloshed away from the pump. A trivial fix that preserved an order reportedly worth tens of billions of dollars. Airbus's head of digitalization later remarked publicly: "The same problem used to take us twenty-four months to investigate." (Source: Appendix C) From then on, Airbus became Palantir's staunchest champion in Europe, building its entire data platform Skywise on top of it, connecting tens of thousands of aircraft and more than fifty thousand users.

There's also a piece of gossip worth recording. Legend has it that Palantir's software was involved in the 2011 operation that killed Osama bin Laden. This claim has never been confirmed, nor ever refuted—journalists writing biographical coverage of Palantir deliberately left this ambiguous footnote in place. But true or not, the rumor itself is Palantir's best sales weapon. (Source: Appendix C)

Before 2016, the number of Palantir Forward Deployed Engineers at one point exceeded that of traditional software engineers. Half or more of the engineers at a software company weren't writing products at headquarters—they were scattered across customer sites around the world. Wall Street couldn't make sense of it for years, dismissing it as "human wave tactics" and "more like a consulting firm."

Then, time delivered the verdict. In 2023 it launched its AI platform, AIP, paired with a playbook called "bootcamps" (detailed in Chapter 8), compressing the enterprise software sales cycle from nine to twelve months down to a few weeks. In Q4 2025, its "Rule of 40"—revenue growth rate plus profit margin, the software industry's health metric where 40 is a passing grade—hit 127%; in Q1 2026, 145%. Single-quarter bookings of $4.26 billion, net revenue retention of 139%, and $7.2 billion in cash on hand. Karp said only one line on the earnings call: "We are a species of our own." Market cap at one point surged past $400 billion. (Source: Appendix C)

Those who once mocked its human wave tactics fell silent. Over twenty years, Palantir proved one thing: that wall cannot be climbed with a ladder, but it can be climbed by people. The people who climbed it were given a formal name—Forward Deployed Engineer.

1.3 What Is an FDE

A One-Sentence Definition

The definition used in this book comes from the best explicator of this model, Bob McGrew—early in his career an engineer at PayPal, later an early executive at Palantir, and then Chief Research Officer at OpenAI; ChatGPT and GPT-4 both came out of the teams he led:

A Forward Deployed Engineer is an engineer stationed at the customer's site, filling the gap between "what the product can do" and "what the customer needs."

In this plain sentence, every word matters.

"Stationed on-site" means your working context is embedded at the customer: joining the customer's group chats, reading the customer's data, attending the customer's meetings, getting to know the person who "knows why the process is the way it is"—not necessarily sitting in the customer's office every day. The "gap" is the reason this role exists: where the product works out of the box, you're not needed; the deeper the gap, the more you're needed—intelligence, finance, manufacturing, healthcare, legal. "Engineer" is the most crucial qualifier: you write production code, not reports. When Palantir named the role, it deliberately kept the words "software engineer" to emphasize to the world: this is not a consulting position. As for "Forward Deployed," it's a military term for troops deployed at the front lines—putting your most combat-effective people closest to the problem.

What It Is Not

The fastest way to understand a new role is by elimination.

It is not pre-sales. Pre-sales work ends before the contract is signed; the goal is winning the deal, and the deliverable is a slide deck. An FDE's work enters deep water only after the contract is signed; the goal is winning the outcome, and the deliverable is a system running in production. Pre-sales makes the customer believe "this can be done"; the FDE makes it actually happen.

It is also not on-site outsourcing—a distinction that matters especially for Chinese readers, because "engineers stationed on-site" has a long and inglorious history in China. The first batch of domestic service providers flying the FDE banner drew the line with on-site staffing in three sentences on their websites: on-site staffing is paid by the hour, while FDEs deliver by phase and are accepted on results; on-site staff write everything from scratch, while FDEs do engineering on top of a product foundation; on-site staffing drags on longer and longer, and when people leave the system stops, while FDEs finish and leave, with the capability remaining in the system and the customer's team. In one sentence: on-site staffing sells headcount; FDEs sell outcomes.

Jove, head of FDEs at Cresta, a Silicon Valley AI customer service company, drew this boundary in even finer detail in a video conversation. His team is expanding from 30 to 100 people this year, and his judgment is: an FDE only makes sense when bound to an AI platform—if you're just doing traditional data integration and system setup, it's hard to distinguish from a traditional implementation engineer or outsourcing. He also has one hard rule for hiring: in the age of AI agents, not knowing how to code is like being illiterate. Another mechanism worth noting is the dual mandate: FDEs are not only responsible for making deployments succeed, but also carry the metric of "making the product more mature"—what is learned in the field must feed back into the platform. (Source: Appendix C)

It is not a consultant. Consultants deliver recommendations per project and are not responsible for execution; FDEs are responsible for the system's final operation, with the endpoint being "the customer's team can use it independently." The collaboration between Anthropic and fintech company FIS is a specimen case: engineers embedded at FIS co-built an anti-money-laundering AI agent, compressing investigations from hours to minutes, but the stated goal of the collaboration was not to hand over a system—it was "to transfer knowledge so FIS can build agents on its own in the future." A consultant wants you to need him forever; an FDE wants you to eventually not need him.

It is also not a traditional product engineer. Product engineers face abstract users—personas (what users look like), funnels (how many people go from visit to payment), daily active user counts; FDEs face concrete customers—a bank's risk control department, a farm in Iowa, a patrol on the outskirts of Baghdad. Inside Palantir there is a famous role division: platform engineers handle "one capability serving many customers," while Forward Deployed Engineers (internal codename "Delta") handle "one customer, mobilizing many capabilities." What platform engineers pursue is a feature that works everywhere; what FDEs pursue is thoroughly solving the problem of the one customer in front of them first.

How Did This Trend Take Off

A role invented in 2003—why did it only become a superstar in 2025?

The most direct trigger was generative AI. Large language models created an unprecedented gap: anyone can produce a stunning demo in five minutes, but wiring that demo into an enterprise's real data, permissions, compliance, and workflows is an order of magnitude harder. The model companies gradually figured it out: the next battleground is not model quality, but deployment capability.

The numbers sketch how steep the curve is: in the first nine months of 2025, FDE job postings grew eightfold; on YC's job board, more than a hundred startups listed this role that barely existed three years earlier. The venture firm a16z directly called it "the hottest job in tech," adding a vivid analogy: enterprises buying AI is like your grandma getting an iPhone—she wants to use it, but she needs you to set it up for her.

A cluster of Financial Times reports from November 2025 offers the best snapshot of this trend. Fournier, head of forward deployed engineering for OpenAI in Europe, said his team was founded just a year ago and is about to grow to 50 people: "Demand has exceeded our expectations." De Jong, Anthropic's head of applied AI, put it more colorfully: "The needs of a Fortune 500 bank and those of an AI-native startup are two entirely different species." — which is why her team grew fivefold in a year. Pretorius-John, head of Palantir UK, distilled the company's creed into one sentence: "Software is only valuable when it truly matters to the end customer." Even Cohere, a model company, had its CEO Gomez step up in support: "We embed engineers from the very start of a contract, and pull back once the customer is running smoothly." (Sources in Appendix C)

The intensity of the talent grab can be measured by three hard numbers: OpenAI's forward deployed team started with 2 people and grew to 52 in a year; Salesforce, which literally wrote the textbook on "not doing implementation yourself," publicly committed to hiring 1,000 FDEs; even Deloitte, a firm that sells consulting for a living, launched a dedicated FDE business line in December 2025. (Sources in Appendix C)

Then came the giants voting with their feet. On May 11, 2026, OpenAI announced the creation of a "deployment company": majority-owned by itself, joined by TPG, Bain Capital, Brookfield and 19 other top-tier investors, with initial funding of over $4 billion and a pre-money valuation reported by the media at around $10 billion — along with the acquisition of a consulting firm with 150 deployment engineers. Hours later, Anthropic was reported to be forming a rival venture with Blackstone. On the same day, the two largest model companies elevated "deployment" from a cost center to a strategic asset — capital markets voted for the FDE in the most expensive way possible.

The deepest reason is best captured by McGrew: "AI agents are a category with no incumbent, so there is an enormous amount of product discovery to do." What CRM software should look like had a standard answer twenty years ago; what agents should look like, nobody knows — including the customers themselves. The answer can only be found on-site at the customer.

History has come full circle here: in 2003, Palantir invented the FDE because it "didn't know how spies worked"; in 2025, the entire industry collectively embraced the FDE because it "didn't know how agents should work inside enterprises." Twenty-two years, the same answer.

1.4 The FDE's Responsibilities and Traits

A résumé born for this role

If you want to answer "what kind of profession is the FDE," McGrew's career is almost the textbook answer.

His first job was at PayPal, as an early engineer. That group later became known as the "PayPal Mafia," profoundly shaping all of Silicon Valley. After leaving, he joined Palantir in its startup days and rose to executive level, personally witnessing the FDE evolve from an emergency measure into company strategy. When he managed product and engineering teams, he offered a famous metaphor: Forward Deployed Engineers build "gravel roads" to value on-site at customers, and the product team decides which gravel roads are worth widening and paving into "highways" that serve the next ten customers.

Later, he became OpenAI's Chief Research Officer, leading the development of ChatGPT, GPT-4, and the o1 reasoning model. In other words, this is someone who built the platform on one side of the wall, crossed over to the field on the other side, and ultimately built the very technology that made that wall unprecedentedly high.

An interesting moment occurred at an AI conference hosted by YC in 2025. McGrew expected founders to crowd around him asking "how did you invent ChatGPT," but everyone chased after the same question: How exactly does Palantir's FDE model work? The inventor of ChatGPT was most pressed about a delivery methodology. That detail is the best footnote to the direction of the times.

Three layers of traits

Synthesizing more than twenty job postings from various companies and firsthand accounts from practitioners, the FDE's traits can be summarized in three layers.

The first layer: a broad enough technical generalist. An FDE doesn't need to be the deepest expert in any one field, but must be able to solve full-stack problems independently on-site at the customer: writing code, debugging APIs, understanding data pipelines, working with the cloud, grasping the temperaments of large models, and knowing the "utilities" of enterprise environments — single sign-on (log in once, access everywhere), permissions, and compliance certifications. The market has a clear price tag for this combination: a 2026 compensation report shows that mid-level FDEs at top AI labs have a median total annual compensation of about $385,000, senior about $610,000, and principal over $1 million — higher than most pure R&D roles at the same level, because the market knows how scarce such people are. (Sources in Appendix C)

The second layer: the ability to translate technology into business outcomes. This is the dividing line between an FDE and an ordinary engineer. One frontline practitioner's words are widely quoted: "The model is usually the cleanest part. The hard part is finding the workflow nobody documented, the data source people actually trust, and the person who knows why the process is the way it is." Palantir's hiring bar puts it even more bluntly: "The candidate's expressiveness, clarity, and ease of communication should make me happy to have them run a meeting with a customer." Interviews also screen for this translation ability. The FDE interviews at OpenAI and Palantir feature a signature segment called "problem decomposition": you're handed a huge, vague, real enterprise problem, and for sixty minutes you write not a single line of code — they only watch how you ask follow-up questions, how you scope the problem, and how you build order out of chaos. The interviewer's advice: understand the problem before diving in — slow is smooth, and smooth is fast. In an ordinary interview, "I optimized the query by 40%" is a perfect answer; in an FDE interview, the perfect answer is: "I optimized the query by 40%, which got the customer's analysts their reports two hours earlier every day and tripled the team's processing capacity." A technical achievement only counts as answered once it's converted into the customer's language.

The third layer: ownership, plus a bit of "rebelliousness." A saying circulates among practitioners that captures exactly the accountability this role demands: "Deployment goes down at 2 a.m. You don't file a ticket, you don't blame another team, you don't go back to bed. You fix it. Period." Palantir also has a subtler expectation for business-side roles: deep domain knowledge combined with the courage to be a "rebel" — able to see the absurdity in the customer's status quo and bold enough to drive tenfold change, not incremental change. CEO Karp's behavioral benchmark back in the day was the "French waiter": embedded in the service process, sensitive to real needs, yet with enough confidence and taste to guide the customer from "what they think they want" to "what is actually good for them."

What a day looks like

In day-to-day terms, an FDE's time is roughly split like this: forty to fifty percent embedded on the customer side writing code and tuning systems, twenty to thirty percent aligning direction with customer management, decomposing problems, and making architectural decisions, ten to twenty percent distilling patterns learned in the field back into the company's product line, and the rest on eval optimization and knowledge sharing — writing playbooks, internal evangelism, and training customer teams.

Hidden in this schedule is an important message: the FDE is not an "engineer sent abroad," but an "engineer with a two-way mission" — delivering results to the customer on one end, and feeding intelligence back to the company on the other. That is also the theme of the next section.

1.5 Everything Speaks Through Results

If the FDE's working creed had to be summed up in one sentence, it would be: everything speaks through results.

First, distinguish "data" from "results." The history of enterprise software has never lacked projects with great-looking data and terrible results: feature checklists 100% ticked off, but only 5% of users actually using them; four-nines system availability, while business departments would rather keep using spreadsheets. The vast majority of the 95% of failed projects in that MIT report were not lacking dashboards — the numbers were all there, but the value never arrived, because no one was accountable for "that line on the financial statement."

The FDE model institutionally ensures that "results" are not diluted, through three specific mechanisms.

  • Tying payment to outcomes: In its early government projects, Palantir made heavy use of "pay only if it works" arrangements. McGrugh recalled it bluntly: "Early on, it made sense for the startup to bear all the risk—you pay us once it works." This logic has evolved into something more refined in the AI era: Sierra charges by "resolved conversations"—no resolution, no payment; many FDE service providers deliver in phases and accept payment based on outcomes. Once billing is tied to results, everything the delivery team does gets re-prioritized—you won't spend three weeks polishing a feature nobody uses, because "nobody uses it" comes out of your own pocket.
  • Defining success before work begins: The first step of an FDE project is not writing code; it is defining "what counts as success" together with the customer. Palantir's bootcamps require customers to first lock down an extremely focused core battlefield—"reduce scheduling conflicts on a production line by 30%," not "explore AI-empowered manufacturing"—precisely to prevent the project from drifting into the unfalsifiable under the banner of "exploration." The OpenAI and John Deere collaboration is a textbook example: first, they reviewed hundreds of real work cases together with agronomy experts and built a custom evals system, and only then began iterating on the model. The final figure of "up to 70% reduction in chemical usage" was not a marketing narrative packaged after the fact; it was the target set before work began.
  • The final judge is behavioral change in the customer's organization: The report contains a pointed finding: only about 40% of enterprises provide employees with official AI tool subscriptions, while as many as 90% of employees routinely use personal consumer-grade products to solve work problems. This means many projects that are "successfully launched" actually exist in a state of "official systems idling while employees go around them." In the FDE philosophy, system launch is not the milestone; the customer team changing how it works is. Sierra deliberately names the role "agent engineer," and when explaining its hiring criteria, CEO Møller said: they only take on two kinds of problems—genuinely hard ones and ones with real business impact—and both must hold at the same time.

"Let results speak for everything" sounds like common sense, but in practice it offends the entire structure of interests: sales can no longer over-promise, because the delivery team is accountable for outcomes; the customer's IT department can no longer get by with a "feature checklist," because the business unit's usage rate becomes the acceptance criterion; and the FDE can no longer claim exemption with "I built it to spec," because whether the spec itself was right also goes on their tab. This is exactly why this role is precious—and why it is expensive.

1.6 The Four Faces of the FDE in a Team

An FDE lives simultaneously in four worlds; he is the connector between them.

  • To the customer, he is an "embedded product manager + full-stack engineer": He observes the customer's real work like an anthropologist—the most valuable discoveries often come from "watching," not "asking"—and like an entrepreneur, he builds things on the spot right where he observes. Palantir institutionalized this two-person pairing: the Deployment Strategist, codenamed "Echo," is responsible for understanding the customer's mission, stakeholders, and adoption path, while the Forward Deployed Engineer, codenamed "Delta," is responsible for technical implementation. Working as a pair, one diagnoses and one builds; neither is dispensable.
  • To the company's product lines, he is an "outpost and intelligence officer": This is the most fundamental difference between FDEs and traditional delivery teams. Traditional implementation is a cost of sales—every person-day spent must be earned back from the contract; a healthy FDE organization treats field work as R&D—when three customers hit the same integration gap, that is not three troubles, that is a piece of product intelligence; when five deployments all need the same workflow, it should be abstracted into the platform's next standard capability. A former Palantir engineer recalled that key components of the Foundry platform were born at customer sites as far-flung as Zurich, Houston, São Paulo, and Toulouse, grew bottom-up, and eventually fed back into a product generating billions of dollars in annual revenue. (Source: Appendix C)
  • To sales, he is an "amplifier of trust": Enterprise customers have been let down too many times and are immune to all slides. The FDE rebuilds trust through two actions: first, hands-on work—building something that runs on the spot, on the customer's own data, in the customer's own environment; second, honesty—daring to say no to the customer's flawed premises. Palantir's bootcamps industrialized this trust-production process: customers bring real data, a deployable prototype is built in one to five days, and executives click through it with their own hands. Early bootcamps had a paid conversion rate of only 5% to 10%; the company has disclosed that later conversion rates approached 75%. Trust can be engineered into existence. (Source: Appendix C)
  • To the organization itself, he is a "talent crucible": An easily overlooked fact: Palantir has produced a strikingly dense community of founders. This is no surprise—the daily training of an FDE is to build something valuable end-to-end and get people to use it, in an environment of constrained resources, ambiguous requirements, and complex stakeholder relationships—almost a full rehearsal of founder training. Srinivas, who later founded Decagon; Møller, who built Sierra's agent engineering team; and several authors of the most widely circulated methodology articles in this industry all came out of Palantir's forward deployed engineering roles. One company's talent spillover became an entire industry's talent infrastructure. (Source: Appendix C)

Taken together, these four identities point to the same conclusion: the FDE is not a box on an org chart, but an upgrade in how the organization learns—turning "understanding the customer" from secondhand information relayed through layers into an engineer's firsthand muscle memory.

1.7 How to Recruit FDEs

A bucket of cold water first: the FDE is one of the hardest roles to hire for in the software industry, because it requires one person to excel simultaneously on two dimensions that usually trade off against each other.

Barry, a former Palantir engineer, put it plainly in a retrospective article: Palantir's standard for hiring FDEs was "engineers who could get into Google or Facebook"—because they go to customer sites to build systems, not to tune parameters; but technical skill alone is far from enough—people in forward deployed engineering also need creativity, judgment, and customer-facing charisma. He added a stinging remark: this is far more expensive and difficult than hiring a traditional pre-sales team.

Breaking down hiring-market practice, FDE recruiting has three key stages.

  • Candidate profile: Hire "curious bulldozers," not "refined craftsmen." a16z's advice to startups used the phrase "curious doers": strong initiative, a lack of deference to the status quo, and hunger for the customer's problems. McGrugh put it more concretely: FDE teams need two kinds of people—"domain rebels," who know the industry but don't worship its conventions; and "prototype speedsters," who prioritize speed over perfection and accept that the first version will be thrown away and rewritten. Conversely, two types favored in traditional engineering culture are red flags for the FDE role: the "craftsman" who places code elegance above customer outcomes, and the "loyal executor" who treats every word from the customer as gospel.
  • Interviews: Use a "problem decomposition round" instead of formulaic questions. The "problem decomposition" mentioned earlier is the soul of the FDE interview: give the candidate a vague, sprawling problem with real business rough edges—"A bank's compliance team manually reviews 30,000 transaction alerts every day, 90% of which are false alarms; what would you do?"—and then observe for sixty minutes. What is being assessed is not the answer but the process: did they clarify constraints before acting, did they distinguish root causes from symptoms, did they remember that a real user sits at the other end of the system, and can they articulate the trade-offs clearly. Palantir also embeds about twenty minutes of behavioral questions into each technical round and explicitly rejects candidates who are technically strong but culturally mismatched—the most important item in the culture assessment is a sense of order in the face of ambiguity.
  • Pricing: Accept a hybrid structure of "engineer compensation plus operating flexibility." 2026 market data can serve as an anchor: the median total annual compensation for a Palantir Forward Deployed Engineer is roughly $215,000; mid-level FDEs at top AI labs earn about $385,000, senior ones about $610,000; Anthropic's roles have base salaries between $200,000 and $300,000. Another noteworthy detail is bonus design: Palantir's bonuses are often tied to operating metrics such as account expansion, sitting somewhere between engineering bonuses and sales commissions; a16z's advice is to align incentives with account managers, but don't put hard sales quotas on FDEs—that would steer behavior toward closing deals rather than delivering outcomes. Compensation structure is the final step in role definition: how you pay people ultimately shapes their behavior. (Sources in Appendix C)

1.8 How to Become an FDE

A different perspective: if you are an engineer, product manager, or consultant who wants to enter this fast-growing market, what's the path?

Start with a self-assessment: the glamour and the cost of this role are two sides of the same coin. In practitioner communities on forums, discussions of the FDE role show an unusual honesty. The positives: the best combination of technical substance and brand endorsement, one of the few roles where you can accumulate technical, business, and customer resources simultaneously. The costs: traveling a quarter to half of the time is the norm—OpenAI's job postings explicitly state travel of up to 50%; your work rhythm is defined by customer urgency, not your own schedule; and there is one recurring warning—the risk of burnout is real.

One comment is sharp enough to quote verbatim: "Some see it as a brand springboard, others say it's consulting with a cool title—both are right. The difference is whether your company channels field learning back into the product, or sells you by the person-day." That sentence is both a criterion for choosing an employer and the theme of Chapter 7 of this book. (Sources in Appendix C)

Then fill in the gaps: what you need to learn isn't technology, but the craft of "translation." A technical foundation is just the ticket to entry—most engineers have it; what's truly scarce are three translation skills: translating business problems into technical problems (Chapter 2), translating technical solutions into language executives understand (Chapter 3), and translating field experience into knowledge the team can reuse (Chapter 7). To practice these three, hands-on beats coursework: join a pre-sales engagement, do an on-site stint, deliver training to a real user, and see in which kind of discomfort you grow fastest.

For interview prep, rewrite your resume to be "customer-outcome oriented." The principle was stated earlier, but it bears repeating: every technical achievement on your resume must complete the last mile into customer language. "Built a retrieval-augmented system" is engineer language; "the retrieval-augmented system I built cut customer service first-response time from 4 hours to 8 minutes, and the customer proactively asked to expand it at renewal" is FDE language. Also prepare two kinds of stories: one about establishing order amid unclear requirements, and one honest failure—Palantir-style interviewers are obsessed with "tell me about a real failure," because the essence of this job is advancing through uncertainty, and people who won't admit failure have no capacity to evolve.

When choosing a company, ask three questions in return. First, "What is your product platform?"—an FDE without a platform foundation is pure labor outsourcing. Second, "How does field learning flow back into the product?"—ask them for a recent example of field experience that became a product feature; if they can't produce one, that's a red flag. Third, "Who does the FDE report to?"—reporting into product or engineering usually means the model is taken seriously; reporting into sales means you risk becoming a labor pool for pre-sales.

1.9 The FDE's Everyday Toolbox

To close this chapter, here is the current tool landscape for this role. Tools will become outdated, but the capability layers behind them won't. Five layers, from beneath your feet to behind your back.

  • Platform foundation layer | The company's arsenal. The premise of the FDE model is "bringing the platform into the field"; otherwise it degenerates into custom development. Palantir's Foundry and AIP center on something called the "ontology"—modeling an enterprise's data, logic, and actions into a semantic layer so that AI runs on a foundation that "understands the business"; OpenAI's model APIs and agent toolchain; Sierra's agent platform. When evaluating any FDE opportunity, the thickness of this layer is the first priority.
  • The AI engineering layer, the individual's craft. The daily craft after 2025 includes: prompt engineering and context management; retrieval augmentation, letting the model look things up before answering; evals, establishing quantifiable yardsticks for fuzzy business quality—this is the signature skill that distinguishes the AI-era FDE from traditional implementation engineers; agent architecture, tool calling, multi-agent collaboration, keeping humans in the loop at critical junctures; and engineering optimizations for cost and speed.
  • The data and integration layer, the first battle upon entering the field. Nearly every FDE project spends its first week wrestling with this layer: data pipelines, enterprise system connectors, permissions and identity authentication, vector databases for AI retrieval, and data governance and anonymization (removing sensitive information). As one practitioner put it: seventy percent of project delays get stuck here, but it's invisible in the demo—it's the part of the iceberg below the waterline.
  • The delivery and collaboration layer, survival gear in the customer's environment. Working within the customer's security perimeter means "dual adaptation": you must master your own modern toolchain while also bending to the customer's environment—it may be an intranet physically isolated from the internet, you may only be able to deploy in the customer's cloud environment, and you may not even be able to access code hosting sites. Containerization (the technology of packaging up environments and taking them with you), infrastructure as code (managing server environments with code), and contingency plans for "getting the environment running in an offline conference room" all belong to this layer.
  • The knowledge accumulation layer, the lever for scale. This is the most easily overlooked layer, yet it determines whether the team can escape "revenue growing linearly with headcount": playbooks, component libraries, deployment checklists, and the writing habit of turning "a solution for one customer" into "a pattern for a class of customers." Chapter 7 will expand on it in depth.

Taken together, the five-layer toolbox is the complete outline of this role: standing on a platform, wielding engineering in hand, eyes fixed on outcomes, back connected to the product line.

That is the FDE. Over the next seven chapters, we enter the heart of the methodology, starting from the most fundamental choice in a project—how to make sure you are solving the right problem.

Chapter 2: Solving the Right Problem

"On the wrong problem, all execution is wasted."

2.1 An Autopsy of the Proof-of-Concept Graveyard

Silicon Valley has a term: "POC purgatory." Projects that enter it can't get out: not dead enough to declare failure, not alive enough to justify more investment. So they "continue to advance" year after year in quarterly reports, like a ward full of patients on life support.

MIT researchers performed a systematic autopsy on this graveyard. They identified five major roadblocks on the path to scaling enterprise AI projects, ranked by frequency of occurrence:

  • Employees unwilling to use the new tools—ironically, these same people use ChatGPT enthusiastically in private every day
  • Concerns about the quality of model outputs
  • Poor user experience
  • Lack of executive support
  • Difficult change management

Note what's absent from this list: models not smart enough, compute not cheap enough, technology not advanced enough—none are on it. What kills these projects happens almost entirely at the level of "problem definition" and "organizational reality," not the technical level.

One detail from the report, cited again and again, deserves its own telling. A company spent $50,000 on a professional contract analysis tool with an impressive feature list. But one senior lawyer at the company simply wouldn't use it—she kept drafting contracts with free ChatGPT. Her reason was simple: the purchased tool's summaries were too rigid and couldn't be customized to her habits. The procurement department's report said "deployed," but the real daily reality was the official system idling while employees went around it. (Sources in Appendix C) This detail reveals the true cause of the first roadblock: it's not that employees are old-fashioned—it's that consumer products have spoiled their appetites. People who use these tools enthusiastically at home every day can't tolerate the "artificial stupidity" in the office.

There is an even more sobering comparison: projects done with external specialized vendors succeed at roughly twice the rate of internal builds. Why do external teams win more often? Not because they are smarter, but because they cannot afford to lose—when you get paid based on results, the cost of defining the wrong problem comes out of your own pocket. An internal project at a Japanese company is a textbook cautionary tale: three or four of the strongest engineers were pulled together into a task force, the demo was dazzling, leadership nodded—yet from day one, no one could answer "by what standard do we measure whether this system is good?" Six months later, the project quietly vanished from the reporting materials. No one declared it a failure; it simply died a natural death. (Source in Appendix C)

That is the first principle of this chapter: on the wrong problem, all execution is wasted; and the density of wrong problems inside enterprises far exceeds what anyone imagines. This chapter covers only one thing: how to make sure you are solving the right problem before writing the first line of code.

2.2 PSF: Finding the Problem-Solution Fit

A core concept in internet startup methodology is PMF (Product-Market Fit): when the product is right, the market pulls growth on its own. In the world of the FDE, the corresponding unit is not "product and market" but "problem and solution"—I call it PSF (Problem-Solution Fit).

The distinction is subtle but critical. PMF asks "is there a market for my product?"—a supply-side perspective. PSF asks "is this specific customer problem worth solving, and can it be solved with our capabilities?"—a demand-side perspective. Inside an enterprise customer, there may be hundreds of "what could AI do here" opportunity points, but only those that pass three gates are truly worth doing.

Gate one, the pain-point test: is this problem a specific pain for a specific person? Note that's two "specifics." "Improving customer service efficiency" is not a pain point; it's a direction. "The customer service manager spends three hours every Monday morning manually consolidating last week's escalated tickets from four systems, when her real job should be analyzing why escalations happen"—that is a pain point. Magruder offered an even sharper standard: go solve one of the five problems the CEO cares about most. The reasoning is pragmatic: only problems of that magnitude can help you crush internal bureaucratic resistance (Chapter 3 will expand on how to use this to get past the IT department). Møller, head of agent engineering at Sierra, puts the same principle another way: only take on two kinds of problems—those that are genuinely hard, and those with genuine business impact. Hard without impact is showing off; impactful but not hard, and it won't be yours to do.

Gate two, the economics test: how much is solving this problem worth? Many pain points are real but not valuable; projects that can't do this math won't survive the next budget season. You can estimate roughly: how many person-hours does this problem consume per week? What does that translate to in labor cost? How much does one error cost? What could the freed-up staff do instead? Palantir's bootcamp simply moves this gate to the front—before starting, the customer must lock in a "core battlefield" and give quantified targets, like "reduce scheduling conflicts by 30%" or "shorten inventory turnover by 15%." That report contains a widely cited finding that shows just how easily this gate gets skipped: more than half of enterprise AI budgets go to front-office sales and marketing, yet the returns concentrate in back-office contract review, procurement, and risk control—the "unsexy" areas. Everyone is solving "problems that demo well," not "problems that are worth money."

Gate three, the feasibility test: given our current capabilities and this customer's data reality, how far can we get? This is the gate most easily drowned by enthusiasm. Two questions must be answered on the spot: where is the data, and in what state? The answer is often worse than expected—scattered across seven systems, three versions that don't reconcile, the most authoritative copy living in some veteran employee's personal spreadsheet. And: what accuracy threshold does this problem require? Between "99% usable" and "90% usable" lies an order-of-magnitude difference in engineering effort, while for many business scenarios, 90% plus human review is actually the optimal solution—judging this requires not technology, but an understanding of business consequences.

Only after passing all three gates have you touched problem-solution fit. And these three gates must be passed on-site at the customer—making judgments from secondhand information in a headquarters conference room is the root cause of every error in this chapter.

2.3 Refusing the Expensive "Proof-of-Concept Graveyard"

In 2025, a Chinese vendor specializing in enterprise AI deployment wrote a rather blunt passage on its website to discourage potential customers: if the scenario isn't validated yet, go attend a demo event first; if your data can be exported in a single table, lightweight services are enough; if you just want to learn about AI, use the free options. The harshest line is the last: "FDE is a heavy investment. We would rather you start later than start at the wrong time." (Source in Appendix C)

That passage deserves to be pinned on the wall of everyone doing enterprise AI. It expresses a truth that runs counter to sales instinct: refusing the wrong projects is the most important profitability capability of the FDE model.

Why are wrong projects dangerous? Because the FDE cost structure is front-loaded—the best engineers, the most expensive travel, the longest on-site commitment all happen before payment arrives. Once you sink into the mire, it's not a matter of losing one deal; it's your entire elite team being tied up while opportunity costs avalanche.

  • Former Palantir engineer Barry recalls that the company once "burned millions of dollars on customer pilots; many projects had a profit margin that was literally negative infinity, because we did them for free." The perspective he adds next is the real point: Palantir could afford to burn because it treated pilots as an R&D portfolio to invest in—like venture capital, it didn't matter if most bets went to zero, as long as the winners won back everything. But if you have neither its capital depth nor a mechanism for "converting failed pilots into product assets," then every wrong pilot is pure bloodletting. (Source in Appendix C)
  • So an FDE team needs a "mechanism for refusal," not just the courage to refuse. Three actionable lines of defense.

Defense line one: every proof of concept must have "graduation criteria." Write them down at kickoff for every validation project: after how many weeks, using which metric, reaching what value, the project "graduates" into paid deployment; if not, both sides part ways gracefully. Palantir's bootcamp takes this logic to the extreme—it is not a proof of concept, it's the industrialized replacement for one: one to five days, the customer brings real data, a deployable prototype is built on-site, and executives decide on the spot. Either see the real thing within days, or don't start. Traditional proofs of concept become graveyards precisely because they are "indefinite, metric-free, and referee-less."

Defense line two: watch for three high-risk signals. Drawing on practitioners' experience, if two or more of these appear, be highly alert. First, "no owner of the land"—the project has no clear business-side owner inside the company, only the IT department as the point of contact; the IT department cares about compliance and stability, and compliance and stability are never reasons to start a new project. Second, "look but don't touch"—the customer demands you prove your capability first but refuses to provide real data; validation without real data is doomed to produce self-deceiving false positives. Third, "galactic-scale requirements"—a customer who asks to "cover every scenario across the entire company" in the first meeting is usually not ready to do even one scenario.

Defense line three: give "refusal" a graceful off-ramp. Refusing doesn't mean breaking off the relationship. The best approach is to translate "not now" into "when": "This scenario's data foundation is missing three things. We suggest starting with another scenario, fixing those three things along the way, and coming back next quarter." Packaging the refusal as a roadmap both protects your capacity and preserves the relationship—Chapter 3's "lighthouse customer selection" will continue along this line.

2.4 Pain Points: The First Driving Force of Deployment

互联网创业方法论里有个著名概念叫 MVP（Minimum Viable Product，最小可行产品）：用最小的产品验证市场的需求。FDE 对应的概念，我称之为 MVD（Minimum Viable Deployment，最小可行部署）——用最小的工程投入，在客户的真实环境里，对真实的痛点，验证一次价值的真实发生。

Where do the right problems come from? The textbook answer: from requirements research. The FDE's field experience says: from pain. And pain never shows up in conference rooms—it only shows up at the place where work actually happens.

  • Palantir wrote this playbook in blood twenty years ago. Look back at the Iraq battlefield story in Section 1.2: soldiers needed a roadside bomb early-warning tool—a need that could never surface in any interview, because soldiers didn't know they "could ask software for this"; they assumed it was just part of patrol life. Pain points are "seen" by engineers, not "spoken" by users. It was only because the Forward Deployed Engineer went on missions with the patrol units and personally witnessed the hesitation and fear of convoys approaching suspicious road segments that the crude map tool that changed the battlefield came to exist.
  • This method has a name in anthropology: "participant observation." In the Toyota Production System it's called "genchi genbutsu"—go to the actual place, look at the actual thing, get the actual facts. The FDE turned it into an actionable field methodology, which I call the "shadow work method."

Follow a real user through their real day. Don't interview them—sit beside them and watch them work: which systems they open, which spreadsheets they copy-paste between, where they frown, which "official processes" they bypass. This is exactly what OpenAI's FDE team did on the John Deere project: fly to Iowa, go into the fields with agronomists and farm owners, watch how they make spraying decisions, watch which information actually enters the decision, watch how seasonal deadlines govern everything. That frequently quoted practitioner maxim describes precisely what this method is meant to discover: "The hard part is finding the workflow nobody documented, the data source people actually trust, and the person who knows why the process is the way it is." Each of these three things can only be found on-site.

Focus on observing "workarounds," not "processes." The official process diagram tells you how the organization "should operate"; workarounds tell you how it "actually operates"—every workaround is an unmet pain point screaming. Why do employees insist on exporting data to spreadsheets and recalculating it? Why does everyone in the department agree that "for this table, you go to Xiao Wang"? Why, despite having a data system, does someone always manually verify the numbers before decision meetings? Workarounds are the organization's scars; beneath every scar lies a system failure—and an opportunity for the FDE.

Beware of "translated pain points." If the need you hear has been relayed through the client's IT department, procurement department, or consultants, it distorts with every handoff—the IT department translates business pain into technical requirements ("we need a data platform"), procurement translates it into compliance items ("must meet standard X"). Traditional software projects often go wrong right here: the vendor is accountable to the translation, not to the pain itself. So your first priority is to bypass the translations and reach the nerve endings of the pain directly. This is also the core duty of Echo in Palantir's two-person model—understanding the client's "mission" rather than their "requirements": requirements are secondhand accounts of the pain; the mission is its firsthand source.

Once you've found the real pain point, the next step is to verify at minimal cost: can our solution actually relieve this pain?

2.5 Validating Value with the "Minimum Viable Deployment"

Internet startup methodology has a famous concept called MVP (Minimum Viable Product): using the smallest possible product to validate market demand. The FDE's counterpart, which I call MVD (Minimum Viable Deployment), uses the smallest engineering investment to validate, in the customer's real environment and against a real pain point, that value genuinely materializes.

The difference is one word, but it goes to the bone. An MVP validates "should we build this product," with the market as judge; an MVD validates "can this solution generate value for this specific customer," with that customer's specific business as judge. A solution validated successfully at ten customers can still fail at the eleventh—different data foundations, different organizational inertia, different shapes of pain. That is the cruelty of enterprise delivery: value cannot be inherited; it must be validated one by one.

The MVD has three field rules.

Rule one: Real data, no exceptions. Validating with "anonymized (sensitive information removed) sample data" provided by the customer, or with demo data you construct yourself, is the first brick in the proof-of-concept graveyard. Real data hides every devil: field meanings that contradict the documentation, thirty percent null values, coding rules from three years ago, and—most fatal of all—data that itself records a broken process. - How to implement this rule: Palantir's bootcamps write "customers must bring their own real business data" into the rules, and the John Deere project put "reviewing hundreds of real operational cases" before modeling. A solution that works on fake data is just an elaborately crafted self-comfort.

Rule two: Narrow the incision, not the ambition. The common mistake is to interpret the MVD as a "castrated version of the big plan"—seventy percent of features cut, resulting in something that resembles nothing. The right approach is to shrink along a different dimension: don't cut value, cut scope. Don't pursue "an intelligent customer service system covering the whole company," but rather "covering only return-and-exchange tickets, yet end-to-end with no human intervention"; don't pursue "supply chain optimization for the entire group," but rather "resolving scheduling conflicts on this one production line, yet genuinely saving 20 person-hours every week." Make the incision small enough that the value density is high enough—high enough that the business unit can see it with the naked eye and spreads the word voluntarily. The expansion path of legal AI company Harvey is the textbook for this play: don't roll out firm-wide; first penetrate one global practice group deeply, turn the first batch of partners into believers, then expand horizontally after six months of real-world results.

Rule three: Fix a hard deadline to force trade-offs. The MVD validation cycle should be measured in "weeks," not "months." Palantir's bootcamps run one to five days; Sierra's fastest publicly reported go-live was four weeks; Decagon's typical deployment is four to eight weeks. The point of the deadline isn't speed—it's forcing both sides to make honest trade-offs: anything that cannot demonstrate value within these weeks is not yet core value. A six-month "minimum validation" will almost inevitably regrow into a big project that wants everything—that's the proof-of-concept graveyard breaking ground again.

The Bootcamp: Industrializing the MVD

Palantir's AIP Bootcamp, launched in 2023, deserves a standalone breakdown as the benchmark for "industrializing the MVD"—it is currently the only MVD pipeline validated at scale.

互联网产品获客的第一课是筛选种子用户：100 个爱你的用户，胜过 10000 个觉得你还行的用户。FDE 世界里的对应物，是灯塔客户——那种不仅能给你收入，更能给整个行业发信号的客户。

First, the numbers: starting from fewer than a hundred pilots in 2022, the number of sessions doubled year after year; media tracking shows cumulative sessions surpassed a thousand long ago, averaging nearly 6 per day at the 2025 peak; the traditional enterprise software sales cycle (nine to twelve months) was compressed to weeks; US commercial revenue grew 137% year-over-year in Q4 2025, and the company publicly attributes nearly all of that growth to this. (Source: Appendix C)

Now the process: it breaks the MVD into five standardized moves. Day 0, preparation: both sides lock in an extremely focused core battlefield—"optimize scheduling for a specific production line," "reduce inventory turnover days"—rejecting all grand narratives. Day 1, integration: connect to the customer's existing systems, extract siloed data, and build an initial ontology model. Days 2-3, build: the FDE and the customer's technical staff work back-to-back writing code and configuring rules, wiring large models into the business flow to create automated workflows that execute real actions. Days 4-5, demo and decision: the output is not a report but a live software interface; business executives click through it themselves and watch AI generate recommendations based on their own company's data—after the shock, negotiations begin directly.

The brilliance of the bootcamp is that it simultaneously solves the MVD's three classic problems: the real-data problem (customers bring it themselves), the deadline problem (capped at five days), and the judge problem (executives use it with their own hands). It also incidentally solves a deeper problem—trust. Letting decision-makers operate a system built on their own data beats a hundred-page feasibility report.

How fast do contracts get signed after the bootcamp? Palantir disclosed a real-world pace on its earnings call that peers might not even believe: a large healthcare company attended the bootcamp in December and, five weeks later, signed a five-year agreement worth $26 million annually; a global bank signed a $2 million initial contract one month after the pilot, then expanded it four months later into a three-year deal worth $19 million annually; the pharmacy chain Walgreens first piloted in 10 stores, saw a 30% improvement in in-store operational efficiency, then rolled out to 4,000 stores within eight months, with AI-driven end-to-end workflows automatically handling roughly 384 billion decisions per day that previously required human work. (Source: Appendix C) These numbers answer the question of "what happens after the MVD": projects that pass validation don't grow slowly—they scale in leaps. The customer has already seen the value with their own eyes in five days; what remains is just the commercial process.

Of course, replicating this model has prerequisites: it requires a mature platform underneath, otherwise you can't even set up the environment in five days. For teams without a platform, an executable simplified version is the "two-week sprint validation": in the first week, get on site, connect the data, and define metrics; in the second week, build a prototype that solves only a single problem but runs on real business, then demo it to the business side over the weekend and decide on the spot whether to proceed or stop. The format can be trimmed, but the military rules cannot.

2.6 Should You Accommodate the Customer's Existing Environment Early On?

During the MVD phase, there is a disagreement that almost every project runs into: the customer's existing technical environment—those legacy systems that have been running for twenty years, the small tools built by individual departments, and strict security and compliance boundaries—to what extent should our solution accommodate it?

Both camps have a point. The "accommodation camp" says: validating within the customer's real constraints is the only true validation. The "rebuild camp" says: doing deep adaptation for a legacy environment that is about to be replaced wastes the precious validation period on engineering destined to be thrown away.

FDE practice offers a middle path: be compatible with legacy systems on data, but never accommodate them in architecture—I call this "read old, write new."

At the data layer, go deep on compatibility with the legacy environment. Read the customer's data from wherever it lives—even if it's on an old mainframe, in spreadsheets on a shared drive, or behind a private interface of some ancient system. Legacy mainframes in finance and the heterogeneous systems of hundreds of clinics in healthcare have always been the core battleground of deployment work. There is no shortcut for data-reading compatibility, because data is the prerequisite for validating value, and data will never move house to accommodate your architecture. The good news is that this layer of work is being transformed by AI itself: field mapping that once required manual interpretation, cross-system data movement, and extracting data from legacy systems without interfaces can now largely be handed to agents—for example, using browser agents to simulate manual operations and pull data from legacy systems without interfaces. Integration costs are dropping by orders of magnitude. (Source: Appendix C)

At the architecture layer, resolutely refuse to become a parasite of the legacy environment. The system during the validation period should run within boundaries you control, interacting with legacy systems through interfaces, rather than embedding code into the legacy systems. There are three reasons: first, there is more than a fifty percent chance the validation-phase solution itself will be overturned and rewritten—the deeper the parasitism, the greater the waste; second, writing into legacy systems requires going through the customer's change management process, with cycles measured in months, which fundamentally conflicts with the week-level rhythm of the MVD; third, maintaining a "withdrawable" posture is itself a negotiation chip and a sign of honesty—the FDE finishes and leaves; a parasite can never leave.

At the process layer, follow people's habits, not the system's habits. This is the one technical teams most easily get backwards. The technical environment can be uncompromising, but people's habits must be accommodated. If business users' core actions happen in spreadsheets and email, the MVD's interface should appear in spreadsheet plugins and email, not require users to log into a brand-new portal. OpenAI's deployment at BBVA started from the ChatGPT interface that 120,000 employees were already using, rather than building something from scratch—a model example of following habits. Remember the cause of death from Section 2.1: employees' unwillingness to adopt new tools ranks first among the five major roadblocks. The biggest enemy of a new system is not the old system, but old habits.

2.7 User Research Where "Actions Speak Louder Than Words"

  • To close this chapter, let's turn the lens back to the origins of the methodology and discuss how FDE-style user research differs from traditional research.
  • The creed of traditional research is "asking": surveys, interviews, focus groups. The creed of the FDE is "watching" and "doing"—actions speak louder than words. There are three reasons, each building on the last.
  • First layer: customers don't know what they want. This isn't condescension—it's a law of cognition. Facing an entirely new category—intelligence analysis software in 2004, AI agents in 2025—users have no frame of reference to describe their needs. Palantir's demo loop works precisely because it doesn't ask "what do you want," but instead says "here's what I built—tell me what's wrong with it." People's judgment about "what's wrong" is far stronger than their imagination about "what they want." Finding fault with a solution is a human gift; describing an ideal solution out of thin air is a human weakness.

3.5 技术内容营销：打造持续输出的信任引擎

Second layer: what customers say and what they do are two different things. The example of the lawyer group in Section 2.1 is the best proof: in a survey asking "are you willing to use professional legal AI," the procurement department will tell you the willingness is strong—after all, they just spent $50,000 on tools; but observe the lawyers' actual behavior, and they're using ChatGPT to draft contracts. In a corporate context, "what is said" is contaminated by too many factors: political correctness, politeness toward vendors, protection of one's own role. Only behavior doesn't lie. The shadow work method observes behavior, and the MVD measures behavior—both are built on the premise that "doing is truer than saying."

Third layer: the highest-quality research happens in shared labor. In interviews, the customer is a "research subject"—guarded and performing; when working side by side, the customer is a colleague—relaxed and genuine. During those two or three days in the bootcamp when FDEs and the customer's engineers write code back to back, the density of information exchanged exceeds any formal research—the customer will casually mention during a debugging break, "actually, we never trusted this field," or "this process nominally goes through the system, but in reality we still make phone calls." These remarks would never surface in a formal interview because they seem "informal." Yet they are precisely the critical intelligence that determines deployment success or failure.

  • There is also a new variable in 2026 worth adding here: interviews themselves are being transformed by AI. Ding Xindong, CTO of Tezign, shared this approach: instead of sending out structured questionnaires, use agents to conduct autonomous interviews across the entire company—communicating in differentiated ways based on different scenarios and each person's actual pain points, and finally aggregating a global diagnostic map showing "what stage each product line is at, what its pain points are, and what approach is best suited to advance it." Questionnaires elicit answers; interviews surface pain points—AI has driven down the cost of the latter. (Source: Appendix C)

Putting the three layers together, the FDE research methodology can be condensed into one sentence: use the shadow work method to find pain points, use the minimum viable deployment to validate the solution, and use shared labor to win the truth.

At this point, the right question has been locked in, and the value has been preliminarily validated. The next battlefield is turning the validated single-point value into an actual contract, and a relationship that has truly begun—how to win the customer.

Chapter 3: Winning the Customer Chapter 3: Winning the Customer

"Enterprises buying AI is like your grandma getting an iPhone—she wants to use it, but she needs you to set it up for her." — a16z (Andreessen Horowitz) 3.1 Selecting Your Lighthouse Customers

The first lesson in customer acquisition for internet products is selecting seed users: 100 users who love you are worth more than 10,000 users who think you're okay. The counterpart in the FDE world is the lighthouse customer—the kind of customer that not only brings you revenue, but also sends a signal to the entire industry.

The strategic value of a lighthouse is multiplied under the FDE model, for three reasons.

First, lighthouses are the strongest sales asset. Enterprise customers have long decision chains and high risk aversion, so peer endorsement is the shortest path to persuasion. The founding story of legal AI company Harvey is a textbook example: the first major client announced in February 2023 was Linklaters, a top global law firm with 3,500 lawyers and 43 offices. Once this lighthouse lit up, major clients like PwC and Cleary Gottlieb followed in quick succession—the legal industry cares most about pedigree, and winning Linklaters was equivalent to getting a pass to the entire top law firm market. Palantir's early history was isomorphic: the CIA was its most demanding client, and also the one with the most endorsement value; the intelligence community's trust later became the key to opening up Wall Street, manufacturing, and government markets.

The process by which Harvey lit this lighthouse is itself an FDE lesson. In November 2022, Linklaters formed a dedicated group—the Market Innovation Group, led by partner David Wakeling—and began secretly trialing the product of this then-unknown startup. Note how they validated it: not by watching demos, but through real-world use across the entire firm. By the end of the pilot, 3,500 lawyers had posed roughly 40,000 real work questions to the system, spanning 250 practice areas and 50 languages. Wakeling's conclusion was the line repeatedly quoted in legal circles: "I've been in legal tech for 15 years, and I've never seen anything this game-changing." Another partner demonstrated a concrete scenario to the media: asking the system to prepare a ten-page document for a US client on "how to open a bank in Luxembourg"—"it actually produced it." (Source: Appendix C)

Second, the lighthouse determines your product's DNA. Under the FDE model, field learning flows back into the product—which means your first ten customers are, in effect, shaping your product. Choose the wrong lighthouse, and the product will be steered in a direction with no general applicability. This is what a16z's first piece of advice to startups, "sell smart," is about: a young company cannot satisfy everyone; it must choose an ideal customer profile with at least some commonality in system environment and use cases, so that the learning from each delivery accumulates rather than cancels out.

Third, the quality of the lighthouse matters an order of magnitude more than quantity. Your capacity is naturally scarce—a single elite team can deeply serve only a handful of clients at a time, and taking on the wrong one doesn't cost you "one lost deal" but occupies a top-tier team for half a year in a quagmire. So the three high-risk signals from Chapter 2 (no business owner, refusal to provide real data, universe-scale requirements) must be checked here too, plus one lighthouse-specific test: agree early in the engagement on whether, after success, they are willing to stand up publicly—co-publishing a case study, speaking at industry conferences, hosting visits from your prospective customers. A lighthouse unwilling to speak for you publicly is worth at most half its apparent value.

Building internet products requires guarding against "product locusts"—early users who swarm in, use and leave, and mislead the product's direction. The corresponding species in the FDE world is the "demand locust": a client with ample budget and strong demand that drains your team while generating no compounding returns. Three identifying traits: the demand clearly deviates from the company's strategic direction, so even if delivered it yields no reusable capability; treating the FDE as cheap outsourcing, assigning work by headcount rather than aligning on outcomes; heavy internal political friction, where your main job becomes helping one department prove another wrong. Saying no to demand locusts is hard—their contract values are often tempting. But remember Barry's accounting: a wrong pilot burns not just the immediate cost, but also the team's time and morale, and the product compounding that could have grown on the right customer.

3.2 Start with the Dumbest Things

The famous incubator YC has an old maxim: "Do things that don't scale." The founders of the earliest home-sharing platform went door to door photographing hosts' homes, and the founders of online payments company Stripe installed software for users on the spot.

When asked on a podcast about the relationship between FDE and this maxim, McCrory gave a precise formulation: the FDE model is doing things that don't scale, at scale.

This sentence captures the philosophical underpinning of FDE customer acquisition: in the enterprise market, there is no scalable shortcut to earning trust—only the dumb way. Three levels, each building on the last.

Level one: show up in person. In a16z's checklist of advice for forward deployed teams, the last item is just four words: show up in person. The rationale: "It's a cliché, but clichés become clichés because they're true"—showing up not only improves sales, but multiplies your success rate when mapping a customer's internal power dynamics and driving adoption of new tools. Enterprise customers' trust is priced in "number of meetings" and "shared experiences." Remote meetings build familiarity; only fighting side by side builds trust.

Level two: get your hands dirty. OpenAI's FDEs followed agronomists into the fields of Iowa; Palantir's engineers lived for weeks on oil drilling platforms and in aircraft final assembly plants; Harvey's FDEs gave adoption demos to partners one by one inside law firms. None of these scenarios was "efficient," but each produced something remote communication never could: firsthand feel for the customer's situation. That feel translates directly into solution quality—only after stepping in the farm mud do you understand why that seemingly perfect mobile interface is unusable in bright outdoor light.

Level three: be a servant first, a mentor later. The most common mistake FDEs make when entering an account is arriving with a "we're here to save you" posture. Get the posture wrong, and every intelligence channel closes. The correct sequence is to start with the most humble services: fix a data problem for the customer's analyst, fill in a missing API document for the IT department, automate a weekly report for a business team. These "dumb tasks" buy three strategic assets: a real map of the organization (who decides, who persuades, who is the hidden key node), the truth of the data environment (which data is trusted, which is for show), and most importantly—certification of the identity "this outsider is one of us." Only once that identity is established do your words begin to carry weight.

The end of the dumb way is not staying dumb forever. Chapter 7 will cover how to distill this dumb effort into replicable playbooks—but before scaling, you must first wade through the mud and blaze the trail worth replicating.

3.3 The Trust Dividend: The Endorsement Mine of Flagship Customers

Enterprise procurement is a market of "extreme information asymmetry and extremely high cost of failure," and decision-makers' trust in information follows an extremely steep ranking: a vendor's own marketing is worth less than analyst reports; analyst reports are worth less than peers' public case studies; peers' public case studies are worth less than peers' private recommendations. The last tier—"someone I know used it and says it really works"—converts at rates that crush every other channel. And the FDE model is precisely the best machine for producing "private recommendation material": what you deliver is not a software license, but a line from the customer's executive at an industry dinner—"they really know their stuff."

Mining this treasure requires actions at three levels.

Level one: turn the delivery into "a story worth telling." The precondition for a customer executive to spread the word for you is that your delivery can be told as a story that makes them look good in front of their peers. This requires the delivery outcome to have three narrative elements: a concrete number ("chemical usage reduced by 70%," "anti-money-laundering investigations from hours to minutes"), a concrete person ("our agronomists worked in the fields alongside their team"), and a concrete contrast ("it used to take three months; this time, five days"). When the delivery ends, proactively prepare this narrative for the customer's internal champion—one page, three charts, and a version that can be told in 30 seconds. How detailed the materials are determines how faithfully others retell them.

Level two: turn the customer's success into the customer's social currency. After the pharmacy chain Walgreens deployed to 4,000 stores in eight months through the bootcamp model, it became a star at industry conferences; market research firm J.D. Power went even further—after attending a Palantir bootcamp as a customer, it began running bootcamps for its own customers. (Source: Appendix C) This is the ideal state: the customer showcases your methodology as its own industry leadership. Endorsement upgrades from "thank you" to "proud to use you."

Level three: beware the endorsement "backlash mechanism." The enterprise market holds grudges. One high-profile failed delivery spreads far faster than ten successes—because failure stories make better drinking material. This is why the "rejection mechanism" in Chapter 2 and the "activation discipline" in Chapter 4 matter so much: the fatal weakness of the lighthouse strategy is not failing to find a lighthouse, but letting the lighthouse go dark in your hands.

3.4 Using Data to Map Out the Client's Full Landscape

「模型通常是最干净的部分。难的是找到那个没人写进文档的工作流程。」 —— 一位一线 FDE「无论技术如何进化，组织内部的人性和权责博弈，始终是比技术更复杂的难题。」 —— 申悦，FDE 一线从业者

Data map: What data sources does the client have, who owns each one, what is their quality, who controls access, and is there any dark data you would never even think of—a spreadsheet hoarded by some veteran employee, a report that only circulates via email, paper ledgers. The point is not "what exists" but "which one is trusted." In almost every organization, the official data source and the data source employees actually trust are not the same one—and the latter is the one you need to connect to.

Process map: The real operating picture of the target business process—not the version in the process documents, but the version observed through shadowing, including all undocumented steps, exceptions, and workarounds. Especially mark three points: the step that consumes the most time, the step where errors carry the highest cost, and the step with the most intense emotions. These three points are usually where the value is richest.

Organizational map: Who initiates, who pays, who uses it, who can veto, and who is the uncrowned king. The most common cause of death for enterprise projects is not technical—it is failing to account for the organizational math: your internal champion's position is not senior enough, or too senior (so senior they have no time to manage you), or the right people were never included. In particular, find the "knowledge hubs": people whose titles are not high, but whom everyone turns to when they hit real problems. They are both the best source of requirements and the seed nodes for future adoption.

4.2 快速迭代：部署期的「热修复」文化

互联网产品迭代靠数据实验，用小步快跑逼近最优。企业部署场景里做不了严格的数据实验——样本太小、干扰太多——但它的精神内核，快速、小步、基于证据的迭代，在部署期变成了一种工作方式：像抢修线上事故一样，快速响应用户的每一个小抱怨——我称之为「热修复」。

Systems map: The true face of the technical environment—the list of systems to integrate and their interface status, security and compliance boundaries, and change management processes. The systems map determines your deployment architecture and schedule. Many projects' timelines are doomed from day one because nobody asked: releasing one version on the client's side takes a six-week process.

Political map: The most sensitive and most critical one. Whose cheese does this project move? Is the process targeted for automation the source of power for some department? Whose work will your solution make seem redundant? That report listed "employee unwillingness to adopt new tools" as the number one roadblock, and the root of resistance is mostly not laziness but fear—fear of being replaced, fear of being proven incompetent, fear of losing one's reason to exist. The function of the political map is to identify the carriers of these fears in advance and, in the solution design, arrange a "new path" for them rather than a "dead end." The renewal topic in Chapter 5 will return to this point: the people you design as "victims" will become the most determined opponents at renewal time.

Only when all five maps are complete have you truly "entered the field." This due diligence usually takes one to two weeks, carried out separately by Echo and Delta with daily check-ins. It looks like pure cost, but the math works like this: what two weeks of due diligence saves is three months of sprinting on the wrong battlefield.

3.5 Technical Content Marketing: Building a Trust Engine of Continuous Output

传统软件的质量只有两种状态：功能对或错，测试过或不过。人工智能系统的质量是连续的、概率的、场景相关的——同一个回答在演示里惊艳，在某个具体业务语境里可能是灾难。更要命的是，「好」的定义权在业务方，不在工程方：模型觉得完美的回答，业务专家可能一眼看出外行。没有这把评估标尺，人工智能部署的迭代就是蒙眼狂奔——你以为在优化，其实在随机漂移。

Content marketing is a classic customer acquisition weapon for internet companies. The customer acquisition logic of FDE companies gives their content marketing a unique positioning: not chasing traffic, but pursuing the "pre-sale of professional trust."

Enterprise clients find you not by scrolling past your ads, but by: hitting a hard problem, searching and asking around, discovering that some company has published extremely professional public analysis of this problem, and then concluding—"They get it, go with them." The role content plays here is the "pre-qualification" at the very front of the client's decision chain. Palantir has published scenario-based technical articles for years; OpenAI and Anthropic turn enterprise client cases into detailed technical narratives; a single landmark industry essay from a16z set the tone for an entire field—none of this is brand promotion; it is carefully cultivated trust assets.

3.6 Passing Procurement, Legal, and Security Reviews

The "gates" of the enterprise market are procurement, legal, and the security review committee. Many technically successful FDE projects die at these three gates, and they die with no technical dignity: contract terms fall apart, data processing agreements get stuck, and the security questionnaire drags into its fourth month.

The key to passing is how you view these three gates: treat them as obstacles and you will crash in the last mile; treat them as part of the deliverable and you can actually build an advantage—because most technology companies are careless about these three things, and if you are serious, you win.

  • The procurement gate: first understand that procurement's metrics are not your enemy. The procurement department's job is to control cost, risk, and compliance. When they push down prices, it is not personal—it is their duty. The response is not to push back hard, but to give them tools that let them "cover their bases": clear phased pricing (giving price reductions a graceful step down), comparable market benchmarks (giving approvals a basis), and most importantly—measurable outcome commitments (making the accusation of "overpaying" impossible to sustain). Outcome-based pricing has an unexpected advantage here: what procurement finds hardest to approve is "spending on things whose results cannot be articulated." A quote like "pay X per resolved ticket" is instantly understandable to procurement.

The legal gate: polish the data processing agreement like a product. The legal focus of enterprise AI projects is highly concentrated: Is the data used to train models? Where is the data stored and who can access it? Who pays when a security incident occurs? Who is responsible when AI output goes wrong?

Smart teams productize the standard answers to these questions—pre-built agreement templates, clear model usage statements, tiered incident liability frameworks. Palantir survives in the intelligence world precisely by building "permissions and audit" into the product core (who viewed what data is traceable end to end); Anthropic, in its work with financial clients, made "traceability and auditability" a selling point of its agent design. Legal trust is designed, not negotiated.

  • The security review gate: use a "pre-answered white paper" to save two months. The standard move of enterprise security review is a questionnaire with hundreds of questions. If you wait for the questionnaire to arrive before answering, every item requires cross-departmental confirmation, and the wait is measured in months. The efficient team's approach is to proactively maintain a "security white paper": deployment architecture diagrams, data flow diagrams, encryption and permission schemes, compliance certifications, and standard answers to every question asked in past reviews. Eighty percent of most questionnaires can be copy-pasted directly from the white paper. Your response speed itself is the most intuitive signal of security maturity—security teams have seen it all, and they know what a frantic answer means.

3.7 Ecosystem Leverage: Standing on the Shoulders of Channels

The ecosystem strategy of the FDE era is to stand on the shoulders of three types of partners.

Category one: cloud providers and large platforms. AWS, Microsoft Azure, and Google Cloud are themselves among the main entry points for enterprise AI procurement. Getting into their co-selling systems is like obtaining a pass straight to enterprise purchasing lists. Cloud marketplaces also solve a practical pain point: customers can use their existing cloud spending commitments to purchase your services, bypassing the procurement process for new vendors.

  • Category two: consulting firms and system integrators. This is the most dramatic ecosystem shift of 2026. On the founding partner list of OpenAI's deployment company, Bain, Capgemini, and McKinsey sit side by side—the world's largest consulting and integration giants have gone from "potential competitors" to "equity-holding allies." The logic is clear: model companies have the technology, consulting firms have client relationships and industry depth, and integrators have delivery manpower—only when the three converge can they swallow the giant market of "AI transformation" whole. For startups, the lesson cuts both ways: guard against consulting giants eating your delivery layer under the banner of ecosystem partnerships, but also recognize the real dividends of allying with regional and industry-specific integrators—they hold client relationships you couldn't build in three years.

The same script played out in China almost simultaneously. In July 2026, ByteDance's Volcano Engine signed a strategic partnership with EY, one of the Big Four accounting firms: jointly building solutions around core scenarios such as data governance, financial management, and marketing growth. Most notably, the two parties plan to build a thousand-person FDE team to "create an AI-native delivery team." Volcano Engine President Tan Dai put it bluntly: the FDE team should "place engineers with dual backgrounds in technology and industry on-site with customers, deeply involved in the entire process of solution delivery." On one side is a consulting giant with client relationships; on the other is a model platform lacking industry depth—FDEs became the welding point between the two. (Source: Appendix C)

Category three: your customers' customers. The highest form of bundling is embedding yourself into your customer's own value chain. After attending Palantir's bootcamp, J.D. Power began running bootcamps for its own customers—Palantir's capabilities spilled over through J.D. Power's client relationships, bringing customer acquisition costs close to zero. When designing your delivery, you can pre-build this "re-deliverability": can this solution be used by your customer to serve its own customers? If yes, you go from vendor to part of your customer's business model—that is the deepest moat.

3.8 Scheduling: Who Goes First Is Itself Strategy

Scarcity can create attraction—invitation-only access and waitlists are classic plays for internet products. The FDE scheduling problem is opposite in shape but similar in principle: your capacity will always be less than demand, so "who goes first" itself becomes a strategic tool.

First, the harsh reality of constraints. A qualified forward deployed squad (one business-side person plus two or three technical-side people) can typically deliver no more than two high-quality projects at a time. OpenAI started with just 2 engineers in its early days, Sierra's deployment cycles are measured in months, and Harvey's deployment at a single firm takes six to nine months—elite delivery capacity is inherently scarce. Scarcity itself isn't the problem; the problem is failing to price that scarcity.

  • The first principle of scheduling is to prioritize by strategic value, not by contract size. The decision matrix has only two dimensions: the customer's lighthouse value (strength of industry signal) and the customer's learning value (how much reusable capability can be accumulated). Customers high on both dimensions can—and should—be prioritized even at a loss: Barry recalls that Palantir ran free pilots early on and burned millions of dollars betting on exactly this matrix. Large contracts that score low on both dimensions are actually the most dangerous: the money looks big, but it locks up your elite team for half a year.
  • The second principle is to productize the "wait." If a customer has to queue for three months before you can start, don't let those three months go to waste: give them a data preparation checklist (so you can move fast on day one), organizational warm-up advice (which key people to win over first), and lightweight remote diagnostics (to maintain momentum and clear mines in advance). If the waiting period is managed well, the delivery cycle after kickoff can be shortened by a third—the queue transforms from a burden on customer experience into a component of delivery quality.

The third principle is to never promise parallel work beyond your capacity. Your delivery quality depends on the sustained focus of the same team—every project carries a full set of customer context, and each switch requires a full reload; the cost of running multiple projects in parallel is far greater than imagined. Better to let sales sell "we're booked until next quarter" as scarcity (which often justifies a higher price) than to have the team frantically juggling three customers. The better business gets, the harder it is to resist taking on more; but the cost of collapsing from overcommitment is often far greater than the revenue from one extra deal.

There is a unique customer acquisition play in the FDE world: turn what you do well into open-source tools and demos, and get engineers at target customers using them first.

先看流失。企业客户的流失与消费产品的流失在形状上截然不同：消费产品流失是静悄悄的卸载，企业客户流失是一场漫长的凌迟——先是使用率阴跌，然后例会上开始有人质疑「这东西到底值不值」，接着续约谈判被「明年再看」无限推迟，最后在某个预算季被连根拔掉。更可怕的是连带伤害：第 3 章说过企业市场是记仇的，一次公开化的失败，会在行业的小圈子里被反复咀嚼多年。

The logic: executives initiate enterprise purchases, but engineers hold technical veto power. Getting target customers' engineers using your product in their daily work is like pre-planting a vote of trust on the technical side of the decision chain. Three typical vehicles.

  • Open-source tools and components. Open-source the generic modules accumulated during delivery—eval frameworks, connectors, deployment templates. Anthropic made the Model Context Protocol (an open standard for connecting models to external tools) a public specification, and the artifacts FDEs deliver at customer sites are built on this protocol—the first lesson customer engineers learn is Anthropic's tech stack. The process of standard adoption is the process of trust accumulation.
  • Self-service "technology showrooms." Enterprise products usually lack self-service trials, but they can have technology showrooms: public demo environments, sandboxes (isolated practice environments), and interactive tutorials. Palantir's bootcamp is essentially a "trial" turned into a ritual; for teams with limited resources, a quick-start package that lets customer engineers connect sample data and get it running within an afternoon is the best Sales Engineer.

Presence in engineer communities. In the technical communities of your target industries, your engineers should show up consistently—answering questions, sharing pitfalls, contributing code. The conversion path from this kind of presence is long, but it reaches exactly the people who hold technical veto power. For FDE companies, recruiting and customer acquisition converge on this action: the best candidate pools and the best sales leads often come from the same community.

3.10 Writing Proposals and Proof-of-Concept Plans

The highest-value-per-word documents in the enterprise market are proposals and proof-of-concept plans—every word worth its weight in gold, no exaggeration.

The bad news: most technical teams' proposals make the same mistake—they talk throughout about "what we will do" instead of "what you will get." A good FDE proposal follows a strict inverted-pyramid structure.

  • Layer one: business outcomes, in a single paragraph. The first paragraph of a proposal must state the business outcome in the customer's language: "Within eight weeks, cut your bank's average anti-money-laundering investigation processing time from 4 hours to 15 minutes, freeing up investigative capacity equivalent to 2.5 times your current team." Without this sentence, the customer's executives won't read any further.
  • 第三道： 值班和响应，要让客户感觉到你一直在。凌晨两点系统挂了，FDE 的反应速度，就是客户对这段关系的体感温度。第 1 章引过那句从业者的铁律：「部署在凌晨两点挂了，你不提工单，不怪别的团队，不回去睡觉。你修好它。」这话的精神要落到机制：值班轮换、事故复盘、以及每次事故后给客户的诚实通报——企业客户能接受事故，不能接受隐瞒。
  • Layer two: the value validation path—how you'll prove it was achieved. Specify acceptance metrics, measurement methods, baseline data, and exit criteria for each phase. The signal this layer sends is "we dare to be tested"—in an enterprise market scarred by overpromises, daring to be tested is the rarest form of sincerity.

Layer three: the delivery method—how we'll do it. Only in this layer do the technical details appear, and they should be written so non-technical readers can follow along: architecture diagrams with business annotations, milestones with decision points. Be especially explicit about what the customer needs to provide—data access, key personnel's time commitment. Clearly stating what the customer must do serves two purposes: it defuses risks early, and it also screens customers.

Layer four: risks and countermeasures—how we've imagined we might die. Most proposals avoid risks, as if mentioning them were bad luck. Quite the opposite: what mature buyers trust most is a vendor that "clearly articulates how it could fail": what if data quality falls short, what if key personnel change, what if accuracy doesn't meet the threshold. This layer is the proposal's trust amplifier, and it is the textual embodiment of Chapter 2's principle of "rejecting expensive failures."

  • Proof-of-concept plans have another special feature: they are the vehicle for "graduation criteria." The plan must specify a time limit, acceptance metrics, and the two possible follow-ups: graduation or dissolution. A vague validation plan is essentially a construction permit for the proof of concept's grave.

3.11 From On-Site to Remote: The Boundaries of Hybrid Delivery

FDE delivery has undergone an important evolution in recent years: from purely on-site work to a hybrid model of "remote-first + on-site at key milestones." Palantir's official position also confirms that many projects today are executed mostly remotely, with on-site presence concentrated around key milestones.

  • Designing the rhythm of the hybrid model is a craft. Which things require physical presence? Experience converges on three categories: the early relationship-building phase (first meetings, shadowing work, building trust with executives—trust cannot be built through a screen; it requires face-to-face contact); high-intensity co-creation (bootcamp-style joint building, whiteboard sessions for critical architecture decisions); and politically sensitive periods (solution promotion, cross-department coordination, change management—these are the moments when you need to read the air in the room). Which things are actually better done remotely? Deep coding, documentation, routine iteration—work that requires uninterrupted flow.

两种「人传人」的规模化培训，值得对照着看。西班牙对外银行推 12 万人上线时，靠的不是厂商的培训团队，而是两批内部角色：一张全行的「人工智能先锋网络」，负责在各业务部门做工作坊、挖场景；一批被同事称为「人工智能极客」的高级用户，手把手带身边人。咨询巨头埃森哲与 Anthropic 的合作则是另一个量级：三万名顾问接受 Claude 的系统培训，组成全球最大的人工智能实践者网络之一——埃森哲再把这支队伍带进自己的客户。（出处见附录 C）规模差了三个数量级，结构却是同一个：厂商培训的从来不是「用户」，而是「会去培训别人的人」。

  • The hybrid model also carries two hidden dividends. One is cost structure: on-site presence is one of the largest variable costs for an FDE, and a sensible hybrid ratio can expand delivery margins by more than ten percentage points. The other is talent sustainability: teams that travel half the time year-round show significantly higher burnout rates—complaints about travel top the list among practitioners on forums. The hybrid model is not laziness; it is a safeguard for organizational endurance.

But the bottom line must be drawn clearly: an FDE who "never meets anyone" is a false proposition. If a project runs entirely remotely from kickoff to delivery, what you lose is not just the relationship but the very thing this chapter and Chapter 2 have repeatedly argued for—first-hand knowledge of the ground truth. Remote work sustains existing trust and context, but those must first be created in the physical world.

3.12 Overseas Expansion

  • The question of taking the FDE model abroad presented two interesting camps in 2026.
  • The globalization of American companies is led by the giants: OpenAI's FDE hiring map covers San Francisco, New York, and Seattle, plus London, Dublin, Munich, Paris, Zurich, Tokyo, Singapore, Sydney, and Abu Dhabi—the geographic distribution of FDE teams precisely traces the global map of enterprises' willingness to pay for AI: North America, Western Europe, developed East Asian economies, and Middle Eastern sovereign wealth.

FDE expansion from China follows a different logic. The past predicament was "product DNA": domestic vendors had long run highly customized project-based businesses, and their organizational capabilities did not match the standardization and productization demanded by overseas markets. But the FDE model offers a reverse perspective: the delivery culture of Chinese engineers—hardworking, fast to respond, full-stack capable, and accustomed to solving every problem on the customer's site—is precisely what FDE demands. So the question has never been whether Chinese engineers are suited to be FDEs, but whether the platform beneath them is thick enough. Among large companies, ByteDance's Volcano Engine has set up an FDE team for its Doubao large models; among startups, some local teams have written "FDE does not mean on-site outsourcing" on their official websites—both paths are being explored.

On specific expansion strategies, FDE has one innate advantage and one innate constraint. The advantage: the "lighthouse to endorsement" logic works equally well in global markets, and enterprise customers in developed countries have more mature willingness to pay and contractual discipline. The constraint: FDE is a heavily localized business—language, time zones, compliance, and local trust networks each require a local team, not remote support. This means the cost structure of FDE expansion is naturally higher than that of online software expansion, and the pace must be more restrained: first establish a foothold with products that can be delivered remotely, then build local delivery teams in key markets, one country at a time.

The twelve things covered in this chapter boil down to one sentence: the essence of winning customers is becoming the one exception worth trusting in a market that has been let down countless times. Signing the contract is only the ticket to entry—next comes the real hard battle: bringing the system to life and getting people to use it.

4.1 Going Live Is Not Activation: The "Day-One Curse" of Enterprise Deployment

第二本账：风险定价的权力。麦格鲁的建议是，早期创业公司应主动承担风险：「做成了你再付我们。」底气来自对产品力的自信，也来自一个冷静的计算——企业客户对新供应商最大的疑虑是「你们行不行」，免费验证是这个疑虑的溶解剂。当怀疑被溶解，后续的定价权反而回到你手里：客户买的不再是「一个赌」，而是「一份已被亲眼验证的确定性」，确定性是可以溢价的。

First, define this chapter's core concept. In consumer internet, "activation" means a new user completes a key action and experiences the product's "aha moment." In enterprise deployment, activation means the target user group forms a stable habit of using the system in their daily work—not applause at a demo, but the system still being used frequently three months later without anyone nudging.

In other words: for enterprise software, "going live" is an administrative event, while "activation" is a behavioral event. The former can be celebrated; only the latter counts.

One set of numbers from that MIT report deserves to be laid out again at the start of this chapter: only about forty percent of enterprises provide employees with official AI tool subscriptions, while as many as ninety percent of employees use personal, consumer-grade AI tools for their daily work. This means the real state of many enterprises is "dual-track": official systems idling while shadow AI runs rampant. The system went live; activation never happened.

  • Why do enterprise deployments so often die at the activation stage? Because it requires passing three gates, entirely different from those of consumer products. Consumer products die from "not being fun"; enterprise deployments die in the users' hands: users find it inconvenient—one extra step beyond their old habits, and nobody uses it; users find it untrustworthy—one mistake, and trust resets to zero; users find it irrelevant—then even fewer will touch it. None of these three gates can be cleared from headquarters; you can only clear them in the customer's meeting rooms, workshops, and workstations. That is why activation is the FDE's home turf, and the biggest difference from traditional implementation that "delivers and leaves": traditional implementation treats the acceptance checklist as the finish line, while the FDE treats changed customer behavior as the finish line.
  • The seven sections of this chapter correspond to the seven weapons of activation.

4.2 Rapid Iteration: The "Hotfix" Culture During Deployment

Internet products iterate through data experiments, approaching the optimum through small, fast steps. In enterprise deployment scenarios, rigorous data experiments are impossible—samples are too small, noise too high—but the spirit of that approach, fast, small, evidence-based iteration, becomes a way of working during deployment: responding rapidly to every small user complaint, like fixing a production incident—I call it "hotfixing."

Consumer products iterate in units of "versions," weekly or biweekly. During deployment, the FDE's iteration cadence is measured in "days" or even "hours": in the morning a business user says "this output is missing the supplier code field," and by afternoon the field is added; today the workshop supervisor says "this interface is hard to tap with gloves on," and tomorrow the buttons are doubled in size. This responsiveness means far more to users than the features themselves—every overnight fix is a top-up of user trust. In the first month, users will form a judgment: "this team means business," or "another one that leaves after delivery."

The hotfix culture has three execution essentials.

First, feedback must reach the person writing the code directly, with no messengers in between. Previously, user feedback had to pass through customer success, product managers, and scheduling before reaching engineers—each handoff loses half the context, and by the time it gets scheduled, a week has passed and the user's enthusiasm has gone cold. Under the FDE model, feedback goes straight to the person writing the code—ideally, that person is sitting right next to the user. This is exactly the loop Palantir shortens: Forward Deployed Engineers can build, test, learn, and feed back on the spot, without waiting for a formal product requirement to complete its process.

Second, iteration priorities are ranked by "usage blockage," not "feature importance." The trade-off logic during deployment is the opposite of that during product development. In product development, requirements are ranked by strategic value; during deployment, the question to ask is "what is blocking tomorrow's usage"—a color scheme issue that makes workshop workers feel "this was made for office white-collar workers" is the highest priority; a powerful prediction feature that users cannot use yet is deferred until after activation is complete. Win usage first, then win depth.

Third, at the end of each day, ask one question: which moment tomorrow did today's changes make smoother for the user? Fix things this way, and you're no longer just "responding to requests"—you're proactively clearing away every friction point between the user and the system. A fix is not just bug removal—every fix is a deliberate act of activation design: you are dismantling, one by one, the friction points between the user and the system. The list of friction points is hidden in the process maps from on-site discovery and in everyday observation.

OpenAI's FDE methodology has a corresponding rhythm: early co-creation (on-site whiteboarding and alignment), validation (building an evals system), and delivery (multi-day on-site builds). Note that the delivery phase is still measured in "multi-day on-site" units—the reason fixes can happen this fast is that people stay right next to the problem.

4.3 Iterating in the customer's environment: evals-driven quality improvement

  • If 4.2 was about the "speed" of iteration, this section is about its "sense of direction." In AI deployment, the carrier of that direction is a practice that only became mainstream after 2024: evals—using a set of standard cases to continuously score the system's outputs.
  • Traditional software quality has only two states: the feature is right or wrong, the test passes or fails. The quality of AI systems is continuous, probabilistic, and context-dependent—the same answer can dazzle in a demo yet be a disaster in a specific business context. Worse still, the authority to define "good" belongs to the business side, not the engineering side: an answer the model considers perfect may strike a business expert as amateurish at a glance. Without this evals yardstick, iterating on an AI deployment is running blindfolded—you think you're optimizing, but you're actually drifting at random.

The engineering practice of evals has already crystallized into three steps among leading teams.

Step one: grow it out of real cases. An eval set cannot be invented by engineers; it must come from the customer's real business corpus. The essence of this step is making business experts' tacit judgment explicit as an executable yardstick.

Step two: make the business side the judges. Evals are not a self-indulgent tool for engineers; the reviewers must include the business side. The best approach is to shape evals into a form business experts can participate in: side-by-side output comparisons, simple better/worse labeling, regular review sessions. This process yields a double benefit: the eval set becomes more and more accurate, and the business side's understanding of the system deepens—watching the system answer their own cases better and better, trust accumulates through what they see with their own eyes, not through reports.

Step three: wire evals into the production loop. Launch is not the end of evaluation. Continuously collect real inputs and outputs from the production environment, sample and evaluate on a regular schedule, and alert immediately when scores drop—this turns "quality" from a one-time acceptance check before launch into continuous care throughout the lifecycle. What users are most sensitive to is not average quality but quality stability: one failure can collapse trust in a way that takes ten successes to rebuild.

The John Deere collaboration is the most complete public sample of "evals first," and deserves a closer look. The company set out to solve herbicide waste: traditional sprayers cover the entire field, while its "See & Spray" technology uses 36 cameras plus machine vision to spray only the weeds while traveling at 12 to 15 miles per hour—covering three football fields per minute. The vision for precision agriculture is grand: the US plants 12 trillion corn and soybean plants each year; the best farmland yields 200 bushels per acre, and top growers reach 600—"if every single plant could be individually cared for, yields could be transformed." Those are the words of Justin Rose, a technology executive at John Deere.

But farmers don't want technology; they want trustworthy advice. OpenAI's Forward Deployed Engineers flew to Iowa, went into the fields alongside agronomists, first reviewed hundreds of real operation cases with the experts and built a custom evals system, and only then iterated rapidly on the model—and they had to beat the farming calendar, because missing planting season means missing a whole year. The final results: chemical usage reduced by up to 70%, and farmer interaction frequency increased sixfold. Note the order of these two numbers: only after evals defined what "good" means could the model deliver "good." (Source in Appendix C)

The deeper meaning of evals is this: in plain terms, it turns the old master's notion of "what counts as good" in his head into a scoring standard the system executes automatically every day. This is the FDE model in miniature—the customer's knowledge is no longer just words in a requirements document, but a yardstick living inside the system.

4.4 Taking a different path to lower the barrier to use

Getting users to value with the fewest actions is a commonplace of product design. But the barrier to using enterprise systems often lies in obstacles outside the system itself. Four "barrier-lowering" techniques have been repeatedly validated in deployment practice.

Technique one: parasitize the interfaces users already have. Wherever the user's main battlefield is, that's where your system should appear: if they work in spreadsheets, build a spreadsheet plugin; if they approve things in email, make approval happen inside email; if they work in a ticketing system, embed AI suggestions into the ticket cards. Requiring them to log into a new system is like building a wall between you and them that they must climb every day. OpenAI starting from the existing ChatGPT interface of BBVA's 120,000 employees, and Anthropic using open protocols to bring models into customers' existing workflow tools, follow the same logic. Every additional login leaks a chunk of activation rate.

6.7 建立价值度量系统以小博大

BBVA is the best sample of "entering the organization by following old habits." It began working with OpenAI in May 2024, and its first step was to hand out just 3,300 ChatGPT Enterprise accounts—no company-wide campaign, just letting seed users play on their own. Employees soon created more than 20,000 custom mini-assistants on their own initiative, about 4,000 of which were heavily used; management did not ban "shadow AI" but did the opposite: "We give everyone a safe platform so they can experiment with confidence." This was paired with structured training: 250 executives (including the chairman himself) took classes first, and the bank built an "AI pioneer network," cultivating a group of power users internally known as "AI geeks." Data from over a year later: employees save about 3 hours per week on average, and 83% are active weekly users. In December 2025, the two parties took the momentum to announce a bank-wide rollout: 25 countries, 120,000 employees, plus the launch of an end-to-end transformation roadmap called "Eight Things." From 3,300 to 11,000 to 120,000, each expansion happened only after the previous step's usage data was published—that is activation by "letting habit walk on its own." (Source in Appendix C)

  • Technique two: activation rates hide in default values. A new user's first reaction to a blank system is "now what?"—and most churn happens in those three seconds. The FDE solution is to pre-install the "first use": preset templates, pre-filled examples (based on the customer's own data), pre-set guidance (the first time it opens, there is already a to-do that belongs to them). From day one, the system should understand what the user might want to do better than the user does—this is where the things observed during those first days of on-site shadowing come in handy.
  • Technique three: translate "ask the AI" into "click a button." Enterprise users' proficiency with "talking to AI" is far more uneven than one might imagine. Making users compose their own questions amounts to shifting the engineering burden onto the very people least equipped to carry it. The mature approach is to package high-frequency scenarios into one-click actions: "Generate last week's anomaly report," "Verify this batch of invoices," "Draft this customer letter"—behind each button lies a complete prompt and workflow, tuned through evals. Conversational interaction is for exploration; button-based interaction is for the daily grind.

Technique four: be a "copilot" first, talk about "autopilot" later. For high-risk, high-resistance processes, don't push full automation in one step. Let the system first exist as an "adviser"—the AI drafts, the human confirms; the AI annotates, the human adjudicates. Through repeated confirmations, users calibrate their trust in the system's judgment; only when the confirmation pass rate gets high enough does automation go on the agenda. John Deere's solution still reserves final decision authority for agronomists, and agent design in financial compliance scenarios generally keeps a human gatekeeper. The copilot strategy isn't conservatism—it's the optimal path between activation rate and risk.

4.5 The protracted integration war

「如果你不把现场学习转化为产品资产，你得到的只是一堆定制项目。」 —— Barry，前 Palantir 前线部署工程师

Every FDE veteran carries a body of scars, all from the same war: the integration battle against the customer's "legacy systems"—those old systems that have run inside the enterprise for years and that no one dares touch.

The brutality of this war exceeds the imagination of anyone who has never been on the front lines. a16z's description hits the nail on the head: the context AI applications need—history, business logic, permission systems—is all locked inside enterprise databases, APIs, and workflows, and connecting them "has never been an optional extra, but a required course." Finance's legacy mainframes, healthcare's hundreds of heterogeneous clinic systems, manufacturing's dozens of siloed shop-floor systems—what these environments share is: outdated documentation, broken interfaces, and half the people who understood them already retired.

This war has four strategic points.

  • Treat integration as a campaign, not a chore. In most project plans, integration is written as a single line "system integration: 2 weeks," then balloons into four months in execution. The root of the error is treating integration as a technical chore—it is at once technical archaeology, organizational politics, and data governance—a genuine hard battle. The right approach: draw the system map during pre-engagement due diligence, rank and schedule integration risks by severity, and chew the hardest bones first—integration carries the greatest uncertainty; start a day late and the entire project timeline runs naked for a day.
  • Second, solve data problems before model problems. That report contains a widely quoted insight: many AI projects fail for the underlying reason of "garbage in, garbage out"—the AI taps ungoverned data sources, randomly hits one of ten versions of the same document, and the output is naturally untrustworthy. Palantir's solution is the ontology: first model the enterprise's data assets into a layer with business semantics, clarifying "which field is authoritative, which version is valid, who may see what," and the AI runs on this foundation. The universal lesson of this path: data governance is not a prerequisite for AI projects—it is the AI project itself.

7.2 坏事传千里——失败部署的复盘传播

The US Navy's "shipbuilding operating system" is the most spectacular footnote to this lesson. In December 2025, the Secretary of the Navy and Palantir's CEO jointly announced a $448 million contract: initially covering two major shipyards, three naval docks, and one hundred suppliers. Shipbuilding's data environment is a textbook disaster: ERP systems, decades-old databases, and paper blueprints coexisting. Two numbers from the pilot phase silenced everyone: at nuclear submarine maker General Dynamics Electric Boat, submarine production scheduling was compressed from 160 manual hours to under 10 minutes; at Portsmouth Naval Shipyard, material review time dropped from weeks to under an hour.

The Secretary of the Navy emphasized: "This is not a concept, not a pilot, not research—this is already being done." (Source: Appendix C) Note the order of this case: only after great effort to bring data into a unified semantic layer could the efficiency miracle happen—not the other way around.

  • The same logic repeats in three completely different industries. Fast-food chain Wendy's: syrup shortage scheduling across 6,450 stores in North America used to take 15 employees a full day of checking; after connecting to the data platform, a plan comes out in five minutes. Mortgage giant Fannie Mae: using AI to detect mortgage fraud with a detection rate above 99%, far exceeding the old rule-based system. Citibank: customer credit approval compressed from hours to minutes—the AI reads credit history, transaction habits, industry risk, and affiliated companies in one go. Three industries, one common precondition: first organize scattered data into a semantic layer machines can understand, and only then does intelligence have somewhere to land. (Source: Appendix C)

7.3 借势而上：站在行业的风口上

Third, use AI to fight AI's integration war. An important variable after 2025: integration work itself is starting to be automated by AI. The scenario a16z envisioned has partially become reality—for legacy systems without APIs, browser agents simulate humans to fetch data; field mapping, format conversion, and API documentation interpretation are largely handed to models. The standard leading teams set for themselves is worth quoting: "Automate the integration process as much as possible—process mining, data pipelines, system integration, API documentation—this speed advantage compounds." Using AI to do the work of AI deployment may be the most elegant part of this role.

借势的第一层动作是「定义议题」。风口的注意力是公共资源，谁的内容定义了议题，谁就收割了注意力。a16z 用一篇行业雄文为整个赛道定了调；OpenFDE 这样的社区，在聚合从业者的同时也在聚合定义权。对具体公司而言，想抢下议题的定义权，最好的办法是把压箱底的东西公开出来：打法手册、失败复盘、价值数据——独家的一手认知，是议题定义权的唯一货币。

Fourth, know when to go around rather than conquer. Not every legacy system is worth integrating head-on. For some systems the right answer is "shadow reads" (read-only snapshots, nightly syncs), for some it's "manual ferrying" (keeping a human step during the transition), and for some it's simply "declaring isolation" (that process's data is out of scope for this project—tell the client explicitly). An engineer's pride always wants to conquer every fortress; the FDE's judgment shows in choosing the battlefield—remember your goal is not total technical victory, but customer outcomes.

4.6 Change Management: Getting the Client Organization to Stand Up for You

圈层一：从业者社区。FDE 是一个正在形成中的职业共同体——2025 年它还散在各家公司内部，2026 年已经有了 OpenFDE 这样的专门社区、专门的薪酬报告、专门的面试指南。职业共同体的形成，对先行者是天赐红利：为共同体贡献内容（方法论、工具、数据），你的名字就会与这个新兴职业绑定。概念的传播路径历来如此：先行的布道者，最后都成了概念的代名词。

This section addresses the biggest soft resistance to activation: the organization. Of the report's five major roadblocks, "employee resistance" and "change management" together account for nearly half. Going live technically requires only engineers; changing behavior requires the whole organization.

In the FDE context, change management centers on cultivating three groups of people.

Champions | Your internal allies: Behind every successful deployment stands a champion inside the client: someone who genuinely believes in it and is willing to stake their credibility to clear the way for you. David Wakeling of Linklaters is the classic example—as the firm's head of market innovation, he was Harvey's counterpart, advocate, and protector within the firm. The keys to cultivating a champion: give him material he can present (stories he can tell), give him battle honors (turn his vision into a career highlight), give him safety (when things fail, you stand in front). One well-treated champion is worth ten product demos.

Influencers | Informal opinion leaders: Every organization has its uncrowned kings: senior analysts, veteran shop-floor masters, the person in the department you "just ask." They hold no formal power, but they hold trust. Their one remark "this thing actually works" outweighs three all-hands emails from management; their one remark "just for show" is enough to let the system die silently at the grassroots. During activation, deliberately cultivate these people: invite them to be the first users, take every one of their complaints seriously, and make adopting their suggestions visible—"this field was added per Master Wang's suggestion." Making influencers co-authors is the shortest path to breaking the "I don't use what I didn't build" mindset.

The aggrieved | Interest groups touched by the solution: Automation inevitably redistributes work, and redistributing work inevitably creates losers: approval roles whose presence is compressed, departments whose information moats are pierced, veterans whose "signature skills" are replaced. Ignore them and they become the system's most stubborn underground resisters—passive non-cooperation, spreading incident stories, voting no at acceptance. Mature change management designs "a way out for the aggrieved" in advance: redirect freed-up headcount to higher-value work (with a public commitment not to lay off), transform "gatekeepers" into "coaches" (the old masters' experience is used to train the system), and let the aggrieved see their place in the future. This is both humane and pure pragmatism—the cost of resistance far exceeds the cost of appeasement.

The partnership between ticketing platform Vivid Seats and Sierra shows what "full mobilization on the client side" looks like during activation. After deciding to adopt agents, the company's product, customer experience, and engineering teams all went all in—"once we made the decision, we went 100% in, with everyone testing." The result: from kickoff to launch in under four weeks, and after launch, self-service issue resolution rose 40% and customer satisfaction rose 35%.

But more valuable were the follow-on changes: with routine questions taken over by agents, the customer experience team shifted from "putting out fires in a queue" to fixing root-cause process problems, even finding spare capacity for fan-delight projects like "surprise upgrades"; and agent conversation data began feeding back into the product—"if ten thousand people ask about the same feature in a month, we can immediately prioritize it. The best moment is when a pattern leads to a product improvement so users never need to ask for help at all." (Source: Appendix C) The highest state of activation is a system that makes itself unnecessary—this case also foreshadows Chapter 7's "field feedback into product."

If things are buzzing while you are on site but cool off rapidly once you leave, that is not activation—it is backup dancing. A truly activated organization moves forward on its own.

4.7 I, Robot—Automating the Delivery Work Itself

Automating delivery to customers is the product itself; automating your own delivery work is efficiency. This section covers the latter—it is the first lever for FDE teams to escape "revenue growing linearly with headcount."

  • A striking proportion of FDEs' daily work consists of templatable repetitive tasks: initializing deployment environments for new customers, standard pipelines for data ingestion, questionnaire responses for security reviews, pre-launch checklists, and formats for periodic customer reports. Every action performed a second time should trigger a question: "Can this become a script, a template, or a checklist?"

The practices of leading teams have already crystallized into four types of assets.

Deployment templates: Package environment setup, permission configuration, and monitoring integration into one-click infrastructure-as-code. When a new customer comes on board, a standardized environment can be spun up in the customer's cloud environment on day one, instead of spending a week configuring from scratch. Decagon can compress deployment for simple scenarios to 15 days, backed by a highly templated onboarding layer. (Source: Appendix C)

Integration component library: Connectors for mainstream enterprise systems (pre-built system integration components), written once and reused everywhere. Palantir's ontology model essentially takes this logic to its extreme: the output of data ingestion is not a one-off pipeline, but reusable data assets with business meaning.

  • Checklist culture: Security review checklists, launch readiness checklists, and handover checklists for when you leave. Checklists are humanity's oldest tool against complexity, and the cornerstone of quality stability for FDE teams—they turn "reliance on individual experience" into "reliance on organizational memory."
  • Automated reporting: Weekly reports to customers and field intelligence to the company should all be generated semi-automatically. An FDE's hourly cost is too high to be spent on copy-pasting—and even less should "forgetting to report" sever the loop between the field and the product that Chapter 7 will discuss.
  • How substantial the compounding returns of automation can be is corroborated from one angle: a16z lists "building or buying tools to automate service delivery" as a key recommendation for building forward deployed teams, and judges it to be the key variable that lets this generation of AI companies run faster and clear lower deal-size thresholds than the previous generation of enterprise software companies. Sierra's publicly reported fastest launch took four weeks, and Palantir compressed its sales cycle from nine months to a few weeks—not by engineers working overtime, but by turning yesterday's delivery into today's scaffolding.

This section also answers the most common objection—"Isn't FDE just a human-wave tactic?" The essence of a human wave is not having many people, but everyone doing non-reusable, one-off labor. When every delivery by an FDE team paves the way for the next delivery, it is no longer a human wave but a machine that is accelerating itself.

The seven weapons of activation deployment are done: the speed of hot fixes, the direction of evals systems, the cleverness of lowering barriers, the patience for integration wars, the soft skills of change management, and the leverage of automation. The system is alive and the people are moving. But the cruelty of the enterprise business is this: activation only buys time; renewal is survival. The next chapter: defending the renewal.

5.1 Renewals and Churn

Business starts with the math: acquiring a new customer costs several times more than retaining an existing one. Enterprise business magnifies this calculation by two orders of magnitude: the customer acquisition cost for a large customer—months of sales cycle, bootcamp-style investment, proof-of-concept costs—easily runs into hundreds of thousands of dollars, while the cost of renewal approaches zero. The renewal rate is the lifeline of the FDE business model.

First, churn. Enterprise customer churn differs sharply in shape from consumer product churn: consumer churn is a silent uninstall, while enterprise customer churn is a long drawn-out death by a thousand cuts—first usage quietly declines, then someone at regular meetings starts questioning "is this thing really worth it," then renewal negotiations are indefinitely postponed with "let's revisit next year," and finally, in some budget season, it is uprooted entirely. Even more frightening is the collateral damage: as Chapter 3 noted, the enterprise market holds grudges—one public failure will be chewed over for years within the industry's small circles.

The causes of enterprise customer churn can be grouped into five categories, ordered by preventability.

Value evaporation: The system is still running, but no one remembers what problem it solved. This usually stems from a break in the value narrative—after the original business problem that justified the project is solved, no one keeps restating to the organization (especially newly appointed managers) "why we have this system." Value is not proven once; it must be continuously re-proven.

Champion departure: Your internal ally gets promoted, transferred, or leaves, and the successor never experienced the original decision, feels no natural affinity for the system, or is even hostile—"the predecessor's vanity project." There is a piece of jargon in enterprise software circles: "When the champion moves, the contract wobbles."

Quality drift: The business changes, the data changes, the models get updated, and the system's output quality slowly declines; user trust slowly collapses along with it—by the time management notices the collapse, it is usually too late.

Cost backlash: The more the system is used, the higher the bill, and the stricter finance's scrutiny. If the value narrative cannot keep up with the bill's growth, "success" itself becomes an obstacle to renewal.

Vendor withdrawal symptoms: The customer's wariness of "being locked in." The consulting firm Gartner has even issued a startling prediction: by 2028, 70% of enterprises will be forced to abandon AI agent solutions led by Forward Deployed Engineers due to excessive vendor costs and insufficient internal skills. (Source: Appendix C) The prediction itself is a warning to all FDE teams: if your model makes customers feel held hostage, the market will rebel collectively.

Now, how to measure "retention." Consumer products look at retention rates; enterprise business requires three levels (full metrics in Appendix A): usage and depth of engagement (behavioral level), health scores (relationship level), and net revenue retention (financial level, measuring whether the same cohort of existing customers pays more or less this year than last year). Net revenue retention is the chief judge: above 100%, it means your existing business grows even without signing a single new deal—this is also the most direct proof of "delivery as operations."

What does top-tier performance on this metric look like? Palantir's Q4 2025 earnings report gave three numbers: net revenue retention of 139%—existing customers growing nearly 40% automatically; contracted but unrecognized revenue also up 145%—no worry about a lack of business for years to come; and a record-breaking $4.26 billion in total contract value signed in a single quarter. Management specifically explained one detail: the 139% figure excludes revenue from customers signed in the last twelve months—it is purely "existing customers' trust appreciating in value." What this company was most ridiculed for in its early years was "project-based work, no repeat purchases"; twenty years later, it has proven with the same cohort of customers that if close-at-hand delivery can continuously create value, renewal is not a sales problem but a matter of time. (Source: Appendix C)

The remaining five sections of this chapter address the five causes of churn death respectively: performance and stability (preventing quality drift), the art of trade-offs (preventing cost backlash), onboarding and training (preventing usage decay), organizational maintenance (preventing champion risk), and reactivation mechanisms (preventing value evaporation).

5.2 Optimizing System Performance and Stability

The rule for consumer products is: one second slower to load, retention drops a notch. Performance problems in enterprise systems have a different pathology: enterprise users actually tolerate "slow" better than consumers (they're used to the sluggishness of legacy systems), but their tolerance for "unreliable" approaches zero.

OpenAI 的回应分两步。第一步，2024 年组建前线部署工程团队——从 2 名工程师起步，科林·贾维斯带队，快速扩张到遍布旧金山、纽约、伦敦、都柏林、慕尼黑、巴黎、苏黎世、东京、新加坡、悉尼的全球网络，一年内从 2 人涨到 52 人。招聘启事写得毫不掩饰：「嵌入到那些模型表现至关重要、交付迫在眉睫、模糊性就是默认状态的客户中去」，出差最高 50%。欧洲区负责人富尼耶说得更直白：「市场需求超出了我们的预期。」（出处见附录 C）

Reliability carries ten times the weight in an enterprise context as in consumer scenarios, for three reasons. First, the output of enterprise systems feeds into real business decisions—the loss from a single wrong inventory recommendation can wipe out a year of the system's gains. Second, errors get amplified and spread—the system errs once, and the story circulates in the department for three months; it gets things right a hundred times, and no one remembers. Third, trust in enterprise systems is built in quarters and destroyed in minutes.

The four fortifications an FDE team builds to guard reliability.

The first: Make explicit service commitments, and make them visible. Commitments on availability, latency, and error rates shouldn't just live in the contract—they should become monitoring dashboards the customer can view themselves. Turn "the system is stable" from a position you have to defend into a fact the customer can check anytime—transparency itself is a trust asset.

The second: Design guardrails for the "probabilistic" nature of AI. AI systems can never be 100% correct; accept this reality in engineering and manage it in product: outputs the model is unsure about must be flagged or routed to humans; high-risk actions must have human sign-off; every major model update must be re-run through evals to prevent old problems from recurring—the evals system built in Chapter 4 becomes part of the production guardrails here.

A model example of guardrails: In its work with financial institutions, Anthropic made "auditability and traceability" a core design principle, with a replayable evidence chain for every agent decision. In domains like finance and healthcare, auditability isn't a bonus—it's the ticket to entry.

The third: On-call and response must make the customer feel you're always there. When the system goes down at 2 a.m., the FDE's reaction speed is the customer's felt temperature of the relationship. Chapter 1 quoted that iron rule from practitioners: "If the deployment goes down at 2 a.m., you don't file a ticket, you don't blame another team, and you don't go back to bed. You fix it." The spirit of that line must be embodied in mechanisms: on-call rotations, incident retrospectives, and honest notifications to the customer after every incident—enterprise customers can accept incidents, but not concealment.

The fourth: Plan capacity and cost in sync with growth. Growing usage is a happy problem, but handled poorly it becomes an assassin at renewal time. The performance team must always run half a step ahead of the usage curve: before the customer's peak business season arrives, capacity plans, rate limits, and degradation contingencies are already in place. Eliminate slowness before the customer ever feels it—the best performance work is work no one notices.

5.3 Lossy Service—Letting Go of Unnecessary Persistence

"Lossy service" is a concept from internet product design: proactively degrade in extreme scenarios to preserve core value. In the FDE context this concept has a deeper variant—it concerns the eternal tug-of-war between customization and standardization, which I call "the art of restraint."

「安全偏执」变成企业市场杀手锏的证据，写在客户的评价里。网络安全公司派拓网络把 Claude 部署给 2500 名开发者，工程总监的评价很直接：「他们比别家更在乎安全——每个会都在谈安全影响。作为最大的网络安全公司，这对我们很重要。」结果：功能开发速度提升两三成，初级开发者完成集成任务快了 70%。制药巨头诺和诺德用它写临床研究文档：过去十几周的报告，现在十分钟出稿。与此同时，它与咨询巨头埃森哲合作培训了三万名 Claude 专业顾问，企业客户数两年内从不足千家涨到 30 万家以上。（出处见附录 C）

The backdrop is a gravity every FDE team encounters: as long as the customer is there, requests keep coming; as long as requests keep coming, customization never stops. Three months later you look back and find the customer's deployment overgrown with custom features, half of them used by three people, with all the maintenance cost on your shoulders. Let it go further and you're carrying a mountain of "customization debt"—it eats your profits (maintenance costs devour contract revenue) while tying your hands (a platform upgrade can step on a customization landmine).

The wisdom of "lossy" is proactively subtracting along three dimensions.

Feature dimension | Dare to say "no" to long-tail requests: The criterion isn't whether the request is reasonable (most requests are reasonable in isolation), but two questions: does the number of users it serves times the frequency of use justify its lifetime maintenance cost? Can it be generalized into a platform capability (if so, route it into the feedback channel in Chapter 7)? For requests that fail both, the best response is a workaround, not code. An FDE is not an order-taker—order-taker culture is precisely the servility the "French waiter" model rejects.

Commitment dimension | Tiered commitments, not uniformly highest: Not every feature deserves four-nines availability. Guard the core transaction path at the highest standard, and generously accept degradation for reports and exploratory features—degradation strategies (turning off heavy computation at peak times), off-peak strategies (running heavy jobs at night), and tiered commitments clearly stated to the customer in advance. Concentrating reliability resources on the critical path is more honest, and more sustainable, than uniformly mediocre reliability.

Cost dimension | Proactive management of the usage bill: Section 5.1 discussed "cost blowback": the higher the usage, the higher the bill, and when the value narrative can't keep up, success itself becomes an obstacle to renewal. A proactive FDE team acts before the bill becomes painful: offering cost optimization plans (caching, batching, model tiering—using cheaper models for simple requests), redesigning pricing structures (moving from pure usage-based to a smoother "platform fee plus usage" structure), and most importantly—before the customer's finance lead asks, proactively showing them the "value ledger corresponding to this bill." By the time you're called in to explain the bill, you've already lost half; the person who proactively lays out the numbers walks into renewal negotiations holding all the cards.

The essence of "lossy" is acknowledging that resources are always finite, and continuously betting those finite resources on what the customer truly cares about. It's in the same lineage as Chapter 2's "refuse expensive failures": restraint isn't inaction—it's the ability to choose your battles.

5.4 Onboarding New Users Quickly

After a system goes live, the user base doesn't stand still: new employees join, organizations restructure, new departments come into scope. "New user onboarding" for enterprise systems is a rolling process that never ends. Design the onboarding mechanism well, and usage rises over time; design it poorly, and usage naturally decays as the original cohort of trained users churns out.

Enterprise onboarding is fundamentally different from consumer products: consumer onboarding is a one-time self-service flow, while enterprise onboarding is an organizational project of "person-to-person transmission." Three reusable structures.

Tiered training: One-size-fits-all all-hands training is the biggest waste. Effective tiering has three layers: deep training for admins and internal champions (their future role is internal experts); scenario-based training for ordinary users (not features, but "how to do your three daily tasks with the system," capped at 30 minutes); and one-sentence training for executives ("open this, and this number is the answer"). The spirit of tiering continues Section 4.4: each role learns only the part relevant to them.

The "train the trainer" lever: The FDE team will eventually withdraw, and training must be handed over before that happens. Identify the enthusiasts in the customer's organization and develop them into internal trainers and internal answer-people—give them official certification, a dedicated support channel, and exposure in front of executives. This was a core design of the Anthropic-FIS partnership: "transfer knowledge so FIS can independently build and scale its own agents." Teaching the customer to teach itself is the highest form of delivery.

Two examples of "person-to-person" training at scale are worth comparing. When BBVA rolled out to 120,000 people, it relied not on the vendor's training team but on two groups of internal roles: a bank-wide "AI pioneer network" running workshops and mining use cases across business units, and a group of power users colleagues called "AI geeks" who coached people hands-on. The consulting giant Accenture's partnership with Anthropic was another order of magnitude: 30,000 consultants received systematic training in Claude, forming one of the world's largest networks of AI practitioners—and Accenture then brought that force into its own clients. (Source in Appendix C.) Three orders of magnitude apart in scale, yet the same structure: what the vendor trains is never "users," but "people who will go on to train others."

Documentation and self-service systems: Most enterprise documentation is written once and never read, unless it meets two standards: organized by task rather than feature ("how to handle an anomalous refund," not "refund module feature description"), and embedded in the product rather than existing separately (appearing right where the user gets stuck). Documentation is both a deliverable for the customer and a scalable asset for yourself—seventy percent of it can be inherited for the training system of the next similar customer.

5.5 Organizational Maintenance and Single-Point Dependency

《增长黑客 AI 周报》记录过一线执行者申悦的观察：在国内不少项目里，FDE 更像乙方外包人力，干的是填坑、擦屁股的脏活累活。她亲历的一个千万级国企项目，大老板签完合同就没了下文，项目里没有真正负责的人，FDE 被架在真空层——向上没有决策者，向下没有执行者，最后只能靠研究「汇报逻辑」来争取验收。另一个广东的民营家装公司，厂二代老板开门见山：帮她把 6000 人裁到 3000 人。可 AI 能替代的是任务，不是岗位，项目就在反复挨骂中艰难推进。

Section 5.1 listed "champion departure" as the second leading cause of death for enterprise renewals. This section addresses it specifically, because both its prevalence and its lethality are severely underestimated.

Single-point dependency forms almost naturally: the project was initiated by the champion, the relationship is maintained through the champion, and the success narrative is told by the champion—then one day he leaves. The successor arrives with his own agenda, your system doesn't crack his top twenty priorities, renewal season comes, no one speaks for you, and the contract dies silently. Countless systems in this industry that "were clearly working well but got cut" died from this script.

这个质疑未必有标准答案，但它和第 5 章引过的高德纳预测——2028 年前 70% 的企业可能放弃 FDE 主导的方案——形成了奇妙的呼应：FDE 模式最大的敌人，也许不是技术，而是它自己的成本结构。这个问题本书不打算给标准答案，但它是每一个想入场的人，都该先想清楚的问题。

The fortifications need to be built in three places.

Grid the relationships: From the day you recognize the single-point risk, systematically broaden the relationship network: beyond one champion, develop at least two more independent relationship lines—the community of daily users on the business line (internal trainers are natural nodes), and one level up, an executive sponsor. Executive relationships don't need frequent maintenance, but they must stay visible at key moments (quarterly reviews, before renewals). The test of a relationship grid: if any single person leaves, the information channels don't break.

Institutionalize the value: Rewrite the system's value from "the champion's political achievement" into "the organization's asset." Concrete actions: regular all-hands reporting of value data (so the using departments themselves feel they can't live without it), repeated telling of success stories in the customer's internal meetings (forming collective memory), and embedding the system into process documentation (once the system is written into standard operating procedures, replacing it requires rewriting the process—this is the strongest institutional lock-in). The goal is for any newly appointed manager to conclude in the first week that "this system is a fixed asset here."

Make departures a ritual too: When the champion leaves, most vendors' reaction is passive lament. The right reaction is to treat it as a relationship-building opportunity: hold a dignified "mission accomplished" ceremony for the departing champion (a thank-you letter, a summary of achievements, giving him portable career capital—such as the right to share the case externally), while immediately starting the handover with the successor—framed as "helping the successor achieve quick wins," not "persuading him to keep our system." The departing champion's new employer is a potential entry point to your next customer; treating departures well turns churn risk into an acquisition channel.

5.6 Designing Health Scores and Wake-Up Mechanisms

In this final section, all the actions for protecting renewals are consolidated into one system: the customer health score framework. Its goal is to turn "relationship decay" from a sudden death into a slow curve detected long in advance—you always have time to intervene.

What does health measure? For consumer products, watch retention—daily active users is enough. For enterprise customers, you must watch four types of signals: usage signals (the trend in weekly active users, what fraction of people use key features, whether it's real use or box-ticking), value signals (how the originally agreed business metrics are doing, whether the ROI story still holds), relationship signals (the champion's employment status, the number of executive touchpoints in the last 30 days, how quickly the customer responds to your team), and commercial signals (the trend in the ratio of usage to billing, contract expiration dates, competitor movements). Combine these four signal types into a single health score and alert when it falls below the warning line. The greatest use of the health score isn't the score itself—it's that it forces the team to go through every customer, one by one, every week.

Quarterly business reviews: turn "what value did we create" into a fixed quarterly event. The quarterly business review (QBR, a regular meeting where vendor and customer align on value and plans each quarter) is the most important renewal engineering in enterprise services, yet it's often reduced to a perfunctory presentation. A good quarterly review has three disciplines: speak the customer's language, not your product's language ("this quarter we saved you X hours of work," not "this quarter we launched these features"); make the customer's business side the protagonist, not the audience (let the champion tell his team's story while you provide the data ammunition); and close with "next quarter's value plan"—a renewal shouldn't be a one-time negotiation before expiration, but a series that naturally continues being written every quarter.

Wake-up mechanisms: what to do when usage declines. Consumer products wake users up with push notifications and emails; to wake up enterprise customers, you need the combination of "people plus data"—the moment the health score drops, a tiered response kicks in immediately: mild decline (usage down in one department) triggers an on-site follow-up visit by internal trainers; moderate decline (overall activity down thirty percent) triggers a dedicated diagnosis by the FDE team—usually one of three causes: business changes, personnel changes, or quality drift, each handled accordingly; severe decline (near deactivation) brings in the executive level for an honest conversation about "whether it's still worth continuing"—sometimes the answer is a graceful exit or downgrade, preserving the relationship and reputation and leaving the door open for a future reunion.

At this point, the five lines of defense for protecting renewals are complete. But enterprise relationships that only defend and never attack will wither—in the customer's organization, there is always the next unsolved problem. 6.1 A World of Free Validation

The internet economy turned "free" from a gimmick into a strategy. The FDE trade can't avoid the "free" hurdle either—it just looks different: in the enterprise AI era, the most expensive free thing is the free proof of concept.

First, look at the scale of this free economy. Palantir's AIP bootcamps essentially turned free validation into an assembly line: customers bring real data, a deployable prototype is built in one to five days, at zero or token cost. Starting from fewer than a hundred sessions in 2022, with sessions doubling year after year, to nearly 6 per day at the 2025 peak—counting several top engineers for several days per session, that's tens of millions of dollars per year in free investment. Former Palantir engineer Barry put it even more bluntly about earlier days: "We burned millions of dollars on customer pilots; many projects had literally negative infinity margins because we did them for free."

Why does free validation work? Three ledgers balance out.

The first ledger: customer acquisition. Traditional enterprise software acquires customers with a sales army: travel, dinners, bids, lengthy negotiations—money spent heavily and uncontrollably. Bootcamps changed the playbook: instead of persuading the customer, let the customer persuade himself—an executive personally clicking through a system running on his own data beats a hundred slides. Palantir's sales cycle compressed from nine to twelve months to weeks, and US commercial revenue grew 137% year over year in a single quarter—the free bootcamp is widely credited as the main engine. Free validation isn't a cost; it swaps sales expenses for engineering expenses—and engineering expenses can accumulate into product, while sales expenses cannot.

The second ledger: the power of risk pricing. McGrew's advice is that early-stage startups should proactively take on risk: "Pay us when it works." The confidence comes from belief in the product, and from a sober calculation—enterprise customers' biggest doubt about a new vendor is "can you actually do it," and free validation is the solvent for that doubt. Once the doubt dissolves, pricing power returns to your hands: what the customer buys is no longer "a gamble" but "a certainty already verified with their own eyes," and certainty commands a premium.

The third ledger: even failures must be worth something. Free validation will inevitably have failures—that's portfolio investing 101. The difference lies in where failures go: a traditional sales failure leaves a pile of travel receipts; a failed FDE-style free validation leaves an understanding of an industry, a set of reusable components, and a body of evals data. As long as you've built the feedback loop mechanism from Chapter 7, even failed validations are deposits in the company's account.

But free validation has one fatal prerequisite: it must be a "graduation system," not an "unlimited residency system." Which brings us to the next section.

6.3 Outcome-Based Pricing: Customers Pay for What They Get

In advertising there's a highly effective approach: track user behavior and target precisely. The pricing principle for FDEs is just as straightforward: charge the customer for the value they receive.

  • The dominant pricing model of the online software era was per seat—paying for "usage rights." That logic is being eroded in the AI era: when an AI agent can do the work of 10 people, charging per seat becomes a joke—would you pay for 0.1 of a seat? Hence the rise of "outcome-based pricing," and Sierra's charging by "resolved conversations" is the most prominent example: customers don't pay for software; they pay for "problems solved."
  • The evolution of outcome-based pricing has four tiers; the higher you go, the closer to value—and the harder to execute.

Usage-based: billing by the model's metered units, call volume, or processing volume. The advantage is clarity and measurability; the drawback is that it tracks cost rather than value—high usage may mean high value, or it may mean an inefficient system. Usage-based billing for model APIs is the industry's baseline, but application-layer companies rarely make it their sole pricing model.

Action-based: billing per "completed return processing" or "generated compliance report." A step beyond usage-based, as the pricing unit starts to carry business meaning.

6.4 Deepening Existing Accounts: From One Department to a Wide Web

The enterprise market has an iron law: the biggest revenue growth lies not in new customers, but inside existing ones. The industry measures this with net revenue retention; excellent FDE-driven companies consistently post net revenue retention above 120%—without signing a single new deal, existing revenue grows by 20% on its own. Palantir's commercial story is, at its core, a story of deepening existing accounts: from one intelligence team to an entire institution, from one factory to an entire conglomerate, from government agencies to a commercial empire.

For the playbook of deepening existing accounts, the industry has a vivid phrase: "land and expand." Landing is covered in the previous five chapters; expansion has three directions.

  • Horizontal | From one team to adjacent teams: Once you've built intelligent ticketing for the customer service department, the after-sales department and technical support next door are your easiest next customers. In horizontal expansion, the most persuasive evidence lies inside the customer: the same company, the same data environment, testimonials from colleagues in the neighboring department—this is expansion with the least sales friction, requiring almost no rebuilding of trust. Harvey's expansion within law firms followed exactly this rhythm: enter through a single practice group, six months of hands-on validation, then expand horizontally across the whole firm.
  • Vertical | From the execution layer to the decision-making layer: The initial project usually serves frontline executors; vertical expansion propagates value up the chain: analytics and early warnings for middle managers, decision cockpits for executives. The significance of vertical expansion isn't just revenue—it's security. As Chapter 5 noted, a system loved only by the grassroots has no defender during budget season; only a system that has entered the executives' field of view enters the organization's ranks of "fixed assets."
  • Depth | From auxiliary tool to core process: The deepest expansion is turning the system from "a helpful tool" into "an indispensable process"—from "giving suggestions" to "executing business actions," from "optional" to "part of the standard operating procedure." Every step of deep expansion comes with greater responsibility and a higher threshold of trust, but it also builds the deepest moat: replacing a tool just means swapping software; replacing a system embedded in a process is like performing surgery.

  • 6.6 Turning Penalty into Reward: The Pricing Psychology of Usage and Expansion
  • Good mechanism design can turn a penalty into a reward. In the FDE business model, this wisdom applies to a delicate scenario: what to do when customer usage exceeds expectations.
  • The crude approach is "punitive overage": once the contracted usage is exhausted, the excess is billed at punitive rates, or the system throttles and slows down directly. This was common in the early days of cloud computing, with disastrous results—customers deliberately suppressed usage to avoid overage, utilization dropped, value shrank, and renewal became a lose-lose. This is the same principle as telecom's tiered data pricing, but it must be made clear in the contract: what the customer sees is not "use more, pay more," but "use more, pay less per unit—we're getting a better deal together." Same bill, different narrative, different relationship trajectory.

  • Converting failure into organizational assets requires three mechanisms.
  • Blame-free post-mortems: The only rule of a post-mortem meeting: focus on the issue, not the person. Once post-mortems are tied to performance, people start embellishing failures—and embellished failures have zero teaching value. Blame-free doesn't mean no accountability; it redefines "accountability" as "whether the lessons were extracted honestly and completely." A project lead who conducts an honest post-mortem of a failure may contribute more to the organization than one who succeeds by luck.
  • Structured lessons: Post-mortem output cannot be "be careful next time" style musings; it must be searchable, actionable knowledge: what are the high-risk signals for this customer type? Which integration assumption was overturned? Which eval metric was designed wrong? These conclusions should go into the "minefield" section of the playbook and into the due diligence checklist for new projects—so the next team's "pre-engagement due diligence" automatically carries historical lessons. Failure is only truly learned from when it's written into the process.

Moderate external sharing of failures: This is the most counterintuitive layer: sharing failure lessons externally (anonymized) is highly effective trust marketing. As discussed in Section 3.5, Barry's "airing our own dirty laundry" article became the best evangelism for the Palantir model—readers can tell who's delivering booth talk and who's telling trench truth. A team willing to publicly dissect its failures sends the signal "we've already paid tuition for this industry's detours"—exactly what enterprise customers most want to hear.

  • 7.3 Riding the Momentum: Standing on the Industry's Tailwind

Together, these currents created a rare situation: customers are actively looking for you, discussing the very thing you want to sell.
  • The first move in riding momentum is "defining the agenda." The attention of a tailwind is a public resource; whoever's content defines the agenda harvests the attention. a16z set the tone for the entire space with one landmark industry essay; communities like OpenFDE aggregate practitioners while also aggregating agenda-setting power. For a specific company, the best way to seize agenda-setting power is to make public what you've been keeping under wraps: playbooks, failure post-mortems, value data—exclusive first-hand insight is the only currency of agenda-setting power.

  • 7.4 Building a Propagation Loop Outside the Client Organization
  • Word-of-mouth propagation that relies not on advertising happens mainly through three circles in the FDE business.
  • Circle one: practitioner communities. FDE is a professional community still taking shape — in 2025 it was scattered inside individual companies, but by 2026 there were already dedicated communities like OpenFDE, dedicated salary reports, and dedicated interview guides. The formation of a professional community is a heaven-sent dividend for early movers: contribute content to the community (methodologies, tools, data), and your name becomes bound to this emerging profession. That has always been the propagation path of concepts: the early evangelists end up becoming synonymous with the concept itself.
  • Circle two: the client's industry community. Every industry has its own small circles — bankers' forums, law firm partners' annual meetings, hospital administrators' summits. The trust dividend discussed in Section 3.3 flows mainly within these closed circles. The ticket into these circles is not advertising, but client advocates who are invited to speak — your job is to make sure they have something worth saying and can say it well. A ten-minute talk a client gives on your behalf at their industry summit beats a hundred days of booth presence at a trade show.
  • Circle three: the ecosystem partner network. Section 3.7 covered the customer-acquisition value of ecosystem bundling; here we emphasize its propagation property: cloud vendors' Solutions Architects, consulting firm advisors, and integrators' project managers move through different client sites every day — the list of "reliable teams" they mention is the fastest and most accurate promotional channel. The key to cultivating this circle is letting them "win": let ecosystem partners gain performance, case studies, and reputation from working with you. Those who help others succeed are remembered by the entire network.
  • The flywheel formed by the three circles together is: delivery creates stories, stories spread through the circles, propagation brings new opportunities, and new opportunities create new stories. The difference between this flywheel and traditional marketing is that its fuel is entirely "delivered value" — the essence of word-of-mouth propagation is making every past effort pull in future business.
  • 7.5 Built-in Virality Factors in the Product
  • Good products spread by themselves — a line like "Sent from so-and-so mail" in an email signature was the earliest self-propagation design. Systems delivered by FDEs can also embed "built-in diffusion mechanisms," letting the system itself become the expansion's salesperson.
  • Factor one: visibility design. A system's outputs naturally circulate — weekly reports, analysis results, approval workflows. Make every output carry its "origin": "Generated by so-and-so system" in the report footer, a handling-entry link inside anomaly alerts. When a beautiful analysis report is forwarded to a director in the next department, the people who see it will naturally ask "what was this built with." The essence of visibility design is letting the system's value exhibit itself within daily workflows, rather than relying on dedicated presentations.

Factor two: collaborative onboarding. Design features that require cross-role collaboration: cases flagged by investigators need supervisor review, analysts' reports need business-side confirmation — every collaborative action naturally introduces the system to a new user. New users brought in through collaboration are not "marketed to"; they are "brought in by the work itself," and their acceptance is completely different.

  • Factor three: a self-service exploration layer. Beyond the core delivery, leave a low-risk practice area (a sandbox) where client employees can try things on their own, like "have AI do an analysis for me." The use cases that grow out of the sandbox are often the most vital expansion leads — a scenario an employee discovers by playing around themselves has deeper roots than one a salesperson pushes ten times. Let everyone help you with discovery, and let the FDE do the hard tackling.
  • Two new products in 2026 turned this factor into an open play. Sierra launched "Ghostwriter" in March: clients upload their own standard operating procedures, historical conversation logs, even a photo of a whiteboard, or describe a goal in plain language, and the system automatically generates a production-ready agent covering voice, chat, and email channels in over thirty languages — for the first time, clients' business staff could "self-serve build agents," while the forward deployed team shifted from "hand-building every one" to "acceptance testing and backstopping." Decagon's agent operations workflow walked this path even earlier: clients' customer service managers write workflows in natural language ("for returns over 30 days, check membership tier first, and process if policy-compliant") without waiting for engineers' schedules — reportedly, Chime achieved a 70% self-service resolution rate, Duolingo an 80% issue deflection rate, and fitness platform ClassPass cut customer service costs by 95%. (Source in Appendix C) When clients start building themselves, your role shifts from construction crew to design institute — this is the ultimate form of factor three.
  • Factor four: cross-boundary value evidence. Automatically generate a "quarterly value report" each quarter and let it flow naturally to the client's executive layer. This report is both the renewal engineering of Chapter 5 and the door-knocker for vertical expansion — every number the executives see plants a seed for "where else is this system worth deploying."
  • The common principle of built-in factors is: if you want others to spread the word for you, you must first let them genuinely realize value from using it. Any design that propagates for propagation's sake will be recognized in enterprise settings as "vendor tricks" and met with distaste. There is no viral spread in the enterprise market, only "chain reactions of word-of-mouth" — slow, but solid at every step.

  • When a system expands from one department to ten, you are effectively redrawing the organization's power map: information monopolies are broken (data once held exclusively by one department is now visible company-wide), approval authority is compressed (things AI answers in seconds no longer need three days of layered sign-offs), and professional barriers are leveled (veteran employees' "secret know-how" gets encoded into the system). Every one of these changes creates silent enemies.
  • For handling organizational politics, FDE veterans have distilled three principles.
  • Principle one: expansion must bring along "the locals." When entering each new department, first find that department's "local power broker" — the most respected senior employee — and invite them into the adaptation process: "For this scenario, what's different in your department?" Their answers make the solution more precise, and their participation lets it move unimpeded through their turf. People do not oppose what they helped build — this is the most reliable lever in organizational behavior.
  • Harvey's expansion into top law firms is a classic case study in reading organizational politics. Three groups coexist within a firm: partners — those who pay, but don't do the work themselves; paralegals — those who do the work, but don't sign off; and knowledge management lawyers — attached to neither side, yet able to see both sides' blind spots. Generic survey questionnaires would compress these three groups into the same set of dropdown options, yielding a pile of distorted average scores; Harvey's forward deployed team instead designed separate interviews and demos for each group: showing partners "how much money this deal makes," showing paralegals "three fewer days of overtime this week," and showing knowledge management lawyers "a place for the firm's tacit knowledge to finally accumulate." A deployment must make all three groups feel they are beneficiaries at the same time — miss any one group, and the system will be quietly shelved at the corresponding stage. (Source in Appendix C)
  • Principle 2: Make every step of expansion have "beneficiaries." Before expanding, work out clearly what each relevant department gains: the department whose data silos were penetrated gains globally visible analytics; the department whose approval authority was reduced gains focus on exceptions (AI handles the routine, humans focus on the hard cases). Expansion without beneficiaries is invasion; expansion with beneficiaries is liberation—the only difference is whether you have thought through and clearly articulated "what they get out of it."
  • Principle 3: Always leave the "old order" its dignity. The processes replaced by the system were once designed by some capable person; the knowledge encoded was once some expert's life work. In your rollout messaging, respect for the old order is respect for its guardians: "This process has supported the company for the past decade; now we will amplify its ten years of experience a hundredfold." Only an old world treated well will not sabotage the new one.

7.7 Codifying Replication Efficiency into the Playbook

  • timewell.jp《The GenAI Divide》综述（2026-05：日本企业自建失败案例、三报告对照）
  • The opening of this chapter listed the playbook as a first-order lever; this section unpacks its engineering details—because most teams' playbooks end up as document graveyards, "dead on arrival."
  • The playbook's first attribute: it lives in the process, not in the knowledge base. A playbook that can only be found in a document system effectively doesn't exist. The effective approach is to embed it in the workflow: during due diligence on a new project, the system automatically surfaces the due diligence checklist for that customer type; during deployment, the checklist is a hard gate in the task system; during retrospectives, playbook updates are a required output. Knowledge not embedded in process is merely archaeological material.
  • The playbook's second attribute: organized by "scenario," not by "function." Failed playbooks are written along the company's org structure ("Delivery Process," "Technical Standards"); successful playbooks are written by customer scenario ("Anti-Money-Laundering Playbook for Financial Services," "Production Scheduling Playbook for Manufacturing," "Knowledge Base Playbook for Law Firms"). Each scenario playbook contains a fixed set of seven items: typical pain points and fit-check criteria, a due diligence checklist (high-risk signals specific to that scenario), known landmines in data and integration, metric templates for evaluation and acceptance, a role map for change management, reference structures for pricing and expansion, and a lessons-learned repository from past retrospectives. Harvey's ability to replicate its law-firm playbook to PwC and Cleary rests precisely on turning the experience of its first customer (Linklaters) into inheritable scenario assets.

  • The playbook's third attribute: it must be "alive"—with an owner, versions, and depreciation. Each scenario playbook has a designated owner (usually the most senior delivery team), a mandatory review and update after every project, and a full overhaul every six months—deleting outdated content and merging duplicates.   • 7.8 Planning and Polishing Productization

The final section covers the crowning achievement of the third-order lever: crystallizing field experience into product—the alchemy of the entire FDE model.

  • Start with the most spectacular success case: Palantir's Foundry. Today this platform, generating billions of dollars in annual revenue and underpinning a market cap in the hundreds of billions, had its core components born at customer sites as far-flung as Zurich, Houston, São Paulo, Toulouse, and Baku—tools built independently by Forward Deployed Engineers in each location to solve local problems, which through years of natural selection and platform consolidation eventually grew into a unified product. Barry recalls the process as "entirely bottom-up": in 2014 the company's product strategy was literally "strong opinions, loosely held"—letting a hundred flowers bloom in the field, with survivors absorbed into the core.
  • This process was no pastoral idyll; it had a rigorous internal logic, which can be distilled into "four questions of productization."
  • Question one: Is this field solution "idiosyncratic" or "universal"? The test: has the same problem independently appeared at three or more customers? The same pain point at three customers is a product signal; one customer's special request may just be a quirk. Palantir's rule of thumb is to let "multiple forward deployed teams repeatedly reinventing the wheel" naturally expose commonality—when three teams have each built something similar, the platform team knows: it's time to consolidate.
  • Question two: Is the cost of generalization smaller than the benefit? McGrue has warned that field code is "fast and rough," and the cost of generalizing it "often exceeds writing the first version." A productization decision must run the numbers: the investment in generalization versus the customization costs saved across future customers. If the math doesn't work, keep it at the component layer—don't force it onto the platform. Every capability in the platform is a lifetime maintenance commitment.
  • Question three: Can it be used by "people who can't write code"? Field tools are used by engineers; platform capabilities are used by everyone. The hardest step in productization is often not technical but interactional: turning an engineer's script into a feature a business person can configure. Decagon's agent workflow configuration (letting operations staff define workflows in natural language) is the model for this step—choose the right abstraction layer, and the user base of a capability grows a hundredfold.

Palantir's solution is to maintain "radical delegation from headquarters to the front line"—leadership sets only the goals; the tactics belong to the field.
