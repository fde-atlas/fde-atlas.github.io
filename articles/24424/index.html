What Is AI Implementation Support? | Types, Costs, and How to Choose AI Adoption Support

Many companies tout "AI accompaniment support," but what they actually offer varies widely. Some call holding a few training sessions "accompaniment," while others call attending a monthly regular meeting "accompaniment." From the client's perspective, it is hard to tell what you are actually buying.

The background is clear. According to the Ministry of Internal Affairs and Communications' 2026 Information and Communications White Paper, 86.4% of Japanese companies now use generative AI in some part of their operations. Getting AI in is no longer difficult. What is difficult is keeping it running in production; in July 2024, Gartner predicted that at least 30% of generative AI projects would be abandoned after proof of concept by the end of 2025. They get in, then stall. That is why support has shifted from "build and deliver" to "run it together."

In my view, accompaniment support means an external expert works alongside an internal team for a fixed period to advance AI adoption, and leaves behind a state where things run on their own internally once it ends. The last part is what matters: support with no defined ending is not accompaniment—it is just a long outsourcing contract.

Below, I will organize, in order, where accompaniment support sits among the types of AI adoption support, what external parties and internal teams each handle, the forms of pricing, and what to check when choosing a provider. The job role that actually goes on-site and implements AI is summarized in What is an FDE? The meaning and work of a Forward Deployed Engineer.

Five types of AI adoption support and where accompaniment support fits

AI adoption support becomes easier to navigate when divided into five types by what you are buying. This is not about which is superior; what you should choose changes depending on which stage your company is at right now.

TypeWhat you buyWhere it endsSituation it suits
Training and literacy educationEmployees' knowledgeDelivery of the trainingFew employees use AI yet
Vision and strategy consultingPlans and prioritiesDelivery of a reportNo decision yet on where to start
Contract developmentA system built to specDelivery of the systemWhat to build and the specs are clear
Tool implementation (SaaS)The product and its configurationAccount activationA product exists that fits the problem
Accompaniment supportA system that runs internally and the ability to build itHandover to the internal teamTried it but it is not running in production; want to build in-house capability

Accompaniment support spans vision, development, and adoption. It does not end with a plan alone, nor with building alone; it stays involved until the system is used and the numbers move, and then lets go. In return, the internal side must also commit time.

If you are torn between in-house development and outsourcing, I have organized the decision criteria in Should AI development be in-house or outsourced to a development company?. Accompaniment support is the middle-ground option: "building while transferring the technology."

What external parties and internal teams do in accompaniment support

I believe most accompaniment support fails because it starts with unclear division of responsibilities. If the external party does everything, it is fast, but no one can touch it afterward. If everything is left to the internal team, there was no point in hiring accompaniment in the first place. Decide a lead owner for each phase.

PhaseExternal party's role (accompaniment side)Internal team's role
Define problems and KPIsInterview the business and propose candidate metrics that AI can moveDecide which KPI to move and appoint an owner
Prepare data and permissionsIdentify required data, access rights, and security issuesDecide data owners and approvers and make the data usable
BuildBuild the first version and demonstrate how it was builtWork hands-on together and take over as lead from the next version
EvaluateBuild evaluation criteria and a testing frameworkJudge "usable / not usable" through the lens of the business
Move to operationsSet up monitoring, logs, and procedures for when problems occurRun daily operations and gather frontline feedback
Hand overLeave code, design, documentation, and the reasoning behind decisionsContinue improving on their own

Accompaniment support that starts with the right-hand column of the table empty will stop when the period ends. Conversely, if the internal roles have names attached, things keep moving even after the external party leaves. "Evaluate" in particular should remain an internal responsibility. Only people who know the business can decide what counts as usable.

For how to proceed when a prototype is done but stuck before production, I wrote phase-by-phase guidance in Breaking out of AI PoC stagnation.

Pricing structures for accompaniment support

For pricing, look at "what you are paying for" before the amount. There are three main forms.

FormWhat you pay forWhere it fits wellPoints to note
Monthly, fixed-termAn expert's involvement for a set periodTaking one business process all the way to productionWithout deciding what must be completed within the period, extensions keep piling up
Fixed-price packageDeliverables with a defined scopeWork with clear boundaries, such as diagnostics, evaluation, or planningWork outside the scope is billed separately
Performance-basedThe amount KPIs moveWhen the target numbers are clearKPI definitions and measurement methods must be agreed before signing

As our examples: FDE implementation support starts at 3 months and 1 million yen per month; AI usage security diagnostics takes 2–3 weeks at a fixed price from 500,000 yen; FDE team launch support starts at 3 months with individual quotes. With any form, check whether the contract states "what will remain inside the company at the end of the period." If it does not, it is not accompaniment—it is selling time by the slice.

Some companies estimate by accumulating person-months. Person-months mean paying for workload, so costs rise as work increases. Since the goal of accompaniment support is to transfer building capability in-house and reduce external work, it tends to run counter to person-month-based estimates—worth keeping in mind. How to choose accompaniment support: 5 checkpoints

I have narrowed down what to verify in sales meetings to five points. In each case, how the other party answers reveals what they really offer.

1. Will the person who made the proposal do hands-on work?

If the person who made the proposal and the person who actually joins your team are different, the promises made in the proposal never reach the front line. Ask: "How far into the build process will the person who wrote this proposal be involved?"

2. Is the ending defined from the start?

Good accompaniment support ends by creating a state where things run even without them. Ask: "When the contract ends, what will remain inside our company, and who will take it over?" and check whether the answer is concrete.

3. Will they leave behind an evaluation framework?

AI is not finished once built; accuracy changes as the business and data change. Whether they leave evaluation criteria and tests behind determines whether you can keep improving after the handover.

4. Do they address security and permissions from the start?

Since you're handing internal data to AI anyway, putting off decisions about who can access what will bring everything to a halt right before production. Check whether data handling and permissions come up in the very first meeting.

5. Can they talk about something that actually runs in production?

Every company has prototype success stories. Ask them: "Not a prototype, but what's the longest-running thing you have in production, and for how many months? What numbers did it achieve there?" Even if they can't name the client, they should be able to give the duration and the numbers.

The criteria for comparing companies that call themselves FDEs are covered in more detail in How to Choose an FDE Support Company.

When Accompaniment Support Isn't the Right Fit

Accompaniment support isn't a universal solution. In my view, if any of the following three apply, it's better to choose a different form of support first.

  • What to build and the specifications are already fixed. In this case, contract development is faster and cheaper.
  • There's no one in-house to hand it over to, and no plan to place one. The results of accompaniment are capabilities that remain within the company; without a receiver, nothing stays.
  • The KPIs you want to move haven't been decided yet. At this stage, it's more efficient to start with a short ideation session or training, and move to accompaniment once the KPIs are set.

Conversely, if all three of these hold—you've tried something but it isn't running in production, you don't want to keep relying on outsourcing, and you have staff who know the business—then accompaniment support is the best fit.
