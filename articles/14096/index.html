Forward deployed engineer travel: what postings say

FDE Interview Prep · 2026-09-25 · 18 min read

Summary

This article examines travel requirements in forward deployed engineer job postings from OpenAI, Palantir, Databricks, Anduril and others, showing that travel varies widely by team and city within a single employer — from one trip every 4-8 weeks (Databricks AI FDE) to 70-90% across Europe (Palantir NATO). A September 2026 count of 806 postings across 103 job boards found 512 mentioning travel and 388 stating a percentage, mostly in the 1-25% and 26-50% ranges. The author notes that wording matters (required vs. may be required), remote roles can still involve heavy travel, the highest-travel postings usually require security clearances, and candidates should convert percentages into days away per month and ask hiring managers about cadence, decision-making and notice.

Key points

  • Travel for FDEs ranges from one trip every 4-8 weeks to 70-90% of the year, varying by team and city.
  • Of 806 postings counted in September 2026, 512 mentioned travel and 388 stated a percentage.
  • Wording matters: OpenAI says travel 'is required' for some roles but 'may be required' for others.
  • The highest-travel postings, like Palantir NATO and Anduril Air Defense, require security clearances.
  • Convert percentages to days away per month and ask hiring managers about cadence and notice.

What a travel percentage means in days

No posting in the tables says whether its percentage counts working days, calendar days or nights away. Until the team tells you, assume working days, and count travel days as away. Here is the arithmetic on a month of twenty working days. It is our arithmetic, not a market figure.

Share Days away a month What it feels like
10% 2 a two-day visit
25% 5 one site week
50% 10 every other week
75% 15 home one week
90% 18 the hotel is home

A stint turns a share into a season. Anduril’s Air Defense posting asks for travel to remote regions for up to two months at a time, up to 80% of the year. Source 7Forward Deployed Engineer, Air Defense (FDE)PublisherAnduril IndustriesSource typecompany job posting Up to 80% of a 52-week year is 41.6 weeks away Source 7Forward Deployed Engineer, Air Defense (FDE)PublisherAnduril IndustriesSource typecompany job posting, which leaves about ten weeks at home. In two-month stints, that is roughly five stints a year, with home time squeezed in between them.

A common mistake is to convert the share and stop. The same days away, spent in one city an hour’s drive from home or spread across three time zones, are the same percentage and very different months.

Weeks away or day trips: how travel is spread

The same share can come in at least five shapes, and postings and published accounts hint at which one you are signing up for.

  • A rhythm. Databricks’ AI FDE postings ask for willingness to travel once every 4-8 weeks to see customers, as needed.   • A stint. Anduril’s posting sets stints of up to two months. Source 7Forward Deployed Engineer, Air Defense (FDE)PublisherAnduril IndustriesSource typecompany job posting Long stints matter more than the total if you have fixed weekly commitments at home.

  • Local. OpenAI’s Tokyo FDE posting expects travel mainly within Japan, and its Tokyo FDE manager posting says travel up to 40%, mainly local.   • Regional. Palantir’s NATO posting is based in London and travels throughout Europe. Source 11Forward Deployed Software Engineer - NATO (London)PublisherPalantir TechnologiesSource typecompany job postingSource 12Forward Deployed Software Engineer - UK Government (London)PublisherPalantir TechnologiesSource typecompany job posting Your base city and your customers’ cities are different questions.

Here is a quick scenario to test yourself. Two postings both say 40%. On the first, you drive to one customer across town every Tuesday and Wednesday: eight of twenty working days. On the second, you fly to another continent for eight working days in a row each month: also eight of twenty. The first costs you commute time; the second costs you a weekend or two, jet lag and sleep. The percentage hides that difference, so ask for the shape.

Questions to ask before you accept

Some employers raise travel before any call. Palantir’s US Government FDSE application form asks whether you support the travel percentage on the posting, and whether you support frequent business travel to Europe or the IndoPacific. Source 38Apply: Forward Deployed Software Engineer - US Government (Washington, D.C.) - Palantir on LeverPublisherPalantir Technologies (Lever)Source typecompany job posting Answer those with the same number you will give a person.

Ask the rest before the offer, while you can still ask to be considered for a team closer to your number. Three questions do most of the work:

Three questions that turn a percentage into a calendar

  • Recruiter: “If I took this role today, how many nights a month would I sleep away from home?”
  • Hiring manager: “Walk me through the last three months for the person on this team who traveled most, and the one who traveled least.”
  • Hiring manager: “When a customer needs someone on site next week, who decides it’s me, and how often has that happened this year?”

If the answers stay vague, ask to speak with an engineer on the team, and ask for the travel expectation in writing with the offer.

How to answer the travel question in a recruiter screen

You may be asked some version of “Are you comfortable with the travel?” on the application form or in the first call. The weak answer is “I’m flexible.” It can get you through the call, and a mismatch can surface in your first engagement, when fixing it is hardest.

A strong answer has three parts: your number, your limit, and one question back. Write yours before the call. Here is the shape:

“The posting says up to half-time travel. I can do up to two weeks on site a month, in stretches of up to a week, and longer around a go-live with a month’s notice. What wouldn’t work is being away most weekends for months at a stretch. How is travel spread on this team right now?”

Fill it in with your own numbers, and keep the reason short: you don’t owe anyone the details of your family or health.

Three mistakes to avoid:

  • Answering in percentages. “I’m fine with the posted percentage” means nothing to you until you convert it. Speak in weeks a month and the longest stint.
  • Giving conditions without a commitment. A list of what you can’t do, with nothing you can, gives the recruiter nothing to staff against.
  • Leaving “flexible” on the record. If you said it early, give your real number in the next round, before an offer is on the table.

The free question How much travel can you commit to? has a model answer to compare yours against. If you have worked on site before, prepare the question about working inside a customer’s environment as well, since your story is the evidence behind your number.
