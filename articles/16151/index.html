FDE Portfolio: Ways to Prove Delivery Capability Without Public Code

Original title: FDE 作品集：没有公开代码也能证明交付能力的方法

FDE中国 · 2026-09-07 · 9 min read

Summary

This article tackles a practical dilemma for forward deployed engineers: the work that best proves delivery ability—client-site problems, decisions, and outcomes—is usually covered by NDAs, so it cannot be shown publicly. The author proposes a three-layer portfolio structure: case narratives, verifiable artifacts, and distilled retrospectives, combined with a five-step anonymization framework (change industry, transform numbers, abstract the tech stack, strip identifying details, peer self-check) to make cases both compliant and credible. It also recommends using open source and side projects to fill evidence gaps, and distinguishes job-seeking versions of the portfolio from promotion versions, where the emphasis shifts from personal problem-solving to method reuse and team impact. Common mistakes like writing job descriptions instead of judgments, and an overly large case scope, are flagged with a self-check list.

Key points

  • Portfolio has three layers: case narrative, verifiable artifacts, and reusable retrospective insights, forming a closed evidence loop.
  • Five-step anonymization: change industry, scale numbers, abstract tech stack, strip details, peer check.
  • Review NDA scope before rewriting; the bottom line is no harm and no guessing about clients.
  • Open source and side projects should complement missing evidence and align with your FDE positioning.
  • Job-seeking version emphasizes how you solved problems; promotion version emphasizes method reuse and team impact.

FDE (Forward Deployed Engineer) job seekers and promotion candidates face the same awkward situation: the things that best prove your ability—the problems, solutions, costs, and results at the customer site—are precisely the parts most tightly covered by NDAs. Code can't be published, data can't be taken out, client names can't be mentioned, so many people simply submit a blank page, leaving only a paragraph of job responsibilities on their resume. This piece offers a method that remains executable under confidentiality constraints: build the portfolio with a three-layer structure of case narratives, verifiable artifacts, and retrospective insights; rewrite cases with an anonymization framework; fill in the evidence chain with open source and side projects; and use the same portfolio to the fullest in both job-seeking and promotion scenarios. Readers preparing for interviews can use this together with The Complete FDE Interview Preparation Guide.

Why an FDE can't rely on GitHub alone to prove themselves

Open source contributions and code profiles can prove engineering ability, but not delivery ability. An FDE's core value is created in the customer's environment: clarifying requirements with incomplete information, making trade-offs under hard constraints, and pushing a project past the finish line with insufficient resources. Almost none of this process can be shown with a single line of code—the decisive work might be three requirements interviews, one negotiation to shrink scope, or a weekly report that clearly explains the risks. In other words, an FDE's work is inherently "process and judgment," not a "runnable product," which means the portfolio must be organized with a structure different from an engineer's.

Another real constraint is confidentiality. In domestic toB projects, NDAs, data compliance, and industry regulation will almost inevitably cover your daily work. But that doesn't mean there's no solution—confidentiality restricts client-identifiable information and specific data, not your methods for handling problems, your decision-making frameworks, or reusable artifacts. The entire craft of the portfolio lies in rewriting "what happened" into "how this type of problem is solved"—the latter is both compliant and what interviewers and promotion reviewers actually want to see.

The portfolio's three-layer structure: case narratives, verifiable artifacts, retrospective insights

The first layer is case narratives, answering "what have you accomplished." Each case is a narrative piece written to a fixed skeleton: background and constraints, your role, key decision points, results and costs. The key decision points are the soul—what interviewers want to see is how you weighed trade-offs with incomplete information, not what the final solution looked like. Each case must include at least one decision description of the form "there were two options at the time, I chose one, and here's why"—otherwise it's just a project description. A usable narrative skeleton: two sentences on the client's background and constraints; two or three sentences on your role and goals; two to three paragraphs on the key decision points, each developed as "what I faced at the time, what the options were, what judgment I based my choice on, and whether it was right in hindsight"; and a final paragraph on results and costs, including the problems you failed to solve. You don't need many cases—three told thoroughly beat ten glossed over.

The second layer is verifiable artifacts, answering "is what you say true." Artifacts are the physical evidence of your cases and the part where anonymization demands the most skill: the structure and samples of an eval set (with synthetic data replacing real data), templates of weekly reports and issue lists plus one anonymized sample page, a skeleton version of an architecture diagram (hiding the client's business modules while keeping the structural relationships), and the table of contents with selected excerpts from a troubleshooting manual or onboarding doc you wrote. Artifacts need not be many—one or two per case is enough—but each must withstand scrutiny: for everything you include, you must be able to explain in five minutes why it looks the way it does.

The third layer is retrospective insights, answering "what reusable things have you distilled from your experience." This layer is the most easily overlooked, yet it best distinguishes senior from junior: a junior FDE's portfolio stops at cases, while a senior FDE distills the commonalities across multiple cases into methods—a process for building an eval set, a troubleshooting checklist for a class of problems, a questioning framework for requirements clarification. Retrospective insights also serve as your interview ammunition: when the interviewer asks "if you could do it over," your answer should already be written in the third layer, not improvised on the spot.

The three layers also need explicit correspondences: each case should have at least one artifact attached, each artifact should support at least one decision point, and the retrospective insights should cite at least two cases as evidence. Organized this way, the portfolio forms a closed loop—a reviewer entering from any point can follow the chain to see complete evidence, rather than three mutually isolated lists. The check for the closed loop is simple: randomly pick a conclusion and see whether you can reach the original record supporting it within three steps; if you can't, the chain is broken.

How to write cases under confidentiality constraints: an anonymization framework

Here is a five-step anonymization framework you can apply directly. Step one, change the industry without changing the structure: describe the client as "a chain retail company in a southern China region" or "the data department of a joint-stock bank"—industry-level granularity is enough to make the narrative credible without pointing to a specific entity. Step two, transform the numbers: scale all performance and size figures proportionally or rewrite them as ranges, preserving the order-of-magnitude relationships—the point of the numbers is to show your sense of scale in handling problems; exact values have no value. Step three, abstract the tech stack: replace proprietary components with generic descriptions, e.g., "in-house gateway" can become "the client-side unified access layer," keeping the structural relationships unchanged. Step four, strip identifying details: remove names, sensitive points in the timeline, and unique business rules. Step five, self-review: give the rewritten case to a peer who wasn't on the project and ask two questions—can they understand it, and can they guess who the client is. The answer to the first must be yes; the answer to the second must be no.

Two rules of discipline should be made clear upfront. First, before rewriting, review the scope of the NDA's terms, and when in doubt, ask your company's legal or compliance team rather than deciding on your own; this also applies to material from previous employers in a portfolio built before joining. Second, the bottom line of anonymization is "no harm, no guessing": don't write anything that could let an insider infer the client and affect them, and don't state facts you're not sure of. Better to write a bit more abstractly than to risk it for the sake of a good story—losing one case doesn't matter, but damaged professional credibility offers no second chance.

Using open source and side projects to supplement the evidence chain

Case narratives prove you "have accomplished things"; open source and side projects prove you "keep building." For those under heavy confidentiality constraints with few writable cases, this layer is especially important. Direction should be targeted: not building yet another practice app, but filling the gaps in your cases' evidence. If a case shows integration ability, a side project could be a set of connectors or a developer toolkit; if a case shows eval methodology, you could open source a lightweight evals scaffold; if a case shows understanding of a certain type of scenario, you could write a series of breakdown articles. The core principle is that your public work should corroborate your FDE positioning, not tell separate stories.

Quality and maintenance matter more than quantity. One project with documentation, evals, and iteration records beats five half-finished ones; a continuous commit history and version evolution are themselves public evidence of "delivery discipline." If time is limited, prioritize two things: write the project's README to the standard of "a stranger can get it running in fifteen minutes"—this is a direct demonstration of FDE delivery documentation ability; then add a design trade-offs note explaining why you did it this way and what you gave up, which follows the same approach as case narratives.

If you don't have a suitable side project direction, start from "questions you've been asked repeatedly about your own projects": compile the usage and deployment questions you've answered multiple times during delivery into a public Q&A or getting-started guide. This involves no client information, and it directly demonstrates your ability to turn tacit knowledge into shareable documentation—a skill that takes up a larger share of FDE daily work than many candidates realize. Starting with lightweight public artifacts like these and gradually evolving them into a project with a maintenance rhythm is easier to sustain than planning a big engineering effort from the start.

Two scenarios for the same portfolio: job hunting and promotion

In the job-hunting scenario, the portfolio is used for "proactive presentation rather than passive submission." Put a one-line pointer on your resume, and call on it at the right moments during interviews: when the system design round hits similar constraints, directly cite the trade-offs you made in the case; when behavioral interviews ask for a conflict story, the case is the detailed version of that story. After the interview, attach a sanitized portfolio to provide evidence for decision-makers who weren't part of the interview. A practical tip is to tailor the emphasis to the target role: for AI application roles, put your evals and performance tuning cases first; for traditional toB roles, put your integration and deployment cases first. For differences in what roles assess, see the article FDE Interview Complete Preparation.

In the promotion scenario, the portfolio's value shifts to a different coordinate system: reviewers don't look at your storytelling ability, they look at your radius of impact. For the same case, the job-hunting version emphasizes "how I solved it," while the promotion version must emphasize "who reused this methodology, how much rework it reduced, and what default practices it changed on the team." So keep a separate version for each scenario: the case narrative layer is largely shared, but the retrospective layer is written completely differently. Before a promotion defense, it's advisable to rework the third layer, rewriting the narrative from "personal experience" into "team asset."

The two scenarios also differ in material format. The job-hunting version should be concise—ideally a document or page readable in ten to fifteen minutes, with key points up front—because interviewers spend very little time on each material, and conclusions must be visible on the first screen. The promotion version can be more complete, attaching the full text of the methodology and records of reuse, since reviewers follow a formal review process where depth directly affects the rating. Both versions draw from the same source library and are assembled separately, which keeps maintenance costs manageable.

For maintenance rhythm, spend half a day each quarter on an incremental update: run the quarter's noteworthy cases through the sanitization framework, update artifacts, and add retrospectives. Day to day, rely on the simplest accumulation habit—jotting down notes as you go during delivery, which is exactly the habit the entry-level section of the FDE Career Path Map says you should develop. Trying to reconstruct details from six months earlier when you need them will almost certainly be inaccurate.

Common Pitfalls and a Self-Check List

Before wrapping up, here are a few high-frequency pitfalls. First, writing job descriptions: sentences like "responsible for client requirement coordination and system delivery" carry no information; every sentence in a portfolio should carry a judgment or a result. Second, cutting cases too broadly: using one narrative to cover a year-long project is doomed to be shallow; cutting to the three months with the densest decision points is more powerful than covering the whole year. Third, showing only successes: a serious rework paired with a quality retrospective often builds more trust than a smooth case, because reviewers all know things never go perfectly in the field. Fourth, letting artifacts overshadow the story: dozens of pages of document screenshots are less effective than a one-page excerpt that clearly explains the trade-offs.

  • Does every case have at least one dilemma-style decision point, with the trade-off rationale clearly written?
  • Have you self-checked against the five-step sanitization framework—could an outsider guess the client?
  • Can every artifact withstand five minutes of follow-up questions?
  • Does the retrospective layer include at least one method that has been reused by others?
  • Are the job-hunting and promotion versions maintained separately, with different narrative emphases?
  • Does your side project reinforce your FDE positioning rather than exist in isolation?

If you can't answer three of the six questions, your portfolio isn't ready to use yet—fill the gaps before thinking about presentation.

Summary and Next Steps

The methods in this article can be distilled into one sentence: the goal of a portfolio is not to show off code, but to convince strangers that you can drive a client's hard problem to a result under constraints. The three-layer structure solves "what to write," the sanitization framework solves "whether you can write it," open source and side projects solve "what to do when evidence is insufficient," and the dual job-hunting/promotion versions solve "who to show it to."

Next steps: first pick the project you remember most clearly and write your first case using the three-layer structure and the sanitization framework—done beats perfect; then plan a side project or open source contribution that complements your positioning; job seekers should embed the portfolio into their interview preparation process, see FDE Interview Complete Preparation; those employed should put quarterly maintenance on their calendar; for a more systematic discussion of capabilities and paths, see the FDE Competency Model and Four Paths for Engineers Transitioning to FDE, or start from the FDE Knowledge Guide to navigate the site.
