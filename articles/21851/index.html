Putting Work into Words Before Bringing in AI | How Visualization, Continuing from Legacy System Modernization, Becomes the Foundation for AI-Driven Development | Career Support Media for FDEs, Engineers, and IT Freelancers

Summary

This interview features two Japanese engineers from a major financial group's IT company. B spent about three years rebuilding a legacy system running for roughly 30 years, then moved to an AI-focused firm. There he spent about a year documenting business knowledge and procedures as onboarding materials, then discovered these documents could also drive AI: routine tasks were handed to AI, letting the team handle deferred low-priority work without hiring. The article argues AI-driven development requires first verbalizing business processes, connecting data, and building rule-based guardrails for consistent quality, with AI auto-updating documentation to keep it alive.

Key points

  • B rebuilt a 30-year-old legacy system over about three years before moving to an AI-driven company.
  • He spent roughly a year verbalizing business knowledge and system procedures into onboarding documents.
  • Human-readable documentation turned out to work well as instructions for AI agents.
  • Result: routine and deferred tasks now run via AI without adding headcount.
  • He stresses rules and harnesses for stable quality, plus AI-based auto-updating of docs.

Mr. B and Mr. C, working at a system company within a major financial group, spent three years rebuilding a legacy system that had been running for about 30 years. Afterward, Mr. B moved to a company that actively embraces AI, and at a data platform product during its startup phase, he spent a year building something. It wasn't AI—it was "documentation that put business operations into words."

When people read this documentation, new hires got up to speed quickly; when AI read it, routine tasks could be automated. In this article, we asked the two of them about the "work that comes before introducing AI," which also relates to how FDEs (roles that embed themselves in the customer's workplace, understand the business, and solve problems) operate.

What Is AI-Driven Development?

AI-driven development is a development style in which AI handles much of the code and work, while humans discuss with AI and take responsibility for design and quality decisions. You often hear "things get faster if you leave it to AI," but AI won't function unless the on-site business processes are in a form that AI can read.

Key Points of This Article

What Mr. B practiced was not introducing AI, but verbalizing the business operations of the workplace he joined as a newcomer. The story is about how that accumulation ultimately became the "road" on which AI could run.

For about 12 years since graduating, he worked on developing and operating core systems at a system company within a major financial group. He was in charge of migrating and rebuilding a legacy system that had been running for about 30 years, taking roughly 3 years from planning to release. He then moved to a company that actively embraces AI, where he experimented with AI adoption on a large-scale data platform product. He is now introducing AI-driven development from scratch on a different team.

For about 16 years since graduating, he has worked as a programmer, SE, and PM at a system company in the same group. He worked with Mr. B on the legacy modernization project. He currently serves as a PM at a company handling systems for financial institutions, and is also involved in consulting on AI-driven development for financial institutions.

The Two Who Met on the Legacy Modernization Front Lines

First, could you tell us about your careers so far?

Mr. C: I joined straight out of school at a system company within a major financial group. It was a company responsible for the group's systems as a whole, covering everything from planning to development, operation, and maintenance. I worked there for about 16 years, as a programmer, then an SE, and finally as a PM.

My background is in finance, particularly system development in the insurance domain.

What kind of systems were you in charge of?

Mr. C: A system for managing sales performance. For products like insurance, where there are many sellers and organizations involved, a mechanism to consolidate and manage the performance of sales offices scattered across the country is indispensable.

Unlike online products where customers apply directly, what makes this domain interesting is the large number of people involved in selling.

Could you tell us about the project you two worked on together?

Mr. B: What I worked on with Mr. C was rebuilding that system. It was a system that had been built up over about 30 years, running on an old language.

It had been patched repeatedly and had become what you'd call spaghetti code. Making changes was difficult, and it was hard to use the data freely.

Why did you decide to rebuild it?

Mr. C: At the time, I was seconded to the department that used the system, so I experienced firsthand how hard it was to use. I also shared the struggles of the maintenance side together with Mr. B.

The users' desire to "make it more modern and easier to use" and the builders' concern that "things can't go on like this" came together. It also happened to be a time when the company wanted to take action on its legacy systems.

We spent about 3 years, from planning to release, on two goals: migrating to the cloud and rebuilding the system in a maintainable form.

From the editors What these two have in common is that they experienced both the user side and the builder side, and understood "on-the-ground pain points" in both business and technical terms. This overlaps with the perspective required of FDEs (roles that embed themselves in the customer's workplace and solve problems).

Business Knowledge That Exists Only in the Heads of Skilled People

After that, you changed jobs. What kind of workplace did you join?

Mr. B: After finishing the legacy modernization, I felt I had reached a milestone in my career. It was around the time generative AI was spreading to the general public, and I felt that AI was definitely the future.

I decided that wherever I went next, it would be the company pushing AI adoption the hardest, and so I changed jobs. My first assignment was a platform product handling large-scale data.

What was the state of the workplace when you joined?

Mr. B: The product was 1–2 years old and in a phase of trying to stabilize. The team consisted of 1–2 full-time employees and the rest were contractors, with a maximum of about 6–7 people.

As for the operations, part of the reason things ran smoothly was that veterans could simply "get it done."

Why wasn't the work the veterans were running documented?

Mr. B: I think the team recognized it as an issue. But these things tend to be seen as a hassle and never get done.

The person who built it has the whole picture in their head, so they don't feel the need to write procedure manuals. The people who worked desperately to build the product during its startup phase are especially prone to this. That was the phase when I joined as a newcomer.

What was another challenge?

Mr. B: Technically, the architecture was complex. It combined multiple clouds and data platforms, mixing parts I had experience with and parts I knew nothing about.

I also had zero background knowledge of the business domain. There was simply a huge amount to learn.

Leaving What You Learn in a Form the Next Person Can Read

In that situation, what did you start with?

Mr. B: I decided to record the steps I took to catch up, just as they were. The first step was understanding the business domain—that is, the business itself. I figured this would be the first hurdle for anyone.

Next, I organized the overall system architecture by category—what was running where—and wrote it out as architecture diagrams.

StageWhat Was DoneWhat Was Documented
① Learn the businessOrganize background knowledge of the business domainText explaining the overall picture of the business
② Learn the wholeOrganize what runs where, divided by categorySystem architecture diagram
③ Record every changeKeep a record of what was done with version upgrades and new feature additionsProcedures and points to note for each task
④ Stack them upContinue for six months to a year, arranging them so that reading from the top brings you to a certain levelA complete set of onboarding materials

How long did you keep up the recording?

Mr. B: Version upgrades and new feature additions happen every time. Each time, I recorded what I had done.

If you continue for six months to a year, the knowledge needed to understand that product becomes reasonably well organized. It reaches a state where, if you read from the top in order, you can at least reach my level.

Has Mr. B always worked this way?

Mr. C: Yes, ever since his second year at the company, he has always been like that. He treats things he has done once as things "next year's self won't do."

Leave the procedures for next year's person. Make it so that next year's self doesn't have to do it again and can just say "look here." I think that's a very systems-engineer-like personality—not wanting to do the same thing twice.

He used the same approach when training junior staff. After hearing about it, I started imitating it myself.

Was there a trigger that made you start thinking this way?

Mr. B: In my first year, when I asked a senior colleague a question, I was told "look it up yourself." I thought, "You could at least leave this stuff documented."

So I simply decided that I wouldn't do the same thing.

From the editorial team: The approach of a newcomer to a site turning their own understanding directly into materials for the next person is almost identical to what an FDE does first at a customer's site. The very process of understanding the business becomes the deliverable.

Procedures that are easy for people to understand are also easy for AI

Why did those records lead to AI-driven development?

Mr. B: At some point, I noticed something. When I had AI read the procedures I had organized, the AI worked well. Onboarding materials that are easy for people to understand were also easy for AI to understand.

So I thought, instead of hiring new people, maybe we should just have AI do it. The product involved many routine tasks, and the procedures I had organized could be executed as-is as routine work.

From the first step to the final release, I tested one by one whether AI could do it.

PeriodWhat was done
First six months or soUnderstanding the business and product while putting the work into words
Next six months or soHaving AI read the written documentation and testing whether tasks previously done by people could be delegated to AI
About one year in totalSome tasks reached a state where AI could execute them

Did you plan the AI adoption from the start?

Mr. B: No, not at all. I believed I first had to learn the business of that product myself, so I spent six months on that. As a result, the written documentation came together.

When I looked at it, I thought it would be useful for the next person who joined as well. Furthermore, since AI utilization was also being called for, I realized, "If this works, maybe AI can do it too."

Rather than planning it, I looked at what had come together and then thought about the next step—that was the order of things.

Has everything been replaced by AI?

Mr. B: Not everything has been done. However, some of the tasks that people used to do have taken a form where AI can do them.

I actually think that in almost any product, there are tasks that veterans just knock out quickly but that are still lying around. If you put them into words, they can be delegated to AI.

Is putting business into words a common foundation for AI utilization?

Mr. C: I think so. It's strange that people look at documents but AI isn't shown them, right?

First, it's important to connect information and create an environment where AI can act easily.

A structure that delegates routine tasks to AI without increasing headcount

What results did you achieve with AI adoption?

Mr. B: The initial result with the first product was creating a state where "even without hiring new worker-type people, things can be done by delegating to AI."

While developing with priorities, tasks that had low priority and were put off could now be done by having AI work on them in parallel. We became able to make the business judgment that work can get done without adding even one person.

ItemBefore adoptionAfter adoption
Business knowledgeIn veterans' headsOrganized as documents anyone can read
Routine tasksDone manually by peopleProcedures fed to AI for execution
Postponed tasksLow priority, couldn't be startedCan be advanced by delegating to AI in parallel
Adding headcountIncrease in line with workloadThe range that can be handled without adding people expands

How did the newcomers react?

Mr. B: From the engineers who joined, I hear comments like they were surprised at how quickly they could actually add features.

On the other hand, veteran engineers are rarely assigned routine tasks in the first place, so they don't have many occasions to use this system. However, from the veterans, there may have been the effect that organizing the business reduced the effort of answering questions. That's because they can now say, "It's all summarized here."

I think this is less about introducing AI and more about the effect coming from the organizing that preceded it.

New engineers quickly becoming productive is a big value, isn't it?

Mr. C: I think so. A fast ramp-up is a tremendous thing.

Variation in quality and documents left untouched

When using AI, doesn't the quality of output vary from person to person?

Mr. B: Honestly, I think variation in the final output depending on the person is a risk for a company. That's why we need to find the right boundary between the parts that run on rules and the parts that run on AI inference. Personally, I lean quite heavily toward the rules side.

In recent terms, this is a "harness"—a mechanism that solidifies the surroundings so AI doesn't go off track. It's also a part where engineering knowledge comes into play.

Whoever presses the switch to run the AI, a certain level of quality or higher comes out. I believed that building that mechanism is a responsibility of the team as a whole. Rather than thinking "this person can't use AI, so they're no good," we have a system where anyone who uses it gets consistent quality.

Do the procedure documents you worked hard to create ever end up neglected?

Mr. B: I think this happens in many organizations. Once every few years, someone motivated creates documentation, but before you know it, it's out of date.

Since it hasn't taken root as a culture, even if an oddball comes in and tidies things up, there's no one to carry it on.

How are you trying to solve that?

Mr. B: We're now working on automating updates with AI. Once you put in the effort to create it, AI keeps it updated afterward. I think the key is to create a setup where people don't have to work hard at it.

Why isn't this done in many organizations?

Mr. B: I think it's a combination of people not noticing the need, and few people wanting to do it.

When I joined the current team, the leader had lots of things he wanted to achieve with AI in his head. But there was no one to work through the tasks to execute them. I was the one person who stepped in and pushed it all forward at once. I think it's often just like that.

In terms of evaluation, it's unglamorous work, isn't it?

Mr. C: Yes. It's actually very valuable, but it's not something that shows up as immediate, visible results.

With AI, individuals can create tools endlessly. But then it ends with creation, and no one knows where the tool is afterward—it gets lost within the company. The person who created it gets recognized, but the subsequent operation and maintenance are hard to get credit for.

From the editors: "A system that delivers consistent quality no matter who uses it" and "a system that recognizes maintenance work" are the two things the client side should decide on first when delegating to people coming in from outside (FDEs and contract engineers).

Paving the "road" for AI to run on, first

Do you have a message for companies taking on AI-driven development?

Mr. C: I think many people initially expect that "AI-driven development will let us develop in half or a third of the time." But the first thing needed for that is connecting your data. It's the most basic of basics, but once that's connected, everything afterward becomes much easier.

There's an analogy I heard within the company that I found very clear. Even if you bring an F1 car into a traditional shopping street downtown, it can't run. If you just drop AI into paths that people have been using, it won't work well. The point is that paving the road for AI to run on is the key to success with AI.

What changes when you connect information?

Mr. C: When generative AI like ChatGPT and Gemini first appeared, AI only worked standalone and information wasn't connected, so I found it hard to use.

Later, things became connected via APIs, MCP, and so on, making it possible to have AI summarize documents on Drive, connect with calendars and communication tools, and so on. Once things were connected, it felt like everyone started using it all at once.

First connect the information and set things up so it's ready to use, and I think the smart people in the company will start using it, and it will spread from there.

Is there anything you notice when talking with non-engineer executives?

Mr. B: I see it as a matter of taking inventory of business operations. Unless you show AI the documents that people are looking at, there's no way it can do the work. This may be surprisingly hard for business-side people to grasp.

That's why, if there's someone who can explain things clearly from an engineering perspective, people understand why it needs to be done, and it's more likely to take hold.

How do you think about it from a business perspective?

Mr. B: I don't think it makes sense to pour engineers' efforts into routine tasks. Effort should go into more creative, non-routine work—the parts where you think "this looks promising as a business, so let's try it once." It's a waste when people are tied up with routine responses and can't do that.

So the mindset is: make routine work reach a state where everything finishes at the push of a button. Beyond that lies a state where people can spend their time on work that leads to business value.

What do you think is important in AI-driven development?

Mr. B: Rather than using AI itself, I think what matters is being able to explain, on your own, "why you chose that approach." AI presents many options, but ultimately, humans must decide which approach to adopt.

To do that, you need to organize what the problem in front of you is, what you want to solve, and what constraints exist, and then choose from AI's proposals. It's not about ending with "I asked AI and it worked"—I think what's important in AI-driven development is thinking through the judgment that comes before that step as well.

What's your view on that point?

Mr. C: AI gives you lots of answers, so I think what matters is using AI until you can explain those answers in your own words.

What's sought after are people who can tell a story—not just the fact that they built a feature, but what problem it solved and what value it created. That's the part that doesn't come from just using AI.

Before "introducing AI," there's "putting your operations into words"

I felt the importance of the work of picking up balls that tend to get dropped—things that don't lead to short-term results in the development flow—while articulating them, being considerate of people who join later, and building a state where things can be AI-ified.

In particular, my personal impression is that for companies with structures that outsource via SES, and the developers participating in them, work can only be done with a short-term perspective, so documentation gets missed, and there are workplaces where documentation isn't valued (it seems to me there are problems on both the client and contractor sides).

Hearing this talk, I felt that it's important for both the client company (development leads) and the developers (whether employees or contractors) to share a sense of ownership in carrying out more AI-driven development.
