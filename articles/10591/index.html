This new profession whose job duties are "incomprehensible at first glance" — how long can it stay "hot"?

Original title: 这个“一眼看不懂工作内容”的新职业，能“火”多久

中国青年报 · 2026-09-02 · 7 min read

Summary

A China Youth Daily feature on Forward Deployed Engineers (FDEs), who embed with clients to identify pain points and build AI solutions, closing the last mile of AI adoption. Citing practitioners and a new research report by Lu Xinglong of FDE Frontier, the article explains why the role is surging, the mix of technical, industry and communication skills it demands, and the core challenge of winning client trust. It also asks whether FDEs are a transitional job, like prompt engineers, and concludes that AI-implementation skills will remain in demand even if the title evolves.

Key points

  • Maimai FDE job postings rose 21x year-on-year in H1 2026; 37.3% from AI-native firms.
  • Wuhan Optics Valley plans to train 1,000 FDEs and deploy 1,000 agent products over three years.
  • FDEs need technical, industry know-how and communication skills, acting as cross-domain translators.
  • Winning client trust is the hardest part; small teams often start with free prototypes and ROI estimates.
  • Practitioners expect the title may change, but AI-implementation skills will stay valuable.

Full text

Building an AI tool for a clothing company to generate e-commerce images, using AI to help lawyers organize documents and analyze cases, teaching doctors to read CT scans with AI assistance... These seemingly unrelated tasks share one thing in common: a single professional group — the Forward Deployed Engineer (hereinafter "FDE").

This is not a job whose duties are obvious at a glance, but it has been all over the AI world for months. Wei Lei, founder of the OpenFDE community, summarizes the FDE's role as "bridging the last mile of enterprise AI adoption." He has felt FDE's popularity surge since May this year. Not long ago, he organized a 200-person "Forward Deployed Engineer Conference" in Hangzhou.

Multiple data sources at home and abroad show that FDE positions are entering an explosive growth phase, at one point becoming the most-watched AI job direction. In the first half of 2026, the number of FDE-related job postings on the professional social platform Maimai surged 21-fold year over year, and 37.3% of companies posting FDE jobs were AI-native enterprises. Meanwhile, some local governments have also foreseen FDE's prospects. Wuhan Optics Valley plans to cultivate 1,000 FDE talents over the next three years and drive 1,000 AI agent innovation products into enterprise scenarios.

"The FDE boom hits on a real problem: there is still huge room for growth in the speed of AI adoption," Lu Xinglong told reporters. Since 2023, the intelligence level of large models has been cutting-edge enough, but society has yet to find a truly suitable way to genuinely convert this new round of AI-driven technological change into real productivity that empowers all industries. As an FDE and founder of the research community FDE Frontier, Lu Xinglong released the "Global FDE Development Research Report" at the recent HICOOL 2026 Global Entrepreneurs Summit.

His research team summarized in the report: "Entering the second half of 2026, the focus of AI competition is shifting from capability demonstration to industrial value. What enterprises care about is no longer just whether they can obtain AI, but how to bring general-purpose models into real business operations — connecting industry knowledge, enterprise data, existing systems, and organizational accountability to produce stable, acceptable, and continuously improvable production outcomes."

Lu Xinglong believes the FDE is precisely the key role in bringing AI to every industry. Business staff may not realize what AI can do, while technical staff lack industry experience and don't know how to use AI to solve specific problems. The FDE role exists precisely to bridge this "mismatch."

The Enterprise Doctor, Cross-Border Translator, and All-Rounder

FDE is not a brand-new concept invented out of thin air. The model of "sending people to work on-site at clients" has been explored in the business world for over 20 years. But in the traditional software industry, the dominant business model is selling standardized SaaS (Software as a Service) products, and the FDE model has long been seen as an expensive, non-mainstream choice.

Song Minpeng, who mainly does business in Guangdong, told reporters that in the AI era, many traditional technical roles are gradually disappearing while many new ones are emerging. Although these new roles inherit some functions, they are more "diversified." Therefore, once AI showed potential for use in production, and for practical reasons of more precise hiring and differentiation from other roles, the industry felt "a term was needed to define this role," and the FDE was pushed back into the spotlight.

What exactly is this role? Song Minpeng sees himself as an "enterprise doctor": he first sorts out and identifies the company's problems, finds spaces where AI can optimize, then abstracts the business logic from the concrete, distills it into an executable AI pipeline, and then concretizes it back into each department and each step for execution.

"We usually ask clients: which step in the whole process is the most tedious, yet you have to do it every day? Those pain points are where AI can most easily replace and optimize." Song Minpeng recalls that while serving a real estate company, he even went out with sales agents on "site visits" (i.e., on-site inspections of property developments) and found they had to manually record store information, phone numbers, and coordinates. After returning, he developed a tool that could automatically recognize and enter all the information just by taking photos. He says only by going deep into the front line and following the process end to end can you discover which business details need optimization.

FDE is a truly "diversified" role. Li Jinjin, director of the Artificial Intelligence and Microstructure Laboratory at the School of Integrated Circuits, Shanghai Jiao Tong University, once called FDEs "cross-border translators" during a training session — they need to understand the underlying logic of AI and big data, deeply grasp the core pain points of an industry, and also possess "technology transfer" capabilities.

"Actually, an FDE is an all-around hexagon warrior," said Chen Han, an FDE working in Jiangsu. He has many years of R&D and product experience, but in the past year of exploring the FDE role, he found that "market-side" skills like communication are his natural weakness. "Even stronger FDEs also have the ability to close deals."

The Real Difficulty Is Winning Over Clients

On the afternoon of August 27, at an FDE meetup in Beijing hosted by the OpenFDE community, Wei Lei talked about the challenges facing the FDE model: "For many small teams or OPCs (one-person companies), it's hard to earn the trust of large enterprises or large hospitals, and thus hard to break into big projects." His community has more than 8,000 members, and he hopes it can act as a connector linking FDEs with enterprise needs and open up new cooperation models.

In the eyes of these "cross-border translators," the hardest language to master is not technology, but "winning over clients." Wei Lei says that in the process of transforming around AI capabilities, many enterprises have not yet realized what kind of role they need to help them.

Chen Han's clients are concentrated in manufacturing. He senses these companies' own anxiety: they often haven't figured out exactly what to do, but all hope to use AI to improve efficiency and product quality, build competitiveness, and escape the rat race and homogenization.

Lu Xinglong believes that enterprises' confusion is precisely where the FDE's value lies: "FDEs must go deep into the front line and proactively uncover needs that clients themselves haven't even recognized, rather than passively receiving them." For FDEs in independent teams, they need even more to master ways to quickly prove themselves — typically by entering a company for free, quickly finding pain points, and launching one or two software prototypes, using actual results to persuade the client.

Chen Han's company once won a bid for a project worth several million yuan with "zero industry cases" behind it — a very difficult process. Proposals from FDEs are sometimes seen as "laymen directing experts." Chen Han believes communication should "play to your strengths and avoid your weaknesses": don't discuss the client's business domain, but present the solution to show exactly where a problem lies and how they plan to solve it, and win the client's trust through a relatively low overall cost and a clear ROI (return on investment) calculation.

Helping AI Become an Industry Expert

When talking with FDEs, "Know-how" is a word that constantly comes up. In the AI world, it refers to the technical knowledge, experience, and skills accumulated through practice in a specific field — and crucially, it is usually not publicly available. If the FDE is the role that helps AI transform from a "jack-of-all-trades" into an industry expert, then Know-how is their most important "weapon."

Chen Han believes the development of general-purpose large AI models has entered a plateau, where growth in model parameters can hardly bring qualitative improvements anymore. To make AI more useful in the future, the only way is to obtain Know-how from business domains. He has noticed that large enterprises in traditional industries are also recruiting FDE talent, but with some special requirements beyond AI capabilities — for example, an automaker wants FDEs with vehicle manufacturing experience and automotive industry Know-how, and an energy company wants candidates with 5–10 years of experience in the energy industry. "This shows that future FDEs will differentiate into experts in different fields, like consultants," Chen Han says. But he believes not all enterprises can afford the cost of building their own teams. The value of third-party FDE teams lies in consolidating an industry's Know-how and reusing it across multiple clients in the industry, achieving a higher return on investment.

At the aforementioned FDE meetup, the reporter found that one reason many FDEs struggle to advance their work is that, out of data security concerns, enterprises open up only limited access to FDEs.

Wei Lei mentioned that this distrust is mutual. One future direction for FDEs is for FDE teams to form joint ventures with enterprise demand-side and industry-side partners, becoming a long-term community of shared interests, jointly accountable for business outcomes, in exchange for long-term trust and mutual benefit.

Is FDE a "Transitional" Role?

Once a "mismatch" emerges between AI capabilities and business needs, patch-up roles spring up. In 2023, when large AI models were still rather "clumsy," prompt engineers saw a surge in demand. They were responsible for translating human needs into language that AI could more easily understand, guiding the models to produce high-quality output. However, as AI became increasingly "smart," prompt writing gradually became a common workplace skill, and demand for this role has clearly declined over the past two years.

Will the FDE be the next "transitional" role? One view among practitioners is this: once AI solutions in most industries become standardized and companies return to the traditional model of purchasing mature solutions, demand for FDEs who specialize in exploratory work will indeed decline.

Song Minpeng used to do digital transformation for enterprises, and he felt he transitioned naturally into the "digital-intelligent" track. He believes the name of this role may change in the future, but the ability to understand business logic, abstract requirements, and implement AI in concrete scenarios will be needed for the long term. Therefore, he considers it a role that is not particularly friendly to newcomers, and better suited to product managers or frontline business staff with business experience who are switching careers.

Chen Han has noticed that quite a few FDE training courses have already appeared in the industry. He wants to remind fresh graduates: it is virtually impossible to independently take on enterprise-level contracts relying on training alone. That said, he also notes that young people have a unique advantage: they grew up in the AI era, are naturally familiar with AI, believe AI can solve problems, and are not easily constrained by old knowledge frameworks, so new ideas emerge from them more readily.

Lu Xinglong believes that in the future, industry experts transitioning to FDE will have greater advantages and broader prospects compared with technology experts making the switch. "The essence of the AI era is iteration. FDEs interact with clients every day and talk with people about AI transformation, which allows them to quickly build up their own understanding. In the AI era, there is no distinction between newcomers and veterans—only a difference in how fast you iterate. The work FDEs do requires constant iteration, and FDEs need to keep iterating themselves," he said.
