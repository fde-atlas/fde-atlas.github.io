Summary

This article by Japan's DeFactory explains MVP (Minimum Viable Product) development: building a product with only essential features to release quickly and gather early user feedback. It gives three purposes—easier hypothesis validation, faster grasp of user needs, and avoiding wasted costs—and three reasons MVP suits new business launches, including lower initial investment and faster PDCA cycles. It contrasts waterfall and agile development, arguing MVP works best combined with agile practices. Success tips center on direct user interviews, open-ended questions, and uncovering latent user insights. A closing dialogue cites BizDev Tech marketplace data: PdM roles average ¥960,000 and PM roles ¥850,000, above backend development's ¥710,000, with rates rising alongside decision-making discretion.

Key points

  • MVP development releases minimal-feature products early to validate hypotheses quickly.
  • Three benefits: hypothesis testing, faster user-need discovery, and cost reduction.
  • MVP pairs well with agile development for rapid PDCA cycles.
  • Success depends on direct user interviews using open-ended questions.
  • BizDev Tech data shows PdM/PM rates exceed backend rates, tied to decision discretion.

If you are involved in product development or new business development, you may have heard the term "MVP development."

In today's era of rapidly changing business environments, there is a demand for on-the-ground capabilities that can respond quickly to user needs.

MVP development is a development approach well suited to such times and is used in many new business development scenarios.

In this article, we will explain what MVP development is, three reasons it is suited to new businesses, and key points for making MVP development successful.

MVP stands for "Minimum Viable Product," meaning "a product equipped with only the minimum necessary features."

It is used when providing users with a product with minimal features and repeatedly improving and adding functionality.

It is similar to a prototype, but a prototype is a product created to demonstrate or uncover issues before mass production, and it does not necessarily include only the minimum necessary features.

MVP development is a development approach in which, based on the hypothesis "Is this kind of service what users want?" for the target users, implementation is kept to the bare minimum and released quickly, allowing feedback from users to be obtained at an early stage.

By verifying whether the hypothesis is correct early on and reflecting it in development, you can reduce risk and lower the probability of failure—in other words, increase the likelihood of success.

So what exactly is the purpose of MVP development? Here, we introduce three purposes.

1. To make hypothesis verification easier

It is often said that "90% of new businesses fail."

In today's era of rapid market changes, there is an aspect where you cannot know what the optimal solution is until you release it to the market.

Therefore, in new business development, a series of quick actions is required: formulating a hypothesis, releasing it to the market in small units quickly, obtaining user reactions, and verifying and improving.

2. To quickly grasp user needs

Addressing issues after the product is complete places a heavy burden on schedules, costs, and more.

Based on the hypothesis, you first verify user reactions with a small unit equipped with only the minimum necessary features. By repeatedly verifying and developing based on user feedback, you can reduce the probability of business failure and lead it to success.

3. Reducing unnecessary costs

Addressing issues after the product is complete imposes a significant burden in terms of schedule, cost, and other factors.

Even if you invest a large development budget to build a high-performance service, if it is not what users want, not only will sales not grow, but the ongoing maintenance costs alone may affect management.

With MVP development, you can obtain user feedback before the product is complete, making issues clear. Even if it fails, you can respond immediately with improvements, enabling development with reduced wasted costs.

If you happen to have a nearly completed product and know it will definitely succeed, MVP development is unnecessary. You can simply build the finished product from the start.

However, the reality is that you cannot know what kind of product is best, or whether new business development will succeed, until you release it to the market.

Until now, the mainstream product development process was "waterfall development," which proceeds step by step in order, starting from the stage of clarifying the features to be implemented.

Waterfall development is characterized by thorough requirements definition and design done in advance.

This method was valued because it proceeds according to plan, wasting no resources, and allows team members to know at which stage of the development process they are.

"It takes a long time to complete"

"It is difficult to make changes midway"

were its disadvantages.

That is where "agile development" emerged. Agile development is a development approach that shortens the overall system development time by performing implementation and testing in small units of work.

Unlike waterfall development, it repeats trial and error in small development units while approaching a 100% complete state, so it has come to be used by many companies as a method that enables speedy development.

MVP development refers to the prototype in agile development. MVPs are developed as tools to observe customer reactions, aiming for rapid productization.

There are various reasons why MVP development is suited to new business development, which requires speed, but here we explain the three main reasons.

As mentioned above, traditional product development was mainly carried out as a consistent flow until the product was completed.

As a result, user feedback could not be obtained until the product was finished and released, which risked creating products with no real demand.

However, with MVP development, using a product with only the minimum necessary features shortens development time, allows immediate user feedback, and makes it possible to consider improvements and the addition of new features. There is no worry of straying far from user needs.

When launching a new business, things are often exploratory, so you want to keep costs down and reduce the number of staff assigned to the project.

Trying to build a service with all anticipated features from the start requires enormous development effort and cost.

However, MVP development can start with a service that has only minimal features, so it is possible to keep initial investments such as costs and personnel low.

MVP development is a form of development that improves the service with minimal features, so it is highly agile. This offers the advantage of being able to run the PDCA cycle quickly and many times over.

In development that aims for a finished product from the start, the scale is large, so the PDCA cycle inevitably slows down. The key to a product demanded by the market is how quickly you can grasp user needs and make improvements.

With DeFactory, this entire process can be completed in "about 14 business days" (varies depending on implementation period and development requirements), enabling even faster PDCA.

MVP development is well suited to new business development, but to make it a success, you need to keep certain points in mind.

One reason new business development fails is "not correctly understanding user needs." It is important to grasp what problems and issues users are facing.

To do this, conduct user interviews and "directly listen" to the voices of target users. Even a 5- or 10-minute direct interview with target users will increase accuracy.

Target users may also become your initial users, so the secret to success is finding target users you can consistently interview.

User insight refers to users' deep psychology—the hidden, unspoken truths they are not usually aware of.

As Steve Jobs famously said, "Customers don't know what they want," so it is not easy to grasp the true feelings that even users themselves are unaware of.

There are various methods for exploring user issues, such as "web analytics," "Q&A sites," and "surveys."

However, as mentioned above, the most accurate approach is to "directly listen" to users' voices.

To discover fundamental desires and unseen user "needs," it is important to seek information from "user actions and the results of those actions" through interviews.

One tip for interviews that draw out users' true feelings is to use "open questions." Avoid presenting solutions (answers) during interviews (closed questions).

"Users will probably act in a certain way through a certain channel to solve a certain issue." Based on such a hypothesis, the key during interviews is to verify its consistency and increase the accuracy and resolution of the issue.

As mentioned above, agile development is one method of developing in small cycles at the feature level.

Development proceeds in the order of "plan → design → implement → test" starting from the highest-priority requirements, making it an agile development method that is resilient to specification changes in projects.

On the other hand, MVP development refers to products and services with the minimum necessary features implemented, as well as that approach. It allows you to build products and services that meet customer needs while reducing unnecessary time and wasted costs.

Most cases of failure in new business development are caused by not properly running the cycle of "hypothesis → validation (MVP development) → effect measurement and evaluation."

MVP development, which involves minimal implementation, and agile development, which uses small cycles at the feature level, are highly compatible, and combining these two methods is a key to success in new business development.

MVP development is not suited to "products with high development costs" or "development requiring complex features."

MVP development starts in small, minimal units and repeats the "build → measure → learn" cycle many times, so services or products with high per-cycle costs require a certain level of corporate resources.

Also, if it turns out during the repeated improvement cycles that the initial direction was wrong from the start, the original development policy must be changed, which can end up costing more time and money than expected.

Even if you understand MVP development theoretically up to this point, "what it really means for you personally" is another matter. Let's get a feel for it through a conversation between Tanaka, who has spent 10 years dedicated to backend development, and his senior colleague Tanabe, who has built a career as a freelance PdM.

Honestly, MVP development sounds like an excuse for sloppy work—like "just throw something together and ship it," a way to justify cutting corners.

It's the opposite. Looking at the project data of BizDev Tech, which our company runs, the average rate for PdM (product management) category projects is 960,000 yen, with a maximum of 1.45 million yen. That's higher than the average of 710,000 yen for backend development. No company would pay that much for sloppy work.

Now that you mention it... But I've heard that even with the same "product planning" skill tag, rates vary widely depending on the project. What makes the difference?

That's exactly the point. The average rate for product planning projects is 700,000 yen, but the top is 1.7 million. That's a 1 million yen gap. What separates them, as the article says, is whether you can "listen to users directly and validate hypotheses." Someone who just does interviews and feels satisfied, versus someone who rebuilds hypotheses from there and turns them into the next MVP—the discretion and rates they're entrusted with are completely different.

I've been a backend engineer for 10 years, but honestly I'm not comfortable with terms like "hypothesis validation" and "user insights." Is it hard to break into this area with technical skills alone?

It's tough. Implementation ability is just a prerequisite. What gets evaluated is what comes after that.

So what should I actually do to land projects without being filtered out on paper as "no experience"?

If you think you'll be judged on your resume alone, you're not prepared. According to BizDev Tech data, there are 401 "PM (project management)" projects, averaging 700,000 yen and topping out at 1.7 million. If you have even a little PM experience, start there, and deliberately go for work where you "question the requirements themselves" rather than "translate requirements into specs."

PM and PdM are just different names, but looking purely at rates, which one is more lucrative to target?

PM (project management) projects average 850,000 yen with a high ceiling of 1.8 million. PdM (product management) averages 960,000 yen—fewer listings, but a higher average. In other words, the closer you get to decision-making, the higher the rate. The trend is clear.

I see... So right now I'm on the "implement the requirements I'm given" side, and to raise my rate I need to move to the side that "creates the requirements themselves."

Exactly. The rate gap isn't about technical skill—it's about how much decision-making discretion you're entrusted with. Technical skill is the prerequisite; the real contest starts after that.

As a development approach that keeps pace with market changes, it's increasingly being adopted not only by startups but also by large enterprises.
