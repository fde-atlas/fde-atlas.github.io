Designing AI Audit Trails | Record-Keeping Techniques for Later Explanation

Original title: AI監査証跡の設計｜後から説明できる記録術

日本Fde協会 · 2026-11-19 · 5 min read

Summary

An article by the Japan FDE Association on designing audit trails for AI systems: recording who ran what, when, and with which data, so results can be explained and reproduced later. It argues audit trails differ from permission design and accuracy evals and are nearly impossible to retrofit, so requirements should be fixed before rollout, with logging at execution time (including failed runs), linkage of personal IDs to AI logs, masking decisions for sensitive data, and early agreement on tamper prevention, identification, and retention policies.

Key points

  • Audit trails must let anyone later explain who, when, and with what data an AI action ran.
  • Link the actual user's ID to AI execution logs, not just service accounts.
  • Store prompts, outputs, and referenced data sources together to enable reproduction.
  • Write records at execution time and keep logs of failed runs.
  • Decide per business context what stays raw versus masked in logs.

In this article, FDE refers not to Full Disk Encryption but to Forward Deployed Engineer, a role that works on-site with customers to solve problems from both technical and business perspectives. After introducing AI into business operations, the question you will inevitably face is: "Can you explain later what this AI did?" Designing AI audit trails is not about accuracy evaluation or permission design; it is the work of leaving records that allow you to explain, after execution, who did what, when, and with which data. By reading this article, you will be able to design the log items to record, the timing of recording, and the policies for storage and reference, applying them to your own business workflows.

Why AI Audit Trails Matter Later

When AI is integrated into business operations, it takes over some of the judgments and operations that people used to perform manually. The tricky part here is that when a problem occurs, you may find yourself in a situation where "with humans, you could trace the history through work logs and approval records, but with AI, you cannot."

Trouble Always Comes Later

What we have repeatedly seen in AI implementation projects is the fact that incorrect outputs or unexpected data references are discovered "some time after implementation." The moment an issue is discovered, what is required is the reproduction of that single case: when it was generated, with what input, and using which data. Without evidence, the investigation stops there.

The Hardest Area to Add Later

Audit trails, unlike permission design or accuracy evaluation, are extremely difficult to add after the fact. Past executions that were not recorded can never be recovered. That is why we value deciding the requirements for evidence in parallel with the stage of showing a working prototype.

Log Items to Record in AI Audit Trails

So what exactly should you record? The starting point is a minimal set that allows you to explain to a third party later "who executed what, when, and with which data." For AI agents and LLM executions, AI-specific items are required in addition to human operation logs.

Record IDs That Can Be Traced Back to the Individual

The most commonly overlooked item is identifying the executor. Logs in AI environments often only contain service account names, making it impossible to trace back to the person who actually gave the instructions. This frequently occurs in organizations spanning multiple departments. You need to decide upfront on a design that allows cross-referencing the logged-in individual's ID with the AI execution logs.

Inputs, Outputs, and Referenced Data as a Set

With AI, unless you record the input prompt, the generated output, and the referenced data sources (documents retrieved via RAG, database records, etc.) as a single set, you cannot reproduce the results later. Below is a starting framework for the design.

ItemWhat to RecordWhat It Answers Later
ExecutorIndividual ID, whether delegatedWho
OperationInput, output, model/tools usedWhat
TimeExecution date and time (with time zone)When
DataReferenced sources, target recordsWith which data

Be Careful with the Sensitivity of the Logs Themselves

If you record inputs and referenced data in full, personal information and confidential data will end up inside the logs. It is safer to decide with the relevant departments, for each type of business, what to keep in original form and what to limit to masking or reference keys only. Do not make blanket decisions about retention periods or storage locations; determine them according to the sensitivity of the data being handled.

Designing Recording Timing, Storage, and Reference

Once the items are decided, the next step is "when and where to write them, and how to retrieve them later." If this part is done carelessly, you end up in a state where logs exist but cannot be traced when it matters most.

Design Insights from a Manufacturing Site

At a manufacturing client where AI was integrated into core operations such as shipping and receiving management, we made it a principle to write the evidence at the same time as execution. With a batch-writing approach after processing completes, records of executions that failed midway would be missing. We separated the recording into immediately before and after execution, keeping records of failed executions as well. This proves valuable in later investigations. A similar lesson was shared by a SaaS company executive: their biggest regret during an incident was "not having kept failure logs."

Tamper Prevention and Ease of Reference

Audit trails need to achieve both immutability after the fact and quick retrievability when needed. From our experience at startups, you can start small and grow by securing just append-only storage and an index that supports cross-searching by individual ID, time, and data. We recommend agreeing with the relevant departments before implementation on three points: tamper prevention, individual identification, and retention policy. Permission design itself is covered in Designing AI Agent Permission Management, the overall picture of controls in Introduction to FDE Internal Controls, and accuracy-side validation in Evaluation Design for Business AI. This article is separate from those and focuses specifically on execution history itself.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q. Are audit trail logs the same as accuracy evaluation logs? No, they are different. Accuracy evaluation records measure the quality of outputs, while audit trails are records for explaining later who executed what, when, and with which data. Since their purposes differ, we recommend designing them separately.

Q. Can we leave personal information or confidential data in logs as-is? Careful judgment is required. Decide with the relevant departments, for each type of business, what range to keep in original form and what to limit to masking or reference keys. Do not make blanket decisions about retention periods or storage locations; design according to the sensitivity of the data being handled.

Q. Are internal controls and audit areas covered in the FDE certification? The FDE certification is a private qualification operated independently by the association, and its exam areas include information security, internal controls, and auditing. It is not a national qualification, and whether or not you hold it does not legally restrict engagement in this work.
