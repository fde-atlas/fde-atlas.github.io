Government Defense FDE Salaries and Security Clearance Requirements

Original title: 政府国防 FDE 薪资与安全许可要求

FDE Times · 2026-10-09 · 5 min read

Summary

Drawing on a 2026 salary guide from Valletta Software and job postings from Palantir, Anduril, and OpenAI, the article argues that government and defense FDE roles pay no more than commercial ones: OpenAI's government FDE band starts $39,200 lower than its commercial equivalent. The real costs are security clearance waits (about 243 days for a first Top Secret clearance per DCSA data), heavy travel (an Anduril role allows up to 80% per year), and ethical concerns, including internal Palantir disputes over ICE work. The author advises candidates to clarify clearance requirements, travel terms, and client identity before signing.

Key points

  • OpenAI government FDE pay band is $145,800–$280,000, below commercial roles.
  • First-time Top Secret clearance takes about 243 days for private-sector applicants.
  • Anduril's FDE Air Defense role requires up to 80% travel for $113k–$155k.
  • Palantir employees have questioned the company's ICE partnership.
  • Read postings for clearance level, travel share, and pay before applying.

Key Takeaways

  • According to a 2026 salary guide, OpenAI's government FDE salary band is $145,800–$280,000, below the commercial band of $185,000–$300,000.
  • A first-time Top Secret clearance application for private-sector employees currently takes about 243 days; an Anduril role requires up to 80% travel year-round.
  • Palantir employees have questioned the company's partnership with ICE; before signing, make sure to ask directly who the clients are.

According to a 2026 salary guide published by Valletta Software, OpenAI's salary band for Forward Deployed Engineers (FDEs) serving government clients is $145,800–$280,000. Engineers with the same job title serving commercial clients have a salary band of $185,000–$300,000. At the bottom of the range, engineers working for the government earn $39,200 less.

This contradicts a common belief among engineers: government and defense work is harder, so it should pay more. The "harder" part is true; the "pays more" part is not supported by job postings or public data.

If you are working as an FDE, or considering moving into the role, this is a career decision worth thinking through carefully. In this field, what you pay is not money, but waiting time, days away from home, and possibly your ethical alignment with the clients you serve.

Higher pay is a misconception

Valletta Software is a software company, and its guide is blunt: in its view, government projects typically come with security clearance requirements, longer on-site time, less flexibility, and yet lower pay.

This is a vendor's analysis and should be treated as a secondary source. However, the signals from the job postings themselves are consistent.

Palantir's Forward Deployed Software Engineer (FDSE) role for the US government has an estimated base salary of $135,000–$200,000, with travel "as needed." Anduril's FDE Air Defense role pays $113,000–$155,000 in the US market. That is below many commercial FDE salary bands, while its working conditions are the harshest in this set of roles.

Reading down the columns, two things are clear: the closer a role is to defense, the heavier the requirements; yet the pay does not rise accordingly.

Security clearance: behind "willing to apply" lies eight months

Many people assume that without a security clearance there is no point in applying. The reality is a bit more flexible. Palantir's US government roles accept both people who already hold a clearance and those who are eligible and willing to apply, so a security clearance is not always a hard requirement on day one.

But "willing to apply" comes at a cost. Citing data from the US Defense Counterintelligence and Security Agency (DCSA), ClearanceJobs reports that a first-time Top Secret clearance application for private-sector employees currently takes 243 days for the full process—about eight months.

Imagine you accept an offer for Palantir's Federal Health and Civil sector role, which is directly tied to a Top Secret clearance, and you have never held any clearance. If the current timeline holds, your clearance may not be approved until nearly three quarters after you start.

The job posting does not say what you will be doing during that time. For an engineer with two to eight years of experience, eight months is a significant gap on a resume—don't let it remain an unknown.

So in the interview, ask: while waiting for the clearance, what projects will new engineers work on, with whom, and how will their results be evaluated? Also remember, these roles require US security clearances. If you are not eligible to apply, no matter how strong your technical skills, these roles are out of reach.

80% travel: do the math

All FDEs travel, but the Anduril role is in another league. Candidates must be able to travel to remote parts of the world, for up to two months at a time, totaling up to 80% of the year.

Put on a calendar, 80% of 365 days is roughly 292 days away from home, leaving about 73 days for everything else. With a salary band of only $113,000–$155,000, this is not hardship compensated by high pay: you earn less than many commercial roles while spending most of the year on site.

Some people will find this appealing. Deploying air defense systems in remote areas is a rare experience, hard to get from an office job. But weigh it against your family situation and your own health, and don't take the job expecting "the money will make up for it."

Ethical constraints: ask before you sign

The first two costs can be calculated; the third is harder to measure, yet may carry more weight. As an FDE, you sit next to the client, and the code you write runs on their data. In government projects, the client may be an agency whose practices you do not agree with.

LeadDev reports that Palantir is one of the most important technology partners of US Immigration and Customs Enforcement (ICE). In a January 2026 article, LeadDev also reported growing internal discontent, with some employees questioning whether Palantir should continue working with ICE.

People inside the company are themselves debating this question. For FDEs, the risk is more personal: engineers at headquarters can keep their distance from the product, while FDEs are on the front line and see firsthand how systems are used.

The practical advice is: draw your own lines before signing. Ask the employer directly: which agencies does the company serve? Can engineers decline a particular client project? Has anyone declined before? If the answers are vague, factor that into your evaluation of the offer.

So, is it worth it?

The answer depends on what you get in return for these costs. If money is what you're after, the available data suggests the commercial side pays more: the OpenAI salary bands cited by Valletta, and the comparison between Anduril and many commercial FDE salary bands, all point in the same direction.

If what you want is the work these job postings describe—deploying air defense systems in remote areas, or working under a Top Secret clearance framework—then that is a legitimate reason to accept the costs above.

For those targeting these roles, every job posting has three things worth reading closely: the security clearance level, and whether it says "currently held" or "eligible to apply"; the percentage and duration of travel; and the salary band, compared against commercial roles at the same company.

On your resume, if you have experience working on-site under constrained conditions, following strict security protocols, or spending extended time on the front lines, state the number of weeks and the context. This is exactly the kind of evidence employers in this field need to see.

Government and defense won't pay you extra for your sacrifices. If you still choose this path, choose it for the mission, and know clearly whom you are serving.
