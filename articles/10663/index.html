Connect a software capability to a working customer workflow

FDE Interviews · 2026-08-09 · 9 min read

Summary

This first lesson from FDE Interviews uses a synthetic freight pilot to teach the last-mile work of forward deployed engineering: connecting a software capability to one customer's data, permissions and deadlines. It argues a successful demo proves only the cases tested, and walks through tracing one example end to end, separating observations from interpretations, and coordinating owners across data, security and operations. It closes with a four-part evaluation: delivery, correctness, usability and outcome.

Key points

  • A demo only proves the cases and environment actually tested.
  • The last mile spans code, data contracts, permissions, rollout and support.
  • Identifier mismatches like 00127 vs 127 require a domain mapping decision.
  • Trace one approved case end to end and report where the trace stops.
  • Usage alone doesn't prove value; evaluate delivery, quality, adoption and outcomes separately.

Transcript

Forward deployed engineering connects what software can do to one customer's data, workflow and operating constraints. The work mixes hands-on building with discovery, evaluation and coordination. And a successful demo is only one piece of evidence. You don't need freight experience for this. An exception here is a shipment problem an operator has to investigate and resolve.

A vendor can demo a useful search or summary, and the customer still can't use it day to day, because their records don't join, users can't reach the right documents, the output arrives too late, or nobody owns the failure path. The cause is simple. The capability was built on data the vendor controls. The workflow runs on data, permissions and deadlines the customer controls. Those are possibilities, not proof. The product may be missing something too, so investigate both.

The last mile is the work connecting a capability to a real operating outcome. It can be code, configuration, data contracts, permissions, user interaction, rollout and support. Most of those tasks already have owners. So the challenge is usually coordinating their boundaries, not finding work that literally belongs to nobody.

Take a synthetic pilot. A demo summarizes a delay email and suggests the shipment. Read the packet before writing another prompt. It found the shipment in 8 of 10 selected emails, which shows the path works on those cases, not representative quality. The export arrives nightly. Outbound model calls are blocked in the target network, and nobody is named for failed imports.

Billing uses zero zero one two seven. The shipment export uses one two seven, with a trailing space. That's a domain decision, not a cleanup. A system that pads every ID to five digits can only mean one record by zero zero one two seven, while a system that issues both as separate accounts has two, and the export alone can't tell you which you're looking at. So ask the data owner for the mapping rule, and test for collisions.

A useful first move is to trace one approved example end to end. The incoming exception, the shipment identity, the contract that applies, the proposed output, the operator's decision and the recorded outcome. Mark where the trace stops, and the evidence you need to continue. That stopping point is the finding you report. And write it in three parts. Two IDs differ is an observation. They're the same customer is an interpretation. Asking the owner for the mapping is the next test.

Then measure more than shipped code. Was the agreed software delivered and tested? Is it correct enough on the eligible work? Can authorized operators use it with acceptable effort? And does that use improve the agreed outcome, at an acceptable cost and risk? Usage alone doesn't prove value. A mandatory tool can have high usage and poor outcomes.

So the last mile connects a capability to someone's real data, permissions and deadlines. A demo proves only the cases it ran, one traced case tells you where it breaks, and your job is to coordinate the owners, not to overrule them. The full lesson is on fdeinterviews.com.

Where you are. This is the first lesson. You need no freight experience: an exception here is a shipment problem that an operator must investigate and resolve. Use that workflow to understand why customer-facing engineering can extend well beyond installing a product.

One task, from request to outcome

A vendor can demonstrate a useful search or summarization capability, and a customer may still be unable to use it in daily work because source records do not join, users cannot access the right documents, the output arrives too late or nobody owns the failure path.

The gap has a simple cause. The capability was built and tested on data the vendor controls; the workflow runs on data, permissions and deadlines the customer controls, and every difference between the two is work somebody has to do.

These are possible explanations for a stalled pilot, not proof that the product is sound or that most pilots fail for one reason. The product might lack a required capability, or the proposed problem might not justify the cost, so investigate both the platform and its fit to the workflow.

The last mile is a useful name for the work connecting a capability to an actual operating outcome. It can include code, configuration, data contracts (the agreed meaning and format of each field where one system hands data to another), permissions, user interaction, rollout and support. Those tasks already have owners in many organizations; the challenge is often coordinating their boundaries rather than finding work that literally belongs to nobody.

A useful first move is to trace one approved example end to end: incoming exception, shipment identity, applicable contract, proposed output, operator decision and recorded outcome. Note where the trace stops and what evidence you would need to continue, and do it in an approved environment with suitable data, not by copying customer records into an arbitrary demo tool.

The identifier example needs a domain decision. Stripping whitespace may be appropriate under a source contract; dropping leading zeros may merge distinct identifiers in another system. The mechanism is worth seeing: a system that pads every identifier to five digits can only ever mean one record by 00127, while a system that issues 127 and 00127 as separate accounts has two, and the export alone cannot tell you which kind of system you are looking at. Ask the data owner for the mapping rule and test for collisions, because “clean the data” is too vague to specify a safe transformation.

The nightly export may be fine for a daily review and useless for a 15-minute intervention; which one matters depends on the workflow. No faster model can make yesterday's source current.

Ownership, made concrete

Different organizations divide the work differently, so treat roles as starting points for a responsibility discussion, not stereotypes about who cannot help.

Work to resolve Likely collaborators Evidence of a useful handoff
Product capability or defect Product and platform engineers Reproduced issue, supported behavior and a tracked fix or limitation
Source mapping and quality Customer data owner and integration engineers Approved mapping, representative examples and rejection rules
Workflow and adoption Operators and workflow manager Observed task, tested output and agreed use conditions
Access and processing Security, identity and data owners Verified permissions and approved processing path
Delivery and operation FDE, services, platform or customer team Named responsibilities, release checks and recovery procedure

An FDE may implement several of these pieces and coordinate the rest. Owning progress does not mean granting yourself permissions or unilaterally changing the customer's business process. Record the responsible decision-maker and the next check when a dependency sits outside your authority.

Documentation and discovery together

Good documentation can make a standard integration straightforward and save a great deal of repeated bespoke work. Where the workflow or source semantics are uncertain, documentation alone may not contain the missing answer; inspect permitted data samples and observe the real task with the people who perform it.

A short discovery note should keep observation, interpretation and next test apart. “Two IDs differ in formatting” is an observation, “They are the same customer” is an unverified interpretation, and “Ask the source owner for the canonical mapping and test collisions” is a next step. Keeping them apart stops an early guess from turning into a production assumption.

Hands-on engineering includes implementing and testing the resulting contract, and it also includes deciding not to build a proposed feature when the evidence shows it cannot meet the need or should not proceed under the current constraints.

Palantir's FDSE role description provides one primary example of customer-embedded work spanning initial conversations, architecture, data and production applications. Treat a company's posting as evidence about that role, not a universal definition of every FDE job. The next lesson compares actual ownership across adjacent roles.

More than shipped code

For the synthetic pilot, distinguish four questions:

  • Was the agreed software delivered and tested?
  • Does it produce sufficiently correct results on the eligible work?
  • Can authorized operators use it in their workflow with acceptable effort?
  • Does that use improve the agreed outcome at an acceptable cost and risk?

Adoption matters, but usage alone does not establish value: a mandatory tool can have high usage and poor outcomes. In the other direction, an accurate system with low usage may have an access, timing or usability problem, so investigate before blaming users or declaring the entire capability useless.

Nor does a finite evaluation prove that all future cases will work. Record difficult segments, human fallback, missing outcomes and operating limits. Later modules turn these questions into runnable data, evaluation and recovery exercises.

The trace above, drawn as one spine: each stage is a place the path can stop, and the stopping point is the finding you report.

Do this before moving on

Create a six-row issue register from the pilot packet. For each observation, write its consequence, a responsible role, the next evidence request and whether it blocks a daily-review pilot, a 15-minute pilot or both under the stated facts. Explain any additional assumptions.

Then write a three-sentence status update: what the demo established, the most important unresolved dependency and the next concrete test. Do not call the pilot production-ready or quote the selected 8/10 as a representative accuracy estimate.

Finally, choose a current FDE job posting and save its date and source. Mark responsibilities involving implementation, customer discovery, operation and reusable product work. Overlap with other roles is fine, since the point is to understand the actual scope, not to invent duties unique to a title. A good answer cites the posting and lists questions it leaves unanswered.

Key takeaways

  • The last mile connects capabilities to specific data, workflows, permissions and operating conditions.
  • A demo supports only the cases and environment actually tested.
  • Trace one task end to end and distinguish observations from assumptions.
  • Coordinate explicit ownership while preserving the customer's decision rights.
  • Evaluate delivery, quality, adoption and outcomes as related but separate questions.

Check yourself
