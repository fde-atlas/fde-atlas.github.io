The 95% Number, Examined: MIT NANDA on Enterprise AI Pilots

Vibe Engines · 2026-08-01 · 21 min read

Summary

This piece dissects the widely cited MIT NANDA finding that roughly 95% of organizations see zero return from enterprise generative AI. It argues the number counts organizations, not pilots — about one in four organizations that piloted a task-specific tool reached production — and that "no measurable P&L impact" can reflect measurement or attribution failures rather than technical failure. The author presents this as the commercial case for forward deployed engineers: capture baselines early, agree on one owned metric, and instrument adoption. The report also found external partnerships reached deployment about 67% of the time versus 33% for internal builds, though it is a preliminary, interview-based study and its figures are directional.

Key points

  • The 95% figure counts organizations, not pilots; about 25% of piloting organizations reached production.
  • Failures split into three types: measurement, attribution, and genuine failure — each needs a different fix.
  • The report is preliminary, based on interviews and surveys, so treat numbers as directional.
  • Buying beat building: ~67% vs ~33% reaching deployment, from only 52 interviewed organizations.
  • The author argues this gap justifies the FDE role: baseline, one owned metric, instrumented adoption.

It is the most-quoted statistic in enterprise AI: roughly 95% of organisations getting zero return from generative AI, usually retold as 95% of pilots producing no measurable profit-and-loss impact.17 It appears in board decks, in sales objections, and in every article about the AI bubble. It is also routinely misread in both directions — as proof the technology does not work, and as a problem someone else has. Read carefully, it is neither.

What this claims, and what it argues against

Rung 1, and what it argues against is the default assumption of the period: that model capability is the constraint on enterprise value, so a better model produces a better outcome. The claim — usually quoted as roughly 95% of enterprise AI pilots showing no measurable impact on profit and loss, though the report's headline and funnel count organisations — is an argument that the constraint is somewhere else entirely.

The whole sentence turns on one word, and reading it carelessly is how the number gets misused. "Measurable" is doing an enormous amount of work. A pilot can change how people work, reduce time on a task, or produce a capability the organisation keeps, and still not show up in a P&L line — because the effect was not instrumented, because no baseline was taken before it started, because the benefit landed as capacity rather than cost, or because the attribution was never agreed. "No measurable impact" is a joint statement about the intervention and the measurement, and the failure can sit on either side.

Which is what makes it the commercial argument for forward-deployed engineering rather than an argument against the technology. If capability is abundant and outcomes are rare, then the scarce work is the part between them — integration, workflow change, instrumentation, and agreeing in advance what would count as evidence. That is a description of a job, and the number is the clearest available case for why someone pays for it.

What the finding actually says

MIT NANDA’s July 2025 report, The GenAI Divide: State of AI in Business 2025, states that roughly 95% of organisations are getting zero return from enterprise generative AI, and phrases the same finding elsewhere as integrated pilots producing no measurable impact on profit and loss. Press coverage mostly repeats the pilot phrasing.17 a preliminary report built on interviews and surveys, not a peer-reviewed paper — treat the exact figure as directional

Two words decide what this supports. Measurable: the finding is about attributable, demonstrated effect on a financial statement — not about whether users liked the tool, whether the model was accurate, or whether anyone felt more productive. And pilot: a bounded trial, not a production deployment, which selects for exactly the projects most likely to end before an accounting period closes. Note too what the report counts: its funnel is of organisations, not pilots — 20% piloted a task-specific tool and 5% got one into production, so about one in four of those that piloted succeeded.

“95% of AI projects fail” is not what it says, and repeating it that way will lose you an argument with anyone who has read the coverage. A project can deliver real value and still show no measurable P&L impact — because nobody captured a baseline, because the effect was distributed across teams, or because it was never mapped to a line anyone reports. That distinction is the whole subject of this page.

Three separate failures hiding inside one number

They need different fixes, and conflating them is why the statistic gets quoted without changing anything.

FailureWhat actually happenedThe fix
Measurement failureThe system worked and nobody can prove it — no baseline was captured before the change, so the before-picture does not exist.Capture the baseline in week one, before you build anything. It is unrecoverable afterwards.
Attribution failureSomething improved, and three other things changed in the same quarter. The finance team will not credit yours.Agree one number, with one owner, in their reporting language, before the pilot starts.
Actual failureThe workflow was wrong, adoption never happened, or the system solved a problem nobody was paid to care about.Scoping. The problem was chosen badly, and no amount of engineering repairs that.

The first two are the reason a forward deployed engineer exists as a role. Both are solved by work that happens before and after the model — discovery that finds a workflow with a number attached, and instrumentation that makes the change attributable. Neither is model work, and neither gets done by shipping an API.

Why this is the commercial case for the role

Read alongside a16z’s services-led-growth argument, the picture is coherent rather than contradictory. a16z’s thesis is that companies should deliberately accept lower gross margin on deployment work because the resulting customer outcome is defensible.8 The NANDA-style finding is what the market looks like when nobody does that work: capability is available, outcomes are not, and the gap between the two is a services gap rather than a model gap.

What the number supports

  • Enterprise AI value is bottlenecked on deployment and measurement, not on model capability.
  • Most organisations cannot currently prove the value of work they have already done.
  • A role that owns the arc from problem selection to demonstrated outcome has an obvious economic justification.
  • “It works” and “we can show it worked” are different projects, and the second is usually unstaffed.

What it does not support

  • That the technology does not work. The finding is about attribution, not capability.
  • That 95% of pilots fail. The report’s funnel counts organisations, and about one in four that piloted a task-specific tool reached production.
  • A precise figure. It comes from a preliminary report built on interviews and surveys, so quote it as approximate.
  • Fatalism. Every failure mode named above is preventable by work that costs days, not quarters.

Bringing this number up yourself is a strong move, and only in one form: “most pilots never establish what would count as working, so let us agree that first — what number, measured how, and who signs off that it moved?” That reframes you from a vendor claiming an exception into the person solving the failure they have probably already lived through.

What it changes about how you work

Capture the baseline before you build

How many cases per week, how long each takes, what the error rate is today. In week one, while it is still available — after you change the workflow, the before-picture is gone permanently and every later conversation is anecdote against anecdote.

Agree one number, and who owns it

Not four metrics. One, in their reporting language, with a named person who will confirm it moved. The full method is in proving value & ROI.

Instrument adoption, not just accuracy

A system with excellent evals and no users produces exactly the outcome this statistic describes. Workflow DAU, override rate and deflection rate tell you whether anything actually changed.

Set the quality bar against humans

“Measurable” requires a comparison. Inter-expert agreement gives you a defensible one, and it also makes the target achievable — see evals for client work.

Say so when a deployment should not renew

Some pilots in that 95% were kept alive by reluctance to call them. An FDE who says it early, with evidence, is more valuable than one who defends a doomed account for two more quarters.

Measurement, not modelling

How the MIT NANDA report was built

The figure comes from The GenAI Divide: State of AI in Business 2025, by Aditya Challapally, Chris Pease, Ramesh Raskar and Pradyumna Chari of MIT's Project NANDA. It is dated July 2025, covers research from January to June 2025, and describes itself as preliminary findings. It is a report, not a peer-reviewed paper.

  • Collect three kinds of evidence. A systematic review of more than 300 publicly disclosed AI initiatives and announcements; structured interviews with representatives of 52 organizations; and survey responses from 153 senior leaders gathered at four industry conferences. Company data and quotes are anonymized.
  • Split the tools into two classes. General-purpose LLM tools such as ChatGPT and Copilot, and embedded or task-specific GenAI: enterprise systems, custom-built or bought from a vendor, aimed at a particular workflow. The headline failure rate is about the second class.
  • Follow organizations through a funnel. For each class, the share of organizations that investigated a tool, piloted one and successfully implemented one.
  • Define success. A task-specific tool counts as successfully implemented when users or executives describe a marked and sustained effect on productivity or P&L. The methods note adds deployment beyond the pilot phase with measurable KPIs, and judges ROI six months after the pilot, adjusted for department size.
  • Score industries. A disruption index combines five publicly observable indicators to rank sectors; the authors test other weightings and report that the top and bottom of the ranking hold.
  • Compare how deployments were staffed. Interviewees' projects are grouped as strategic partnerships (buy), internal development (build) or a hybrid of the two, and their success rates compared.

The cause it proposes: a learning gap

The report's own explanation is what it calls the learning gap. Its interviews describe enterprise tools that do not retain feedback, adapt to context or improve over time, and users who happily draft with ChatGPT but will not trust it, or a brittle internal tool, with work that depends on remembering past corrections. On this reading the barrier is neither model quality nor regulation nor infrastructure but whether the system learns inside the workflow, and the authors present agentic systems with persistent memory as the way across. Their secondary findings fit that story:

  • Buying beats building. In the interview sample, external partnerships reached deployment about 67% of the time and internal builds about 33%.
  • A shadow AI economy. Only 40% of companies said they had bought an official LLM subscription, but workers at more than 90% of surveyed companies reported regular use of personal AI tools for work.
  • Budgets favor the front office. Asked to split a hypothetical $100 of GenAI budget, executives put about half into sales and marketing, while the clearest savings the report documents came from back-office work, such as cutting outsourced processing and agency spend.
  • Disruption is narrow. Only technology, and media and telecom, show clear structural change.
  • Mid-market moves faster. Top mid-market firms reported about 90 days from pilot to full implementation; enterprises, defined as firms with over $100 million in revenue, took nine months or more.

That is a different cut from the three failures named on this page, measurement, attribution and genuine failure, and the two fit together: the learning gap is one cause of genuine failure, while the other two are how a system that worked can still land in the 95%.

Read with its own caveats, the report is narrower than its headline. The 95% appears three ways, as organizations getting zero return, as integrated pilots with no measurable P&L impact, and as custom tools that never reach production, and the funnel behind it counts organizations: the 5% that reached production is a quarter of the 20% that piloted. Success rests on what users and executives said, inside a six-month window after the pilot. The authors flag that their figures are directional, that organizations willing to discuss AI problems may differ from those that declined, that success definitions vary across organizations, and that the buy-versus-build gap may reflect the capabilities of the organizations that bought rather than the approach itself. The text is also not fully consistent: it gives sales and marketing about half of the hypothetical budget in one place and about 70% in another, and counts the sectors as eight in one place and nine in another.

Per 100 organizations, 20 piloted a task-specific tool and 5 reached production, so 15 pilots stalled. That one funnel supports three different failure rates: 75 percent of piloting organizations did not reach production, 91.7 percent of investigating organizations did not, and 100 − 5 = 95 percent of all organizations had no task-specific tool in production, a denominator that includes the 40 that never looked. The headline 95 matches only the last reading, which is why the same report gets quoted for very different claims.

Two more divisions keep the figure honest. Because 95 percent is a share of organizations, it does not by itself say what fraction of the $30–40 billion went to the organizations that failed. And the buy-versus-build result, about 67 against 33 percent reaching deployment, is a ratio of about 2.0 measured on 52 interviewed organizations, small enough that a few cases move it. The general-purpose column cannot be pooled with the other: its 80 percent pilot-to-implementation rate measures adoption, mostly individual productivity, not a measurable effect on P&L.

The trade-offs of running a pilot that can be shown to work

Acting on the report means spending effort before and around the model rather than on it. Each choice that makes an outcome provable also gives something up, and it is better to name the cost to the customer up front than to discover it at renewal.

Common mistakes when acting on the 95% finding

  • A metric that cannot move inside the windowThe report judged return about six months after the pilot. A number whose movement arrives a year later, such as annual churn, will read as zero however well the system works. Pair it with a leading indicator that responds within weeks and that the owner agrees is tied to it.
  • Counting freed hours as savingsTime saved lands as capacity, and capacity shows up on no financial statement until something changes: overtime falls, a contractor is not renewed, the backlog clears. Agree in advance what the freed time will turn into, or the pilot works and still joins the 95%.
  • Comparing against no AI at allWorkers at more than 90% of surveyed companies already used personal AI tools. The real baseline is often people pasting work into a consumer chatbot, so measure the official tool against that, and study the shadow use first; it shows which tasks people actually want help with.
  • A baseline that does not match the after-pictureMeasure a quiet month before and a busy month after, or count a case differently once the system routes them, and the comparison is noise. Fix the definition, the sample and the season of both measurements when the baseline is taken.
  • Reading the learning gap as a model problemThe report's explanation is that tools do not retain corrections or context inside the workflow, not that models are too weak. A larger model leaves that untouched; feedback capture, persistent context and a route for corrections address it. The argument rests on interviews, so test it on your own deployment.

What this number can and cannot support

Start with what kind of document it is: a research report, and the most-cited statistic from it travelled far beyond the context that produced it. As with any survey figure, the answer depends on who was asked, how a "pilot" was defined, what window was allowed for impact to appear, and who decided whether impact was measurable. Those definitions decide the number, and a figure this round and this quotable should be treated as directional rather than precise.

It does not support "AI does not work" — which is the reading it is most often recruited for. A pilot showing no measured P&L movement is equally consistent with the technology working and the organisation being unable to capture or demonstrate it, and that ambiguity is intrinsic rather than a flaw in the reporting. It also does not support any claim about a specific deployment, yours included.

What it does support is a change in how you run one, and this is the part worth keeping whatever happens to the statistic. Agree the measurement before the build. Name the metric, take the baseline while the old process is still running, and get the person who owns the number to agree in advance that a movement in it would count. A pilot with no pre-agreed measurement cannot succeed — not because it will not work, but because there is no state of the world in which it can be shown to have worked. Most of the 95% is likely to be that, and it is avoidable for free.

Questions people ask about The 95% Number, Examined

What methodology did the MIT GenAI Divide report use?

The report, The GenAI Divide: State of AI in Business 2025 from MIT's Project NANDA, combines three sources gathered between January and June 2025: a review of more than 300 publicly disclosed AI initiatives, structured interviews with representatives of 52 organizations, and a survey of 153 senior leaders at four industry conferences. A task-specific tool counted as successfully implemented when users or executives described a marked and sustained productivity or profit-and-loss effect, with return judged about six months after the pilot. The authors call the results preliminary and directional, note that they rest on interviews rather than company reporting, and anonymize all company data.

Does the MIT report really say 95% of AI pilots fail?

Not in those words, and its own funnel says something narrower. The report states that 95% of organizations are getting zero return, and that only about one integrated pilot in twenty is producing value worth millions. Its funnel for task-specific tools counts organizations: 60% investigated one, 20% piloted one and 5% reached production. So about one in four of the organizations that ran a pilot got a tool into production, and the 95% is closer to the share of all organizations without a success than to a failure rate of pilots. Success also had to be visible to users or executives within about six months, which misses slow or unmeasured gains.

What does the MIT NANDA report mean by the learning gap?

It is the report's main explanation for why pilots stall. Most enterprise GenAI tools, it argues, do not retain feedback, adapt to context or improve over time, so they break on edge cases and must be briefed again for every task. Interviewees liked ChatGPT for drafting and quick tasks but would not trust it with work that depends on remembering past corrections and client preferences. The authors conclude that the barrier is not model quality, regulation or infrastructure but whether a system learns inside the workflow, and they point to agentic systems with persistent memory as the way across. It is an argument built from interviews, not a measured effect.

Is it better to buy or build enterprise AI, according to the MIT report?

In its interview sample, deployments built through external partnerships with customized, learning-capable vendor tools reached production about 67% of the time, against about 33% for tools built in-house, which the report summarizes as buying succeeding twice as often. The authors attach strong caveats: the figures come from 52 organizations and are self-reported, success definitions varied, and organizations that chose to buy may differ in risk tolerance, procurement skill or technical capacity from those that built. They state explicitly that the correlation does not prove causation. It is a good reason to ask why a build is planned, not proof that buying will work.

How widespread is shadow AI according to the MIT GenAI Divide report?

Very. Only 40% of the companies in the study said they had bought an official LLM subscription, yet workers at more than 90% of the companies surveyed reported using personal AI tools such as ChatGPT or Claude for work, often several times a day and usually without IT approval. The authors read this as evidence that individuals get value from flexible consumer tools while official projects stall, and they suggest studying that shadow use before buying enterprise alternatives. It also complicates the headline: some of the value the 95% figure misses may be real but unrecorded, happening outside any official pilot.

4 questions to test what you just read

Work each one out before you open it — a number, a prediction or a reason, not a definition. Producing the answer is what makes it stick. Your progress is kept on this device.

Using the report's funnel for task-specific tools (60% investigated, 20% piloted, 5% reached production), what fraction of the organizations that piloted got a tool into production?

Show answer

5 ÷ 20 = 25%, one in four. Because every share is of all organizations studied, the 95% is the share of organizations without a successful deployment, not the failure rate of pilots, which by these numbers is closer to 75%.

The buy-versus-build gap was about 67% against 33% in 52 interviews. Why might that gap not mean that buying causes success?

Show answer

The figures are self-reported, and the organizations that chose to buy may differ in risk tolerance, procurement skill or technical capacity from those that built, which the authors themselves flag. A 52-organization interview sample also may not represent the wider market, so the gap is a correlation to investigate, not an effect.

Executives put about half of a hypothetical $100 GenAI budget into sales and marketing. Why is that weaker evidence about real spending than it sounds?

Show answer

It is a thought experiment, not accounting: the report says GenAI spend is not yet formally tracked, so it asked for an imagined split. The same text gives about 70% elsewhere, and it treats the sub-category breakdowns as directional at best.

A pilot cut handling time, but no baseline was recorded. How can it still count toward the report's 95%?

Show answer

Success required users or executives to describe a marked, sustained productivity or P&L effect, judged about six months after the pilot. Without a before-picture nobody can show the change, so a working system is reported as no measurable impact: a measurement failure, not a technology failure.
