Why the FDE Org Reports to the CPO, Not Sales or Services

Falkster.AI · 2026-11-05 · 12 min read

Summary

The article argues that FDE organizations should report to the CPO, not sales, services, or engineering. It cites NICE, ServiceNow, and Palantir, which all mentioned FDE teams on their Q3 2026 earnings calls within eight days, yet each reported only sales or delivery metrics—engagements, time to value, demand—never what the field contributed to the product. The reporting line determines the scorecard: sales produces bookings, services utilization, engineering harness quality. The FDE's real output is a product decision signal, and only the roadmap owner can act on it quickly. The author predicts a public company will report a field-to-product metric by end of 2027.

NICE reported on Wednesday, and the forward deployed engineer is in the script. Scott Russell, in his prepared remarks on the call: "Since the end of Q2, we have fully staffed our FDE team, and engagements are ramping significantly." Asked about it later, he described "a pretty significant shift in our go-to-market around use of forward-deployed engineers."

Last Wednesday it was ServiceNow. Gina Mastantuono on the call: "With the help of our Forward Deployed Engineers, we have seen an accelerated time to value of 80% year-to-date." Bill McDermott named the unit: "Our Applied AI FDE team."

And Monday was Palantir, written up yesterday. Alex Karp: "we really cannot satiate demand."

Three public companies, eight days, the same seat in the earnings script. Now the numbers attached to it. Engagements ramping. Time to value. Demand. Every one is a sales number or a delivery number. Not one of the three reported what the field put into the product. That's a tell about where the org sits.

This is part 15 of The FDE Transition, and the first of a new arc about where the FDE org belongs. The answer in one line: it reports to the CPO.

The short version

  • The reporting line is the scorecard, and the scorecard decides what the field produces. Under sales, bookings. Under services, utilization. Under engineering, a clean harness.
  • All three are things a company wants, and none is the reason to put an engineer inside a customer's building.
  • The reason is that a deployment is the fastest signal a product has about what to build next, and the FDE's real output is a decision about the product: a workflow that should be a feature, an eval the roadmap should be held to, a customer's process that is the design spec.
  • Only the function that owns the roadmap can act on that in hours instead of quarters.
  • In the AI era that function sits under the CPO, with design, product marketing, and the field in one org, because a loop split across four reporting lines can't close fast enough. NICE just moved its FDEs into go-to-market. I think it'll move them again.

What the script measures is where the org reports

A team reports the number its reporting line asked for. That's the whole mechanism.

NICE's FDE line sits in a paragraph about go-to-market, and Beth Gaspich's remarks on the same call put deployment itself with the partners: "increasingly leveraging GSIs and other partners to deploy our platform." So the engineers are sales-side capacity, and their number is engagements. ServiceNow's team is called Applied AI and measured by time to value, a delivery number, and a good one. Palantir's is measured by the conversions Ryan Taylor walked through last week, pilots into deals.

Milos Mandic's survey found the same spread: 24 FDEs reporting into five different places. Antoine Buteau wrote the clearest piece on this in May, and his first line belongs on the wall: "Org design decides what forward deployment becomes." Under sales it becomes deal support. Under customer success, account rescue. Under services, a business of its own. He stops short of naming a winner, "there is no universally correct reporting line," and that's the point I'll disagree with, because the next two years decide it.

Everything a company can measure about an FDE team from the outside is a lagging number. The one leading number, the share of what came back from the field that shipped for every customer, the generalization rate, only shows up on a product scorecard. Anywhere else it has no owner, and a number with no owner is a number nobody reports on a call.

Why not sales, services, or engineering

Sales first, because sales asks first. An FDE closes deals, so the headcount request comes from sales and the engineer lands under a bookings number. Within two quarters the promise is whatever closed the deal and the handback is a liability nobody has time for. NICE's wording is honest about the intent: capacity so customers can "deploy and adopt in a much more faster, agile, and not resource-constrained way." A fine goal for a sales org. Not a product loop.

Services next. A utilization scorecard keeps the engineer on the account, because the account pays. At a hundred FDEs that's the support desk with a field title from part 14, and the economics chapter has what it costs. Lyra Technology Group announced its FDE program on Wednesday, 16 engineers across eight managed service providers, more than 50 by the end of 2027, and made the placement explicit: each engineer belongs to a single MSP, not a centralized shared-services team. Dakota Ferguson's diagnosis is right, "The hardest part of AI isn't the technology. It's the last mile," and Deniz Sagnaklar says the goal is a managed AI practice "that stays in place." For an MSP holding company that's reasonable. It's a services design: the practice stays, the engineer stays, and there's no product on the other end to absorb anything.

Engineering is the harder case, and the org design chapter allowed it. Engineering owns the harness, the codebase contract says what it owes the field, and an engineering leader will hold the bar on what an FDE ships. But engineering's scoreboard is the system: reliability, the cost of a change, whether the rows pass. Right things to count, wrong things to hold the field to, because the field's output isn't a system. It's a decision about what the system should become for everyone. An FDE who reports to engineering brings a finding back to a function whose job is to build what product decided. The finding waits for a roadmap it doesn't own.

Compare Legora. In Basil Chatha's interview on Monday, Jacob Lauritzen describes the company's forward deployed engineers as the people who build the integrations and feed learnings back to the core product, next to about a hundred legal engineers who ship no code. I wrote about the split on Tuesday. The FDE's named output is what goes back into the product. That's a product placement whatever box it's drawn in.

The loop only closes under one roof

This is the argument that runs across all four of these series, and the FDE's piece of it is the simplest. The product outcome in the AI era is a loop: customer, spec, build, story, adoption, back to the customer. Building got cheap. The loop didn't. What decides the outcome now is how fast a thing learned at one customer becomes a spec, a build, a claim, and an adopted workflow at every customer.

The forward deployed engineer sits at both ends of that loop at once, the customer end and the build end. That's why the seat exists, and why part 1 called the FDE the Product Builder arriving from the services side. Design turns the customer's workflow into the spec, the subject of The Design Transition. Product marketing turns deployment proof into the story, and that series made the case that the story is a product surface now. The CPO decides what gets built for everyone. Four functions, one loop.

Put the four under four executives and each reports its own number, and the loop closes in quarters, through planning cycles, if at all. Put them under the CPO and there's one scorecard, and the field finding that arrives on Tuesday is a roadmap decision by Thursday, because the person who reads the handback owns the roadmap. The CPO Transition makes the case from the top. From the field it's just this: the FDE produces the product's fastest input, and the input belongs to whoever owns the output.

I've sat in the CPO seat. The thing you can't buy is a true signal from inside a customer's building the same week. An FDE team that reports to you is that signal with a budget. One that reports to sales is a rumor in the pipeline review.

Three predictions, labeled as mine

First: by the end of 2027, at least one public software company reports a field-to-product number on an earnings call, the share of roadmap items that started in a deployment, or product changes that generalized from field work. The company that does it first will have moved its FDE org under product before it had the number, because the number doesn't exist anywhere else.

Second: the labs' Applied AI teams and the enterprise vendors' FDE teams converge on product leadership within two years. The reason is the next part of this arc. As agents take over the on-site build, the human FDE's remaining output is judgment: what's worth automating, what the customer's process should become, what the product should absorb. Judgment about what to build is a product decision, and the more the build moves to agents, the less the reporting line can stay with the function that measured the build.

Third: the FDE orgs that stay under go-to-market keep reporting engagements and time to value, and they'll be the ones whose FDE headcount per dollar of AI revenue rises instead of falls. NICE reported AI ARR of $392 million, up 46 percent. The number I'd watch over four quarters is how many FDEs that takes.

The decision this quarter

If you're the exec: move the FDE org's reporting line to the CPO before the next planning cycle, and change the scorecard in the same memo. Generalization rate per pod. Roadmap items that started in a deployment, with dates. Days from a field finding to a roadmap decision. Dotted lines to sales for the promise and to engineering for the harness. Perspective AI's playbook from June said "product or engineering, with a dotted line to sales, never the reverse," with a day-90 productization gate. I'd narrow it to product and keep the gate.

If you're the CPO: the org only belongs to you if you read it. Name the product manager who reads every handback packet, and staff the editor's desk before you take the headcount. A field team under a product org that doesn't read the field is a different box on the same chart.

If you're the FDE: find out whose number you're on. If it's bookings, your best finding this year dies in a pipeline review. Write the productization case anyway and send it to the PM who'd own it, by name. Reporting lines change. The habit is yours now.

One thing to try this week: the last three product changes that started in a deployment, with the date the field found it and the date the roadmap decided. Three rows, two columns. Fill it in and you have a product loop, and the chart should say so. If you can't, the FDE org reports to the wrong place, whatever the chart says.

Frequently asked

Who should forward deployed engineers report to?+

The chief product officer. Not sales, because a bookings scorecard turns the FDE into a sales engineer and the promise drifts. Not services, because a utilization scorecard turns the team into a consulting firm. Not engineering, because engineering owns the harness and the code, which is one layer of the job, and the thing the field produces at the end of a deployment is a decision about what the product should become for every customer. Only the function that owns the roadmap can act on that decision in hours, and in the AI era that function is the product org under the CPO.

What did NICE, ServiceNow, and Palantir say about forward deployed engineers on their Q3 2026 earnings calls?+

On November 4, 2026, NICE CEO Scott Russell said the company had fully staffed its FDE team since the end of Q2 with engagements ramping significantly, and called it a significant shift in go-to-market around the use of forward-deployed engineers. On October 28, ServiceNow CFO Gina Mastantuono said that with the help of its forward deployed engineers the company had seen an accelerated time to value of 80 percent year to date, and CEO Bill McDermott referred to its Applied AI FDE team. On November 2, Palantir CEO Alex Karp said the company really cannot satiate demand. All three framed the team in sales and delivery terms; none reported a number about what the field contributed to the product.

Why is the reporting line of the FDE org the scorecard?+

Because teams optimize for whatever their reporting line measures. In Milos Mandic's State of FDE 2026 survey, 24 respondents report into five different places. Antoine Buteau's May 2026 piece on where forward deployment reports makes the point directly: org design decides what forward deployment becomes. Under sales the team is measured on bookings, under services on utilization, under engineering on the quality of the harness and the code, and under product on what was generalized. The FDE's real output, the field finding that changes the roadmap, only gets counted on one of those four scorecards.

What is the loop the CPO owns, and where does the FDE sit in it?+

The loop runs from the customer through the spec, the build, the story, and adoption, and back to the customer. The forward deployed engineer sits at the customer end and the build end at once, which is why field signal is the product's fastest input. Design turns the customer's workflow into the spec, product marketing turns deployment proof into the story, and the CPO decides what gets built for everyone. When those four report to four different executives, the loop can't close fast enough to matter.

What are Falk's predictions for the FDE reporting line?+

Labeled as predictions, not facts. First, by the end of 2027 at least one public software company reports a field-to-product number on an earnings call, such as the share of roadmap items that started in a deployment, and that company will already have moved its FDE org under product. Second, the labs' Applied AI teams and the enterprise vendors' FDE teams converge on product leadership within two years, because the agents taking over the build work leave the human with judgment as the main output, and judgment about what to build is a product decision. Third, FDE orgs left under go-to-market will keep reporting engagements and time to value and will be the ones with rising FDE headcount per dollar of AI revenue.

What should a leader decide this quarter about the FDE org?+

Move the reporting line to the CPO before the next planning cycle, and change the scorecard with it: generalization rate per pod, the number of roadmap items that started in a deployment with dates, and the days from a field finding to a roadmap decision. Keep a dotted line to sales for the promise and to engineering for the harness. If the CPO can't name the product manager who reads every handback packet, fix that first, because a reporting line into a function that doesn't read the field is only a different box on the chart.

What is The FDE Transition series?+

A twice-weekly series on falkster.com for forward deployed engineers and the leaders building around them. Part 1 is the thesis that the FDE is the Product Builder arriving from services. Parts 2 through 14 cover the shortage, the handback, the codebase contract, the promise with sales, month two, the scorecard, the career, comp, the buyer's contract, when not to hire, the eval, the tooling, and the org at 100. Part 15 opens a new arc on the FDE org inside the product outcome org under the CPO.
