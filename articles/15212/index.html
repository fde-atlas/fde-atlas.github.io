Palantir Interview Process (2026): Decomposition, the Onsite Rounds and How to Prepare

Prepare.sh · 2026-10-06 · 6 min read

Summary

This guide breaks down Palantir's 2026 interview loop for Software Engineer and Forward Deployed Software Engineer candidates: a recruiter screen, a technical screen (HackerRank or live coding with SQL and API tasks), three one-hour onsite rounds, and a hiring manager interview. It highlights decomposition—turning a vague real-world problem into components, data models, and a scoped first version—as the round most likely to decide the outcome, especially for FDSE. It also covers learning and re-engineering rounds, behavioral expectations around mission conviction and failure stories, flat leveling with roughly $237K median total compensation per levels.fyi, and a four-week preparation plan.

Key points

  • The loop runs recruiter screen, technical screen, three onsite rounds, then hiring manager interview, about 3-4 weeks total.
  • Decomposition is widely seen as the decisive round: scope a shippable first version from a vague problem.
  • FDSE loops emphasize decomposition and customer thinking; SWE loops weight coding and system design more.
  • Behavioral rounds directly test comfort with government/defense work and require genuine failure stories.
  • levels.fyi shows one SWE level, ~$237K median total compensation in the US.

Palantir's most talked-about round has almost no code in it. In decomposition, you get a vague real-world problem and an hour to turn it into something a team could build, and candidate guides consistently call it the round that decides the loop. This guide is for engineers preparing for Palantir's Software Engineer and Forward Deployed Software Engineer (FDSE) loops. It uses Palantir's own Getting Hired pages wherever they say something concrete and candidate reports for the rest. Practice questions tagged to the company are in our Palantir question bank.

The loop, stage by stage

Palantir says the process is tailored: "The number and format of interviews that you'll go through will be personalized to your skillset and the type of role that you're looking for." The shape below is what candidate guides report most often.

Recruiter screen. About 30 minutes. Palantir's phone interview page asks you to be ready to talk about your background, experiences and interest in Palantir. One candidate guide says recruiters here "filter out more candidates than most companies do at this stage," so a vague "why Palantir" can end the process before any code.

Technical screen. An August 2026 candidate guide describes "a 90-minute HackerRank assessment or a live CodePair or Karat coding session." The HackerRank version, per the same guide, mixes a coding problem, a SQL query that joins and aggregates across tables, and a small REST API task. A 2022 new grad candidate report describes a three-part problem where each part builds on the last. Practise with Best Time to Buy and Sell Stock for the coding part and Filter With EXISTS and Query Utilizing BETWEEN and AND for the SQL part.

Onsite. Three one-hour rounds, drawn from a pool. One candidate guide reports that "Every onsite interview will have 20 minutes of behavioral questions," so each round is partly a motivation interview too. The rounds you may see:

  • Decomposition. A broad, real-world problem that you break into components, data models and APIs, then scope to a first version, per candidate guides. Palantir's own competency page describes the skill as tackling "technical challenges that are open-ended in nature, with multiple possible solutions that will incur different trade-offs," and advises: "Deliver a functioning idea first, then expand it afterwards." One guide calls it "probably the most important technical round you will face," with "a real focus on thinking about the end-user."
  • Learning. You read documentation for something unfamiliar and extend it in stages. One 2022 new grad report describes reaching the third of five tasks before time ran out, so pace matters. Create and Use a Composite Action is good practice for picking up an unfamiliar tool from its docs and using it quickly.
  • Re-engineering. Finding real bugs in unfamiliar code and ignoring red herrings. Palantir's matching competency is "The ability to work within the constraints and complexity of existing systems, codebases, and infrastructure." Guides disagree on the size of the codebase; one puts it at 80 to 250 lines. Diagnose Nginx CPU Bottleneck trains the same habit of reading a running system before changing it.
  • Coding and system design. Weighted more heavily for SWE than for FDSE, per candidate guides. Palantir's own guidance: "We will not ask you about tricky or obscure features of some specific programming language."

Hiring manager interview. About an hour. Candidate guides describe it as revisiting whatever the onsite panel was least sure about, then going deep on ownership and past failures.

SWE or FDSE: two kinds of engineer

Palantir says the interview process "varies based on the role you're most excited for (Forward-Deployed Software Engineer or Software Engineer)." It explains the difference in one line: "You can think of a Dev's focus as 'one capability, many customers,' while a Delta's focus is 'one customer, many capabilities.'"

For interviews, that shows up as emphasis. Candidate guides say decomposition is close to guaranteed for FDSE, while SWE loops put more weight on coding and system design. If you are applying for FDSE, rehearse decomposition first. If you are applying for SWE, do not skip it.

The behavioral side

Palantir's Getting Hired page says interviewers will ask "how you've executed, challenged yourself, motivated others, and made judgments" and about where you have failed, and that "We want to know what motivates the people we meet." Two things make Palantir's version different from most:

  • Mission conviction is tested directly. One candidate guide lists discomfort with government and defense work as a real reason for rejection, and another says technically strong candidates are turned down when their motivation reads as generic. Know which Palantir products (Gotham, Foundry, AIP) and which kinds of customers you would be working for, and be honest with yourself about it before the interview.
  • Failure stories are expected. Prepare a real failure, what you learned and what you changed, not a strength dressed up as a weakness.

levels.fyi gives a range of $190K to $265K a year and a median of $230K across 713 submissions. Palantir's own posting for Software Engineer, New Grad lists an estimated base salary range of $145,000 to $155,000 a year. Expect the level conversation to be about scope and team rather than a level code.

How to prepare

  • Week 1: the technical screen. Timed sets that mix one coding problem, one SQL join-and-aggregate query and one small API task, so switching between them feels normal.
  • Week 2: decomposition. Take three messy prompts (disaster response logistics, a hospital's bed capacity, fraud signals across bank systems) and for each: ask clarifying questions, name the users, sketch the data model and APIs, and cut it to a first version you could ship in weeks. Say it out loud, on a timer.
  • Week 3: learning and re-engineering. Pick an unfamiliar open-source library, read its docs for 10 minutes and extend it. Then read 200 lines of someone else's code and find the bugs before running it.
  • Week 4: motivation and failures. Write down why Palantir, specifically, including how you feel about its government work, and two failure stories with real stakes.

Why candidates don't get the offer

Palantir does not publish rejection reasons. Patterns from candidate guides published in 2025 and 2026:

  • A weak decomposition round: jumping into code, no clarifying questions, no plan for a first version (candidate guides name decomposition among the top reasons).
  • Generic motivation, or visible discomfort with Palantir's government and defense customers (candidate guides).
  • A thin recruiter screen, which one candidate guide describes as a stricter filter than at most companies.

FAQ

What is the Palantir decomposition interview? A round where you break an open-ended, real-world problem into components, data models and APIs and scope a first version. Palantir describes the skill as handling problems "with multiple possible solutions that will incur different trade-offs."

How long does the Palantir process take? Palantir says it usually responds within a week or two after each interview. Candidate guides put the whole loop at three to four weeks.

Is the onsite virtual? It can be. Palantir's Getting Hired page refers to being invited on site "in-person or virtually."

Which programming languages can I use? Palantir accepts most languages "as long as they're imperative."

Do I pick my team? Not up front. Palantir says "Team placement at Palantir is heavily contingent on the information gathered during the interview process," including your interests and what the business needs.

Is FDSE easier than SWE? Not easier, differently weighted. FDSE loops lean on decomposition and customer thinking; SWE loops lean more on coding and system design.
