What's the difference between a Forward Deployed Engineer and consulting?

Original title: 前线部署工程师和咨询有什么区别

FDE Times · 2026-10-18 · 5 min read

Key Takeaways

  • OpenAI has established a subsidiary dedicated to embedding engineers inside enterprises; FDE services have become an official business unit.
  • The risk is the slide: traditional services companies have gross margins of 30-40%, while Palantir, despite doing extensive on-site deployments, maintains a gross margin above 80%.
  • The dividing line between FDE work and consulting is the feedback loop to the product; you should always clarify this point in interviews.

In May 2026, OpenAI launched the OpenAI Deployment Company. This subsidiary is dedicated to embedding engineers directly inside enterprises, starting with over $4 billion in committed capital. Decrypt's headline pulled no punches: OpenAI just launched a consulting arm.

Gergely Orosz, author of The Pragmatic Engineer, sees a bigger trend: many newly emerging Forward Deployed Engineer (FDE) roles are housed in semi-independent companies, separated from the organizations building AI products, looking more like consulting or systems integration.

This raises a question for every FDE and aspiring FDE: is the services work you're doing strengthening the product, or are you just selling hours under a different name? The answer lies not in the job title on your offer, but in whether what you learn on the frontline flows back into the product.

Why would software companies take on services work?

a16z is blunt about the cost: customizing for each customer initially drags down gross margin and accelerates burn rate. It cites two familiar names as examples. At IPO, ServiceNow's gross margin was 63.2%, and Workday's was only 54.1%, both far below the software industry's ideal of around 80%.

The gap didn't last. According to a16z, by 2024, ServiceNow reached 79% and Workday reached 75%. In other words, ServiceNow recovered 15.8 percentage points and Workday recovered 20.9 percentage points.

TSIA, a research firm focused on the technology services industry, puts the trade precisely: short-term gross margin for long-term defensibility. It adds one condition: turning one-off deployments into reusable templates.

This condition explains why gross margins can recover: custom work only becomes an asset when it doesn't have to be redone for the next customer.

The slope toward consulting

The investment fund N47 focuses on the other side of this argument. It points out that traditional services companies have gross margins of 30-40%. N47 predicts that many companies trying the FDE model will gradually slide into consulting businesses, merely wearing the cloak of a software company.

The key lies in the counterexample N47 provides. Palantir, the company most closely associated with the FDE model, maintains a gross margin above 80% despite doing extensive on-site deployments. So the gap between 30-40% and above 80% does not depend on how many engineers are sent to customers.

In N47's view, the healthy model is to systematically flow frontline innovations back into the product's core capabilities. This is exactly the difference between Palantir and an outsourcing company with lots of engineers.

Therefore, behind the same job title may lie two very different jobs. Imagine the first company: you write a connector, a prompt, or a data pipeline for Customer A, the product team packages it, and Customer B can use it right away.

At the second company, you rewrite everything from scratch for each customer, and the value you create boils down to how many days you put in.

The dividing line is the feedback loop

Several independent sources arrive at the same criterion. KDnuggets writes that without a feedback loop from the frontline to the product, the role is just consulting.

Valletta Software puts it differently: the obligation to flow customer-specific work back into the product is exactly what distinguishes an FDE from a consultant; if no one is responsible for this obligation, "FDE" is just a label.

The table below organizes these signals into two extremes for easy comparison. In practice, a company may exhibit traits from both columns.

Which column is OpenAI in?

From Orosz's perspective, OpenAI placing its deployment business in a separate subsidiary is a signal worth watching: organizational distance from the product team is exactly why feedback loops tend to break.

But a separate legal entity does not necessarily mean a broken loop. The key is whether there is a process for bringing frontline work back into the product.

A pure deployment company receiving such a large amount of committed capital shows that the services model is attracting investment at scale. For companies that have already chosen this path, the question worth asking is: where does the frontline work ultimately go.

For job seekers, this is where you need to look carefully. If at the company you're about to join, what FDEs build and learn on the frontline never flows back into the product, then even with "engineer" in the job title, your career path will look more like a consultant's.

Which skills put you in the right-hand column?

The core skill is spotting which part of a customer-specific need can be shared. Imagine you're tasked with writing a script to clean order data for a retail chain.

An average engineer stops once the script runs. An excellent engineer extracts the customer-specific configuration, writes tests, and submits a proposal to the product team—the very approach TSIA describes as turning one-off deployments into templates.

Your resume should work the same way. Instead of writing "deployed a solution for Customer X," explain which of the things you built for one customer became components reused by other customers, or shortened the next deployment.

In interviews, there are two ready-made questions. KDnuggets suggests asking: who on the product team reads the FDE's frontline experience, and how often. Valletta suggests asking: who specifically is responsible for turning customer-specific work into product features.

If the interviewer can't answer either question, you know which column you're facing. KDnuggets still believes that as long as this feedback loop exists, FDE is a good career choice. So what you need to do is pick a place with a loop, and after each project leave something behind that makes the next customer's deployment faster.
