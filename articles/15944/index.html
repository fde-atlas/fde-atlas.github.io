The Frontier Doctrine: Stop Scaling the Intermediary

Michael Couch | couch.cx · 2026-11-09 · 6 min read

Summary

Michael Couch argues that forward-deployed engineering risks becoming a faster, pricier middleman if it merely embeds elite engineers to build software for enterprises. Citing McKinsey data (80% report individual AI productivity gains but only 37% see earnings impact), Google's DORA findings, and BCG's research on citizen builders, he frames the core problem as the "Proxy Tax"—friction from handing domain knowledge across boundaries. He proposes that every pod must leave three artifacts: a production system, reusable modular assets, and an empowered internal operator. A 90-day Dependency Reduction Test measures success via targets like sub-14-day changes and over 50% of routine changes shipped independently by the domain team.

Key points

  • FDE's purpose is making enterprises independent, not indispensable.
  • The "Proxy Tax" is friction from translating domain knowledge across handoffs.
  • Pods must leave a production system, reusable assets, and capable operators.
  • A 90-day test requires changes live in under 14 days and 2x–3x returns.
  • Technology should disappear into institutional capability, not scale intermediaries.

The purpose of a forward-deployed engineering team is not to become indispensable. It is to make the enterprise increasingly independent of it. The next generation of technology teams will be judged not by how many projects they ship, but by how much more capable the people around them become. The team is not the product. The capability it leaves behind is.

The enterprise technology conversation has arrived at a credible operating model, but it is dangerously close to missing the real point.

Across the market, forward-deployed engineering (FDE) is being heralded as the ultimate modern delivery mechanism. OpenAI describes embedded engineers sitting beside enterprise operators, discovering workflows, and reaching production. Palantir has long deployed elite software engineers directly into customer trenches to own operational outcomes. McKinsey promotes an "agentic mission factory" for redesigning business workflows with reusable capabilities.

The consensus seems settled: assemble small, multidisciplinary pods that combine strategic direction, engineering depth, domain context, and operational accountability.

It sounds formidable. But a skeptical executive could look at that description and ask a devastating question: Isn't this just agile product delivery, repackaged with faster AI tools and more expensive engineers?

They would have a point.

If all we have done is take the traditional engineering team, arm them with LLMs and code assistants, and embed them inside a business unit to build software faster, we have not transformed the enterprise. We have simply created a more agile, more expensive middleman.

The radical proposition is not that engineers can build faster. It is that forward-deployed engineering must dismantle the intermediary layer entirely, rather than become its latest and most elite incarnation.

The productivity paradox: Why this is the defining moment

The empirical evidence of this tension is no longer theoretical; it is visible across every major enterprise benchmark in late 2026:

  • McKinsey’s October 2026 Findings: While 80% of surveyed corporate respondents report measurable improvements in individual productivity from AI tooling, only 37% report any positive impact on company earnings, and a mere 6% qualify as enterprise "AI high performers." Individual speed is not translating into organizational value.
  • Google’s DORA Research: The State of AI-Assisted Software Development report proved that AI acts strictly as an amplifier of an organization's existing culture and delivery systems. If an enterprise has broken feedback loops, politicized governance, and disconnected operators, AI simply accelerates the generation of unmaintainable software.
  • BCG’s Emerging Roles: BCG's research documents the emergence of "citizen builders" and "domain anchors." The industry recognizes that domain experts are beginning to build, proving that empowering the frontline is no longer a fringe theory.

The inescapable conclusion is this: We are getting dramatically better at producing software while remaining comparatively poor at changing how an organization actually works.

That is the real frontier. It is not about pretraining another model, writing another prompt framework, or staging a more dazzling keynote demo. It is about fundamentally re-architecting the relationship between technology, the people who understand the work, and the organizational systems that enable them to act.

Our real enemy is the Proxy Tax

For forty years, enterprise IT maintained a system where people who understand the work were forced to explain it to people who don't, so that those people could write a specification for someone else, who could commission a stranger to build something that almost works.

I have called this the Proxy Tax: the compound friction of lost context, diluted intent, and bureaucratic delay that an organization pays every time domain knowledge is handed across a corporate boundary.

Result: Serial handoffs, lost exceptions, 6-month release cycles, and expensive software that operators bypass with shadow spreadsheets.

Result: Zero translation latency. The operator shapes the tool directly. Working software in days, continuously improved, and permanently owned by the domain.

AI changes the cost of generating syntax. But if our only response is to insert a new caste of elite AI engineers who sit between the operator and the system, we have simply made the middleman faster.

The true objective of a frontier pod is to eliminate the middleman entirely: to pair the person who understands the domain directly with an engineer who can translate intent into durable capability, while coaching the operator to take over the reins.

The three non-negotiables: What every pod must leave behind

How do you know if an FDE pod was genuinely transformative or merely an expensive contractor burst?

At couch.cx, we enforce a strict doctrine. Every serious Frontier Pod engagement must leave behind three permanent artifacts:

Something measurably better for a real human, customer, or workflow. Not a slide deck, and not a prototype in a sandbox. An application or automated system operating in production against live enterprise traffic, tested against real edge-case exceptions, and audited against an empirical baseline.

A modular component, data connector, evaluation dataset, prompt harness, or standardized skill that lives in version control. Something that future teams can import directly, ensuring that subsequent transformations are faster, cheaper, and safer.

Someone inside the client organization who can now diagnose, adapt, evaluate, improve, or independently operate what was built. A pod can ship an extraordinary technical deployment, but if the client remains helpless without them next month, the pod has failed the third test.

We are not claiming that every accountant, underwriter, or marketer should become a full-time software maintainer. Nor are we claiming that specialized systems engineers are unnecessary.

We are arguing that every engagement must deliberately expand the enterprise's sovereignty: expanding its capacity to act, experiment, and decide without waiting in an external agency's queue or an internal IT backlog.

The Dependency Reduction Test: A 90-day proof

To ensure this does not remain an academic manifesto, we subject every frontier engagement to an empirical 90-day litmus test: The Dependency Reduction Test.

Select a single consequential operational domain with an executive sponsor and a dedicated Domain Champion. Record the baseline, deploy the pod for an eight-week residency, establish the production harness, and measure whether the business can carry the work forward without external intervention.

Measure Operational Indicator Target Milestone
Time to Useful Change Shrinking the distance between identifying a problem and deploying a fix < 14 days to live operational trial
Verified Operational Benefit Audited cost removal, cycle-time collapse, or revenue acceleration Clear 2x–3x hurdle rate on residency cost
Handoff Elimination Direct reduction of the Proxy Tax and translation layers Zero intermediary PMs or analysts between champion and code
Independent Domain Capability Subsequent workflow tweaks initiated and shipped by the domain team > 50% of routine changes made without external support
Reuse & Production Quality Zero unmanaged technical debt or compliance violations Formal SRE/maintainer sign-off & automated evals running

The goal is not zero specialist dependency. In complex enterprise architectures with legacy transaction backbones and strict regulatory audits, that would be foolish.

The goal is eliminating avoidable dependency—the structural friction that exists only to justify an external retainer or preserve internal bureaucratic empires.

The human ending: When technology disappears into the craft

The most powerful version of this thesis is not about turning every company into a software company.

A retailer should not aspire to be a software company; a retailer should aspire to be an exceptional retailer that understands its customers with uncanny intimacy. A bank should not aspire to be an AI lab; a bank should aspire to underwrite risk, allocate capital, and protect depositors with effortless precision. A healthcare system should aspire to deliver compassionate, unhurried patient care.

The technology should not announce itself with fanfare. It should disappear into the human capability of the institution.

We do not want human beings working for the transformation programme. We want the transformation programme to work for them.

Stop scaling the intermediary. Start scaling the capability. The enterprise that learns how to transform itself from within is the only one that will endure.
