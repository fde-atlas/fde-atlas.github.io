Organization and Business Model · OpenFDE

Original title: 组织与商业模式 · OpenFDE

OpenFDE · 前沿部署工程师社区 · 2026-10-16 · 4 min read

Summary

This article frames FDE as an organizational design question rather than a single role, arguing that teams must both structure how engineers reach customer sites and resolve the tension between deep customization and scalable gross margins. It describes five organizational patterns (platform-based Delta/Echo pairs, model labs like OpenAI and Anthropic, cloud providers like Google Cloud, standalone deployment companies, and founders-as-FDEs), and cites Bob McGrew's Palantir retrospective: success means growing contract sizes and accumulating product leverage, not maximizing customization per customer. It closes with seven mechanisms to keep FDE from becoming an outsourced services team.

Key points

  • FDE fails into low-margin services if structure or margin questions are answered wrong.
  • Five patterns: platform pairs, model labs, cloud providers, deployment companies, founder-led teams.
  • Productization threshold: deep custom work for first 1–3 clients, then decreasing customization.
  • FDE KPIs differ from SaaS: growing contracts plus product leverage, not less work per client.
  • Seven mechanisms include quarterly reuse reviews, RFC channels, scope governance, and rotation.

FDE is not an isolated role, but a form of organizational design. It has to answer two questions at once: what team structure to use to send engineers into customer environments, and how to avoid being dragged down between "deep customization" and "scalable gross margin". Get either one wrong, and FDE degenerates into low-margin project-based services.

The FDE of the AI era must be an AI-native team/organization. The five forms below are only structural shells—what really determines success or failure is whether this team's own way of doing discovery, writing code, shipping, and feeding back learnings is AI-native: using agents to compress delivery cycles, using evals to control quality, and continuously feeding field signals back into models and products. Otherwise, no matter how beautiful the org chart is, it's just a fresh coat of paint on the traditional SI.

Five organizational models

Different companies have evolved five typical FDE organizational forms based on their products, customers, and business models.

A strong platform as the foundation, with FDSE (Delta) responsible for on-site customization and engineering implementation, and Deployment Strategist (Echo) responsible for business, product, relationships, and expansion. The advantages are fast customer value creation, strong field feedback, and the platform continuously absorbing frontline experience; the risks are heavy dependence on talent density, high service costs, and easy accumulation of customization debt.

The form of OpenAI, Anthropic, and Mistral: Research provides model capabilities, Product provides APIs and platforms, FDE deploys those capabilities into customer scenarios, and field feedback flows back to models and products. Suited to environments where model capabilities change rapidly—real customer problems expose platform gaps fastest.

The form of Google Cloud: landing Gemini / Vertex AI / agent capabilities into customer production environments on the cloud platform, connecting cloud infrastructure, data warehouses, existing systems, agents / RAG / workflows, and security governance. Here the FDE is more like a production-grade agent / enterprise integration engineer.

A new form that emerged in 2026: model companies scaling deployment capabilities with independent or semi-independent deployment service organizations. OpenAI's Deployment Company embeds FDEs to reach large numbers of enterprises, while Anthropic's enterprise AI services company focuses on bringing Claude into the core operations of mid-sized enterprises. In essence, it answers "AI capabilities change too fast, and traditional SIs and customer IT can't keep up".

Many AI startups have no dedicated FDEs early on, but the founders and early engineers are effectively doing FDE work: joining discovery, writing custom code, integrating data, building PoCs, shipping, collecting feedback, improving the product, and then replicating it for the next customer. As Bob McGrew said, FDE training is essentially founder training.

The core tension: services or software?

Customers need deep customization, but software companies need scalable gross margin.

The entire organizational design of the FDE model exists to resolve this tension. Its benefits are tangible: finding real needs faster, proving ROI faster, winning large customers more easily, shortening sales cycles, strengthening stickiness, and distilling deployment experience into product. But the risks are equally fatal—

High labor costs, gross margin lower than pure software, custom code that is hard to maintain, scope that easily spirals out of control, engineers consumed by customer support, and field teams siloed from core product teams—ultimately becoming "a fake product company, a real consulting company". a16z repeatedly warns: if field experience cannot be distilled into compounding product value, FDE will slide into a services business; Bob McGrew's advice is more direct—if you don't absolutely have to do FDE, don't, because you'll most likely end up doing services.

The productization threshold and the right KPIs

The key to resolving the tension is the idea of a "productization threshold": do deep customization for the first 1–3 customers, and after that each customer's degree of customization must decrease. When reviewing Palantir, Bob McGrew offered a counterintuitive but crucial criterion—

Under the FDE model, success is measured not by "how much customization was done for each customer", but by two things: first, the value of the outcomes you deliver (usually reflected in ever-growing contract sizes); second, whether you are gaining more and more product leverage to amplify those outcomes. In other words, each customer's contract gets bigger because you are delivering increasingly valuable outcomes, and good products mean the FDE doesn't have to pull in three more engineers every time. Overseas Unicorn↗

This is the exact opposite of standard SaaS: SaaS pursues "doing less and less work per customer with constant contract size"; FDE pursues "ever-larger contract sizes with a constant degree of customization", because you are doing increasingly valuable work. The highly customizable general-purpose product underneath (such as Palantir Ontology) is precisely the leverage that makes all of this possible.

7 mechanisms to keep FDE from becoming an outsourcing team

The most dangerous organizational pattern is: sales promises a pile of customization, FDEs frantically fight fires, and the product team absorbs nothing. Healthy organizations use mechanisms to institutionalize "productization".

At the end of every project, you must decide what should be productized.

Every quarter, mandatorily distill connectors, templates, evals, and playbooks.

FDEs can submit RFCs directly to the product team, giving field feedback a formal entry point.

Custom code must have maintenance and replacement plans to avoid endlessly accumulating technical debt.

Sales cannot endlessly promise FDE customization; scope needs governance.

Look not only at revenue, but also at reuse rates and deployment efficiency.

FDEs and core product engineers rotate periodically to prevent field and product silos.

A mature field squad is not a lone hero, but typically includes an FDE lead, FDSWE / full-stack, applied AI / ML engineers, a technical deployment lead / strategist, a solution architect or cloud specialist, and customer success / adoption owners, plus security / data engineers / PMs when necessary. The prototype of this organizational model comes from Palantir's Echo / Delta origins; for how it collaborates with PMs within the product organization and avoids being treated as a "do-everything customization team", see The value of FDEs to each role.
