U.S. Midterm Elections and State AI Regulation Laws: Organizing the Impact on Japanese Companies and Practical Responses from an FDE Perspective

Original title: 米中間選挙とAI規制州法：日本企業への影響と実務対応をFDE視点で整理

FDE総研 · 2026-11-05 · 12 min read

Summary

This article examines how the 2026 US midterms could affect state AI laws, from a forward deployed engineer's perspective. Colorado, California, and Texas all passed AI laws before the election; the realistic impact lies in amendments during 2027 legislative sessions. The three laws differ in scope: Colorado's SB26-189 covers automated decision-making in consequential decisions, California's SB 53 targets large frontier model developers, and Texas's TRAIGA bans specific uses. Federal preemption remains unsettled, so the article analyzes four scenarios and their implications for Japanese companies. It recommends building evidence practices—impact assessments, input data provenance, and human review records—that hold under any scenario, and offers guidance by industry, market-entry model, experience level, and English ability. FDEs should design regulation-agnostic solutions and leave legal interpretations to lawyers.

Key points

  • The three state AI laws passed before the election; changes will likely come via 2027 session amendments.
  • Colorado regulates ADMT in consequential decisions, California targets frontier developers, Texas bans specific uses.
  • Federal preemption is unresolved; the article maps four scenarios with different compliance burdens.
  • Impact assessments, data provenance, and human review records are required across regimes and worth building first.
  • FDEs should design for any scenario and defer legal judgments to lawyers.

How will state AI regulation laws change after the U.S. midterms? How do Colorado, California, and Texas compare?

The AI laws of Colorado, California, and Texas were all enacted before the election. Colorado replaced the old law SB24-205 with SB26-189 in May 2026. What could change as a result of the election is the "timing of enforcement" and "amendments" of each law.

At FDE Soken, when assessing the impact of the election, we separate "laws already enacted" from "parts that could be amended in the next session." In the November 3, 2026 election, state legislators and governors were also up for election in many states. New state legislative sessions begin in January 2027 in most states. Therefore, it is realistic to assume that the practical impact will appear as amendment bills introduced in the 2027 session. Note that this article does not make definitive claims about the election results and legislative composition. As of 12:00 PM JST on November 5, 2026 (10:00 PM ET on November 4), based on major reports we could confirm (CNBC updated at 8:15 PM, CBS News at 10:22 PM ET), the Democrats are projected to hold majorities in both the Senate and the House. In the Senate, the Democrats hold at least 54 seats and the Republicans at least 42, with two seats undecided: Alaska and Mississippi (in Mississippi, Democratic candidate Mr. Colom leads by a narrow margin, with the vote difference reported as ranging from 65 votes to about 1,000 votes depending on the source). In the House, the Democrats hold at least 250 seats (CNBC), and CBS News estimates a final count of 251-268 seats, with 9 districts where AP has not yet declared a winner. All figures are preliminary and not final. Please confirm final seat counts with the latest reports, such as from the Associated Press.

What are the differences among the AI laws of Colorado, California, and Texas?

The biggest difference among the three state laws is their scope. Colorado targets developers and deployers using automated decision-making technology (ADMT) for consequential decisions, California targets large-scale frontier developers, and Texas targets prohibited uses.

After the election, which of the three state laws is most likely to change?

Colorado shows the most movement. The old law SB24-205, after an enforcement delay and litigation, was replaced by SB26-189 in May 2026. Going forward, the focus will be on the Attorney General's rulemaking and the outcome of xAI's lawsuit. Further amendment bills could emerge in the 2027 session, but no basis for this has been confirmed at this time.

In California, in addition to SB 53, the California Privacy Protection Agency (CPPA) has established Automated Decision-Making Technology (ADMT) regulations. These regulations took effect on January 1, 2026, with compliance for businesses using ADMT required from January 1, 2027. In the areas of HR and credit, these regulations may have a greater impact on Japanese companies. Texas's TRAIGA uses a format that enumerates prohibited uses. Until post-enforcement operational guidance is issued, it is prudent to assume there is room for interpretation. In addition, law firm commentary reports that there is a safe harbor under which liability is not imposed if one is substantially compliant with NIST's AI RMF (including the Generative AI Profile) (secondary source; verify against the statutory text).

How do federal preemption debates affect Japanese companies?

Preemption, where federal law overrides state law, is not yet settled in legislation. The impact depends on Executive Order 14365, signed on December 11, 2025, and on how legislation proceeds in Congress.

The developments so far fall into three stages.

  • On July 1, 2025, the Senate removed, by a vote of 99 to 1, the provision for a temporary freeze (moratorium) on state AI laws from the budget bill (H.R.1).
  • On December 11, 2025, Executive Order 14365, "Ensuring a National Policy Framework for Artificial Intelligence," was signed. It includes establishing a task force within the Department of Justice to challenge state laws, and having the Department of Commerce evaluate and identify "burdensome state laws." The DOJ task force was reportedly established in January 2026. Whether the Commerce Department's evaluation has been published is unclear, as reports conflict.
  • In 2026, the White House presented a framework in March, and draft bills have also emerged in Congress. However, no federal law establishing preemption has been enacted. The debate is expected to continue after the midterm elections, but how it will be resolved remains undecided.

An executive order alone does not invalidate state laws. The validity of state laws will ultimately be decided by the courts and Congress.

How should FDE read the preemption debate?

What FDE should take from the preemption debate is "a design that works under whichever obligations remain." Not predicting which state laws will survive.

In implementation settings, customer projects do not stop while waiting for legal conclusions. Impact assessments, the provenance of input data, and records of human review are three elements required in common by the old Colorado law, the NIST AI RMF, and Japan's AI Business Guidelines. Building these three points into the design from the start minimizes rework under any scenario.

What should Japanese companies address first, by industry and mode of market entry?

The first thing Japanese companies should decide is "which decisions, for whom, in which states, to delegate to AI." FDE Soken uses the definition of "consequential decisions" in Colorado SB26-189 as the initial criterion.

What changes by mode of market entry (direct sales, distributors, local subsidiaries)?

What changes by mode of entry is whether your company qualifies as a "developer" or a "deployer." Since obligations differ by role, clearly stating the allocation of responsibility in contracts is the starting point.

  • Direct SaaS sales: Your company may act as both developer and deployer. Confirm the obligations of both positions.
  • Via distributors or OEM: Local partners are often the deployers, so prepare the documentation provided as a developer (model limitations, intended uses).
  • Local subsidiary: Whether you are doing business in a given state becomes clearer. Share a state-by-state compliance table with local counsel.

Design principle: From the point of designing AI features domestically, include "if offered to US customers, will we be the developer or the deployer?" as a requirements field. This is a general perspective: doing so makes it easier to reduce the burden of redoing contracts and documentation when deciding on overseas expansion.

How you prepare for AI regulatory compliance is determined more by your current role and English ability than by years of experience. FDE Soken uses the NIST AI RMF as a common language to divide the stages.

How does preparation change by English ability and company size?

Even without strong English skills, you can start regulatory compliance if you can read legal provisions and official documents. What changes by company size is how to build the organizational structure.

  • English proficiency: You can grasp the gist of state law texts even with machine translation. However, defined terms like "consequential decision" and ADMT should be verified in the original text. It has been reported that "reasonable care" under the old Colorado law is treated differently under the new law. Discussing matters with local customers and lawyers will often require at least intermediate-level English proficiency.
  • Large enterprises: Build a cross-functional team of legal, security, and business units, with the FDE responsible for technical evidence.
  • SMEs and startups: Rather than assigning a dedicated person, it is more realistic to first create minimal documentation focused on the states and use cases of key customers.

Who should handle US AI regulatory compliance—FDE, SWE, IT consultant, PdM, or SIer? Who is not suited for it?

In responding to US AI regulations, the FDE connects the customer's frontline with technical evidence. The value of this role lies in the Palantir-style FDE, where the same person handles both requirements and implementation. Palantir

Who is not suited for regulatory compliance work as an FDE?

Those not suited for regulatory compliance work as an FDE are people who cannot start working until specifications are finalized, and people who take on legal judgments themselves.

Discussions on state law amendments and preemption are expected to continue beyond the 2027 legislative session. Choose a "design that works under any scenario" while uncertainty remains, and pass legal judgments to lawyers and supervisors. People who can draw this line well are more likely to earn customer trust as an FDE. Conversely, an attitude of trying to settle statutory interpretation on one's own poses a risk to both customers and one's own company.
