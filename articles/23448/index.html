What is a Forward Deployed Engineer? The difference between FDEs and consulting projects

Original title: 前线部署工程师是什么？FDE 与咨询项目的区别

FDE Times · 2026-11-09 · 5 min read

Key takeaways

  • Google Cloud will train up to 1,000 FDEs for Accenture and certify these teams, but the new organization will be owned by Accenture.
  • Forrester defines an FDE team as being accountable for outcomes, not projects; SD Times sums up the gap between the two as weeks versus months or even years.
  • For engineers, the title doesn't tell you much. Ask clearly: who is accountable for outcomes, who writes the code, and is the scope locked down in the contract.

In September 2026, Google Cloud committed to training up to 1,000 Forward Deployed Engineers (FDEs) for Accenture to serve enterprise customers. The more notable detail lies elsewhere: a Google spokesperson told TechCrunch that the organization will be owned by Accenture, not Google.

The official announcement from the two companies uses stronger wording: the two sides will "form" a 1,000-person FDE team. According to CIO Dive, Accenture's Gemini deployment teams will be certified by Google Cloud as part of the FDE certification process.

If you're working as an FDE, or want to be one, this has practical implications. The title used to be associated with software companies like Palantir; now a consulting firm is replicating it at a scale of thousands, complete with a platform vendor's certificate.

Before accepting such a job, you need to answer one question: is this still FDE work?

One consulting firm, three vendors

The Google partnership is not the first step. Forrester notes that Accenture launched an FDE practice with Microsoft on March 18, and joined ServiceNow's FDE program at its Knowledge conference in May. In Forrester's view, this shows service companies taking over the FDE model.

In other words, in less than six months, the same consulting firm has attached the FDE title to three different vendors. These FDEs follow the platform: Microsoft FDE, ServiceNow FDE, Gemini FDE.

A Google certification can show how familiar someone is with Gemini, but not whether that person is accountable for outcomes on the customer side. And it is precisely that accountability that defines the role.

The wording matters too. The media wrote that Google will "train" up to 1,000 FDEs; the announcement says the two sides will "form" a 1,000-person team.

These are not the same thing. "Training" refers to a course; "forming" refers to an organization. Titles can be handed out after graduation, but the way of working requires an organization.

Where is the real dividing line

Forrester offers a brief definition: FDE teams are accountable for outcomes, not projects. The role originated at Palantir, and a member of its community forum used the same phrasing: working deep inside the customer's site and committing to outcomes.

Tech Wire Asia describes the day-to-day work more concretely: FDEs work directly with customer teams, including connecting models to enterprise data and existing systems. SD Times puts it more bluntly: FDEs build the required solutions with their own hands.

Putting these descriptions together yields a set of criteria you can use to evaluate any role with the FDE title, no matter who the employer is:

DimensionProject consultingOutcome-accountable FDE
What is deliveredA project (Forrester)An outcome the team commits to (Forrester, Palantir)
SpeedMonths or even years before a solution is seen (SD Times)Value within weeks, with the product ready for production (SD Times)
Proof of completionThe project has been handed overThe product runs on the customer's real data and systems

This table doesn't judge which is better. It shows two fundamentally different ways of organizing work, and the same title can be attached to either.

Same project, two delivery models

Imagine a bank that wants an AI agent to classify customer complaints and route them to the right department. In the project model, you first spend a few weeks gathering requirements, then the team develops to the specification, runs through the pre-agreed acceptance criteria, completes the handover, and moves on to the next contract.

The problem is that these criteria were written before anyone saw how the agent handles real complaints. The agent can pass every criterion on paper and still route the hardest complaints to the wrong department—and those are exactly the ones customers care about most. By then the team is already gone, because the contract scoped the work by deliverables.

In the FDE model, a small team takes on a measurable goal, such as the percentage of complaints routed to the correct department on the first try.

They run the agent on real data and see where it fails. Then they revise prompts, fix data pipelines, write evals for the failing cases, and repeat the loop weekly. The work is only done when that number reaches the committed level.

Both approaches can use the same technology, the same vendor certificates, the same title. The decisive difference is who is accountable for the final number—and that's exactly what you need to clarify before signing an employment contract.

Is consulting the enemy of FDE work?

Consulting skills don't dilute the FDE role. Another member of the Palantir community forum describes the FDE as a combination of software engineer and consultant, working directly with customers. The consulting component has been part of the definition from the start.

Forrester goes further, calling the FDE a temporary "prosthesis" for organizations whose operating models haven't caught up yet.

In other words, even the role itself may be just a transitional phase. Once that phase passes, what remains is the ability to drive systems to real outcomes, not the title.

So the risk isn't consulting firms hiring FDEs; it's the "engineer" half being cut away, leaving only the "consultant" half with a new title. If you can genuinely do both, that's a major advantage at any type of company.

How to read an FDE job posting

If the posting comes from a consulting firm, don't just look at the title or the certificate name. Look for engineering verbs in the responsibilities: build, deploy to production, write evals, connect models to customer data.

If the whole paragraph is about workshops, requirements gathering, and writing reports, you already have your answer.

In the interview, four questions will reveal what the job really is. Who is accountable for a project's success metrics? Does the FDE commit code to systems running in production? How soon will the customer see the first result? Is the scope locked in the contract from the start, or adjusted after the team touches real data?

The answers don't have to be perfect. A team with clear metrics that writes its own code and shows customers value within weeks is a good sign, whether the employer is a consulting firm or a software company.

A team that works off a requirements document for months and leaves after the handover is doing consulting projects, no matter what the job posting says.

The same thinking applies to your resume. "Built a chatbot for the customer service department" is just a deliverable. "Raised first-contact resolution from A to B in N weeks," backed by your real data, is an outcome.

Google can certify 1,000 people in a single training round. But no one can certify accountability for results: you can only prove it with the numbers you've carried yourself.
