What Is an AI Assessment? Designing Task Inventories and ROI

Original title: AIアセスメントとは？業務棚卸しと投資対効果の設計

FDX株式会社 · 2026-08-19 · 15 min read

Summary

This article by FDX argues that stalled AI adoption stems from doing things in the wrong order: companies introduce tools or training before inventorying business processes and quantifying hours, so they can neither pick where AI applies nor measure results. It proposes a three-layer AI assessment: converting tasks into work hours (12 recorded fields, annual-hours formula), judging AI feasibility and reduction rates (considering generative AI, SaaS, then custom development in that order), and calculating investment cost and payback period. It lists five failure patterns and a one-day assessment template, then describes FDX's 1Day AI assessment (100,000 yen, up to 100 tasks) and 1Day FDE service (300,000–400,000 yen monthly), plus a case achieving roughly 75% reduction in document-creation hours.

Key points

  • Assess processes and hours before choosing AI tools.
  • Three layers: task inventory, AI feasibility, ROI calculation.
  • Consider generative AI, then SaaS, then development, in order.
  • Reduction rates should be ranges based on similar cases.
  • FDX offers a one-day assessment covering up to 100 tasks.

The reason AI adoption stalls lies not in tools or talent, but in "sequence." If you start with tool deployment or training without first taking inventory of business processes and quantifying the time they require, you cannot decide where AI should be applied, nor can you measure the impact afterward.

An AI assessment is the process that corrects this sequence. It identifies business processes, converts them into work hours, determines which processes can be replaced by which AI, and even calculates the investment amount and payback period. This article explains the three-layer structure used in practice, the specific items in each layer, and a template for completing the assessment in a single day.

Who This Article Is For

  • IT and corporate planning staff who have deployed generative AI tools but cannot explain the reduction benefits in numbers
  • Those who need to attach ROI evidence to budget approval requests for AI initiatives
  • Promotion leads who cannot prioritize AI-automation requests coming from the field

What Is an AI Assessment? (3 Key Points)

  • Definition: A process that takes inventory of business processes, quantifies work hours, and determines which processes can be replaced by which AI/digital means, including investment amount and payback period
  • Purpose: To replace "putting AI in place somehow" with "if we put X into process Y at cost Z, annual hours will drop by W"
  • Deliverable: A diagnostic report listing, for each process, annual required hours, AI feasibility, estimated hours saved, implementation cost, and payback period

It is important to note that an AI assessment is not tool selection. Tool selection is the final step; before that comes the task of reordering "which processes should be addressed in the first place" using work hours as a common unit.

Why Inventory Must Come Before Tool Deployment

Problems That Start When Everything Is Left to Individuals

According to the Shoko Chukin survey "Survey on SME Use of Generative AI" (January 2026), 64.9% of companies leave generative AI use to individuals. Leaving it to individuals works in the sense of broadening the initial base of users. The problem is that things cannot progress beyond that point.

Because the company does not know who is using AI, on which processes, and how much, it cannot aggregate the benefits. Because it cannot aggregate benefits, it cannot make the next investment decision. As a result, a state becomes entrenched where tool contracts continue but no results are reported.

What Happens When You Get the "Sequence" Wrong

Deployments that skip the inventory step get stuck in one of three ways.

  • Target processes are chosen by who speaks loudest: Because work starts with the most enthusiastic departments, investment concentrates in areas with low company-wide hours impact
  • No baseline exists for measuring benefits: Because pre-implementation time requirements were never measured, all you can say afterward is that things "seem faster"
  • No basis for scaling decisions: Even if one department succeeds, because you do not know how many similar processes exist in other departments, you cannot estimate the scale of expansion

For the structural reasons why PoCs never reach production, see "5 Structural Reasons AI PoCs Fail and How to Avoid Them". The assessment is the step that eliminates most of the failure factors listed there before work even begins.

The Three-Layer Structure of an AI Assessment

Assessments used in practice consist of the following three layers. Skipping a layer means the final investment decision lacks a valid basis.

LayerPurposeOutput
Layer 1: Process organizationConvert processes into the common unit of work hoursMonthly/annual required hours per process
Layer 2: AI feasibility assessmentDetermine what will replace it and how much time will be savedAI feasibility, estimated hours saved, method
Layer 3: ROICalculate investment amount and payback periodImplementation cost, annual improvement cost, payback period

Layer 1 is fact gathering, Layer 2 is technical judgment, and Layer 3 is management judgment. The people in charge change too. Layer 1 is handled through on-site interviews, Layer 2 by engineers with AI implementation expertise, and Layer 3 by someone who understands the context of investment decisions.

Formula for Annual Required Hours

Annual required hours are calculated mechanically using the following formula.

月間所要工数(h) = 所要工数(分) × 回数 × 稼働日数(または月内発生回数) × 対象人数 ÷ 60
年間所要工数(h) = 月間所要工数(h) × 12

Using the example in the table above: 15 minutes per occurrence, 20 items per day, 4 people involved, 20 working days, giving "15 × 20 × 20 × 4 ÷ 60 = 400 hours/month," or 4,800 hours per year.

Here it is important to always state the assumptions behind the estimates. Whether working days were set at 20 or 22, or whether part-time contributors were included in the headcount, such assumptions can shift the results by tens of percent. State the assumptions explicitly in the diagnostic report so they can be verified later.

Where to Set the Inventory Granularity

If the granularity is too coarse, processes become lumps like "sales activities" and AI feasibility cannot be judged. If it is too fine, the item count balloons into the hundreds and the assessment cannot be completed in a day.

A practical guideline is to define each task as "a unit of work that one person completes in a single session." "Creating a quotation" is appropriate, "doing sales" is too coarse, and "entering the date in the quotation header" is too fine. At this granularity, you will end up with 10–20 items per department and 50–100 company-wide.

Practical aspects of the second layer: assessing AI suitability

Once the inventory is complete, determine "what will replace it" for each task. Record the assessment using the following items.

  • AI suitability: whether it can be handled by AI, and if so, the level of confidence
  • Estimated workload reduction (%): the expected reduction rate relative to the current workload
  • AI suitability assessment / conditions, etc.: the prerequisites for implementation (data location, compatibility with existing systems, security requirements, etc.)
  • AI implementation approach (outline): the specific means of realization
  • Rough investment estimate: the approximate scale of investment

Three categories of replacement methods

AI implementation approaches fall broadly into the following three categories. This classification directly serves as a guide to investment scale and implementation difficulty.

CategoryNature of suitable tasksInvestment scaleLead time
Replace with generative AI (ChatGPT, etc.)Tasks such as writing, summarizing, and classifying text—unstructured but with criteria that can be verbalizedSmallShort
Replace with SaaSTasks already standardized by off-the-shelf tools (expense reporting, scheduling, meeting minutes, etc.)Small to mediumShort to medium
Automate with custom developmentTasks heavily dependent on company-specific formats and business rulesMedium to largeMedium to long

In addition to these three, there is the data + AI domain, which presupposes accumulated internal data. Examples include searching past projects, estimating quotations by analogy, and demand forecasting; in practice, this becomes a combination of generative AI and custom development. Since building a data infrastructure is a prerequisite, investment scale and lead time are comparable to the development type.

The practical order of judgment is to consider options in the sequence "generative AI → SaaS → development" and not pass down to lower levels what can be solved at higher ones. Custom development offers the greatest flexibility but is also the most expensive. For how to choose among RPA, generative AI, and AI agents, see "How to Choose an AI for Business Efficiency."

How to set the estimated workload reduction (%)

The reduction rate can only be an estimate at the pre-implementation stage. Writing it as a definitive figure will cause discrepancies with later actual measurements and undermine trust.

In practice, apply a range based on actual results from similar cases. For example, tasks centered on text generation might be 50–70%, document creation with fixed formats 70–80%, and judgment-based verification tasks 30–50%. Then, explicitly state in the diagnostic report that these figures are estimates based on similar cases.

Setting the reduction rate conservatively also has value. The purpose of the assessment is to support investment decisions, not to win approval with inflated projections. Designing so that actual results exceed estimates makes it easier to drive the second and subsequent rounds.

Practical aspects of the third layer: calculating return on investment

Finally, assemble the figures for the investment decision. The items to record are the following six.

ItemDescription
Implementation cost (millions of yen)Costs for initial development, setup, and migration
Annual improvement cost (millions of yen)Annual benefit obtained by converting reduced workload into monetary value using labor cost rates
Estimated payback periodImplementation cost ÷ annual improvement cost
Estimated workload reductionAnnual required workload × estimated reduction rate (%)
Similar casesImplementation track record for similar tasks
Similar resultsReduction results in similar cases (e.g., XX% reduction)

Two points that often cause confusion in monetary conversion

What labor cost rate to use. If none is specified, a standard rate is used, but the rate varies greatly depending on the staff level performing the task. Verification work done by managers and data entry done by part-timers differ severalfold per hour even for the same hour. Setting separate rates by department and job level yields figures closer to reality.

Clearly state what the saved time will be used for. Workload reduction does not automatically translate into labor cost savings. The financial effect differs depending on whether the freed time is reassigned to other tasks, reduces overtime, or avoids additional hiring. If you write "annual savings of tens of millions of yen" while leaving this ambiguous, the review by accounting and finance will inevitably stall.

How to interpret the payback period

The payback period alone is not a sufficient basis for judgment. Even with the same payback period, priorities change if investment scale and risk differ.

In practice, plot items on two axes—payback period and implementation cost—and start with the quadrant of "payback within one year and small implementation cost." This quadrant is usually occupied by tasks that can be replaced with generative AI or SaaS. For areas involving custom development, it is easier to gain internal consensus if you start after building early success stories.

Five patterns of failed assessments

  • The inventory ends with self-reporting only: the time reported by staff tends to be shorter than reality, because ancillary tasks (waiting for confirmation, handling rework) are omitted from reports. Always ask about "surrounding tasks" during interviews
  • AI suitability is judged by non-technical staff: if feasibility is rated highly based on the impression that "AI could probably do it," conditions will fall apart at the implementation stage. Judgment requires hands-on experience with AI implementation
  • The reduction rate is written definitively: unless it is clearly stated to be an estimate, discrepancies with actual measurements will undermine trust in the entire initiative
  • The diagnostic report ends as a deliverable: even if priorities are set, without an execution structure the same discussion will be repeated six months later
  • It is updated only once: both business processes and model capabilities change. Update the inventory at least once a year and re-evaluate tasks previously marked "not feasible"

The fourth pattern is especially common. Treat diagnosis and implementation separately, and design the process to include an execution structure that follows the priorities in the diagnostic report. Options for execution structures are compared in "What Is an AX Implementation Partner? 7 Selection and Evaluation Criteria and How to Avoid Failure."

A format for completing it in one day

An assessment can be done in one day if the methodology is standardized. If you turn it into a research project that takes months, the premises will have changed by the time you finish.

ProcessDurationContent
Advance preparationAbout 30 minutesDepartments and key operations are filled in on a dedicated form
Interviews1 dayInterview each department and inventory operations. Quantify frequency, workload, and headcount
Analysis and delivery2 business daysCalculate AI suitability and ROI, and deliver as a diagnostic report. Explain priorities at the debriefing session

The key to finishing in one day lies in advance preparation. If you try to identify operations from scratch on the day, most of the time is spent on recalling them. Have participants fill in a list of key operations in advance, and on the day focus on quantifying frequency, workload, and headcount, and digging into details.

Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ)

Q1. How does AI assessment differ from consulting-style business analysis?

A. The granularity and purpose of the output differ. General business analysis covers structuring issues and presenting the ideal state, while AI assessment produces a list for each operation covering "which AI method will replace it," "what percentage of workload will be reduced," and "what the investment amount and payback period are." It differs in that it is made concrete to the point where you know what to order next. For this reason, practical experience in AI implementation is required for the judgment.

Q2. How many operations should be inventoried?

A. For a company-wide scope, 50 to 100 items is the practical range. Set the granularity at "the unit of work a person in charge completes in one go." Roughly 10 to 20 items per department is the guideline. Finer than this and the number of items balloons beyond what analysis can handle; coarser and AI suitability cannot be judged.

Q3. How is the workload reduction estimate (%) calculated?

A. Before implementation, it is an estimated range based on actual results from similar cases. Levels such as 50-70% for text-generation-centered operations, 70-80% for format-fixed document creation, and 30-50% for judgment-involving verification work are assigned according to the nature of the operation. Since these are not measured values, the assumptions are clearly stated in the diagnostic report, and the process assumes updating by comparing with actual measurements after implementation.

Q4. How is the unit rate determined when converting reduced workload into monetary value?

A. Ideally, labor cost rates should be set separately by department and job level according to the personnel engaged in the target operations. If not specified, a standard rate is used. It is also necessary to specify whether the reduced time goes to overtime reduction, avoiding additional hiring, or allocation to other work. If this is left ambiguous, the financial department will not accept the benefit amount in review.

Q5. Could the assessment conclude that there are no operations suitable for AI?

A. It is rare for all operations to be excluded, but there will always be operations where "the ROI does not work at this point in time." Examples include data not being digitized, workload being too small so savings fall below investment, or judgment requiring highly specialized tacit knowledge. These are recorded as excluded and carried over for re-evaluation the following year. Since both model capabilities and your company's data readiness change, a single judgment should not be treated as a permanent conclusion.

Q6. Can we conduct only the assessment and proceed with implementation in-house or with another vendor?

A. No problem. Since the diagnostic report is close to a specification document that clearly states the operation, expected reduction, investment amount, and implementation method, it can be used for in-house implementation or for ordering from other vendors. However, there are many cases where the diagnostic report ends as a deliverable and months pass without an execution structure being decided. We recommend deciding, at the assessment stage, who will implement the first three operations.

Summary

  • AI assessment is the process of inventorying operations, converting them into workload, and judging AI suitability and ROI. Tool selection comes after that
  • The structure has 3 layers. The first layer converts operations into workload, the second judges replacement methods and reduction rates, and the third calculates investment amount and payback period
  • In business organization, 12 items are recorded and annual required workload is calculated mechanically. Granularity is "the unit of work a person in charge completes in one go"
  • Consider alternatives in the order of "Generative AI → SaaS → Development." Don't pass down to lower levels what can be solved at higher ones.
  • Workload reduction estimates are estimates based on similar cases, so stating the assumptions is essential. Avoid writing in definitive terms.
  • Payback period alone is not a decision-making criterion. Plot it against implementation cost on two axes and start with items that are "payback within 1 year and low cost."
  • The most common failure pattern is that the diagnostic report becomes the final deliverable and no execution structure is ever decided. Separate diagnosis from implementation, and design the implementation down to who will carry it out.
