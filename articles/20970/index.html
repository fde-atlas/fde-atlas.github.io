A Guide to Notion's FDE Job Requirements and Interview Preparation

Key Takeaways

  • Notion's FDE does three things: builds agents and integrations, leads large-scale data migrations, and brings lessons learned from customers back to the Product, CX, and Engineering teams.
  • Hard requirements include API and data integration experience, the ability to debug across multiple layers, and 4+ years (DACH) or 5+ years (AMER) of customer-facing experience.
  • The most effective way to prepare is to do a small migration project with a validation script, then write up a product feedback note.

Open Notion's Forward Deployed Engineer (FDE) job posting and you'll see a wall of text: agents, integrations, automation, data pipelines, migrations, feedback loops. At first glance it looks like a "do everything" role. Read it carefully, though, and it's actually three different jobs, each requiring different kinds of evidence.

Separating these three jobs matters more than you might think. A resume listing a string of technical terms can hardly address all three requirements at once. What your application should really contain is evidence that you've taken over a messy system, pushed it to production, and lost no data along the way.

One title, three jobs

Notion calls itself the AI workspace where teams and agents think together. The FDE's mission is to bring this product into customers' real systems.

The DACH posting places the FDE on the go-to-market team, positioning it as the person who owns complex technical customer projects end to end. The AMER posting puts the role on the Services team, emphasizing hands-on technical implementation.

The first job is building. The posting asks you to design, build, and operate custom agents, integrations, automation workflows, and data pipelines that connect Notion to customer systems. Note the word "operate": delivering the code isn't enough—you're also responsible while the system is running.

The second job is large-scale migration, including requirements gathering, scoping, and data modeling. The third is the one most easily overlooked during preparation: designing and running a feedback loop with Product, CX, and Engineering that turns difficulties encountered in real deployments into actionable work for those teams.

Where is the real bar?

The required qualifications section is quite pragmatic. You need proficiency in at least one of Java, JavaScript, Node.js, SQL, or Python. Hands-on API and data integration experience appears repeatedly across regional postings. Debugging ability is described concretely: you must be able to trace root causes across multiple layers, from code to APIs to permissions.

On seniority, the AMER posting requires 5+ years of experience in customer-facing, forward deployed, or similar hands-on technical roles; the DACH posting requires 4+ years.

Some regions also have language requirements: the Korea posting requires native-level Korean and working-level English, including spoken communication.

Notion is also hiring an FDE Manager in EMEA to lead a team of FDEs, requiring 8+ years of experience.

The "nice to have" section reveals the company's direction. The AMER posting lists ETL/ELT, data transformation, schema mapping, and validation scripts, as well as MCP, LLM applications, prompt engineering, and retrieval-augmented generation (RAG). The Korea posting adds experience deploying autonomous AI agents in coding workflows or complex business processes.

Example: a small migration verified item by item

Imagine setting yourself a challenge: a 30-person team manages documents and tasks with an old wiki plus a spreadsheet, and now needs to migrate to a new workspace. This is a miniature version of exactly the kind of work in the posting, and you can do it on your own computer.

Requirements gathering means listing the data sources: the wiki has pages, subpages, creators, and edit dates; the spreadsheet has a status column with inconsistently hand-entered values, like "Done", "done", and the Vietnamese "Xong". Data modeling means deciding the target structure: status becomes a single-select field with exactly three fixed values, parent-child relationships are preserved, and creators are mapped by email.

The real differentiator is validation. Suppose the source data has 1,200 records. The migration script reports "success", but that's just the script's word for it. You need an independent script to compare both sides:

import csv
from collections import Counter

ALLOWED_STATUS = {"Not started", "In progress", "Done"}

def load(path, key="source_id"):
    with open(path, newline="", encoding="utf-8") as f:
        return {row[key]: row for row in csv.DictReader(f)}

def validate(source_path, target_path):
    src, dst = load(source_path), load(target_path)
    errors = Counter()
    for sid, row in src.items():
        t = dst.get(sid)
        if t is None:
            errors["missing_in_target"] += 1
            continue
        if t["status"] not in ALLOWED_STATUS:
            errors["unmapped_status"] += 1
        if row["parent_id"] and t["parent_id"] != row["parent_id"]:
            errors["broken_hierarchy"] += 1
        if not t["owner_email"]:
            errors["owner_not_mapped"] += 1
    extra = len(set(dst) - set(src))
    print(f"源={len(src)} 目标={len(dst)} 多出={extra}")
    for kind, n in errors.most_common():
        print(f"{kind}: {n}")

validate("source_export.csv", "target_export.csv")

Suppose the results are: 1,200 records in the source, 1,188 in the target, 12 missing, and 25 unmapped statuses. These numbers are just the beginning.

When you investigate the 12 missing records, you might find they sit in a folder the integration token has no permission to read—exactly the kind of "permissions" issue mentioned in the posting. The 25 status errors come from hand-entered values the mapping table didn't cover.

Then you write a short note to the "product team": if the import tool warned about inaccessible pages up front, the person responsible for deployment wouldn't have to count them by hand. That's a miniature feedback loop.

What order should you do things in?

First pick a real data source, however small: an issue tracker, a personal wiki, a project management spreadsheet. Write the mapping document before writing any code, because that's the requirements gathering, scoping, and data modeling the posting talks about. Then write the migration script in the language you know best—Python and Node.js are both on the list.

Next comes the validation script and an error report broken down by type, with a root cause found for every error type. If you want to cover the nice-to-haves, add a small agent that answers questions about the migrated data, logging the questions it got wrong and why. Finally, write a one-page product feedback note.

Every line on your resume should contain four elements: the source system, the target system, the data volume, and how you verified the results. "Migrated 1,200 records from a wiki to a new workspace; validation script found and fixed 37 errors across 2 categories" is far more convincing than "proficient in Python, REST APIs".

Common mistakes that sink applications

The first mistake to avoid is proving only the building ability. A polished agent demo, without migration and data validation, covers only a third of this role. The second is telling your debugging story as luck ("it finally worked"), instead of clearly explaining which hypotheses you ruled out at each layer—code, API, permissions.

The third is ignoring the customer-facing experience requirement. If you're a pure backend software engineer, look back at times you worked directly with internal users or integration partners, and spell that out.

The last one is not reading the posting for the region you're applying to. The Korea posting, for example, explicitly requires native-level Korean and working-level English.

How many of these does your application cover?

Self-check this week: copy the five items in the left column of the table above onto a single page. Next to each one, attach a link or write down a specific project the interviewer can open right away: a mapping document, a validation script, an incident retrospective, a product suggestion, a small AI agent.

The one that's blank is the one you should fill in first. If several are blank, start with the validation script, because once a good comparison script is done, you'll also have material for the incident retrospective and the product suggestion.

Migrating 1,200 records on a laptop is of course not an enterprise-grade project. But it shows the interviewer that you think like someone who would take responsibility when customer data is lost—and that is the real question behind every line of this job posting.
