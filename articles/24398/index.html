What Is an AI Product Manager? | Role Overview and How It Differs from an FDE

Original title: AIプロダクトマネージャーとは｜仕事内容と、FDEとの違い

FDE Consulting · 2026-11-18 · 9 min read

Summary

Drawing on job postings from HEROZ, PKSHA Technology, and OpenAI, this article outlines what AI product managers do and how the role differs from regular PMs, AI project managers, FDEs, and Deployment Strategists. The author argues AI PMs face three unique demands: defining quality via collected examples since LLM outputs vary, managing per-call model costs that affect margins, and building prototypes before finalizing specs. The key distinction from FDEs is scope: AI PMs serve many customers with a shared product, while FDEs build working systems for a single customer. The article closes with advice for AI PMs aiming to become FDEs, emphasizing hands-on production experience.

Key points

  • AI PMs must define quality standards through evals, since LLM outputs vary.
  • Per-call model costs make pricing and design directly affect product margins.
  • AI PMs serve many customers; FDEs build production systems for one customer.
  • Deployment Strategists own the 'why' and 'what'; FDEs own the 'how'.
  • Aspiring FDEs should gain experience shipping and maintaining production code.

An AI Product Manager (AI PM) is a role that decides what to build—and what not to build—for products and features that use AI. On top of the usual product manager's work, it involves judgments unique to AI: how to define quality for features whose output isn't the same every time, how to think about model costs incurred with every use, and whether the problem is even one AI should solve in the first place.

In Japan, the title and substance of this role are not yet standardized in job postings. Some postings call the role "AI Product Manager," while others are general PdM postings that happen to cover AI products. There are also postings for "AI Project Managers," which abbreviate to the same "PM." This article organizes the job content from postings published by HEROZ, PKSHA Technology, and OpenAI, and compares how the role differs from a regular PM, as well as from AI Project Managers and FDEs (Forward Deployed Engineers). The FDE role itself is explained in What is an FDE? The meaning and job content of a Forward Deployed Engineer.

What AI Product Managers Do, as Seen from Job Postings

Let's look at examples from Japan. HEROZ is hiring a PdM for HEROZ ASK, a generative AI platform for enterprises. The posting states that the product is entering a stage of deepening from general-purpose generative AI into "business-specific AI" deeply embedded in companies' workflows. The specific tasks listed include the following.

  • Regularly trying out the latest generative AI tools and competitors' services, comparing UIs and agent capabilities
  • Not taking customer requests at face value, but judging "whether AI should solve this, or whether operations or another approach can cover it"
  • Building mocks and prototypes with generative AI tools or Figma, and turning them into requirement specifications (PRDs) that engineers can implement
  • Looking at usage rates and retention of shipped features, and LLM operating costs (ROI), to decide whether to fix or discard a feature

PKSHA Technology's posting leans more technical. The Product Manager in the AI HR Development Promotion Office is a PM with an engineering background, responsible for launching, developing, and operating products in the HR domain. Required qualifications are at least 2 years of product management or equivalent experience, and at least 2 years of web application development experience. Preferred qualifications include experience developing, operating, and maintaining products using LLMs such as OpenAI and Claude.

Job postings from overseas AI companies describe the AI-specific work in even more detail. OpenAI's Product Manager posting for financial services starts with shadowing customers' work to understand the documents, data, and tools they use. You build prototypes and demos yourself using the company's own tools such as Codex. You turn real financial workflows and product failures into evaluation frameworks and datasets. You define "good" in terms of accuracy, traceability of sources, completeness, and verifiability, and balance quality against response speed, reliability, and cost. Success is measured by whether tasks were completed, whether outputs were accepted, whether the product was used repeatedly, whether time was saved, and business impact.

How Is This Different from a Regular Product Manager?

In my view, there are three differences.

The first is that you must define quality yourself. For a regular feature, working as specified counts as passing. Features using LLMs can give varying answers to the same input, and can return plausible mistakes. So you need to decide in advance, using collected examples, "which answers count as passing." I believe this is why OpenAI's posting includes turning product failures into evaluation frameworks and datasets as part of the PM's job. I wrote about how to build evaluations in How to Evaluate LLMs.

The second is that costs grow with usage. With regular software, the per-use cost barely changes even as users increase. Features that call LLMs incur model costs on every call. As HEROZ's posting notes in mentioning LLM operating costs, which model you use and how long you let answers run directly affect the product's profitability.

The third is building something that works before the spec. With generative AI features, a written specification alone doesn't tell you whether something is good or bad. You only know whether it's usable once you run it with real inputs. Both HEROZ and OpenAI state in their postings that PMs build prototypes themselves.

Difference from AI Project Managers

Searching job postings, you'll also find the role of "AI Project Manager." Lumina's AI Project Manager posting describes a job leading enterprise generative AI projects, from hypothesis-building around problems through PoC, MVP, production rollout, and adoption. Specifically, it lists interviewing executives and business divisions, defining KPIs, requirements definition, designing use cases for RAG and AI agents, and designing operational rules.

In my view, product managers and project managers differ in how their work ends. A product manager is responsible for growing the company's own product over the long term and getting it used by many customers. A project manager is responsible for delivering customer-specific projects to production within the agreed scope and deadline. Since both abbreviate to "AI PM," it's wise to confirm which meaning is intended, both when reading postings and when delegating work.

Difference between AI Product Managers and FDEs

To the roles covered so far, add the FDE and the Deployment Strategist who works alongside the FDE. Since every company defines these roles differently, this is my own way of organizing them.

RoleFor whomResponsible for whatWrites code themselves?
AI Product ManagerThe many customers who use the company's productDecisions on what to build, and keeping the product in useMostly up to prototyping. Some job postings, like PKSHA's, require development experience
AI Project ManagerCustomers per projectDelivering to production within the agreed scope and deadlineOften does not write code
Deployment StrategistA single customerThe "why" and the "what" of the customer's problemOften does not write code
FDEA single customerThe system itself that runs in the customer's operationsWrites code. Builds things that run in production

Databricks' job posting calls the Deployment Strategist the "product manager" of the customer's problem, stating that the Deployment Strategist handles the "why" and the "what," while the FDE handles "how to build it." In my view, this is applying the AI PM mindset to a single customer. For details, see What is a Deployment Strategist.

The FDE overlaps with the AI PM in owning the decision of what to build, and differs in building it personally and shipping it to production. OpenAI's Tokyo FDE job posting says the role partners with strategically important customers and owns discovery, technical scoping, design, building, and production deployment, and requires directly modifying code when progress depends on it. Success is measured by adoption in production and measurable impact on the business.

The difference between an AI PM and an FDE, in one sentence, is whether the counterpart is "many customers" or "a single customer," in my view. An AI PM finds problems common to many customers and turns them into a product. An FDE embeds into a single customer's operations and builds something that works at that company. There is also a model where the FDE surfaces common problems found in the field, and the AI PM turns them back into product features.

Aiming for FDE from AI Product Manager

AI PM experience carries over directly to the first half of the FDE's work. Listening to problems and judging whether AI should solve them, setting evaluation criteria, and balancing cost and quality are things an FDE does every time in the customer's environment. What tends to be missing, in my view, is the experience of building production systems yourself and fixing operational issues yourself.

The order in which to build those skills, in my view, is the following three steps.

  • Don't stop at prototyping; write the code yourself for a small feature and run it in production
  • Explain the evaluation of what you ran, using both collected examples and business metrics
  • Design yourself the conditions for running within the customer's company, such as permissions, data handling, and logging

The path to becoming an FDE is summarized in How to become an FDE, and how to move from other roles in FDE jobs and career changes.

How to choose whom to engage with, from the client's perspective

From the perspective of a company adopting AI, whether to hire an AI PM or engage externally depends on whether what you're building is your own product or a system for your own operations. In my view, checking the following three points before engaging reduces mismatches.

What to checkKeep an AI PM in-houseEngage an AI Project ManagerEngage an FDE
What is being builtYour own product sold to customersA project with a fixed scopeA system used in your own operations
Who decides what to buildThe AI PM, based on the market and customersThe client decides, refined togetherDecided together from the operational problems
Who fixes things after deliveryThe in-house development teamWithin the contract scope, with operational rules defined before handoverDefine the operational model and hand over in-house

If you sell an AI-powered product yourself, an AI PM is a role you need in-house. If you want to improve operational efficiency or make AI usable within your company, you need someone who embeds into your operations and builds things that run in production, rather than someone who grows a product.
