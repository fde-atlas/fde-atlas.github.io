What exactly is the recently popular Forward Deployed Engineer role?

Original title: 最近流行的前向部署工程师到底是怎样一种存在？

cbinews.com · 2026-06-15 · 5 min read

Summary

This CBINEWS interview with Taimur Rashid, GM of AWS's Generative AI Innovation Center, argues that the forward deployed engineer is best understood as a cross-functional team—engineers, scientists, strategists, sometimes security engineers—embedded with customers, rather than a lone technical hero. Teams of five to seven (sometimes three) run roughly 45-day sprints to build and prove value. Rashid says customers' biggest gap is governance, especially around AI agents: nearly all agent conversations in recent months were about governance, not capability. He stresses that software engineering skills matter because fixing vulnerabilities is harder than finding them, and describes AWS's hiring criteria across functional, domain, and cultural dimensions, plus internal reskilling efforts.

Key points

  • FDE is a cross-functional team, typically five to seven people, onsite, remote, or hybrid.
  • Engagements run as ~45-day sprints: build, prove value, then expand.
  • Governance around AI agents is customers' biggest gap, not capability.
  • Software engineering matters: fixing vulnerabilities is harder than scanning for them.
  • AWS hires on functional skill, AI lifecycle domain knowledge, and comfort with ambiguity.

The buzz around the "Forward Deployed Engineer" (FDE) at AI companies shows no sign of cooling down, and this role is rapidly gaining popularity.

FDEs are being eagerly hired by companies hoping to successfully deploy AI tools and services, making them one of the hottest professions of the AI era. So what exactly is an FDE—is it the "lone technical gunslinger" that OpenAI, Google, and Microsoft have been recruiting? As it turns out, the point is not a single engineer flying around designing and landing AI deployments, but rather a team of engineers working together on-site at the customer.

At least that's how AWS sees it. Taimur Rashid, General Manager of the AWS Generative AI Innovation Center, says the FDE concept predates the current generative AI gold rush by far—as early as the machine learning and cloud computing era, similar engineering teams were needed to help enterprises complete deployments. Below is the transcript of the interview with Rashid.

Q: What is an FDE?

"We see it as a team. It's a cross-functional team made up of engineers, scientists, strategists, and people who can integrate technology and business together. In some cases, security engineers also need to join. I compare them to anesthesiologists—they have to do a lot of preparation work and continuously monitor throughout the entire process. We see ourselves as a forward deployed team, helping customers adopt AI in all its forms, whether generative AI, agentic AI, or emerging trends like physical AI. We're helping these companies themselves become frontier enterprises."

Q: How does an FDE engagement start, and what is its structure?

"The forward deployed model we observe is: when customers visit—for example, we have executive briefing centers in Seattle and Arlington, Virginia—after customers share what they want to do, they quickly ask: 'What's the fastest way to build something quickly with you?' We then forward deploy people, embedding them into the customer's business, running the 45-day sprints we typically design. Through consecutive sprints, we build together and prove value, after which the customer can expand it into a broader engagement."

Q: Where are FDEs actually located—on the customer side, internally, or somewhere in between?

"It's a hybrid, and it depends largely on the customer's preference. Some customers are very insistent, demanding that 'your team must be inside our company,' in which case we deploy most of the team on-site. Other customers can accept remote virtual embedding. There are also hybrid models. The teams we deploy are usually five to seven people, sometimes with a baseline of three."

Q: Is cost savings the FDE's job, or the responsibility of others on the team?

"I expect these teams to design system architectures that take cost requirements into account—whether that's choosing different models for different use cases that don't increase per-token cost, or considering semantic caching. Personally, I think token consumption spending can be high, but as long as revenue is growing and the economics work out, it's acceptable."

Q: What challenges have you encountered in actually deploying FDEs?

"One challenge worth highlighting: customers get very excited about forward deployed resources, but ultimately realize they're not ready to absorb them immediately—they have to sort out process issues, security access, and other operational matters. A great example is the Commonwealth Bank of Australia. They said: 'Prioritization is a big issue. If you're 100% committed to forward deployment, how do we ensure our own team is also 100% committed?' Sitting in the office, you're always distracted by day-to-day work. So they suggested: 'Why not set up a neutral zone in Seattle? You send people, we send people, and give the teams three weeks of uninterrupted focus.'"

Q: Have you encountered customers who want AI but are completely unprepared on security or governance?

"I've certainly experienced that. Some customers do have security processes and capabilities, but they're not rigorous enough for the AI era. Governance is where customers currently have the biggest gap—and I'm talking about governance around agents. Over the past two months, almost 100% of conversations about agents haven't been about capabilities; they've all been about governance. This is a very important focus area for forward deployed teams right now: helping with governance education and building the corresponding frameworks."

Q: How does software engineering fit into the FDE model?

"One of the biggest lessons is that when we forward deploy resources and help customers integrate AI into their systems, software engineering knowledge is extremely important. Customers can now use a Claude model to scan codebases for vulnerabilities, but the hard part isn't assessing the vulnerabilities—it's fixing them. Fixing requires software engineering experience—you have to merge code, test, and deploy. We're broadly seeing that forward software development teams are getting smaller, and they're managing agents that execute the various tasks of the software development lifecycle."

Q: AWS has multiple models—open source and closed source—more complex than any other AI vendor. How do you lock in talent?

"The scale is enormous, and it's not just scale—the complexity of the tech stack too. Our approach is fundamentally three things: First, make sure everyone understands the concepts—you have to know pre-training, post-training, reinforcement learning, fine-tuning. Second, make sure the team is proficient in first-party services. Third, by design, AWS has always put choice at the core. We say: 'Apply the 80-20 rule—which 20% of the expertise covers 80% of most customers' needs?'"

Q: For software developers who want to move into FDE work, which three or four skills should they learn?

"We look at three dimensions. The first dimension is function—are they more engineering-oriented, science-oriented, or security-oriented? Our litmus test is not just functional knowledge but hands-on ability. The second dimension is domain—their domain understanding across the entire AI lifecycle. The third is the cultural dimension—we're looking for people who are good at dealing with ambiguity, skilled at stakeholder management, and have an entrepreneurial mindset. This AI transformation is not for the faint of heart."

Q: OpenAI, Google, and others are all competing for FDE talent. How do AWS's standards differ?

"I don't fully know what other companies are looking for, but I can speak to our past standards. When hiring Solutions Architects, we valued system-level understanding, but I'm increasingly seeing that hands-on experience and cultural mindset are all extremely important. If I had to pick the single most important thing—whether it's upskilling our existing employees or recruiting new talent—it's the application of AI to software engineering and systems integration tasks."

Q: Is AWS also upskilling internal talent?

"Yes. In the coming weeks you'll see some publications on how we're driving this work within our software teams and how it translates into customer-facing roles."
