Summary

This piece opens Datawhale's "FDE Case 100" collection, which documents how forward deployed engineers bring AI into real businesses, arguing there is no standard playbook for enterprise AI adoption. Three cases are presented: a German parts manufacturer using AI to capture veteran workers' tacit knowledge for training new hires; a Zhejiang vehicle administration office automating manual document review, cutting per-case review from about 15 minutes to 3-5 minutes and raising daily volume from roughly 700-800 to about 1,100; and a litigation team using a case workspace instead of a chatbot, improving document processing efficiency by about 70%. Recurring lessons: start from how the business actually works, reuse existing systems, set acceptance criteria from real business problems, embed AI into existing workflows, and stay on site long after launch.

Key points

  • The collection records real explorations, not standard AI adoption answers.
  • Manufacturing case: AI captured veterans' tacit knowledge and reshaped training.
  • Vehicle office case: automated review cut 15 minutes to 3-5 per case.
  • Legal case: case workspace beat chat interface, ~70% efficiency gain.
  • Key lessons: observe real workflows, embed AI, and provide long-term support.

Foreword

AI Begins to Truly Enter Enterprises. If we placed all enterprises on the same AI map today, we would see a highly uneven picture. Some enterprises are still thinking about what AI can do for them, some are still building their digital foundations, and some have already started bringing large models, AI agents, and automated workflows into real business operations—even attempting to change their existing ways of working at scale. It is difficult to summarize the state of enterprise AI adoption today with a single unified stage.

But over the past period, after we truly stepped into enterprises and interviewed one AI implementation case after another, one thing has become increasingly certain: AI has begun to create value in the real world.

It often does not first manifest as a grand "enterprise AI transformation." More often, it happens in specific, even small scenarios: a process that once required extensive manual handling, a piece of knowledge that depended on a veteran worker's experience, a repetitive information-organizing task, a sales follow-up, a report, a production line, or a problem that was never well solved in the past simply because the cost was too high. These changes may not be sensational, but they have already begun to genuinely affect enterprises' efficiency, costs, and ways of working.

This is why we launched the "FDE Case 100" initiative.

What we hope to record is not 100 so-called "standard answers for AI implementation." In fact, no such standard answers exist today. What we want to record more is the exploration being carried out by a group of pioneers: how they discover problems, how they understand the business, how they choose technologies, how they make trade-offs under real-world conditions, and how they gradually integrate AI into real business settings. Some of these explorations have already achieved clear results, while others are still in the process of validation and iteration. They are not perfect, nor can they necessarily be directly replicated. But it is precisely these practices bearing the traces of exploration that let us see the real path of AI moving from technical capability to industrial value. Every major technological transformation needs such a group of pioneers.

In this process, we have also become increasingly aware that AI implementation is no longer just a technical problem. When AI truly enters an enterprise, the questions gradually extend from "can the model do it" to "what problems are worth solving," "how should business departments participate," "how should data and knowledge be organized," "how should new workflows be established," "who will continuously operate the project after it goes live," and—more importantly—how the enterprise itself can gradually build the ability to use, understand, and develop AI through project after project. This means that the changes brought by AI will ultimately enter the organization itself.

The end of a project should not mean the departure of AI capability. Truly valuable AI implementation means that while solving a specific problem, the enterprise also begins to learn how to solve the next one.

From Mentorship to AI-Assisted Training: An FDE Uses AI to Solve the Knowledge Transfer Challenge in Manufacturing

Q1: As an FDE, what was the specific scenario where you applied AI? What did the specific problem in this scenario look like?

At the time, I was serving a parts manufacturing enterprise in Stuttgart, Germany, with about seventy to eighty employees. The company had multiple production lines and many different product models.

This industry has a distinctive characteristic: much of the production knowledge is very detailed. For example, when producing different parts, parameters need to be adjusted, and the proportions of different metal materials are involved. Because the parts ultimately face different usage environments—some may need to withstand highly acidic environments, others need to be wear-resistant—different material ratios, equipment operations, and production experience directly affect product quality.

What truly made the enterprise anxious was that this knowledge did not entirely exist in standard documents; a considerable portion of the experience was held by veteran workers.

The veteran workers knew how a particular product should be adjusted, under what circumstances parameters should change, and how to handle certain equipment states. Much of this belongs to tacit experience formed through long-term practice, which is difficult to fully record in a simple operations manual.

The enterprise's biggest worry was: if these people left, would their experience leave with them?

So after I arrived on-site, I didn't first ask "which model should we use." Instead, I first looked at how they actually trained new employees.

Looking back now, this step was actually more important than which model was chosen later. Because what the enterprise initially said was "we want to consolidate the veteran workers' experience," but this statement could not yet be directly turned into an AI project.

I had to keep going deeper into the business until it became a more specific problem: what knowledge were new employees missing in their learning process, where was this knowledge located, and how could it be found when truly needed.

Q2: Before you got involved, how was this problem solved in the past?

In the past, it mainly relied on "people training people."

After new employees joined the enterprise, they had to go through a relatively long training and internship process, in which veteran workers played an important mentoring role. Based on what I learned at the time, training a new employee could take two to three months or even longer.

Much of the experience in manufacturing is inherently formed through long-term practice. Veteran workers not only know the standard answers, but also know when the standard answers should not be mechanically followed. So the past reliance on the mentor-apprentice system was actually a very natural way of knowledge transfer.

The real problem was that the enterprise's business environment had begun to change. On one hand, the veteran workers' own production tasks were heavy, leaving them insufficient time to continuously mentor new employees; on the other hand, some experienced employees were approaching retirement. Meanwhile, the enterprise also hoped to consolidate experience that had previously depended on individuals into knowledge that the organization could use long-term.

So what I did was first preserve the most valuable experience of the veteran workers, so that questions that used to require repeatedly asking the veterans could get their first answer through another means; truly complex questions requiring judgment would then go back to the veterans. This way, the veterans' time no longer had to be constantly consumed by repetitive knowledge transfer.

From this perspective, what I did goes a step further than "converting traditional training into AI training"—it's about redistributing people's roles in the training process.

Q3 What was your overall approach and the key steps in using AI to solve this problem?

Where the project really took time was actually in how the various stages before and after were connected; building the knowledge base was just one step.

Step one: get into the business first, and put the technology later.

At the beginning, I was used to understanding customer needs through interviews, talking with senior management, middle management, and frontline employees.

But when actually doing the project, I found a problem: frontline employees aren't necessarily willing to spend a lot of time on interviews, and many details of their daily work they themselves may not be able to fully describe in an interview. So I later adjusted my approach.

Besides interviews, I would directly follow employees through their daily workflow, observing what they actually do in a day, where they stop, where they need to ask others, and which questions keep recurring.

The problems identified this way are far more specific than simply asking "what are your pain points."

In this project, I also first met with the supervisors to map out the production line and the new-hire training process, then compiled the problems new hires most frequently encountered, the knowledge where they most easily made repeated mistakes, and which content constituted the tacit experience only veterans possessed, and finally organized all of this into a knowledge map.

Step two: turn human experience into knowledge that AI can use.

Once the knowledge map was done, the next step was to actually begin knowledge processing.

The company already had some knowledge documents, so I first imported these existing materials and then processed them through document parsing, RAG, and other methods. But documents alone weren't enough; a more important part of my work was abstracting the tacit experience that veterans had kept in their heads, making it part of the company's knowledge system so that it could ultimately be understood and retrieved by AI.

There's a common pitfall here: thinking that once you throw documents in and the AI can answer questions, the knowledge base is done. I did not accept the work by that standard.

The high-frequency questions identified during the early research naturally became the test set for later stages. For example, I would check whether document parsing reached about 95%, whether the accuracy of retrieved answer segments reached above 80%, and only when a minimal-scope test met the agreed standards did I consider this technical path preliminarily validated.

I think this is exactly where being an FDE differs from pure technical development: the problems discovered early on must ultimately be revisited for verification.

Step three: put the AI back into the original training process.

Even after the technical pipeline was working, the project wasn't actually finished. If you just give employees a new AI entry point and let them use it on their own, in many cases it ends up never being used.

So I continued working into the business process. The company already had a new-hire training stage, so I integrated the AI knowledge capabilities into the platform they actually used, and further designed a training process similar to online course check-ins.

New hires could see how much learning content they had completed within the required time; mentor veterans could return to the platform to check the new hires' current learning progress; and each course included Q&A sessions as a simple verification of learning outcomes. In other words, the AI entered the existing new-hire training process, rather than adding an extra "chatbot."

Step four: stay with the company to make sure it's genuinely used.

After going live, I kept adjusting the frontend usability, pages, and actual Q&A performance. This process didn't take just a day or two—at the time, the hands-on support alone lasted nearly two months, and the whole project, from initiation and research through to final support, took about six months; counting only the time actually invested in the project, it was about four months.

So enterprise AI deployment is rarely "system launch = project complete." Real deployment often happens after the launch.

How employees use it, what feels awkward, whether veterans are willing to look at it, whether new hires can understand it—these questions would in turn require me to keep modifying the product.

Q4 Compared with traditional methods, what were the actual results after using AI?

In terms of actual business changes, several changes were quite clear.

First, knowledge previously scattered in the personal experience of veteran workers began to be systematically organized, and for the first time the company could fairly completely know which experience truly could not be allowed to disappear when the veterans retired.

Second, the veterans' role began to change, shifting from repeatedly answering all questions to focusing on new hires' learning progress and handling more complex problems.

Third, training itself became more trackable. Previously, where new hires "had gotten to" and "how much they had actually mastered" depended largely on the mentor's own observation; now this progress could be clearly seen and tracked on the platform.

For me, the real value of this project was that the company's experience-transfer process, which had previously depended heavily on individuals, began to become an organizational process that could be accumulated, retrieved, learned from, and tracked.

Q5 Did you encounter new problems or challenges during the AI deployment? How did you solve them?

In this project, I think at least three challenges are worth discussing separately.

The first challenge was that users couldn't articulate their needs, or their experience.

Initially I used interviews, hoping to understand the problems by talking with employees at different levels. But only after doing it did I realize that frontline employees aren't necessarily willing to invest much time, and many tacit work habits are hard to fully express through Q&A.

So I later reduced my reliance on pure interviews and directly followed employees through their daily workflows. This is a small adjustment, but very important for an FDE. Sometimes you can't keep asking users "what do you need"—you have to go to the site yourself and see how they actually work.

The second challenge was that technical metrics cannot be defined by the FDE behind closed doors.

For example, there's no industry standard you can directly apply for what counts as acceptable document parsing rates or retrieval accuracy. My approach was to first run a small-scope test to see what level was roughly achievable under current conditions, then communicate with the client about what they truly needed, and finally have both sides jointly determine the acceptance criteria. So the acceptance criteria themselves are part of the business communication.

The third challenge was that even after the technology worked, employees weren't necessarily willing to use it.

So I later spent a lot of time adjusting the frontend pages, the learning process, and usability. I didn't ask new hires to change their entire work habits to accommodate the AI; instead, I tried to fit the AI into their existing training and collaboration environment.

I've always stuck to this principle when doing enterprise projects: if the client already has a tech stack, platform, or model that can solve the problem, I reuse it as much as possible, avoiding tearing down and rebuilding everything the client already has just to showcase technical capability. Because, from the client's perspective, clearly understanding "what already works and what truly needs to change" is itself a process of building trust.

Q6 Based on your experience, how can we better deploy AI into real business?

After completing this project, I have a clearer understanding that an FDE's most important capability actually doesn't lie in "how much you know about models"

I prefer to understand the FDE as a team: someone on the team needs to truly go into the client's site and judge the business problems; someone needs to turn requirements into something that can run in the client's environment; and more specialized technical personnel are needed to ensure the architecture, performance, and future scalability.

But no matter how the roles are divided, in the end everyone is doing the same thing: turning a company's vague problems into deployable, verifiable AI solutions, and then truly pushing them into the company's business processes. If I were to compress the lessons from this project, I would summarize them in four points.

First, don't start from "what AI does the company want to build"—start from "how does the company actually work every day."

It's great when users have clear needs, but even without them, as long as their business SOP is clear enough, I can go through the process step by step to judge where AI fits. So for enterprises, sorting out their core business processes before doing AI is itself very important.

Second, don't rush to overturn what the customer already has.

When I do preliminary research, I first look at what tech stack, platforms, and models the enterprise is currently using, and what can continue to be reused. If existing capabilities can solve the problem, reuse them as much as possible. What an FDE should do is find a better solution between the customer's cost and business outcomes—not prove that their own technical solution is the most complex.

Third, problems discovered early on should directly become the testing criteria for later stages.

In this case, the new hires' frequently asked questions didn't stay in a report after the interviews—they were directly turned into a knowledge base test set. I think this is a crucial step. Only this way does AI ultimately validate "whether it can solve real business problems," while purely technical metrics take a back seat.

Fourth, AI must enter the process—it can't stop at the Demo stage.

A knowledge base being able to answer questions only proves the technical pipeline works. Only when new hires are actually willing to learn, mentors are actually willing to review, and learning outcomes can be accepted and verified does it start entering the business. That's why I spent nearly two months accompanying them at the end. The truly difficult part of many enterprise AI projects lies in this last mile—how to genuinely connect AI with the enterprise's existing business processes.

This is my understanding of the FDE role. AI capabilities will keep getting stronger, and the barrier to building AI applications will keep getting lower, but an enterprise's own processes, organization, and real business environment won't automatically become clearer because of this. What an FDE truly needs to solve is this last mile between AI and the enterprise. Technology will change, models will change, and service formats will change, but the ability to "go deep into the field, discover problems, validate solutions, and enter the process" will, I believe, remain valuable for a long time.

From Manual Document Review to Automated Case Processing: An FDE Uses AI to Improve a Vehicle Administration Office's Online Processing Efficiency

Q1 As an FDE, what was the specific scenario where you applied AI? What did the specific problems in this scenario look like?

This case is a bit special—at the beginning, the customer didn't present an "AI need." When I entered the vehicle administration office, it was initially just to take on a system maintenance project.

At the time, the original system contractor had gone out of business, leaving behind incomplete source code and missing documentation. The whole system involved multiple technologies: a .NET client, an Android display program, as well as web-based management and scheduling systems. Many companies evaluated it and were unwilling to take it on, because this wasn't a problem that could be solved by simply finding an engineer to do basic maintenance—it required truly digging into the legacy system and understanding it. I took on the project.

But looking back now, maintenance was just my entry point into the business scene. The truly valuable needs were only discovered gradually after entering the field. During my on-site work, I began observing the office's online business operations.

Zhejiang's government services are already quite digitalized—many things can be handled online, sometimes without a single in-person visit. But I found a very interesting problem: while the front end had been digitalized, the back end still relied heavily on manual work.

After submitting a request online, people upload various documents, such as ID cards, business licenses, contracts, and invoices. These documents are basically all images, and their photo quality varies wildly.

Staff members have to open them one by one, judge with the naked eye whether the documents are correct and the information complete, and then continue with subsequent review and data entry. This process had been running this way for so long that everyone had gotten used to it. But from my on-site observation, the problems were obvious and could be summarized in three characteristics: slow, exhausting, and unable to scale.

First, slow. Large numbers of images all need to be manually opened, viewed, and checked, with relevant information then entered into the system. Reviewing a single case from start to finish takes about 15 minutes.

Second, exhausting. This work isn't complex—it's just highly repetitive. Staff members stare at photos on screen for long hours every day; some directly reported that "their eyes couldn't take it."

Third, business volume couldn't grow. Behind the office's online business there's also a rather special business relationship. Some services involve an on-site courier company: after the office completes review and certificate production, the courier company handles subsequent packaging and delivery.

The courier company needs to assign staff on-site. If business volume is insufficient and labor costs are high, the service itself faces financial pressure.

So on the surface, this issue was "staff are too tired from looking at images," but breaking it down further, it actually involves the entire business chain. Review efficiency determines processing capacity, processing capacity affects public service, and it also affects the partner's operating costs and whether the whole service model is sustainable.

The longer I stayed on-site, the clearer one thing became: an FDE can't just wait for the customer to write the requirement as "I want an AI review system" before acting. Often, the real need doesn't jump out on its own. You have to first enter the field, see how the business runs, see what people are doing, see which steps repeat every day, and then judge whether there's something worth redesigning with AI.

Q2 Before you got involved, how was this problem solved in the past?

There was no special solution—it was all done by people.

After people uploaded their documents, staff opened the images, reviewed them with the naked eye, and then manually handled the subsequent business. This process had been running for a long time, and for the office, it wasn't even considered a problem that particularly needed solving. I think this point is important.

Looking back today, we might ask, "Such obvious repetitive labor—why not automate it?" But from the business department's perspective at the time, it was workable. The staff were there, the process was established, everyone knew what to do each day. It was a bit slow and tiring, but it didn't affect the system's basic operation.

So when I first proposed to the customer that visual technology could replace part of the manual review, they didn't immediately accept it. Their first reaction was: "Doesn't the current way work? Why spend money on something new?" Also, they weren't confident that AI could actually meet business requirements. Even though some ready-made capabilities already existed on the market, on one hand the prices weren't low, and on the other, no one could guarantee that taking them off the shelf would solve the real problems in the office's actual scenarios.

So the transition from the traditional approach to AI didn't happen because the old way suddenly failed. The real change came from business conditions. When the economic environment changed, the on-site service company also began facing financial pressure and wanted to reduce staffing while increasing business volume. The efficiency problem that previously "didn't hurt enough" suddenly became real. Only then did the solution we had prepared in advance get a real chance to be implemented.

So I later developed a deep insight from doing projects: many AI needs have always existed—they just haven't hurt enough to require change. What an FDE should do is see the problem in advance, and when the business truly needs to change, already have a verifiable solution ready—rather than forcibly manufacturing demand.

Q3 What was your overall approach and the key steps in using AI to solve this problem?

The project didn't start by designing a complete solution and then launching it all at once. In reality, it went through a long process of observation, validation, building trust, project approval, and iteration.

The first step was to see the problem clearly, without rushing to pick a model.

I didn't start by telling the customer "we're going to deploy AI." I first broke down the business to see what the manual work actually consisted of. After breaking it down, I found that the entire review process essentially consisted of a few types of work: viewing images, identifying document types, reading key information, judging whether documents met requirements, filling in relevant data, and moving information between different systems.

Once broken down this way, the technical path naturally emerged. The "seeing" part used machine vision, OCR, and related recognition algorithms; the part requiring information extraction from complex texts like contracts gradually introduced large language models; and the repetitive data entry and inter-system transfers were handled through RPA.

So what we ultimately aimed for was one goal: to have machines replace humans as much as possible in the repetitive tasks of "seeing, reading,

filling, and moving."

The second step was to gradually grind out the capabilities using real business data.

The biggest characteristic of vehicle administration office document review is that the data is extremely non-standard. In the end, we handled about 9 categories of image documents, including contracts, invoices, business licenses, copies of ID cards, and so on. For each type of document, we had to collect real samples, annotate them, train the model, and then go live to observe the results. This is completely different from a lab environment.

For example, the national emblem side of an ID card should normally be photographed as a complete ID card, but some people upload photos showing only the emblem. Contracts are even more troublesome—motor vehicle mortgage registration involves a large number of financial institutions, and every company's standard contract format is different. Some compress the content onto a single page with very small fonts, and many documents are photocopies to begin with, so image quality degrades further after being photographed and uploaded.

So training could never be a one-time thing—we had to constantly bring back anomalous samples from real business operations, supplement positive and negative samples, and keep optimizing. The entire development and iteration cycle lasted about six months, and the recognition rate improved bit by bit from just over 70% to around 98%.

The third step was to embed the AI back into the original business process, rather than making it a standalone tool.

This is what I consider absolutely critical. If you just build a recognition demo that tells staff "what this image is" after recognition, the value is actually quite limited. What really needs to be solved is whether the business can continue to flow after recognition.

So we connected the entire chain: recognition, review, form filling, and subsequent system workflow. The vehicle administration office's existing business involves different web systems, and after review approval, the data still needs to enter the Ministry of Public Security's unified business system. We didn't ask staff to learn a completely independent new way of working; instead, we used RPA to link the different systems together and move the data.

In the end, most normal business could be completed automatically, with staff mainly reviewing results and reports, and only abnormal cases requiring manual verification. In other words, we turned the AI directly into an execution step within the original business process, so staff no longer had to face a standalone tool.

The fourth step was to first build trust with results, then push for formal project approval.

Government projects face another very real issue: it's not something you can launch just because the technology works. At the start, the client had no confidence in the AI's performance, and formally launching the project involved reports, project approval, budgets, and technical feasibility assessments. So in the early stages, we absorbed part of the pre-sales cost and started with a few selected scenarios.

Rather than asking the client to believe our PowerPoint, it's better to let them see real results first. Meanwhile, our existing operations and maintenance services continued to advance, and through delivery after delivery, the client came to see that we weren't just "making empty promises"—our technical and service capabilities gradually gained recognition. Once the AI solution itself had produced some results, combined with factors like headcount reduction, business efficiency pressure, and the government's innovation needs, the project finally got the opportunity for formal approval.

So in this project, trust was built through the results of interaction after interaction—knowing people alone is far from enough. After an FDE enters an organization, the first project may not be the ultimately most valuable one. Sometimes you have to start with a small entry point and do the first thing well before you can truly see the needs that lie ahead.

Q4 Compared with the traditional approach, what were the actual results after using AI?

First, efficiency. A full-process review of one business case used to take about 15 minutes; now it can basically be shortened to three to five minutes.

As review efficiency improved, the daily average caseload also rose from around 700–800 cases to about 1,100.

It should be noted that AI didn't create this demand out of thin air. Many people already had processing needs; they were simply constrained by review capacity in the past, and the business couldn't keep up. Only after AI released the capacity that was stuck in the manual review stage could this demand actually be served.

But I think what matters more than the numbers is how people's work changed. Staff used to spend a lot of time on repetitive tasks like opening images, checking documents, and entering information. After automation, this work decreased, and they could spend their time on answering public inquiries, new business, and things that require more human judgment and communication.

For example, when people come to the service hall, they have many questions that need explaining. Previously, back-office review consumed a lot of energy; now staff can devote more time to serving the public. So the essence of this is reallocating people's time—making one person review faster is only the surface benefit.

For the partner providing on-site services, the change was equally significant. With no increase in headcount—in fact, with fewer people—the overall caseload was still maintained and even grew slightly, giving the service model, which had previously been under pressure from high labor costs, better room to operate.

As for the vehicle administration office, the project ultimately passed acceptance smoothly, and more new business was handed to us afterward. Later, the two sides jointly established an AI innovation laboratory, and we continued to station personnel on-site to jointly develop new applications. In my view, this may say more about whether the project truly created value than any single technical metric. The true mark of a successful AI project is that after acceptance, the client is still willing to do a second and third project with you.

Q5 During the AI deployment, did you encounter new problems or challenges? How did you solve them?

If I had to summarize the hardest part of this project, it was organization and rules—algorithms actually come second.

The first challenge was that the document data was too non-standard, and the recognition rate wouldn't go up.

The vehicle administration office's documents aren't as clean as lab datasets. Common documents like ID cards, contracts, and invoices appear in all kinds of unexpected forms when actually uploaded. These problems were relatively clear-cut, but not easy to solve. When the recognition rate wasn't good enough, we collected more samples; when negative samples were insufficient, we added negative samples; when parameters weren't right, we kept optimizing. These problems were troublesome, but they basically all had engineering solutions—the real difficulty came later.

The second challenge was that AI exposed the "leniency" that had existed in past manual reviews.

Once automated review was running, we found that some business cases had been subject to a certain degree of "leniency" in past manual reviews. At that point, the question shifted from "can the model recognize it" to "what should actually be done once it's recognized."

If we enforced technical standards strictly, it could affect the existing business volume and cooperative relationships; if we maintained the previous loose standards, the automated review would lose its meaning. This is not something the technical team can decide on its own. Our approach was to objectively present the problems and data to the client—which documents had issues, how many cases could be processed per day under different review standards, and how business volume would change—and then let the client make the final decision based on actual operations and management requirements.

After running for a while, once everyone had real business data, we gradually found the balance point among efficiency, compliance, and business volume. Here, the FDE needs to be very clear about their boundaries. Our responsibility is to build the technical capability, surface the problems, and clearly explain the possible outcomes of different choices; but final decisions involving business interests and management rules must be left to the business owner.

The third challenge was organizational coordination.

When a project first starts, you may only deal with one department and one person in charge. But once implementation truly begins, it involves different departments, different roles, and even external partners. Once informatization, digitalization, and intelligentization genuinely change processes, they will inevitably affect some people's existing work and interests.

So beyond doing technical work, an FDE must also know how to communicate, coordinate, and manage projects, and even how to "do the interest math." You need to let different roles see what this change means for them—who will benefit, whose work will change, and who has the final decision-making authority when conflicts arise. If these issues aren't handled well, even the best model will struggle to truly enter production.

Q6 Based on your experience, how can we better deploy AI into real business?

After finishing this project, one thing gradually became certain in my mind: AI applications must grow from within the business—things forcibly layered on from the outside rarely survive.

Now when I visit many companies to exchange ideas, everyone talks about having "AI anxiety." The boss knows AI is important, and the IT department knows AI is important, but when you ask "what specific problem do you want to solve," many people actually can't articulate it. In that situation, if an FDE immediately starts talking about agents, RAG, and large models, it doesn't mean much.

Nowadays I've gotten into the habit of first asking a company a question: can you list the ten most important problems in your business operations?

If you can list those ten problems, we can then judge together which ones can be solved through digitalization, which through intelligence/AI, which are worth investing in now, and which ones—even if AI can do them—don't have a viable return on investment. That's the real first step an FDE should take. So I believe that when an FDE enters a company, they should first act more like a consultant, and only secondarily as a development engineer.

First, find the real problem before discussing AI.

Don't start from "what technology do I know" to look for applications; start from the problems that genuinely occur in the business every day. To judge whether a problem is worth solving, I look at a few very practical indicators: does it happen frequently, does it affect efficiency, revenue,

cost, or service quality, does the business department itself have the will to solve it, and is there a budget? Only when these conditions hold does an AI project have a real foundation for implementation.

Second, enter through a small entry point; don't start with something "big and comprehensive."

My entry point into the vehicle administration office was, at first, merely a legacy system operations and maintenance project. But that small project got me on-site and let me truly see how the business runs. So don't underestimate the value of the first small project. The point of a small entry point is to build trust and to gain the opportunity to observe the business. Only after doing the first thing well can you gradually move into more core processes and see problems you could never see in a meeting room.

Third, an FDE must stay close to the front line for the long term.

Many requirements can't be uncovered in a single interview. The material review problem in this case was something I observed bit by bit during operations work. At first, the client didn't even think it was a problem that particularly needed solving. That's why we now also station people at client sites—because only by staying close to the front line can you know what staff actually do every day, what takes the most time, and which processes everyone has grown accustomed to but which may be exactly the places most worth transforming with AI.

Fourth, don't just build AI as a tool; embed it into the business process.

The real business value lies in whether data can flow automatically after recognition, whether the process can continue to execute, who handles exceptions, and whether the original staff have to perform an extra step. If AI just adds another page and someone still has to manually copy the results and paste them into another system, its value is greatly diminished. AI truly lands when it becomes part of the business process, with no separate AI tool sitting alongside it.

Fifth, organizational capability often determines a project's success more than technical capability.

Technical capability is of course important. Without basic technical skills, you can't take on the work at all. But in real projects, technology increasingly resembles a basic skill. What truly creates the gap is the combination of communication, management, coordination, requirements analysis, and business understanding.

Because what an FDE faces has never been just the model itself; behind it are clients, managers, front-line staff, partners, budgets, processes, stakeholder interests, and the organization's internal decision-making mechanisms.

What an FDE really needs to build is a bridge between business and technology. Technology is only one end of the bridge; the other end is business problems, organizational relationships, and real value. Only when both ends are truly connected has AI completed the final step from "technical capability" to "business capability."

From Desk Work to Intelligent Collaboration: An FDE Uses AI to Optimize Litigation Lawyers' Case Handling

Q1 As an FDE, what is the specific scenario where you apply AI? What did the specific problems in this scenario look like?

We serve litigation lawyer teams, mainly handling complex cases such as commercial contract disputes and corporate equity disputes.

After taking on a case, a lawyer goes through a complete, lengthy process: understanding client needs, reading large volumes of case materials, sorting out factual relationships, finding legal grounds, designing litigation strategy, and finally producing various legal documents.

On the surface, this is a knowledge-intensive industry. But after observing closely, I found that lawyers spend a huge amount of time every day on desk work.

First, case materials are massive and highly unstructured. A typical commercial litigation case may contain dozens of material types—contracts, chat records, financial vouchers, evidence documents—with total page counts often running into the thousands. Lawyers need to read, annotate, and categorize them one by one; material organization alone can take 3 to 5 working days. For complex cases involving a dozen or more parties, just sorting out the relationships among people takes two or three days.

Second, the barrier to translating legal language is high. The parties' original descriptions are often everyday colloquial speech, and lawyers need to distill them into legally meaningful element facts and then match them to the corresponding basis for claims. This translation process depends entirely on the individual lawyer's legal foundation; the efficiency gap between junior and senior lawyers can be 3 to 5 times.

Third, case information requires continuous maintenance. Litigation cycles usually last several months or even over a year, during which new evidence and

points of dispute keep emerging. Lawyers must continuously maintain a complete understanding of the case, and every time they re-enter a case they spend a lot of time restoring memory and reviewing previous lines of analysis.

I gradually realized that the bottleneck in lawyers' productivity is not professional judgment but the efficiency of information processing. They are both decision-makers and executors trapped by desk work. To improve lawyers' overall case-handling capability, you can't just have AI answer isolated legal questions; you need AI to enter the lawyer's complete workflow and become a continuously collaborating partner.

Q2 Before you got involved, how was this problem solved in the past?

The legal industry already had some tools.

Legal databases (such as Beida Fabao and Wolters Kluwer) can look up statutes and precedents, document management systems help lawyers archive files, and there were also some early legal Q&A tools attempting to assist in generating simple text.

But these tools only solved two isolated actions: "lookup" and "generation." Real cases are constantly changing; lawyers need to return to the same case repeatedly to supplement analysis and update judgments. Traditional legal databases are designed around one-off queries, which is completely disconnected from lawyers' actual working rhythm.

The standard way of working in the past was like this: after receiving case materials, the lawyer read and organized them manually; converted natural language descriptions into legal professional language; searched legal databases for relevant grounds; formed judgments based on personal experience; and finally produced reports, litigation materials, or strategy recommendations. The entire process relied heavily on the individual lawyer's brain and experience.

Over the past decade, law firms have also tried introducing more digital tools, but implementation results have generally been disappointing.

There are two reasons: first, each tool only solves a single-point problem, so lawyers have to switch among five or six systems, case context gets fragmented, and no coherent workflow can form; second, there is a disconnect between these tools' usage barriers and lawyers' natural way of working—lawyers have to learn specific search syntax or operation logic, adding extra burden.

In the end, most tools degenerated into aids for looking up statutes, never truly integrating into the complete case-handling process.

The fundamental flaw of traditional solutions is the lack of a role that can understand both technology and the business front line, translating what lawyers truly need into an implementable system.

Q3 What was your overall approach and the key steps in using AI to solve this problem?

The first step was entering the business front line to understand users.

After the project started, I didn't design features right away; instead, I first entered the lawyers' real working environment. Our observation of the law firm's litigation team has been ongoing, because litigation is a very complex matter, and we bring back various anonymized materials from the field to study. We also conduct in-depth interviews with partner lawyers at a very high frequency. From these interviews we learn what they want, where they want to save time, and where they wouldn't feel at ease even if time were saved. This is highly instructive for our system design—what to leave for humans to decide and what to let AI directly handle and deliver—all centered on real needs.

Starting from nine in the morning when lawyers turn on their computers, we observed how they receive client emails, how they leaf through paper case files,

how to switch between multiple systems to find information. I sat in on their case discussion meetings and took notes on how partners broke down a seemingly simple contract dispute. Their discussion focused not just on the legal provisions themselves, but on how to determine the parties' true intent from WeChat chat records.

I would have in-depth conversations with lawyers, asking what steps a case actually goes through from start to finish, what deliverables each step requires, who these deliverables ultimately go to, and what kind of result is truly usable.

Behind this, I held on to one principle: "deliver value." What the AI needs to generate is a result that a lawyer can use directly and deliver to clients, courts, or other professionals.

The second step was to re-deconstruct the case workflow.

We didn't change the way lawyers originally worked; instead, we looked for places within the existing workflow where AI could be embedded. Take early case preparation as an example: previously, lawyers had to manually read through thousands of pages of materials and then compile a case summary by hand—a step AI can now assist with; previously, lawyers had to manually convert parties' colloquial descriptions into legal language before searching—a step AI can now assist with; previously, lawyers had to manually map out the relationships between evidence, people, and legal issues—a step AI can now also assist with. Through this fairly detailed mapping, we ultimately formed 7 standard modules and high-quality legal reasoning, allowing repetitive but high-value work to be automated.

The third step was designing how AI and humans collaborate.

On the technical side, we adopted an architecture where large language models and specialized systems divide the work. The large language model handles natural language understanding, text summarization, and preliminary legal reasoning—it excels at extracting key information from unstructured materials and understanding lawyers' complex needs expressed in natural language. The specialized systems manage the case knowledge graph, structured retrieval of legal provisions, and precise matching of precedents, ensuring the accuracy and traceability of legal citations. The two are integrated through a unified case workspace, so what lawyers experience is a seamless, unified collaboration.

In our design, AI handles the bulk of information processing, analysis, and repetitive work, the specialized systems carry the case context and knowledge workflow, and the lawyers are responsible for final judgment, strategy selection, and professional accountability.

What we aim for is a state where lawyers don't need to learn complex AI techniques or prompt engineering—they just need to express their needs in their own professional language. For example: "Based on these case materials, help me analyze the next litigation strategy," "Help me prepare an evidence list," "Help me draft a report for the client"—and the AI handles the work behind the scenes.

The fourth step was continuous adjustment based on on-site feedback.

Our initial design featured a smart dialog box where lawyers could ask the AI questions directly after logging in. But after observing on-site for a while, a key problem emerged: before every question, lawyers had to re-describe the case background to the AI.

A single case contains a huge amount of information, and lawyers can't possibly recap it fully in every conversation. Conversational interaction was actually adding to lawyers' burden.

So we adjusted the design: changing the product entry point from a chat window to a case workspace. Lawyers first upload case materials, the system automatically builds a case knowledge graph, and the AI interacts with lawyers on the basis of fully understanding the case context. Lawyers no longer need to explain the background every time—they just state specific needs in professional language. This adjustment significantly lowered the barrier to entry for lawyers and helped them accept AI's involvement faster.

Q4: Compared to traditional methods, what are the actual results of using AI?

The changes AI brings are first reflected in efficiency metrics.

One case involved 14 defendants. Under the old approach, analyzing the basis of claims for one defendant took 2 to 3 hours, and completing all of them took 2 to 3 days. With AI assistance, the lawyer handed the case materials to the system, and work that would have taken days was completed in a single evening.

Another case left a deep impression on me. A lawyer took on a case at the last minute with only 3 days until the court hearing, but the case materials exceeded 1 million words. Relying entirely on manual reading, he likely wouldn't have fully grasped the whole case before the hearing. With AI assistance, he completed an overall review of the case in one day—including relationships between people, legal relationships, and the case's overall thread—and went on to develop a hearing strategy. Material preparation efficiency improved by about 70% compared to purely manual work.

But efficiency metrics are only the surface-level results; the more important changes happened at the process level.

In the past, lawyers spent enormous amounts of time on execution work: reading materials, organizing information, searching legal provisions. Now, AI helps lawyers complete these foundational tasks, freeing lawyers to focus their energy on higher-value work: in-depth communication with clients, judging the direction of a case, developing strategy, and making final professional decisions. The lawyer's role is shifting from executor to decision-maker.

In the long run, the deeper value lies in making professional expertise replicable. In the past, an outstanding lawyer's personal experience was hard to transfer to the team, and differences in experience across lawyers led to inconsistent delivery quality. Now, through AI systems, the professional methods of excellent lawyers can be systematically codified, giving more lawyers access to reliable support. This is the true meaning of FDE work: it improves the efficiency of individual projects while making professional expertise reusable and scalable.

Q5: Did you encounter new problems or challenges during AI deployment? How did you solve them?

The first challenge was structuring tacit knowledge.

The difficulty of the legal industry goes beyond knowledge itself. Often, the key lies in how lawyers think: What facts deserve attention? How do you convert a layperson's description into legal language? Which legal bases truly fit? How do different pieces of evidence form logical relationships? These are all forms of tacit knowledge that lawyers have developed through years of practice.

Our approach was to invest significant time working alongside excellent lawyers, observing their thought processes and deconstructing the decision logic behind their judgments. Specifically, we mapped out the thinking frameworks lawyers commonly use in case analysis—such as the "three layers, four steps" method for analyzing the basis of claims and the logical rules for building chains of evidence—and converted these frameworks into process nodes the system can execute. This enables the AI to organize information the way lawyers think professionally, going beyond mere information output.

The second challenge was defining the product form.

Many AI products take the form of a chat window, but that form doesn't fit legal case scenarios. A case is more like a long-term project that needs to continuously hold large volumes of materials, process records, and evolving information—a single conversation simply can't contain it.

As mentioned earlier, we changed the product entry point from a dialog box to a case workspace. Behind this lies a key design principle: let users create a case first, then interact. All analysis results, strategy recommendations, and document drafts are accumulated in the case space, forming a traceable work record. Lawyers don't need to re-explain the background every time, and the AI doesn't need to repeatedly rebuild its understanding. This design transformed the AI from a one-off tool into a collaborative system that accompanies the entire lifecycle of a case.

The last one was changing user habits.

At first, many lawyers entering the product would instinctively look for an input box, wondering "Where do I type my question?"—because everyone was already used to chat-style AI. But after actually using it, they discovered: if the AI already understands the case background and they don't have to keep describing their questions, it's actually more efficient.

Our response was not to force users to change their habits. On first use, we guide them to upload materials first, letting the AI demonstrate the value it can provide once it understands the case background. When lawyers see the AI automatically generated case summary and the automatically mapped relationship diagram, they naturally understand the logic of creating a case before interacting. In this process, we found that letting users see the value with their own eyes is more effective than any explanation.

Q6: Based on your experience, how can we better deploy AI into real business operations?

First, start from business problems and define requirements in business language.

When many teams start working on AI, they first ask "what models do we have" or "what capabilities can we call." But the real questions should be: "Who will use it?" "Why do they need it?" "What is their actual workflow?" Without understanding the business, you cannot judge which problems are worth solving. Without understanding the technology, you cannot find the right approach. In this project, if we had started from model capabilities, we might have built a legal knowledge Q&A tool—but that would only address a tiny sliver of a lawyer's work, and would never truly change how lawyers work.

Second, go into the field and define the problem together with industry experts.

An FDE must go into the field. You cannot understand users through imagination; you must truly observe how they work. During the time we worked closely with lawyers and observed them at work, we found that what we had imagined was not what was actually needed. More importantly, you must work alongside industry experts and find people who truly understand the business. When you don't understand an industry, you have no right to define its problems on its behalf. In this project, the mental frameworks of excellent lawyers were a core input to the AI system's design—without their deep involvement, the system could never be truly useful.

Third, care about implementation details more than model capabilities.

The success of an AI project depends on more dimensions than just technical performance. Will users actually use it? Is the process natural? Is the cost acceptable? Can the results actually be delivered? These questions matter more than model capability itself.

The value of an FDE lies in connecting business, technology, and users, so that AI truly enters enterprise processes and creates value. AI is only the means; changing the way people work is the goal.

I believe an FDE cannot start from scratch and customize for every project. A truly valuable FDE needs to abstract experience from each business implementation into reusable methods, modules, and capabilities, making the next project more efficient. This is also the key to scaling the FDE role in the AI era.

From Manual Analysis to Intelligent Scheduling: An FDE Rebuilds Urban Planning Analysis with AI

Q1 As an FDE, what is your specific AI use case? What do the specific problems in this scenario look like?

The urban planning industry is quite special in itself. Planning and design is not as simple as getting a table, running a few analyses, and generating a report—behind it lies a highly complex professional division of labor.

For example, to plan a temporary hospital, the overall planning department first determines roughly which district it should be located in; then you need to look at what planning constraints and rules apply to that area; and when it comes down to a specific plot, you must further consider a whole series of factors like roads, water, municipal infrastructure, public services, and industry.

So the people we actually deal with span roughly 10 departments.

What makes it even more complex is that this project involves not only ordinary business data, but also spatial data.

In the past, when we worked on government, e-commerce, or other enterprise projects, we mainly dealt with two-dimensional business data—what something is called, what its attributes are, what its parameters are. But urban planning adds a layer of spatial relationships. For an industrial plot, you not only need to know its business attributes, but also its exact coordinates on the map and which surrounding roads or facilities it relates to.

On top of that, each department has its own professional tools, business semantics, data rules, and workflows.

So I later concluded that the real difficulty of this scenario is connecting four things: business, data, space, and tools.

And there is a fifth thing at the end: people.

Because people in the 10 departments work differently, and may even understand the same metric differently. You must clearly understand the business language, calculation rules, and workflows they actually use; otherwise, what the AI generates may look professional, but a real planner will spot the problems at a glance.

Why is this problem worth solving?

Because urban planning itself is changing. In the past, when cities were rapidly expanding, much planning involved making choices in incremental space; now it is more about finding optimal solutions within existing urban space and existing plots. Planning is becoming more and more refined, and the constraints involved in decisions keep increasing.

Meanwhile, planners' original workflow is very heavy.

A leader might say just one sentence: "Help me identify the potential land in the xx area." Then the people below have to start researching, pulling data, doing spatial analysis, running tools, and discussing, before finally producing a report.

So the opportunity we initially saw was whether we could first take over the part of planners' work that is repetitive, has relatively clear rules, and involves traceable data, freeing people's time for things that truly require judgment and creativity.

This later became my basic standard for judging whether a scenario is worth applying AI to: Does it happen repeatedly? Is the repeated cost high enough? Can the workflow be defined? Can the underlying facts and data be traced? Can the professional tools involved be invoked?

If none of these conditions exist, and you're just forcing a scenario for the sake of "AI First," I think it's unnecessary.

Q2 Before you got involved, how was this problem solved in the past?

In fact, the planning and design institutes were not poorly digitized to begin with. They don't lack software—the professional software they use is actually very complex. Planners themselves are also typical high-digital-literacy people.

In the past, after receiving a planning task, a planner would usually first research extensively, then move into GIS-type professional tools, overlay different layers, run scripts, do spatial calculations and annotations, and then form their department's judgment based on the results.

If the final output was a comprehensive planning report, the analyses from multiple departments also needed to be consolidated.

A single-person, single-item analysis used to take about an hour; a complete cross-department report typically took professionals 3–5 days, sometimes close to a week.

This time doesn't mean they were inefficient—the real reason is that urban planning demands extremely high precision.

Where a road is drawn, whether a plot touches a historical and cultural protection line, whether an area's sewage capacity is sufficient—none of these can be answered casually. Once a plan goes wrong, it may affect actual construction downstream, so many results must be repeatedly checked, revised, and discussed.

Take site selection, for example. The system might first filter out 20 candidate plots, but selecting the 10 truly suitable ones from those 20 used to require several rounds of meetings across different departments, with continued judgment based on various professional metrics.

So the traditional approach actually had its rationale: professionals master the rules, professional tools ensure calculation precision, and human experience is responsible for the final judgment.

The problem is that as the data, rules, and departments to consider keep growing, this process has become increasingly heavy. A large amount of professionals' time is consumed on researching, finding rules, switching tools, repeated calculations, and compiling reports.

This also shows that the institutes' digital tools were always there—it's just that they solved the calculation precision of individual steps, and no role truly connected business, data, space, and tools, so cross-department, cross-tool processes still relied on humans to string them together.

Therefore, when we did the AI transformation, we had no intention of overturning the traditional process. My thinking was actually the opposite: keep what was originally correct as much as possible, and take over the parts that machines are better at.

Q3 What was your overall approach and key steps in using AI to solve this problem?

One thing about this project that influenced me deeply was that it gradually clarified something for me: an FDE must first go into the field, and only then decide what to actually use.

Step one: force yourself to become "half a planner."

The urban planning industry is highly professional. When I first entered, they had many professional handbooks, very thick ones. If you don't know many of the terms, you simply can't keep talking with the business people.

For example, if someone asks you "what does candidate plot mean" or "what does potential land analysis mean," and you can't even answer these basic business concepts, it becomes very hard to build trust afterward.

So my requirement for myself at the time was to first become one of them. I didn't need to actually reach a planner's professional level, but I at least had to know what they do every day, why they do it, what a metric actually represents, and why a certain spatial calculation is done the way it is.

We would go to different departments to talk with them, gradually figuring out what their numbers mean, how they are calculated, their business metrics, and their workflows.

This step doesn't look like "doing AI," but I think it's precisely the core work of an FDE. Only by understanding the business do you know what should be handed to the model, what should go to specialized tools, and what must be left to humans.

The second step: don't tackle the whole city at first—shrink the problem until it's small enough.

If you connect all the city's data, all departments, all third-party systems, and all business rules from the start, the project easily sinks into an endless data engineering effort.

So we later deliberately narrowed the scope. From the entire city, down to one district, then down to a specific unit like Mishixiang, and then we picked just two clear business flows to validate.

I later felt this decision was extremely important: only when the scenario is small enough can the data possibly be cleaned up; only when the data is clean can the results be accurate enough; only when the results are accurate enough will business people trust them.

So in phase one we didn't connect all third-party systems and the data platform; we only had the departments participating in the pilot filter out the data they truly needed, and we governed the data for this small slice of the scenario.

The third step: build the three foundations—"data, knowledge, reports"—solidly first.

Why were we confident enough at the time to say the report generated in 20 minutes was "scrutinizable"? It mainly relied on three things underneath.

First is data. The key figures in the report come as much as possible from real data provided by the planning institute and are obtained through professional calculations—we don't let the model guess on its own.

Second is knowledge. We organized relevant planning rules, professional documents, and other knowledge, and used retrieval-augmented methods so the AI could find the basis. For example, why an office building can't be built at a certain location might be because it hits a historical and cultural preservation line. In the past, planners had to search through piles of PDFs for the relevant regulations; now they can locate the corresponding rule much faster.

Third is report templates. Different departments originally had their own reporting habits. We took each department's templates as well as industry templates and eventually distilled a relatively general output structure.

So in the end, the AI works within real data, professional rules, and a fixed report structure—it no longer writes out of thin air.

The fourth step: let the AI orchestrate specialized tools, leaving computation to the tools.

This was also a very important judgment for us later on. Many planning calculations actually have clear rules. Since specialized GIS tools can compute them precisely, there's no need to have the large model do the math itself.

So our division of labor gradually became clear: the model handles understanding and analysis, specialized tools handle facts and computation, and humans make the final decisions.

The AI is more like an orchestrator. For example, after a planner proposes a site-selection task, the AI understands the intent, then calls the corresponding data and specialized tools to complete the analysis, and returns the computed results.

For high-frequency tasks with clear rules, we prefer to solidify them into workflows; for long-tail, more open-ended questions like urban assessment or potential land analysis, we hand them to an AI agent.

The fifth step: the product should grow out of existing work habits.

We didn't turn everything into a chat box, because the true center of a planner's work is the map.

So the final interaction retained the way they were familiar with: filters and specialized tools on the left, the map in the middle, and the Copilot on the right. After the AI calls a tool or completes an analysis, the results are displayed directly on the map.

I later felt this was very important. It's easy for AI products to fall into a trap: since there's an agent, stuff every feature into a single input box. But an FDE has to consider how users actually worked before.

The map is the planner's real workbench, so the AI should serve around the map, letting planners keep working the way they always have, without having to learn a new way of working just for the AI.

Q4 Compared with the traditional approach, what were the actual results after using AI?

The change showed up first in time.

In the past, a complete planning report spanning multiple departments typically took 3–5 days, sometimes nearly a week. Now, in the pilot workflow that has been fully run through, a report that can be further refined and delivered takes about 10–20 minutes.

Looking at a single department only, some analyses and tables that used to take about an hour can now be done in roughly 5–10 minutes.

Phase one involves about 10 departments, and currently around 20–30 specialists are using it.

But I gradually came to realize that the project's real value goes far beyond "turning 3–5 days into 20 minutes." Two other changes are more important.

The first is that planners began to break free from a lot of repetitive operations. Previously, much of their time was spent looking up materials, finding rules, operating tools, and organizing results. Now that this work has been compressed, people can devote more energy to things that truly require professional judgment.

Moreover, the AI sometimes suggests potential analysis factors beyond the original workflow. For example, when judging a district's economic vitality, one might previously look at traditional indicators like transactions or traffic flow, but the AI might suggest also observing other signals such as parking lot gate frequency, housing vacancy rates, or population profiles. These suggestions aren't necessarily the answer themselves, but they can offer planners new research perspectives.

The second change is that the organization itself began to change. In phase one, we worked alongside different departments to sort out their workflows. Later, some departments had already started to solidify processes like "identify, compute, process, output candidate plots" into Skills on their own.

In other words, they were already learning how to build AI workflows themselves.

To me, this is what makes an FDE fundamentally different from traditional outsourcing. A good FDE project is one where, after the delivery ends, the customer starts growing their own AI capabilities.

Q5 During the AI deployment, did you encounter new problems or challenges? How did you solve them?

Of course, and many of the truly hard problems weren't in the model.

The first challenge: departments aren't necessarily willing to give you data and rules.

Among the 10 departments, some naturally supported AI, while others had reservations, worried that once this system went live it would affect their roles and value. So when we asked about business rules or requested data, some people responded very slowly, or even said outright: "All of this is online—look it up yourselves."

But in reality, many rules are known only to the people who actually do the business. At times like this, an FDE can't rely on technology alone.

I gradually figured out a pattern: in a complex organization like this, finding the "execution lead" who can truly drive the project is critical. The project sponsor might be a vice president, but the person who can actually coordinate the 10 departments, knows who is hardest to move, and knows who holds what data is often the person below in charge of overall coordination. We had to talk things through with that person first.

At the same time, I also built fault tolerance into the plan. If a department temporarily wouldn't provide data, I wouldn't let the whole project stall because of it. One fewer department meant one fewer scenario for now; one fewer type of data meant that part simply didn't participate in the calculations for now. But the overall pipeline had to be able to run end to end.

I set a very simple delivery principle for myself at the time: "one document, one map, one table" had to come out. Even with one node missing, the core deliverable form still held. This made me realize that when an FDE designs a solution, no link can be designed as "indispensable"—otherwise, if one department doesn't cooperate, the whole project gets dragged to a standstill.

The second challenge was the tension between model capability and data security. {{44} At the time, due to data security requirements, foreign models couldn't be used freely in the production environment. Similar issues came up again in later stages. For example, people would expect "multi-agent deliberation": legal experts,

In those cases, forcing a multi-agent setup not only makes the architecture more complex, but passing context between multiple agents can also introduce ambiguity, loss, and contamination, and the results actually fail to converge.

So our approach gradually evolved: on the surface you can present different professional perspectives, but underneath you don't necessarily need multiple agents chatting with each other. You can wrap different domain capabilities as tools, orchestrated by a single core agent. And for some optimization problems, a large model may not be the right choice at all—traditional optimization algorithms can be more precise.

This process made me increasingly convinced that the FDE's job is to help customers judge where AI should genuinely be used, and where it shouldn't.

Q6 Based on your experience, how can we better deploy AI into real business?

After finishing this project, my biggest shift in understanding the FDE role was that I no longer saw the FDE as, first and foremost, "someone who understands models."

First, get into the business.

If you're working on legal business, actually follow lawyers through their day; if you're working on urban planning, find a way to become "half a planner." Many companies' stated problems aren't the real problems. Only by walking through their workflow with them will you discover which tasks repeat every day, where the truly expensive costs are, and which seemingly minor steps actually determine the final outcome.

Second, don't start big.

Our project could have opened with a grand story: the entire city, all data, all planning scenarios fully intelligentized. But what actually got the project moving was constant narrowing. From a whole city down to a district, then to a specific unit, then picking two workflows. First find a scenario that's small enough, real enough, and capable of forming a closed loop; once it runs end to end, then expand.

Third, don't worship technical sophistication.

Customers want problems solved, not technical frameworks. If hardcoding solves it, hardcode it; if a fixed workflow suits the current task, use a workflow; if the task is more flexible and an agent suits open-ended tasks, use an agent; if the task requires fine granularity and traditional algorithms are more precise than a large model, use traditional algorithms.

What I value more now is how models, data, tools, people, and evals combine into a genuinely deliverable system—especially evals and fallbacks. Demos are easy to build; the hard part is: who catches bad data? What if the model gives wrong advice? Can computation results be traced? And who is ultimately accountable for decisions? If these questions aren't solved, even the coolest Demo won't make it into real business.

Fourth, understand that the FDE's own role also changes.

In this project I actually went through three stages. At the start, I was like a newcomer and assistant, first learning their industry and becoming one of them. Once I understood the business, I began helping them redefine problems, design processes, and judge where AI was a good fit. Later, when they themselves could build Skills, do VibeCoding, and try building their own AI applications, my role changed again. At that point I didn't necessarily need to write every feature for them; I could step up to a higher level and tell them what pitfalls lay ahead, how to choose an architecture, and which directions were worth investing in.

So now I'm more certain that what an FDE ultimately does is accompany a company through re-examining its own business, finding the real problems, turning business knowledge into executable processes, and then placing AI where it fits best. We should dig in like "bamboo"—and when one day the customer starts understanding AI, transforming their workflows, and can even build new Skills themselves, the project has truly gone from a one-time technical delivery to a continuously growing AI capability inside the enterprise.

From Needle-in-a-Haystack to Full-Pipeline Efficiency: An FDE Uses AI to Rebuild TikTok Influencer Outreach and Fulfillment

Q1 As an FDE, what specific scenario do you apply AI in? What did the concrete problems in that scenario look like?

The business scenario of this project was influencer outreach for a leading domestic e-commerce company within the overseas TikTok ecosystem.

If you understand it through domestic e-commerce experience, a TAP is a bit like a "group leader."

When merchants have new products or marketing events and want to quickly find a large number of influencers to sell for them, they first go to a TAP; the TAP then selects suitable people from its influencer resources, pushing them to accept products, receive samples, and create content, ultimately listing the products on the influencers' accounts. This role solves a very real problem: there are too many products on TikTok for influencers to browse the marketplace one by one, and it's hard to judge what's worth selling from product info alone, so the TAP needs to organize merchants and influencers.

When we engaged with this customer, they hadn't actually defined the problem clearly at the outset. When they first approached me, they asked a very broad question: "How can our team use AI for empowerment?" So instead of answering directly, I turned it around and asked them: how does your business actually run today? Which step is slowest? Who is most exhausted every day?

Where are errors most likely to occur?

Later we went on-site, interviewing nearly everyone from the boss to department heads to each team lead. Through these interviews, I found they were already aware their efficiency was low, but their previous "digitalization" was mostly just "advanced teams using Feishu first"—at the actual business level, a large amount of work was still done manually. Looking at the whole business together, I found the problem ran through several stages.

First was influencer outreach—finding people was hard.

Operations staff had to search for and screen influencers on TikTok, then proactively message them. It used to be like "fishing for a needle in the ocean": operations had to find people themselves, review influencer profiles, judge whether they were suitable partners, and then communicate.

Second was product matching—matching was hard.

The customer's own product pool contained tens of thousands of products, while influencers had different content tags—some were fitness influencers, some lifestyle influencers, some creators of history or philosophy content. Which products should be pushed to which influencers is fundamentally a matching problem.

Third was fulfillment—long cycles.

After an influencer confirmed cooperation, there were still steps like shipping samples, receiving samples, coordinating filming, creating videos, and submitting to TikTok.

The original average fulfillment cycle was about a month. Operations staff spent a huge amount of time "nagging": Has your sample arrived? When will you film? When will you submit? Isn't the deadline coming up?

Fourth was data management—too scattered.

This is actually a more fundamental problem. Some of the data lives on TikTok, some in Excel, and some in the finance system. Business development staff, fulfillment personnel, and finance each maintain their own data, so it's not easy for the boss to figure out what a creator actually goes through from initial outreach to ultimately generating GMV (Gross Merchandise Volume).

So I ultimately concluded that the entire business pipeline had reached the stage where it needed to be reorganized.

Q2 Before you got involved, how was this problem solved in the past?

When the business was still small, this process relied mainly on people. An operations person managed dozens of creators per day and could remember who was at what stage of conversation, who had received samples, and who hadn't shot a video yet. Combined with Feishu and Excel, the business could run normally.

Creator outreach itself also relied heavily on person-to-person communication. Especially for creators with large followings, even those approaching celebrity status, business communication involves many subtle nuances, which in the past were mainly judged and handled by experienced BD staff.

The real problem was that once business complexity increased, this approach started hitting bottlenecks. For example, when the product pool grew from a few hundred products to tens of thousands, operations could no longer rely on experience to match them one by one; as creators multiplied, it became impossible to manually comb through Excel every day to find who needed follow-up; and once data was scattered across different systems, managers could hardly see the whole business funnel in a timely manner.

My understanding is that the old approach solved the problem of "whether the business can run," while AI solves the problem of "whether the business can still run efficiently once it scales."

Q3 What was your overall approach and the key steps in using AI to solve this problem?

The first thing I did was map out the business process. From creator onboarding, to creator outreach, to product matching, then to sampling, content creation, and fulfillment, and finally to data review, I broke down the entire pipeline and examined it: who does each step? What are the inputs? What are the outputs? What judgments are needed? Which tasks must be done by humans? Which tasks can actually be handed to machines?

After breaking it down, I found that not every stage is suitable for AI, so our approach was to redraw the division of labor between AI and humans.

Step 1: Let AI first do "finding the right people"

Creator outreach is a typical example. We can use data such as product information, creator tags, and content attributes to first filter out a batch of creators most worth the operations team's attention.

For example, what are a product's main selling points, and what audience does it suit; what type of content does a creator make, and who is their audience. The system first filters out highly matched candidates and gives BD staff a more precise list, so operations no longer have to "search for a needle in a haystack" across all of TikTok.

But for the final steps—sending messages, communicating, and building relationships—we still keep humans in the loop, because creators are the client's most core asset.

So AI's role here is to shift BD staff from searching for targets to communicating directly with targets.

Step 2: Use AI to solve the matching problem of "which product goes to which creator." In the past, operations had to decide which products to pitch based on creator tags and their own experience; now we extract the product's own selling points and match them against the creator's content attributes.

For example, a coffee product's selling points might include flavor, carbonation, brand, and co-branding; if a creator focuses on career and lifestyle content, then the product may match their content attributes well. AI can complete this first round of filtering, followed by human confirmation—turning what used to be a heavily experience-based initial screening into "AI screens first, humans judge after."

Step 3: Use AI to help creators complete content creation, solving the fulfillment motivation problem. This is the part I find most interesting. Often, creators aren't unwilling to sell products—their real sticking point is that after receiving the product, they don't know "how to shoot it." Because a product video isn't just a simple product introduction; it needs to account for the creator's own style of expression, the product's selling points, and what kind of video content is currently more likely to attract attention.

So we combine these factors: creator characteristics \+ product selling points \+ references to viral content. After analysis, the system provides the creator with several optional shooting scripts. This essentially helps creators complete part of the creative work—an "altruistic" capability for creators, not just TAP improving its own efficiency. In interviews, the client reported that creators currently have a fairly high level of acceptance for this capability.

On this basis, in the future we can further explore AI video generation, giving creators an additional content production method beyond appearing on camera themselves. However, for creators built around a personal IP, we still believe that the real person and real content are the core.

Step 4: Turn "manually chasing people every day" into proactive system reminders. This is the part of the project where efficiency gains are easiest to see. In the past, operations staff had to open Excel every day to see which creators had reached their deadlines, then chase them one by one.

Now the system automatically judges based on fulfillment status: which creators have received samples, who hasn't finished, which tasks are approaching their deadlines, and which have passed system reminders with still no action.

The system sends automatic reminders first; if the creator still hasn't fulfilled, the task is handed to human operations. This way, people no longer need to dig through several spreadsheets every day for work—the system tells you directly: "Here's what you actually need to handle today." This is also, in my understanding, an important difference between AI deployment and ordinary automation: AI deployment is about reorganizing the way people work.

Step 5: Reconnect the scattered data. We also gradually brought data on creator outreach, fulfillment, products, GMV, and more into a unified business view.

What managers really need to see is: how many new creators were added today, how many were contacted, how many entered fulfillment, how much GMV they generated, how much sample cost was invested in a creator, and how much value they ultimately brought to the company—only then can you truly judge whether a creator is worth a long-term partnership.

So from start to finish, I prefer to understand this project as: AI embedded itself into the entire business process.

Q4 Compared with the traditional approach, what were the actual results after using AI?

The most direct change was a shortened fulfillment cycle. In the past, from confirming a partnership to the creator receiving samples, completing creation, and submitting the video took about a month on average; after the project's trial run, the average fulfillment time for collaborating creators is now about 18 days. But to me, this number is not the project's biggest value.

More importantly, the way the whole team works has changed. Previously, operations staff spent a lot of time finding people, finding products, and finding tasks; now AI screens creators for them first, the system matches products first, AI assists creators with content creation, the system automatically reminds about fulfillment, and the system proactively surfaces tasks that need human intervention.

As a result, operations staff can spend more time where humans are truly needed: communicating with creators, building relationships, and handling complex business issues. This change is especially noticeable for high-value creators—in the past, operations might have spent enormous energy on searching and screening; now they can focus that energy on the business relationships that truly matter.

Another change is that managers are finally truly seeing the business. Previously, it was hard for the boss to see what happened at each step from creator outreach to GMV; now, at minimum, a more complete business view can be formed around "creator—fulfillment—GMV."

So if I had to summarize the value of this project, I would say: the team used to manage a pile of spreadsheets; now they manage a business pipeline.

Q5 During the AI deployment process, did you encounter new problems or challenges? How did you solve them?

Of course, and I think these problems are precisely the most shareable part of an FDE case.

That's also why we ultimately didn't build "fully automated influencer outreach."

Many companies' first reaction upon hearing "AI" is "is this going to replace people?" In this project we were very mindful of this. So I communicated with all levels—top, middle, and frontline: the boss told us the business goals, and frontline employees told us where they actually got stuck every day. Only by piecing together information from both sides could the system truly land. That's also why I keep emphasizing a core FDE capability: making the system truly become part of how the enterprise works.

After phase one was done, the client naturally had phase-two requirements, because once the first problem is solved, the enterprise discovers the next layer of problems. The way I see it, an FDE is ultimately someone who stands on the business front line, finds the place where AI can genuinely create value, and keeps pushing it forward. That's also what I find most valuable about this project: we didn't try to prove that AI can do everything. Only when that boundary is drawn correctly does AI truly start to become business productivity.

From Experience-Based Loading to Intelligent Planning: An FDE Uses AI to Optimize Cross-Border Logistics Loading Efficiency

Q1 As an FDE, what is the specific scenario where you applied AI? What did the concrete problems in this scenario look like?

The application scenario in this case is truck loading optimization in China-Russia cross-border trade in the Heihe Free Trade Zone.

Many local companies export electromechanical products to Russia by road. In cross-border logistics, transportation cost is a major concern for these companies, and every cross-border truck trip is essentially a fixed cost investment.

Many local companies export electromechanical products to Russia by road. Cross-border freight is charged by cubic meter, which means this is essentially a business about "cargo compartment volume."

There's a very hard economic line here: a truck of about 110 cubic meters, loaded manually by an experienced driver, usually takes 5 hours and can be filled to 95–98 cubic meters; and 98 cubic meters is the break-even line. More importantly, every cubic meter above the break-even line brings an extra $100 in freight revenue. So loading rate isn't a technical metric—it's a profit switch: below 98 cubic meters you lose money; the fuller the load and the further past the break-even line, the more you earn.

Simply put, a truck pays the same transportation cost no matter how much cargo it carries, including driver costs, fuel costs, border-crossing fees, and so on. If a truck is sent out only partially loaded, the remaining space is wasted, but the transportation cost doesn't go down.

Therefore, for these companies, increasing per-truck load and making full use of the cargo space means the same trip can carry more goods, lowering the logistics cost per item and improving overall profit.

But in real business, "filling the truck" is far more complicated than simply stuffing more things into the vehicle—it is essentially a complex spatial optimization problem.

When I first got involved in this project, many people assumed it was just a simple packing problem—that calculating how to arrange the cargo would improve the loading rate. But once I actually went on-site, I found things were far more complicated than expected.

In the past, companies relied mainly on experienced veteran loaders to load trucks. Based on cargo dimensions, quantities, and past experience, they would judge: which cargo should go where; which spaces could still be used; and how to arrange things to fit more.

But as cross-border trade scaled up, this approach gradually exposed problems.

First, inaccurate judgment of vehicle space.

Much of the vehicle information provided by the Russian side refers to the exterior dimensions of the cargo compartment, but the usable interior space is affected by factors such as compartment wall thickness and insulation layers, so the actual usable space is often 5%–8% less than the theoretical figures.

Second, low space utilization.

In the past, there was no standardized 3D planning solution, and loading relied mainly on human experience. For the same batch of cargo, different loaders could get different results, and overall space utilization was typically only around 60%–65%, leaving a lot of space unused.

Third, in complex scenarios it is hard for humans to find the optimal solution.

For example, when companies face different vehicle types, cargo of different sizes, and multiple vehicles being scheduled for transport simultaneously, they need to consider multiple factors at once—space, weight, transport efficiency, and more—which already exceeds what human experience can handle.

But during my research, I found that the most critical problem was actually that solutions couldn't be implemented once computed: being able to calculate the optimal solution doesn't mean it can be executed on-site.

Many algorithmic solutions can tell companies the theoretically optimal arrangement, but when workers actually execute on-site, they run into many problems: where should loading start? Is the loading sequence reasonable? Does it require working inside the compartment? Does the plan meet on-site safety requirements?

So this problem is essentially a comprehensive one that connects the business site, algorithmic capability, and execution processes—far more than just algorithm optimization itself.

Solving the technical problem is only the first step; what really needs to be done is to get AI into the business process and generate actual value.

Q2 Before you got involved, how was this problem solved in the past?

Before I got involved, companies mainly relied on experienced loaders for planning, but it took longer.

Loading one truck usually took 5–6 hours and didn't achieve optimal loading. Loaders essentially earn their income through physical labor and time, so optimizing this way can improve loading efficiency.

Typically, veteran loaders would judge based on accumulated experience: which cargo should go on the bottom; which items could be combined; which spaces could be utilized.

This approach worked when the business scale was small, but as cross-border trade grew, the problems became increasingly obvious.

First, experience cannot be replicated.

The experience of an excellent loader takes years to accumulate, and newcomers can hardly master it quickly.

Second, humans cannot solve complex combination problems.

With just a few dozen items, humans can still make judgments. But as vehicle types, cargo varieties, and quantities keep increasing, it becomes very hard for humans to quickly find the optimal solution.

In addition, there is another easily overlooked problem: many companies have tried digital tools in the past, but the results were not good.

The reason is that these tools usually only solve the calculation problem without considering actual execution.

For example, the system generates a complex 3D plan, but on-site workers can't understand it, and things end up back with human experience.

So the algorithm itself is not hard to obtain; what's really lacking is a role that connects technology and the field.

Q3 What was your overall approach and key steps in using AI to solve this problem?

The starting point of the entire solution was to first re-understand the business; technical development comes later.

As an FDE, the first step is always to go on-site.

I needed to figure out first: how do workers actually load? Why do they load that way? Which steps affect efficiency? What can machines replace? What must conform to human habits?

Through on-site observation, I found that the loading process can actually be broken down into several steps: obtaining the vehicle's real dimensions, organizing cargo information, calculating the loading plan, optimizing multi-vehicle scheduling, generating loading instructions, on-site execution, and result feedback.

Among these, the earlier planning and calculation steps are suitable for AI involvement, but ultimately it must come back to human execution.

In terms of solution design, we adopted an approach of "large model for orchestration, specialized algorithms for computation."

Many people mistakenly assume that a large model can directly solve all problems, but in reality 3D bin packing involves complex spatial computation, which large models are not well suited for.

So our design approach was to let the large model understand human needs and be responsible for calling different tools, while specialized algorithms perform the deterministic calculations.

Specifically, the large model is responsible for: understanding the questions raised by business staff; parsing task requirements; calling the packing algorithm; and explaining the final results.

The actual 3D spatial computation is done by Python algorithms, including heightmap-based 3D space tracking, greedy placement,

multi-strategy sorting, and genetic algorithms.

In addition, during actual implementation, we made one very important adjustment.

Initially, we wanted workers to view the plans through a 3D visualization system.

But after on-site research, we found that workers in the loading environment are not suited to frequently operating phones or computers.

So in the end we converted the algorithm results into a simpler form: a loading instruction chart that workers can use directly. It includes cargo numbers, placement orientation, cross-section diagrams, and loading sequence.

This turns the algorithm results into actions that can actually be executed on-site.

Q4 Compared with traditional methods, what are the actual results after using AI?

The project has now completed prototype validation. During testing, we simulated loading on a real transport vehicle 16.4 meters long, 2.47 meters wide, and 2.67 meters high (about 108 cubic meters of net interior space), combined with a catalog of 11 different specifications of electric vehicles.

The results show that, without relying on veteran loaders, the AI system stably achieved about 99 cubic meters in real tests, approaching the compartment's volume limit; more importantly, it compressed a process that originally required a veteran's 5 hours of experience-based judgment with fluctuating results into a minute-level, reusable standard plan, turning "every truck stably exceeding the 98-cubic-meter break-even line" from something dependent on people into something dependent on the system.

And at the on-site execution level, the value is even more obvious: manually loading one truck usually takes about 5 hours; with AI planning and guidance, it is expected to be compressed to about 2 hours, improving loading efficiency by about 100%, meaning nearly double the number of trucks can be loaded in the same working hours. After finishing one truck, workers no longer have to wait or burn time—they can immediately start loading the next one.

Once this timeline is connected, the benefits stack up in three layers: first, workers can load more trucks in the same working hours, directly increasing their per-truck income; second, logistics companies improve overall turnover efficiency and maximize per-truck loading, raising operational efficiency; third, shippers stably exceed the 98-cubic-meter break-even line on every truck and approach the volume limit—under the billing structure of "+$100 per cubic meter over," every extra cubic meter is real additional revenue.

So the significance of this project is not about who saves money in a zero-sum game, but about creating incremental value. Compressing 5 hours into 2 hours, the extra truckloads and extra cubic meters are shared among workers, logistics companies, and shippers—a three-way win-win built on the incremental part.

But for me, the greatest value of this project is not just the loading numbers themselves; more importantly, it validated a replicable way of deploying AI: in the past, much of a company's experience existed only in people's heads; now AI can convert it into data and algorithms, and finally into executable plans. Work that only veteran loaders could do before can now be replicated through standardized processes.

Q5 During the AI deployment process, did you encounter new problems or challenges? How did you solve them?

The biggest challenge actually came from the business side; technical issues were relatively more manageable.

The first challenge was the gap between the algorithm's results and on-site execution.

Initially, the algorithm prioritized maximizing space utilization. But on-site staff told me that some solutions, while theoretically better, were very difficult to execute in practice—for example, requiring workers to enter the truck compartment to move cargo, or requiring frequent repositioning of goods.

So we readjusted our objective, shifting from pursuing the mathematically absolute optimum to finding a balance between efficiency, safety, and operational convenience.

Later, we designed the "cross-section method." Simply put, it plans the loading sequence from the inside out by compartment cross-section. This way, workers only need to stand at the door of the compartment to operate, without stepping on cargo to enter.

The second challenge was data acquisition.

The vehicle's true interior dimensions are the foundation of calculation accuracy.

Relying on professional scanning equipment is costly, and enterprises have limited acceptance of it. So we used an iPad Pro with LiDAR combined with laser distance measurement, keeping hardware costs at around 8,500 RMB while maintaining accuracy.

The third challenge was getting frontline staff to accept the new approach.

Technical solutions ultimately need to be used by people. So we kept refining the interaction design, converting complex algorithm outputs into simple numbered cards, cross-section diagrams, and printed loading guides.

This is exactly where the value of an FDE lies: delivering the technology is only the starting point; ensuring it truly integrates into business processes is what matters.

Q6 Based on your experience, how can we better deploy AI into real business operations?

From this project, I believe the most important thing for AI implementation is to start from the business problem and the real needs of the multiple groups behind it, treating technology as the means of solving them, not the starting point.

When many companies start AI projects, they first ask: "Can we hook up a large model?"

But the real question should be: "Which problem in the business is most worth solving?"

An FDE first needs business understanding. Only by going on-site can you know what the real problem is.

Often, business staff will say: "We can't fill the truck."

But technical staff need to break it down further: Is the spatial data inaccurate? Is the planning inefficient? Is there a problem with the execution process?

Second, you need to act as a translator between technology and business.

Large models are powerful, but they need to be used in the right places. In this case, we had the large model handle understanding and orchestration, while specialized calculations were delegated to specialized algorithms, leveraging each one's strengths.

Third, pay attention to the details during implementation.

Whether an AI project ultimately succeeds often comes down to these small issues: Are workers willing to use it? Does the process change too much? Is the cost acceptable to the company?

So the FDE's role goes far beyond AI development; its core value lies in connecting business, technology, and users.

Fourth—and something I value more and more in this line of work: AI implementation requires doing the "economic math." Freight calculated by cubic meter, breaking even at 98 cubic meters, an extra $100 per cubic meter—these commercial mechanisms directly determine where the value of load optimization lands. Only an FDE who understands both the algorithms and the business math can truly translate technical improvements into cost savings that customers can perceive. That itself is a rare capability.

Fifth, good AI implementation is not a zero-sum game; it's about creating incremental value. The underlying business logic of this project was that it let workers, logistics companies, and cargo owners all benefit from the "extra trips and extra cubic meters." Growing the pie is more valuable than fighting for shares of the old pie, and it goes further.

I was fortunate, in a small border city like Heihe, to encounter business scenarios as real as they get—cross-border logistics, free trade zones; and fortunate to meet a group of partners who don't just talk, but were willing to work with me to move AI out of the screen, into the workshop, to the truck door, and into the hands of frontline workers. So I've always believed one thing: the AI that truly matters in the future will grow inside companies' real processes, not stay stuck at the demo stage. That's also what being an FDE means most to me—taking AI from the computer into the real world.

From Personal Productivity to Organizational Capability: An FDE Using AI to Drive Enterprise R&D Transformation

Q1 As an FDE, what is the specific scenario where you apply AI? What did the specific problems in this scenario look like?

This case took place at a fund company. It is an enterprise focused on fund investment research, conducting fund management business across different domains such as stocks and automobiles. We were dealing with an internal R&D team whose requirements changed very rapidly—quite different from the traditional team that spends months on a single large software project.

One of their characteristics was that requirements were very fragmented and very fast. Internal product managers or business departments might raise several requests in a single day. For them, the value AI brought was increasing the number of features a developer could complete per day from three to ten—quite different from the traditional notion of "a project that used to take a year now takes six months."

So, before I got involved in this project, they were already using AI, and some were using it quite deeply. Some used Codex, some used Claude Code, and others used other AI coding tools. The problem was precisely here: everyone knew how to use these tools, but the company found it hard to get everyone using them together.

Everyone thought their own tool was the best. "I'm efficient with this tool; if you make me switch to another, I actually can't get work done." Once personal habits matured, if the company wanted to standardize on tools, it would face new resistance.

From management's perspective, what the company needed was clearly not just a few employees becoming individually more productive. The boss wanted to see the entire R&D team transform, hoping AI could become an organizational capability rather than just personal tools scattered across employees' computers.

So the core problem I faced went beyond "how to teach everyone to use AI coding"—it was specifically three layers of problems.

First, there was no unified understanding. Everyone was using AI, but there was no shared standard for what AI coding could actually achieve or what their own skill levels were.

Second, tools and methods were not unified. Different people used different tools with different work habits, and the original R&D collaboration practices hadn't changed with the emergence of AI coding.

Third, the way the organization worked was starting to change. After seeing some new R&D philosophies, the company's management wanted to further adjust R&D roles and processes, but there was no ready-made answer for how to actually apply these ideas to their own team.

This is also, as I understand it, the biggest difference between an FDE and ordinary technical training: when facing this kind of problem, you can't just answer "how to use this tool"—you first have to determine what the company is really trying to solve.

Q2 Before you got involved, how was this problem being handled?

Previously, product managers wrote PRDs the old way, developers built according to the documents, and different roles proceeded with their original division of labor. Then AI coding tools appeared, and developers started experimenting with various tools on their own—so what emerged first was a very natural form of "personal productivity gains."

Someone would find a tool useful and use it themselves; someone would learn a new method and practice it on their own. There was really no problem at this stage, because individuals only needed to be accountable for their own efficiency.

But when the company wanted to move from "personal productivity" further toward "team productivity," the old approach started to hit its limits.

For example, everyone used to write PRDs in Feishu docs. Later, as AI coding changed the way of working, much of the content shifted to Markdown, TXT, and other files. One person maintaining one document wasn't a big problem, but as soon as the whole team started collaborating around these files, new problems immediately appeared: how should a dozen or so documents be split up?

What does the product manager write? What does the frontend write? What does the backend write? What are testing and operations each responsible for? If everyone edits the Markdown at the same time, how do you avoid conflicts? Can the original role boundaries still apply?

None of these are problems you naturally solve just by learning an AI tool.

So I didn't define the old methods as "wrong." Quite the opposite—they were a mature set of methods suited to the previous development model. It's just that once AI Coding changed how development works, the original processes, documentation, and role divisions also needed to be restructured.

This is also a realization that became increasingly clear to me over time: once AI truly enters an enterprise, what changes is often not just the tools, but the way of working behind those tools.

Q3 What was your overall approach and the key steps in using AI to solve this problem?

At the very start of this project, the client had already decided to adopt Alibaba's related capabilities, but there was actually no clear answer to the question of "what exactly to do after buying it."

So my first step was to re-examine the requirements before thinking about preparing the course.

I first broke down the needs of different roles. The boss cared about whether enterprise-wide transformation could actually happen; developers cared about whether the tool could genuinely improve their efficiency; product managers cared about how the existing development process would change; and HR needed to know what could be gained each day during the training.

So although the final deliverable was a five-and-a-half-day in-person training, I designed it from start to finish as a complete enterprise AI transformation practice.

I did several things at the time.

The first thing was to clarify the boundaries of the problem.

Before officially starting, I prepared a large amount of material, because I wasn't sure what level everyone was actually at.

But there's a very real problem here: the capability level an enterprise describes about itself often doesn't match its actual on-site capability level.

So I came to place more and more importance on one thing: don't fully trust the preliminary research—always validate as much as possible through hands-on work, rapid prototypes, and on-site conversations.

The second thing was to turn the training into a complete business storyline.

Five days and half a day are completely different concepts.

If you're just teaching tools, it might be over in a day. But if you want the enterprise to truly change, you need to design a complete path from beginning to end: why the enterprise should transform, why developers should use it, how the tools should enter the existing process, how roles will change, and how it ultimately becomes the enterprise's own methodology.

So during those five and a half days, I was both teaching and observing; both training and consulting.

Problems discovered on-site would feed into adjustments going forward.

The third thing was customization for the real development process.

This is the part of the whole case that I think best embodies the FDE characteristics.

For example, development collaboration used to revolve around a single PRD; now it has shifted to collaborating on multiple documents, so the old role divisions may no longer apply directly.

So we began redesigning the Spec documents. At the time, there wasn't actually a particularly mature, unified industry standard to copy, and I didn't know whether what I designed was the optimal solution.

So instead of presenting it to the client as the "standard answer" from the start, I chose to keep breaking it down, releasing it incrementally, confirming with the client, and adjusting based on feedback.

In the end, I built a set of Spec documents from 0 to 1. The client initially even thought these were documents already refined inside Alibaba, but in fact, this set was something I gradually built up during the project based on the client's actual situation.

The fourth thing was to keep adjusting on-site, letting the solution follow real-time feedback.

We did three phases with this fund company in total.

One of the most important values of the first phase was that it let me truly see what the enterprise was like on the ground. From the second phase onward, I could keep adjusting based on the experience accumulated in the first phase. In later stages, the client's business staff and product managers gradually joined in, and the project evolved from pure developer training into a more complete organizational transformation.

This is also an FDE way of working I now endorse: get the whole pipeline running first, then keep iterating based on real feedback—the first attempt doesn't have to be a perfect 100.

Q4 Compared to traditional methods, what were the actual results after using AI?

The results of this project can't simply be understood as "how much more money the company made after using AI."

This fund company did not open up complete ROI data that could be disclosed externally. But in terms of organizational change, the results were fairly clear.

In the past, AI was more of an individual capability—everyone had their own methods and tools.

Later, the enterprise began unifying tools, and further unified its development collaboration practices. AI was no longer just a question of "whether a particular developer knows how to use it," but began to enter the team's shared development process.

More importantly, we captured the Spec documents and related methods developed during the process. They no longer depend on any one person's live demonstration and can continue to be reused within the enterprise.

Later, in work with another manufacturing client, some fairly clear short-term data emerged: after three teams adopted this Spec Coding methodology and templates, statistics showed coding time saved by about 1/3, documentation time cut by more than half, and testing time roughly unchanged.

This data came from the client's internal statistics over just a few days, so I consider it genuine feedback, but it should not be understood as a long-term, complete ROI conclusion. A truly valuable evaluation of results requires two weeks, a month, or even longer.

So for me, the most important outcome of this kind of project is completing a shift: from "everyone using AI on their own" to "the enterprise starting to form a shared methodology for using AI."

Q5 Did you encounter new problems or challenges while deploying AI? How did you solve them?

The first challenge was that AI is developing so fast while industry standards haven't caught up.

At the time, everyone was discussing Spec, AI Coding, and development organization transformation, but many methods had not yet been validated at scale over a sufficiently long period.

I worried too: what if the methodology I put together doesn't fit the actual situation on the ground? What if it gets rejected by the client as soon as I present it?

My solution was to expose the uncertainty, then reduce risk through small-step validation, without pretending I already knew the answer.

I would do a portion first, then confirm with the client; then keep adjusting based on external information and whatever information I could get from inside Alibaba. If something was wrong, I'd fix it.

This is actually an ability I think is very important for an FDE: in a field with no standard answer yet, you can't refuse to act just because there's no standard, and you can't assume you're automatically right just because you're a technical person.

The second challenge was that preliminary research was often inaccurate.

Once, we learned in advance that the client team was quite advanced in using AI Coding, so we prepared a lot of advanced content. But when we actually got on-site, we found they hadn't even solved some basic problems.

So what do you do? You can only downgrade on the spot and start from the basics.

The reverse happens too. If I had judged the team's capability as average in advance, but on-site found they were already highly proficient, I would bring out the "ace up my sleeve" content I had originally prepared.

So looking back now, I think on-site capability is itself an important part of being an FDE.

The third challenge was that enterprises want to make a "big investment" right from the start.

For example, immediately buying model all-in-one machines, doing model fine-tuning, or forcibly overturning the entire existing workflow. I actually think these approaches are rather risky.

If the business can be quickly validated with lightweight approaches—such as APIs, SaaS, or agents—I prefer to validate business value first. Unless the enterprise has explicit compliance, security, or similar requirements, there's no need to make the investment especially heavy from the start.

Investment should go from light to heavy: validate the business value first, then deepen gradually.

Q6 Based on your experience, how can we better deploy AI into real business?

I increasingly feel that the most common mistake companies make when deploying AI is treating it as a standalone technical project. In reality, when it comes to actual implementation, technology is only one part of it.

First, understand what problem the business actually wants to solve.

Many companies say: "We want to do AI transformation." But this statement alone cannot guide implementation. Is it about improving individual productivity,

team productivity, or restructuring the R&D process? Different goals call for completely different solutions.

Second, don't over-rely on upfront research; quickly build prototypes and run real workflows.

Because it's hard to accurately assess AI usage levels through questionnaires, I now prefer to quickly build prototypes and quickly run through a real workflow. Even if the first attempt only scores 70 or 80 out of 100, it's more valuable than sitting there discussing the solution until it's 100 points before starting.

Because once things actually run, the context, pain points, solutions, and constraints all become clearer. Optimizing on the second pass gives you a chance to go from 70–80 points to 80–90 points.

Third, accept that AI implementation is a continuous iterative process.

Especially in fields where AI is developing extremely fast, no one can guarantee that their first design will be the best solution.

Rather than trying to solve all problems in one go, it's better to get the first round running and then keep correcting.

Finally, and this is what I consider the most important thing for an FDE: don't look at problems only from the technical person's own perspective. An FDE needs to understand technology, business, and customers at the same time.

When talking to the boss, the boss cares about organizational efficiency and return on investment; when talking to R&D staff, they care about whether the tools are easy to use; when talking to product managers, they care about how the R&D process will change; when talking to HR, procurement, and operations, it's a completely different discourse system again.

So I think an FDE is more like a "translator": converting standardized AI capabilities into something that a specific company or specific team can actually use.

I summarize the value of an FDE in one sentence: translating AI capabilities into ways of working that a company can truly use, run, and accumulate is more important than simply bringing an AI tool into the company.

From hard-to-find materials to precise locating: how an FDE uses AI to reduce procurement waste in industrial companies

Q1 As an FDE, what is the specific scenario where you apply AI? What did the specific problems in this scenario look like?

The client in this case was from overseas. To be honest, before we first went on-site, we didn't know what we would end up doing. Although there had been several rounds of communication beforehand, there was no preset answer at that time—whether the client needed large language models,

agents, or other AI capabilities, no one was sure.

So we didn't go in with a ready-made product; instead, we first did something that seemed very "slow": interviews. The initial exploration of the client's entire business took a long time. We communicated with different departments, understood the organizational structure and business processes, and observed how different business staff felt about AI. After actually entering this specific case, we conducted in-depth interviews over several months with the ERP team and its upstream and downstream teams.

This company is in an industrial setting and needs to manage a large number of mechanical equipment, materials, and assets. From a pair of gloves, a screw, or a pair of earplugs, to a truck or a robotic arm, everything needs to be entered into the ERP database. About 15 teams interact with this database—some responsible for data entry, some for procurement, some for contracts, and some for warehousing.

We interviewed these teams one by one and found a very interesting phenomenon: almost every department was complaining about the same thing—the ERP is hard to use. But "the ERP is hard to use" is not yet a problem that can be handed directly to engineers. So we kept asking: what exactly is hard to use, how do you usually operate it, and at which step does the problem occur?

Sometimes I would sit right next to a business person and watch how they actually work. Because many problems simply can't be surfaced if you only ask in a meeting room. After the interviews, we would continue researching and breaking things down ourselves to do root-cause diagnosis.

After going through this process, we finally identified about 15 functional points that could be further addressed, among which two problems were very typical. The first was dirty data. The second was very weak search capability.

The client was using a relatively old piece of industrial software. Today, people are used to the search experience of Taobao, JD.com, or Amazon—type in what you're looking for and naturally get results. But in this industrial ERP, search wasn't that smooth.

Business people often encountered this situation: "I know this part is in the database—I used it last time—but I just can't find it now." This sentence later became a crucial entry point for our understanding of the whole problem.

Because "can't find it" itself is not the final business loss; the real problem happens afterward. When an employee can't find a certain material, they may assume it's not in the database and re-enter a new record—even though the material already exists. This creates a cycle: can't find the material → re-enter it → duplicate data appears → data gets dirtier → even harder to find the right material → buying the wrong item, buying duplicates, or getting the units wrong.

When it comes to industrial procurement, this involves real money. If a consumer buys the wrong thing, they can get a refund. Industrial procurement often can't do this—once something is bought by mistake, it usually won't be returned; you can only buy another correct one. The wrongly purchased items stay in the warehouse, possibly for years.

A warehouse can't store things indefinitely. Years later, when clearing inventory, they find these items have long gone unused and can only be disposed of according to rules. The money the company truly loses is hidden here.

So we later defined the real problem to be solved as: whether we could reduce erroneous and duplicate purchases that should never have happened by improving data quality and material search capability.

Once this problem was clearly defined, AI's value also shifted from "making the system smarter" to a problem that could genuinely affect business outcomes.

Q2 Before you got involved, how was this problem solved in the past?

The client's ERP had long carried the company's inventory management processes, and each team had formed its own way of working around it. Data entry, procurement, contracts, warehousing—each stage had clear responsibilities, and the company had always operated on this set of processes.

The problem was that as data kept accumulating, the limitations of the original system became increasingly apparent. In particular, industrial material data is very complex. The same item may exist under different names, different descriptions, different units, or different data-entry habits. And since the system's search relied mainly on traditional fields and keywords, business people found it hard to quickly confirm whether the material they wanted was actually in the database.

In the past, when encountering this situation, business people relied on experience. If they knew the code, they could search by code; if they knew the exact name, they could search by name; if they really couldn't find it, they would re-enter it following the original process.

When the data volume wasn't that large and the business chains weren't that complex, this approach worked. But as historical data accumulated more and more, relying on experience as a fallback became increasingly strained, and the same process began to fail.

So this bill can't be pinned on frontline employees—the first thing that couldn't hold up was the search system. The company had actually put its processes into the ERP long ago; the digitalization step was taken long ago. What really got stuck was that the system's keyword retrieval and field matching couldn't handle the many names, descriptions, and units of industrial materials, so the digital tool itself became the new bottleneck.

At this point, simply asking employees to enter data more carefully or search more thoroughly would hardly solve the problem at its root. So we judged that AI should step in here.

Q3 What was your overall approach and the key steps in using AI to solve this problem?

If you only look at the final features, this case isn't "sexy" at all. It boils down to two things: cleaning and standardizing the existing data, and adding semantic search capability. Some might even say, isn't this just RAG or smart search?

I think that statement is technically fine, but if you stop there, you miss the most important part of this case. Because the truly hard work has always been in "why do semantic retrieval in the first place, and what business results actually changed after it was done." Our whole process went through roughly several stages.

Step one: go on-site and start with problem discovery.

We spent months communicating with the business departments, focusing on their actual workflows and listening to what they hoped AI could help with. Business people will tell you lots of requirements, but an FDE can't simply turn a requirements list into a feature list. We need to keep judging: does this problem really exist, where is the root cause, can AI solve it, and how much business value would solving it create?

It was through this process that we gradually broke "the ERP is hard to use" down into specific problems, and further identified data quality and search as the two issues worth prioritizing.

Step two: put the problem back into the complete business chain.

This is where I think being an FDE differs greatly from just building AI features. We didn't stop at "search is bad, so let's build a better search"—we kept digging into why bad search causes business losses. Only then did we arrive at this chain: difficult search → duplicate data entry → data contamination → wrong/duplicate purchases → inventory buildup → eventual write-off → real financial losses. Only when this chain holds do we know how much this AI application is actually worth.

Step three: set priorities together with all stakeholders.

This case involved a dozen-plus departments, so it was impossible for the FDE to unilaterally decide what to do first. We organized the diagnosed feature points into a list, then brought together the heads and representatives of the different business departments to confirm, item by item, what problem each feature actually solved, whether the business truly needed it, which should come first and which later—and we even had the business departments vote.

At the same time, the FDE team internally evaluated each use case along seven or eight dimensions, such as whether the data could be obtained, whether processed data could be written back into the business systems, whether AI could actually solve the problem, how technically difficult it was, and whether business users would ultimately be willing to adopt it.

Because building a feature technically doesn't mean it can actually land. Sometimes the data simply doesn't exist; sometimes the legacy system won't let you write results back; sometimes it's technically feasible but the business department won't allow its system to be touched; and sometimes business users simply don't trust the AI's output. All of these issues need to be mapped out as thoroughly as possible before you actually start building.

Step four: define the technical division of labor clearly, so AI only does what it should.

We didn't stuff everything into the large model. Semantic retrieval was responsible for understanding the meaning behind the business user's phrase "what I'm looking for"

and matching up the different names for the same material; data cleaning and standardization were responsible for removing duplicates and inconsistencies from historical data before retrieval, giving semantic retrieval a clean foundation. Whether to purchase, and how much, was still decided by the business people themselves.

Step five: start with scenarios that can prove value quickly, and allow iteration along the way.

I don't recommend starting with the hardest, coolest thing. If a project runs for months with no results, the client's patience and confidence drop fast. We prefer to first find scenarios where results come easily—ideally within a few weeks, or even around two weeks, the business department can see an initial result.

Once business users see "this thing actually works," confidence is established, and they'll be more willing to actively participate in what follows. And this path is never perfectly straight.

There's often back-and-forth along the way. Sometimes halfway through we find the problem was defined wrong, and we go back to the problem-discovery step; sometimes we find the scope is too broad and pull it back in. What an FDE really does is continuously validate the relationship among "problem, solution, business value," so that every step builds on the validated results of the previous one.

In the end, we made it easier for business users to find materials that already existed. The purpose was to reduce duplicate data entry at the source, and further reduce wrong purchases and duplicate purchases—search was only the means.

Q4 Compared with traditional methods, what were the actual results of using AI?

Our way of measuring results is actually very clear. What we ultimately look at is whether the client bought fewer wrong things and made fewer duplicate purchases. Model accuracy and how much faster employees felt search was are only process metrics; the real business result is whether purchasing behavior changed.

Before this project, the client actually knew that wrong purchases and inventory waste existed, but the problem wasn't visible to them. No one had ever pulled the records out of the historical database and carefully analyzed how much these errors cost each year. So part of our work was turning an invisible problem into a calculable one.

We built a baseline from historical data, then expanded the scope step by step through small-scale experiments. At each stage we validated several questions: had AI reduced duplicate data, had the cleanliness of the database improved, had wrong purchases caused by data problems declined, and had the corresponding purchasing waste ultimately been avoided.

More importantly, this value can't be declared by us alone. In the end we need to arrive at the result through rigorous calculation, and the calculated business value also needs to be confirmed by the client's own business people. Only when the client says "yes, this money really was saved because of this application" do we consider the value truly established.

So compared with the past, the biggest change in this case was turning a state of "everyone knows there's a problem, but no one knows how much the problem is worth" into a business problem that can be discovered, intervened in, and continuously measured. That, to me, is the biggest difference between AI landing and AI demos.

Q5 During the AI deployment, did you encounter new problems or challenges? How did you solve them?

What was truly hard about this project mostly wasn't the model itself.

The first challenge: finding the real problem takes a long time.

We spent months understanding an ERP business process that seemed to have little to do with AI. For many engineers, this may be the hardest part to accept—people are more used to clients giving requirements and themselves writing code. But an FDE is different: often the client doesn't even know where the problem truly worth solving with AI lies. You have to sit down and understand how purchasing is done,

how warehousing is done, how data is entered, how different departments collaborate. Without doing this "dirty, tiring work," the AI that follows may well just be doing a prettier job on the wrong problem.

Diagnosis needs to be deep, yet clients expect value quickly. If a lot of time has already been spent on business analysis up front, and then months pass after implementation starts with no visible results, the organization easily loses patience. So we deliberately start with scenarios where value is easy to prove—first let the business department see it works, then expand gradually.

The second challenge: cross-department coordination is hard.

This case involved a dozen-plus teams, each with different responsibilities and different demands. Some AI applications even change the existing workflows between departments, so conflicts can arise naturally—and many conflicts never get said out loud in meetings. So we hold multiple rounds of discussion: not just big meetings, but also one-on-one conversations with different stakeholders beforehand, to defuse these unspoken concerns in advance.

The third challenge: technically doable, but hard to land.

For example: can the data be extracted, will the legacy ERP let us write processed data back, will the business department allow us to touch existing systems, will end users be willing to trust AI results. If any one of these can't be solved, the use case may fail. So before formal construction, we score different scenarios across multiple dimensions—whether AI can implement it is only one item.

And after going live, things aren't over either. The project ultimately has to be handed over to the business department, who decide whether to truly adopt it. After launch we keep watching: after running for a while, are the results still there, have they changed compared to the goals defined at project kickoff, and if performance has declined, what's the reason and does it need further optimization.

FDE cannot be "finish the work, get paid, and leave." If the business unit ultimately doesn't use it, or the results disappear after three months, then the application hasn't truly landed. The entire delivery therefore keeps looping back to problem diagnosis, scope selection, and validation, making it hard to go straight through in one line.

Q6 Based on your experience, how can we better deploy AI into real business operations?

After finishing this case, I became even more convinced that the most important capability of an FDE is finding problems truly worth solving; building an AI application quickly actually comes second. I would summarize this into a few principles.

First, don't go looking for problems with AI in hand; first go into the business field to find problems.

Many people approach AI applications by holding an AI agent, a RAG, or a workflow product, and then asking customers, "Do you have any scenarios where this could be used?" This easily turns into looking for nails while holding a hammer. We prefer to do the opposite.

First engage with the customer over a long period, understand the business, do problem discovery, and then decide whether the problem should be solved with generative AI, traditional machine learning, or no AI at all. AI technology is only a means.

Second, don't stop at the surface-level needs users raise; keep digging down to the root cause.

When business staff say "the ERP is hard to use," that is not a problem definition. When they say "search is hard to use," that isn't either. Only by continuing to dig will you discover that chasing "search is hard to use" all the way down ultimately lands on real financial losses.

An FDE needs to keep asking: why, and then what, and what did this ultimately affect—until finding a root cause that can be connected to business outcomes.

Third, prioritize use cases that can prove value.

Whether an AI scenario is worth doing, I consider data acquisition difficulty, system integration difficulty, AI feasibility, business complexity, user acceptance, and potential value all at once; the coolest ones often get set aside for later. Especially at the beginning, you should choose scenarios that can produce results quickly, because early results are not only technical validation but also a process of building organizational confidence.

Fourth, define the ROI at the start of the project.

Many AI projects only start asking after launch, "How much value did this actually create?" By then it's often too late.

We clarify from the very beginning: if this problem were solved, how much loss would the enterprise reduce per year, how is that number calculated, where does the data come from, and who confirms it in the end. That way, when the project ends, we prove ourselves with business results the customer recognizes, and model metrics are only a process reference.

Fifth, what should be standardized first is the methodology; the product can wait.

I'm not against standardized products, but AI technology is changing too fast at this stage. If you start out thinking about turning a certain feature into a standard product and then take it to find the next batch of customers, it's easy to fall back into the old rut.

By contrast, I value reusable methodology more. How to do problem discovery, how to do root cause analysis, how to judge use cases, how to define ROI, how to run small-scale experiments, how to validate together with business units, how to measure continuously.

These things are worth consolidating more than any specific AI feature. Because the next customer's ERP may be completely different, and the business processes may be completely different too, but the problem-solving mindset is reusable.

So if I had to summarize this case in one sentence, I would say: the real work of an FDE is to first go deep into the business, restore a vague complaint into a problem that can be solved, validated, and valued, and then decide where AI should appear.

This changes the role of people as well. In the past, people handled everything and machines weren't involved; now the AI handles most of the deterministic work, and people only deal with the risks and anomalies that truly need attention.

Once the AI finishes its judgment, the RPA robot starts executing. It can automatically open a browser, go to the corresponding group website, create the form, fill in the relevant information, and submit it.

So this solution essentially breaks the whole process into parts: business information retrieval → AI judgment → AI supervision → risk identification → RPA execution.

This is also, in my understanding, the biggest difference between an FDE and purely doing technical solutions: we think about how to truly embed AI into the existing business process.

So I first look for the scenario that is closest to the business, most painful, and easiest to generate ROI. It's like picking peaches: pick the one nearest to you and sweetest first.

Q5 Did you encounter new problems or challenges while deploying AI? How did you solve them?

One of my deepest takeaways from this project is that the hardest part of AI projects is often outside the technology.

In the AI era, I'd even say that if a project can already be implemented purely on technical grounds, it's 80% successful. What really tends to kill projects is the business, the people, and the organization.

The first challenge is the business department's acceptance and pace of adoption.

The AI program itself doesn't create value; it's the business department that does. For example, AI saves them time, but the ones who ultimately enjoy that efficiency gain are the business staff, so we must find ways to get the business department to stand with us.

I strongly oppose one approach: having the AI project team sit there designing something on its own and then asking the business department to come use it. Business staff are already busy every day; if you suddenly add a new system and a new process, their first reaction is likely to be "Why are you adding another burden for me?" So our approach is business accompaniment: I spend a few days working alongside the business staff, observing their real workflow, and proactively mapping out the business logic, so they don't have to prepare an especially complete requirements document for us.

Likewise, in a first collaboration you shouldn't overhaul the process aggressively. If I told the business department, "Throw away your entire existing process; we'll design a brand-new AI process," the project would most likely face resistance, because business staff already have their own work habits, and the existing process exists for its own reasons.

So I prefer to start from a very small, very painful scenario, do one point well first, and let the business staff see real ROI. Once they find it genuinely saves them time, they'll start proactively discussing with you, "Could we do this part too?" At that point, the AI project shifts from "you have to make me use it" to "I hope you'll keep doing this for me."

The second challenge is data security and infrastructure.

State-owned and central enterprises, especially research institutions, have very high data security requirements. Much financial data is sensitive and can't simply be thrown into public models, so we had to solve the problem starting from the underlying infrastructure, including computing power, model deployment, and the data environment.

I've previously helped an enterprise procure a large all-in-one AI computing machine, with an investment on the order of over two million yuan, giving the enterprise the basic environment to run large models. For sensitive financial data, a private deployment where data never leaves the domain is also required.

But a very practical problem arises here: if you tell the client upfront, "Let's first spend two to three million yuan on a machine," they'll very likely ask, "Why? I haven't seen what value AI brings me yet." So this loops back to the earlier logic: first prove value with a small scenario, then push for infrastructure investment—not the other way around.

The third challenge is that you can't force the same model onto different scenarios.

When designing solutions, I don't cling to any single model provider either. Finance scenarios demand rigor, research scenarios may need professional computing power, and some content-generation scenarios call for more openness. So my principle is to choose capabilities based on the scenario:

Some scenarios may use Alibaba Cloud's models, some use models from other vendors, and some state-owned and central enterprise scenarios use local private deployments.

I think of this approach as "teaching according to the student's aptitude": first determine what the business needs, then combine the underlying capabilities.

First, enter the business before entering the technology.

One of the most important actions for an FDE is business accompaniment. Don't sit in a meeting room waiting for business staff to tell you their requirements; what you really need to do is follow them as they work and see how they actually do things. Many business rules are hard for even the business staff themselves to explain fully in one go, but if you follow them through it once, you'll discover a wealth of judgments and experience hidden in the process.

Second, find the "nearest, sweetest" peach first.

Don't start with a grand AI transformation project—first find a small scenario. Ideally, this scenario should meet three conditions at once: the business genuinely hurts, implementation is relatively manageable, and ROI can be seen quickly.

First, make the initial project an "icebreaker project" so the business unit gets results, receives praise, and even becomes a benchmark within the group. With that benchmark in place, resources, budget, and organizational support become much easier to obtain later.

Third, you need to truly tie the business side to the results.

AI projects need to break out of the positioning of "a project owned by the technical team." If in the end all the AI team can say is "we built the system," it's actually meaningless. What really matters is: did the business save time? Did human efficiency improve? Did business outcomes change?

So I need to find ways to make the business team share the results with us, and when conditions allow, even link the AI transformation to the business unit's KPIs and OKRs, so the business unit itself has the motivation to push the project forward.

I used to be an architect myself, but over time I've come to feel that in the AI era you can't keep dwelling on "how to implement this technology." Much of the underlying implementation work will likely be done by AI itself in the future.

What truly requires humans is: why am I doing this? How should this problem be defined? What value does it bring to the enterprise? How do you design an architecture that can support the company for the next 3 to 5 years? How do you actually drive the project to landing?

So I believe a mature FDE must possess at least three things simultaneously: communication skills, architectural thinking, and project management thinking.

Fifth, proactively "break out of your circle" and "reach higher."

I personally value two phrases a lot: breaking out of your circle, and reaching higher.

Breaking out of your circle means going out to talk with company CEOs, business leaders, and people from different industries. What they truly care about is:

Can revenue increase? Can costs come down? Can organizational management become more transparent? Can business control improve?

Reaching higher means proactively engaging with people whose understanding is a level above yours, and learning how they see problems. Once your own understanding changes, the way you solve problems changes too.

Finally, I want to say that being an FDE is itself an "intermediate state."

I increasingly understand the FDE role as an "intermediate state": at first, a person may do everything, surviving by solving different customers' problems; gradually, you discover which industry and which scenarios you truly have an advantage in, and you start to focus; when you can independently understand the business, design the architecture, and drive projects to landing, you become an FDE in that field.

But if you stay stuck at "I personally deliver every project," your personal bandwidth is limited. The next step is to consolidate the delivery experience, business methods, and SOPs you've accumulated so that more people can deliver following this methodology; after that, distill recurring common problems into standard products.

From individual capability, to team capability, to replicable product capability—that is the process by which FDE capability is truly amplified.

So if I were to redefine this finance case, it actually goes beyond the scope of an "AI + finance" project; it's more like a complete FDE practice: first enter the business scene and find the real pain points; then break down the business processes and place AI where judgment is genuinely needed; let RPA execute the repetitive actions; use oversight mechanisms to control risk; and finally prove value through a small scenario, then gradually expand AI's application across the enterprise.

This is also my understanding of what an FDE is: truly taking responsibility for a business problem until the technology becomes a business outcome.

From Manual Reconciliation to Intelligent Account Matching: How an FDE Uses AI to Make Offline Retail Reconciliation Fast and Accurate

Q1: As an FDE, what is the specific scenario where you applied AI? What did the specific problems in this scenario look like?

I've always felt that where an FDE truly creates value is in finding a problem that is close enough to the company's business goals. Whether you can build something with AI is actually secondary.

Its funds settlement chain is rather special: the store's own POS system generates one set of data, the mall has another set of settlement data, and ultimately funds are settled to the company through the mall. So in every settlement cycle, the data and documents from both sides need to be re-verified.

When the client first came to us, the problem they raised was actually very plain: reconciliation was too labor-intensive.

This sounds very standard, even a bit "not worth using AI for." But I later found that it's precisely these kinds of things that deserve a careful look.

In the past, store staff or store managers had to take paper documents or scanned copies/images of documents, pull data from different systems, and verify item by item. The whole process was basically done manually, by eye. If something didn't match, both sides had to re-verify, and after corrections new discrepancies might appear again, leading to endless back-and-forth. A single cycle could take one or two person-days.

Behind this there are actually three layers of problems.

The first layer is a business problem. Incomplete reconciliation means that some money that should have been collected may have been missed.

The second layer is a process problem. The people actually doing this work are usually store staff or store managers, who are also carrying sales targets. The more time reconciliation takes, the less time they can devote to sales and store operations.

The third layer is an information problem. The data on both sides isn't naturally connected, and paper documents add to the cost of manual verification, so people can only keep comparing across different sources of information.

What really made me feel this problem was worth doing was what we discovered later: beyond saving labor, it could also directly affect revenue.

In the past, headquarters would even set a "tolerance range" for stores, because everyone knew manual reconciliation was too much of a hassle—if discrepancies were within a certain percentage, they simply stopped pursuing them. But now, if a machine can surface these discrepancies, the money that was previously overlooked has a chance of being identified again. The value shifts from "saving a few person-days" to tangible revenue gains.

So from an FDE's perspective, what I ultimately prioritized was the scenario where business value is easiest to validate, and pushed "the coolest AI scenarios" to later.

Q2: Before you got involved, how was this problem solved in the past?

The original solution logic was very straightforward: pull data from multiple systems, look at totals, look at line items, and investigate the causes of discrepancies.

For a single store or a small number of stores, humans could fully handle it.

But the problem is that as the business scaled, the marginal cost of the manual approach kept rising.

One store might tolerate an hour or two or even a full day of verification, but once the number of stores grows, the same work has to be repeated every cycle. What's worse, besides being "slow," manual work produces continuous rework: find a discrepancy, re-confirm, fix it, verify again; and if employees feel certain discrepancies aren't worth pursuing, they may simply give up on them.

So the old method itself wasn't wrong—the problem was that business complexity exceeded what the manual approach could carry.

This is also why I increasingly feel that when an FDE deploys AI, they shouldn't start by saying "the old process is too outdated, we'll replace it with AI." The real questions to ask are: why did the old way work in the first place? At which point did it start losing efficiency? Which part exactly should AI take over?

In this case, we didn't try to make the entire reconciliation process AI-driven; instead, we first took over the most standardized, most repetitive,

most automation-friendly round of verification, freeing people from massive mechanical comparison work.

This is also why employees ultimately had a fairly high acceptance of the solution. In their view, it was more like "someone has already done the first round of reconciliation for me; I just need to review the results and handle the real discrepancies," rather than being seen as "yet another system I have to relearn."

Q3: What was your overall approach and the key steps in using AI to solve this problem?

What I find truly valuable about this case is how we selected the problem and controlled the delivery scope—OCR or vision itself actually came second.

The first step was prioritizing among the multiple needs the customer raised.

At the time, the customer didn't just have the reconciliation need; they had also proposed directions like automated customer service and competitor comparison.

If you look purely at "what AI can do," all of these are feasible. But from an FDE perspective, I would first ask: which one can generate positive business feedback the fastest?

Customer service involves both efficiency and new business opportunities, requiring a fairly long data cycle to prove how much new business was actually generated; competitor analysis involves multiple downstream operational strategies, and the final business results are hard to verify in a short time.

By comparison, the value of reconciliation is very direct: how much labor is saved can be seen quickly, and how much money is no longer missed can be calculated directly.

So the first judgment focuses on which task is most likely to produce positive feedback the fastest; whether the technology is the most advanced is secondary.

The second step was putting AI in the position where it truly fits.

We ultimately used OCR plus vision to recognize and compare paper/image documents and related information, then ran it on a scheduled cycle through an automated workflow. Simply put, AI handles the massive repetitive first round of checking, filters out the results that can already be confirmed, and then humans handle the parts that truly require judgment.

There's nothing particularly "flashy" about this solution technically.

But I actually think that's typical: what an FDE should do is find a solution that is simple enough, cheap enough, and genuinely solves the business problem—not necessarily the most complex technology.

The third step was considering scalability from the very beginning, not just stopping at a demo.

At the time we only deployed to a few stores in Shanghai—still in the single digits. But the customer's overall store footprint was fairly large, so I had already considered future expansion when designing the solution.

A few stores running once per cycle versus hundreds or thousands of stores running simultaneously within a similar time window place completely different demands on system stability and redundancy when AI makes errors.

So we split it into two phases from the start.

Phase one was kept as lightweight as possible—it could even be done with Cherry Studio plus a local computer; phase two, if truly scaled up, would require more capabilities on the backend data and redundancy side.

I value this point a lot: lightweight doesn't mean no planning. You can validate value at minimal cost first, but from day one you should already know what the next step is if validation succeeds.

The fourth step was to avoid adding extra burden to frontline staff.

This is something technical teams easily overlook. With a new AI tool, if employees have to enter extra data, relearn processes, or add steps, what they see first is "now I have one more thing to do."

So we tried to embed AI into the existing workflow rather than making employees build a new one. In the end, what employees receive are results that have already gone through the first round of processing—they don't need to maintain a system of their own.

I think this is an important detail when AI is actually deployed: don't make users see their own investment first and wait for future gains; let them feel the benefit right away.

Q4 Compared with traditional methods, what were the actual results after using AI?

The most direct change was that time went from "person-days" to "hours."

In the past, one cycle could take one or two person-days, or even stretch longer due to repeated checking. Now the whole process is compressed into a few hours, and employees only handle the parts that truly require human judgment.

But if you just summarize it as "saving labor," I think that still underestimates this project.

What truly surprised me was that AI exposed problems that had been masked by manual processes. In the actual data, some stores had discrepancies of one or two percentage points, others two or three. There was both under-collection and over-collection, so ultimately you have to look at the net difference. But for an offline retail business, even one or two percentage points, when it reaches the net profit level, is already a very large number.

So a big takeaway from this project for me is: sometimes the value of AI is that it finally gives a company the ability to redo things it gave up on because the cost was too high. Now that the cost of checking has come down, that abandoned value has a chance to be recovered.

Currently we've deployed to some stores in Shanghai, and there's room to expand the scope further in the future.

We previously did a case on horizontal price comparison for domestic e-commerce.

At its core, it was still insufficient industry knowledge.

If an industry already has companies that survive specifically on a certain problem, you should first seriously study: what difficulty exactly did they solve? What information asymmetry do they hold? Why is this worth a company's long-term investment?

The second challenge was insufficient requirements research, leading to constant rework later.

Another case was school homework grading and intelligent question recommendation.

The requirement sounded very straightforward: grade first, then score, then analyze, then recommend questions based on each student's situation; the questions would come from the school's own question bank.

But once we actually broke it down, the problem was very complex.

At which point should humans step in?

If these questions aren't clarified early on, requirement changes will keep popping up later.

In the software engineering era, this was called "the product manager didn't define requirements clearly, so R&D kept reworking"; in the AI era, the essence hasn't changed—except the "rework cost" downstream can now also show up directly as massive token consumption and delivery costs.

So now, as we do FDE work, we're more cautious than before. Technical research must be done seriously, solution research must be done seriously, and business research must be done seriously too.

The third challenge was that organizational and responsibility boundaries weren't aligned upfront.

AI projects are easily understood as "the boss wants it, the FDE builds it, and frontline staff use it." But in reality, the boss, middle management, and

frontline staff care about completely different things. If each level's goals, expectations, capability boundaries, and responsibilities aren't made clear early on, it's easy to end up with "if it doesn't work well, it's the FDE's fault."

So now I increasingly emphasize one thing: you must not only align with the boss, but also align separately with each level involved in the project.

Who is responsible for what, who contributes what, what counts as success, and who judges and handles issues when AI goes wrong—all of this should be made as clear as possible upfront.

Q6 Based on your experience, how can we better deploy AI into real business?

If I had to distill this experience into a few basic principles, I wouldn't start with models, agents, or workflows. I'd start with value, priorities, and trust.

First, you must choose the right first deal.

An enterprise may hand you ten requirements at once, but the first project shouldn't be chosen just by which one is most interesting. You need to weigh things holistically: is the business value visible enough, does positive feedback come fast enough, are few enough people involved, is the business process clear enough, how much do users need to invest, is the technical cost controllable, and can the final value be quantified?

There's no such thing as a "perfect hexagonal task"—in the end it's always a trade-off.

The reconciliation case worked well as a first step precisely because it was balanced across these dimensions: few people involved, a relatively clear business process, calculable value, low AI cost, and easy acceptance by employees.

Second, don't chase perfection in version one—get a positive loop running first.

My advice to FDEs is that version one should solve a problem with clear boundaries. Get the customer's approval, get real usage, make the value visible.

Once the first positive loop is running, trust is established. Tolerance for the second requirement will be higher, then you expand further, and further, until a sustained positive loop forms.

That's far healthier than promising a "fully automated, full-coverage, solves-everything-in-one-go" solution from day one.

Third, think through the delivery boundaries on Day One.

Especially with mid-sized and large enterprises, you can't get halfway through and then tell the customer: "You'll need to buy servers for this," "You'll need to add headcount for this," "You'll need to change a whole process for this."

The customer's resources and trust are real. So on day one, when designing the solution, you should lay out the technical approach, resource investment, future expansion path, and possible boundaries as clearly as possible.

Fourth, don't oversimplify AI adoption as "replacing people."

This is something I feel strongly about. If AI takes over seventy or eighty percent of a role's work, it's easy for a company to get the impulse: since efficiency has improved, why not just cut headcount?

But I believe that easily turns an AI project into a confrontation between the company and its employees. A better approach is to redirect the freed-up human bandwidth toward higher-value work.

For example, customer service staff don't have to keep doing only customer service—they can move into user research, sales, or other customer-facing work; a paid-traffic team shouldn't just aim for "fewer people," but rather for lower per-capita error rates and higher per-capita ROI.

This way, what AI brings is an upgrade to the organization's business goals, with cost reduction taking a back seat.

Fifth, the FDE's most core capability, in the end, is still "understanding the business."

What AI can truly scale quickly is backend delivery capability; but what's hardest to replicate is still front-end industry knowledge, insight into customer needs, and understanding of the organization.

So in the future, FDEs may become stratified: a small group who deeply understand the industry, the organization, and consulting, as well as AI delivery; while more people can deliver stable results on standardized, agent-friendly tasks using mature tools.

But whichever layer you're in, the most fundamental questions never change.

I ask my team to keep answering these questions: What does the customer truly care about? How did they do things before? After we finish, how do they do things now? What value did we actually create for them? Compared to the original solution, what are our strengths and weaknesses?

I think this is really the FDE's basic "Business 101."

If you can do it, you can do it; if you can't, you can't. If you know the industry, you know it; if you don't, you don't. Don't let the current hype around AI and FDEs inflate your sense of your own capabilities without limit.

A truly good FDE knows what's worth doing, what can be done now, and how to do it in a way that lets the customer genuinely see the value.

When the first small win lands—the customer sees the value, employees are willing to use it, and the business is willing to keep investing—only then does AI truly transform from a technical capability into part of the enterprise's business.

Their computers hold sample images, design artwork, reference images, customer delivery images, and various product templates, scattered across different computers and

different folders.

So the boss's initial request to me was very specific: could it be that in the future, he could just say one sentence to the AI—like "help me find the template a certain customer used before"—and the AI would pull it right up?

Beyond file search, he had a few other needs. For example, he wanted the AI to see the data from Alibaba International Station and tell him how today's keywords were performing and whether to swap them; he wanted the AI to continuously publish articles on the company website; and for product posters, which used to require handing product images to a person for processing, he wanted to just give the AI a product and have it automatically placed into an existing template.

None of these tasks is complicated on its own. But the real problem was hidden beneath these "small requests."

First, the company's digital assets were never truly organized. The files were all on the computers, but the AI didn't know the relationships between them—and even the people themselves might not know.

Second, the company lacked an environment where AI could actually run. You can't buy a large model account one day and expect the company to be AI-powered the next. The network, computer environment, permissions, file structure, and workflows all affect whether AI can truly enter the business.

The third problem is actually the most important one: trust.

Many bosses have already heard plenty of AI concepts and even know what an agent is, but they don't know what these things have to do with the problems they face every day. So when an FDE comes in, I think the first thing to do is find a problem the boss genuinely cares about, and then tell them: can AI solve this problem better than your current approach?

That's the starting point.

Q2 Before you got involved, how was this problem being solved?

This company had already spent money on digitalization and AI-related services before.

For example, the boss spent about 50,000 RMB on a related service—the vendor could pull some data from the International Station and had built a foreign trade website. The product itself had some value, but it was closer to a standardized product. When the company ran into personalized problems, no one stayed on to walk them through it, and no one told them how to integrate the tool with their own way of working.

I kept wondering afterward: why did traditional software and outsourcing approaches work in the past?

Because in the past, enterprise problems were relatively clear-cut. If I want a website, I find someone to build a website; if I want an ERP, I buy an ERP; if I want a design draft, I hand it to a designer. One problem maps to one tool or one person.

There's nothing wrong with that approach in itself. But as the business grew more complex, the boss began to notice that the company had dozens or even hundreds of tiny, fragmented needs at the same time. If every single need required hiring an outsourcing team to develop, the cost would be unbearable.

More critically, many problems aren't even clear enough to be written up as a requirements document. The boss knows "something isn't smooth here," but he himself doesn't know what should be developed.

What left a deep impression on me was a customer requirement this foreign trade business owner encountered. The customer gave him a product similar to a set square, with a row of irregular markings on it. They had hired designers and done 3D prototyping, but never figured out what those markings actually meant. They were even prepared to copy them by hand, but couldn't copy them accurately.

Under the old way of working, this would have meant finding people, searching for information, and going back and forth with confirmations.

I didn't develop any software for him at the time. I simply threw the photo to AI and asked it to identify what the product was; if it didn't know, it would keep searching for related materials, manufacturers, and supporting documents, then analyze what information would be needed to actually make this product.

In about 10 minutes, AI found the product and figured out that the so-called "irregular markings" actually represented angles.

The owner was quite surprised at the time. Because in his original thinking, this would have taken a day or two of research.

That moment was actually very important. For the first time, he realized that AI doesn't need to be treated as a new piece of software that requires separate learning—it is itself a completely different way of solving problems.

Q3 What was your overall approach and the key steps in using AI to solve this problem?

Once we actually started, I didn't rush to develop features.

The first step was dealing with the on-site environment.

We decided to use the most cutting-edge large models directly, but once on-site at the company, we discovered that their internal network environment, adjusted repeatedly by themselves over the years, was extremely complex: 4 routers, 2 optical modems, 1 switch, plus a soft router—the whole network was wired together in a mess and was often unstable. So, to give the Agent more stable and flexible intranet access permissions and tools, I spent my first day almost acting as a network engineer.

This opening also shows that the first thing in enterprise AI deployment is often to solve the legacy infrastructure problems left behind. If the company's intranet doesn't work and the device environments are inconsistent, even the best Agent can't run.

The second step was reorganizing the company's files.

After the network was sorted out, my second step was organizing the company's files.

This step was much more complicated than simply dumping files into a so-called "knowledge base." Our approach was to reorganize the materials scattered across various computers, establish relationships between files, and let AI know which files belonged to which customer, which product, which project, and how the different materials related to each other.

But we immediately ran into a practical problem: these materials couldn't be handed directly to us.

For the owner, the contracts, customer information, and product files on his computer might be the company's most core assets—some things he wouldn't even show to his own employees, let alone package up and send to an external FDE.

The third step was keeping the data inside the company and sending the development capability in.

Under the traditional approach, we would package the data and take it back for development, but on-site we found that path wouldn't work.

So we adjusted our thinking: we first organized a Harness into a task package, deployed the framework, principles, specifications, and Demo together into the customer's own Codex, then had the customer's local Codex continue the development on his computer and file environment according to our specifications.

We spent dozens of hours upfront building the framework, then let the customer-side Codex run for about 40 hours. Once it finished running, the owner began testing.

The logs, issues, and records produced by testing were then organized by the customer-side AI and sent to us; based on this feedback, we continued to revise the specifications and tasks, and had the customer-side AI complete the adaptations.

This process was very important to me, because it changed the old way of working where "the on-site engineer must always stay next to the customer's computer." The data doesn't need to leave the company, yet development and adaptation can still continue.

The fourth step was turning the delivery into the company's own capability.

More importantly, we don't want to handle these requests for the company forever.

My goal is to turn files into documentation, sort out the relationships between materials, consolidate the knowledge, and then build workflows,

Skills, and Agents. As these things gradually accumulate, the company itself will be able to solve more and more problems.

What I'd rather deliver in the end is the company's own ability to solve its next batch of problems, so that it doesn't have to come to me every time it encounters an issue.

Q4 Compared to traditional methods, what were the actual results of using AI?

From the perspective of a software project alone, it's still hard to draw a conclusion like "efficiency improved by X percent" for this case, because the project is still in its first phase and the delivery isn't fully complete.

However, several very clear changes have already taken place in this project.

The first change is that the company began to truly understand what AI can do.

For that set-square problem, the owner originally thought it would take a day or two of research; on-site, we used AI to find the product's source and the meaning of the markings in about 10 minutes. Later, when he found the vector graphics difficult to make, we asked AI directly what tool to use and produced the vector graphics on the spot.

What's truly important about this result is that the owner began to realize that many problems he previously thought required hiring professionals or following fixed procedures can now be solved in a different way.

The second change is that once trust was established, the company began proactively releasing more requirements.

At the beginning, the owner was only willing to give us a very small, very specific problem with low trial-and-error cost. But after we made some deliveries, he quickly came up with a dozen or so new requirements.

This change says more than any efficiency metric alone, because it means the company has shifted from "Should I try AI?" to "What other problems in my company could be solved this way?"

The third change is that the owner's view of AI began shifting from "buying a tool" to "rethinking how the company works."

I gradually became convinced later that for companies like this, the greatest value of an FDE may not be helping them hire one or two fewer people.

What's truly valuable is that, through a few concrete projects, the owner sees a new way of working, which changes his subsequent strategic judgments.

Especially for the many companies we work with that are in a stage of rapid business growth where manpower can't keep up—if the owner realizes at this point that future new business doesn't necessarily have to be solved by adding headcount, the value of this shift in thinking may far exceed the cost saved by any single tool.

Q5 Did you encounter new problems or challenges during the AI deployment? How did you solve them?

During this project, three challenges left the deepest impression on me.

The first challenge was inconsistent on-site environments.

Our own programs ran perfectly locally, but produced all kinds of errors on the customer's computers. The reason is simple: every computer's environment is different.

At the start, this often happened: I'd send an installation package, they'd say it errored; they'd screenshot it for me, I'd fix it and send another; then a new error would appear.

In standard software engineering these might be very basic problems, but in a real enterprise environment, they genuinely slow down the entire project.

So later we treated the environment as the first on-site problem: after going in, we first spent time getting a clear picture of each computer's network, permissions, and runtime environment, and tried to standardize them in advance. That saved a lot of back-and-forth rework later.

The second challenge was data and privacy.

For AI to truly work, it must access the company's most valuable materials. But these are exactly the materials the owner is most reluctant to hand over.

So we took a different approach: we sent the development capability and task specifications into his environment, letting the customer's own AI do the work locally, so the data never leaves the company.

The third challenge was the owner's and employees' time and cooperation.

The owner has to handle the business; once the product is built, it needs his testing, but he might be out of the office for days; the employees have to work during the day, yet we need to debug their computers.

But for customized products to work, someone has to actually use them—only then can we find out where they fall short of requirements. For a while, we even kept nudging our boss: "Find some time to use it, at least leave us some records."

Later, we built a remote channel ourselves, allowing our AI to connect to the AI on the client's computers in the background and complete some tasks without disrupting employees' normal work. Many long-running tasks were scheduled to run overnight, saving both sides a great deal of communication and waiting time.

Looking back, none of these three challenges was directly about the model, yet each one could determine whether the project could actually get off the ground. This is exactly why the FDE must be on the business front lines.

Q6 Based on your experience, how can we better deploy AI into real business operations?

After completing these projects, I gradually came to see one thing clearly: an FDE must not think of themselves as an "AI outsourcing" provider.

If we merely help clients develop small pieces of software, we will inevitably get dragged into price comparisons with traditional outsourcing firms—and professional software outsourcing companies would likely do it more maturely than we could.

What the FDE should really solve is a different problem: how to bring AI's general-purpose capabilities into a specific enterprise and make them ultimately stick.

First, start with a very small, very specific problem.

Don't open by talking to the boss about multi-agent collaboration, Harness, or enterprise agents. What the boss really cares about is:

"Can you solve the problem I have right now?"

And ideally, it should be a problem he is currently spending time and money on, or one that is affecting the business. Once you solve that problem, trust naturally follows.

That's exactly what we've seen in practice: many bosses are only willing to try one use case at first, and only after seeing results will they hand over the next dozen or dozens of problems.

Second, don't just talk about AI—solve problems on the spot.

Many business owners today have heard far too many AI concepts. What really works is to take the problem they care about right in front of them and let them see your thinking process with their own eyes. Concepts are easy to forget once heard, but when a real problem is solved on the spot, the boss immediately understands what AI has to do with them.

Third, shift the delivery goal from "building a tool" to "building enterprise capability."

I hope that in the future, enterprises won't have to come to me every time they hit a problem. After completing a project, what the enterprise takes away should be a methodology for continuing to solve problems on its own—a specific tool is not the point. After a few deliveries like this, when a new problem arises, the enterprise's first instinct will shift from "finding someone" to "trying it themselves first."

Fourth, the FDE must accept a fact: a lot of the work isn't "AI."

You may have to fix the network, deal with Windows environments, sort out file naming conventions, resolve permissions, wait for the boss to come back from a meeting, and chase employees to do testing. None of this looks like AI technology, but it determines whether AI can actually enter the real business.

The scarcest capability of an FDE is the ability to stand in front of real business problems and dismantle, one by one, all the obstacles preventing AI from delivering value. "How many models you know" is secondary.

Finally, I believe the ideal FDE makes the client increasingly independent.

One day, the enterprise will realize: in the past, when a need arose, I had to find a software company, an outsourcing firm, or a consultant; now, when that need arises, my own data, workflows, and AI capabilities are already in place, and I can try to solve it myself first.

At that point, AI truly becomes the enterprise's own capability, rather than just another line item on a procurement list.

From Manually Supervising Images to Company-Level Production: How an FDE Reshaped the Content Workflow of a Footwear & Apparel E-commerce Business with AI

Q1 As an FDE, what is the specific scenario where you applied AI? What did the concrete problems in this scenario look like?

The scenario I worked in this time was content production for e-commerce products.

My client is a traditional footwear, apparel, and headwear e-commerce company with its own factories and designers, selling mainly on platforms like Taobao and Tmall. It is already an industry leader, with annual revenue of roughly 1 billion RMB.

One characteristic of this kind of company is that it has a huge number of SKUs, and new products launch very quickly.

For a product, taking photos and uploading them is just the beginning. Every new product launch requires main product images and detail-page assets; and now with the rise of video e-commerce, shoppable video content is needed as well. As the number of SKUs grows, the volume of content production multiplies accordingly. The client has massive daily demands for images and videos, so they have long maintained a sizable design and operations team behind the scenes.

Faced with such a large demand, it was easy for me at first to oversimplify the problem: designers produce images too slowly, so let's use AI to help them. But once I actually got on-site, I found things weren't that simple.

First was the cost issue. Under the traditional model, the company needed a large number of design and operations staff continuously processing assets, so labor costs were already very high.

Second, even though the company had already started using AI, that didn't mean the efficiency problem was automatically solved.

Their original usage was quite fragmented—everyone opened their own AI accounts and wrote their own prompts. It looked like everyone was "using AI," but in reality they had just stuffed a new tool into an old workflow. The operational cost hadn't truly disappeared, and there was no qualitative change in efficiency.

The third problem was quality control.

Designers differ in aesthetic sense, experience, and AI skills, so the outputs naturally differ too. A highly skilled designer might quickly get good results, but such people are always a minority in any company. An enterprise cannot build its entire content production system on a few experts.

Beyond cost, efficiency, and quality control, there was another issue the client cared deeply about at the time: who do these assets actually belong to?

In the past, the images and videos designers produced were often scattered across personal computers. When someone left or changed roles, the assets could be lost with them. For a company of this scale, this meant that a huge amount of content—already produced at real cost—had never truly become a company asset.

So we later redefined the problem. What we needed to do was help the enterprise solve a more fundamental problem: how to use AI to transform a content production process that depended on individual skills and ran in a fragmented way into a company-level, reusable, and manageable production capability.

As an FDE, I've always placed great importance on this. I usually stand between the business owner, the actual users, and the AI technology. My responsibility is to first determine what the client's true value proposition is, and then figure out how AI should enter, how to reshape the workflow, and how to integrate existing resources.

Q2 Before you got involved, how was this problem solved in the past?

When the business wasn't yet this large and AI hadn't developed to where it is today, relying on designers and an operations team to support content production was a very natural approach.

Designers understand the products and the brand's aesthetics. Whatever images the company needed, they made. This model had already supported the client in scaling the business to a very large size, which itself proved it worked.

The problems emerged as business complexity kept rising. More and more SKUs, more and more images, more and more videos.

As business volume grew, the most direct way for the company to expand content supply was to add people. So content capacity became increasingly tightly bound to headcount.

Later, when AI came along, the client was actually quite proactive—they didn't avoid using AI at all. They started letting employees use various AI products on their own, hoping to improve efficiency.

But this led to a second stage: it used to be "humans making images," then it became "humans supervising AI making images," with people still having to make repeated adjustments themselves. What's worse, this approach continued to amplify individual differences.

The same set of tools, used by different people, produced completely different results. So from the company's perspective, this still didn't amount to a stable production capability.

Based on this, I concluded that simply purchasing a few AI tools for the enterprise, or teaching employees how to write prompts, could not solve the fundamental problem. What AI truly needs to transform is the entire workflow itself.
