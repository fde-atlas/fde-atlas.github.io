Why enterprise AI companies need forward deployed engineers

Not Another CEO · 2025-12-04 · 13 min read

Summary

Drawing on a decade of executive recruiting at True Search, the author argues that forward deployed engineers (FDEs) determine whether enterprise AI companies survive. Because most AI products share the same foundational models and face little technical differentiation, winning depends on embedding deeply into customer workflows. The article defines the FDE role, distinguishes it from sales engineers and solutions architects, outlines the hiring profile (hands-on engineering plus customer communication plus product thinking), suggests sourcing from internal engineers, early-stage startup engineers, and former founders, and covers interview design, sales-style variable compensation, and reporting lines—most often into product.

Key points

  • FDEs build custom solutions on-site and feed patterns back into the core product.
  • AI products lack moats unless woven into customer data and daily workflows.
  • Ideal FDEs resemble early-stage CTOs or former founders and are rare.
  • Compensation should include variable incentives tied to customer outcomes.
  • FDEs usually report into product to shorten the field feedback loop.

I was at a VC offsite hosted by Crosslink recently where they dedicated an entire session to AI in the enterprise.

The room was packed with founders comparing notes on how to land their first customers, integrate deeply enough to matter, and convert pilots to multimillion-dollar contracts. There were a lot of discussions on how to approach GTM, and the conversation kept circling back to one thing: forward deployed engineers (FDEs).

Everyone is talking about FDEs right now. Founders are actively hiring for these roles. VCs are pushing their portfolio companies to build FDE teams. But not everyone actually understands what a forward deployed engineer does, how to find qualified candidates, or how to structure the role so it actually works.

I’ve spent the last decade helping tech companies build their leadership teams at True Search. We’re the largest technology-focused executive search firm, and we’ve worked with companies like Twilio and Spotify since their earliest days. More recently, I’ve focused on AI-native startups, and I can confirm there is a shift in the types of roles these companies need to succeed.

I’ve placed several forward deployed engineering roles at various companies. What’s become clear to me is that FDEs are actually the key to whether these businesses will survive.

In this article, I’m going to explain what a forward deployed engineer is, why this role is more important than ever, what to look for when hiring an FDE, and share tips on compensation and org structure.

What is a forward deployed engineer?

A forward deployed engineer is someone who lives at the intersection of customer needs and product development. They’re engineers dedicated to specific customers, building solutions on the fly that aren’t part of the core product yet. FDEs also close the loop with product teams to turn custom solutions into platform features.

Palantir is well known for introducing this model in the early 2010s with their “echo and delta” approach. They embedded engineers with customers to deploy and customize Palantir’s platforms, ultimately maximizing value and adoption. But the role is having a moment right now because the problem it solves has become critical for AI companies.

So, what do FDEs actually do?

  • They spend time directly with a customer to understand their workflows, then build custom scripts, integrations, or features to make your product work for them.
  • They introduce functionality that meets immediate customer needs, even if they’re not on your current roadmap.
  • They identify patterns across customers and figure out what should be built into the core platform so you’re not rebuilding the same solution for every customer.

The importance of FDEs in the AI era

Partial usage is a death sentence for any product, but especially for an AI solution. For a product to have any kind of moat in the AI era, it needs to become deeply embedded with your customer’s team and workflows.

AI products are uniquely vulnerable to being replaced by a competitor who delivers more value faster. This is because with AI, there’s little technical differentiation between most products. Most are built on the same foundational models, and the underlying technology changes constantly. New competitors can spin up faster than ever. The company that wins isn’t necessarily the one with the best model (they all have access to the same foundational models); it’s the one that becomes indispensable, and gets woven into a customer’s data, processes, and daily workflows.

FDEs are critical in making that happen.

But to get an AI product adopted in day-to-day workflows, nearly all AI products need access to a company’s internal data; this goes well beyond traditional API integrations. “Ankle biter” features that unlock that marginal extra user, or which drive incremental usage, can no longer be delegated to your team’s engineering backlog; delivering on them today is now critical to whether that pilot becomes an enterprise deployment, and whether your solution is sticky and defensible when the next competitor comes calling.

AI companies, from their earliest days, must deliver on all of this while navigating security and compliance concerns. Traditional customer success and account management play important roles, as does your core engineering team, but they aren’t designed to handle this level of technical complexity or velocity of customer response.

This is where forward deployed engineers come in.

FDEs make the product work in ways that generic implementations can’t. And in doing so, they create the kind of lock-in that actually matters, not through contracts or switching costs, but through genuine value that’s woven into how a customer operates day to day.

FDEs are the difference between a $100k pilot that goes nowhere and a multi-million dollar contract.

FDEs vs. other technical roles

Forward deployed engineers aren’t sales engineers who demo features and answer technical questions during the sales process. They’re not solutions architects who design implementations but don’t build them. They are not members of your core engineering team who pick up the next feature on the roadmap. And they’re not traditional implementation consultants who just execute runbooks.

FDEs work directly with customers. They do hands-on building. They write code. They prototype and iterate. And when something they build for one customer proves valuable, they work with the product team to integrate it into the platform so all customers can benefit.

The role is still taking shape, and it means different things at different companies. But the core remains consistent: FDEs are engineers who can operate in ambiguity, build quickly alongside customers, and translate field insights into product improvements.

The FDE profile: what to look for

The best FDEs share a lot of DNA with early-stage CTOs, engineers, and former founders, and that overlap reflects the reality of what the role demands. These roles are particularly hard to fill because they require a highly skilled engineer who’s also comfortable talking with customers and capable of strategic product thinking.

Unfortunately, there aren’t enough former Palantir FDEs to go around. You need to know where else to look and what actually makes a great FDE.

Technical capabilities

FDEs need to be highly skilled engineers who can build quickly. This isn’t a role for someone who needs a lot of hand-holding or structured processes. You’re looking for people who can operate as an army of one, writing code, debugging issues, prototyping solutions, and iterating without a formal product spec to guide them.

They need to be hands-on. Some senior engineers are great architects but haven’t written production code in years. FDEs can’t afford to be that far from hands-on work. They must be ready to build integrations, write scripts, and ship solutions directly to customers. If they can’t move fast on their own, the model breaks down.

Customer-facing abilities

Technical chops alone aren’t enough. FDEs need to be conversational with clients in a way that many engineers aren’t. They’re meeting with your customer champions, business and operations leaders, and end users. They need to ask good questions, understand what problems people are actually trying to solve, and clearly explain technical tradeoffs.

This also means having a product mindset with a customer-centric approach. The best FDEs don’t just build what they’re told to build. They push back when a request doesn’t make sense. They identify the real problem beneath the customer’s request. And they’re comfortable building solutions on-site, in real-time, with customers watching.

Strategic product thinking

Technical and customer-facing skills are must-haves, but what separates good FDEs from exceptional ones is the ability to think strategically. Any engineer can build custom solutions for individual customers. But the ability to identify patterns, assess what multiple customers need, and prioritize which should be productized (rather than rebuilt each time) is an entirely different skill.

The goal is to minimize last-mile customization. Every bespoke integration you build is technical debt, and every custom script needs to be maintained. FDEs who think strategically are constantly asking: “Should this be part of the core product?” They bring insights back to the engineering and product teams, advocating for features that will reduce the need for one-off work and help customers succeed faster.

This recursive feedback loop is what makes FDEs fundamentally different from traditional professional services. The patterns FDEs identify don’t just help one customer, they become critical parts of the overall product roadmap. This coupling between field work and product development is critical to how enterprise AI companies progress and ultimately win.

It’s also why organizational structure matters so much. FDEs need a direct line to product and engineering teams, not a long chain of command that slows down the feedback loop.

If this profile sounds demanding, it is. You’re looking for someone who can code like a senior engineer, communicate like a seasoned CSM, and think strategically like a product manager. That’s a rare combination, which is exactly why these roles are so hard to fill, and why getting them right matters.

How to find forward deployed engineers

The good news is that FDEs exist. The bad news is they’re not easy to find, and they don’t always look like what you’d expect from a traditional engineering hire. Here are some tips for finding qualified candidates for an FDE role.

Start by looking at internal engineering talent

An obvious way to source FDEs is to look at your own engineering team. You probably have engineers who want more product orientation but don’t want to lose their technical skills. Maybe they’re interested in talking to customers but don’t want to move into product management or customer success. Or they’re drawn to the variety of working on different problems rather than diving deep into one area of the codebase.

These engineers can make excellent FDEs because they already understand your product and your technical stack. The learning curve is shorter, and they can start adding value immediately. The challenge is that you’re pulling strong engineers away from core product work, so you need to be intentional about when this makes sense.

Focus on candidates with early-stage startup experience

The best external candidates for an FDE role usually come from early-stage startups. Specifically, look for founding or early engineers who joined before structured product leadership was established.

Startup background matters because at early-stage companies, customer thinking is required just to survive. You don’t have the luxury of waiting for a product manager to write a spec. You’re often talking directly with customers, figuring out what they need, building quickly, and incorporating feedback into what you ship. You’re iterating without formal processes or safety nets, and that’s exactly what FDEs do.

Consider former founders

Technical founders who’ve been through the startup journey (whether it ended in an exit, a shutdown, or something in between) can make phenomenal FDEs. They’ve lived through a 0-to-1 build experience. They’re used to ambiguity and direct customer engagement. They don’t need their hand held. And they’re often looking for a role where they can still build and solve problems without the weight of running an entire company.

The ones who didn’t succeed can be particularly motivated. They have something to prove, they’ve learned expensive lessons about what works and what doesn’t, and they’re often hungry to apply that knowledge in a role where they can see immediate impact.

Tailor your interview process

You need someone technical to assess these candidates. FDEs are building real integrations and prototyping solutions. They need to be strong engineers. If you try to hire for this role without a technical evaluation, you’ll end up with people who can talk a good game but can’t execute when it matters.

The interview process should be centered on hands-on problem-solving. Here’s what I’ve seen work:

  • Collaborative kickoff session: Walk through a realistic problem and let the candidate ask clarifying questions. You’ll get a strong sense of how they think strategically just from the quality of their questions and how they frame the problem.
  • Take-home prototype: Give them a real coding assignment that feels like an actual FDE project, such as building a prototype.
  • Solution presentation: Communication is critical in this role. They need to be able to explain solutions to both customers and internal teams.
  • Peer references: Go beyond manager references. Peer feedback reveals how candidates operate solo on projects and the level of impact they truly had.

Don’t skip the technical assessment; it’s foundational to everything they’ll do.

Compensation structure

Here’s where things get tricky. FDEs don’t fit neatly into traditional engineering compensation models.

Why traditional engineering comp doesn’t work

Standard engineering comp is straightforward: base salary plus equity, maybe a small bonus tied to company performance. That works for engineers focused on building the core product. But FDEs need to be measured differently. Their success is tied directly to customer outcomes (whether integrations work, whether pilots convert to full contracts, and whether customers expand their usage).

The need for variable compensation

That means compensation needs to reflect those incentives. You want something closer to a sales compensation structure than traditional engineer comp. Base salary should still be competitive with what you’d pay a senior engineer, but you need variable compensation tied to the performance of the accounts they’re working on.

It could be tied to customer success metrics, like pilot-to-contract conversion rates, expansion revenue, or specific milestones, such as successful integrations or go-lives. The key is making sure FDEs have skin in the game when it comes to whether their customers succeed.

We’re still learning

I’ll be honest: there aren’t many established models for FDE compensation because the role itself is still relatively new. Most companies are experimenting, and what works at a 50-person startup might look different at a 500-person company.

The important thing is to avoid treating FDEs like standard engineers (or customer success managers) when it comes to comp. If you do, you’ll either underpay them for the value they create (and eventually lose them), or you’ll attract people who are more interested in the stability of pure engineering work than the customer-facing and strategic nature of the role.

Where FDEs should sit in your org

There’s no single right answer here. Where FDEs report depends on your stage, structure, and your key business goals.

Product org: This is where most companies land, and for good reason. FDEs gather signals from the field. They understand customers’ needs, what’s blocking adoption, and what features would unlock value. If they report into product, that feedback loop is shorter.

Engineering org: This keeps FDEs closer to the technical team and leverages existing relationships. It works well if your FDEs are contributing directly to the core product codebase. The downside is that engineering organizations are optimized for building scalable systems, not moving fast on one-off customer-specific needs.

Customer success: This makes intuitive sense because FDEs work directly with customers. But CS organizations historically lack technical depth. If you go this route, you need a CS leader with a strong technical background who can hire, manage, and support engineers.

Separate function: At early-stage startups, one of the founders is most likely the first FDE. As you grow, and hire FDEs, they will probably still report to the founders. A separate function can work long-term, but for most early stage companies, this adds unnecessary complexity.

The biggest org design challenge is how they coordinate with customer success managers. Both roles are customer-facing, but CSMs focus on relationship management and retention while FDEs focus on technical integration and building solutions. Define those boundaries early, or you’ll end up with territorial conflicts and gaps in ownership.

FDEs are how enterprise AI companies win

If you’re building an enterprise AI company, forward deployed engineers aren’t optional. The stakes are straightforward: in a world where technical moats don’t exist and competitors can spin up overnight, deep integration into your customers’ operations is a must, and FDEs make that happen.

Getting this right requires rethinking how you hire, compensate, and structure technical roles. You need engineers who can code, sell, and think strategically. You need compensation models that reward customer success, not just lines of code. And you need organizational clarity about how FDEs work with product, engineering, and customer success teams.

Start building your FDE function now. The companies that figure this out early will have a meaningful advantage over those that treat it as an afterthought.

Key takeaways

  • Forward deployed engineers (FDEs) are critical for enterprise AI companies to achieve deep customer integration and prevent churn.
  • FDEs build custom solutions for customers on-site, then identify patterns that should become core product features.
  • Look for candidates with early-stage startup experience, former founders, or internal engineers who want customer exposure.
  • Compensation should go beyond traditional engineering comp and include variable pay tied to customer success metrics.
