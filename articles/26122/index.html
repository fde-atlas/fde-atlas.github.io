How FDEs Turn Customer Integration Problems into Platform Improvements

Original title: FDE 如何把客户集成问题变成平台改进

FDE Times · 2026-10-28 · 6 min read

Summary

Using Column, an API-first bank, as its setting, this article argues that FDEs should treat recurring customer issues as signals to fix the platform rather than patching each client individually. It proposes a four-layer framework—live fix, documentation, tooling, platform—advising engineers to unblock customers at the cheapest layer first and escalate once an issue repeats three times. A worked example on duplicate transfers shows how a friction log with endpoints, root causes, and hours spent turns anecdotes into data for product teams. Job postings from Monumint, Bretton AI, and ModernFi confirm that closing this feedback loop is now an explicit FDE responsibility, and the article closes with resume advice on presenting complete closed-loop stories.

Key points

  • Customer friction in API products is a product signal, not just support work.
  • Fix at the lowest of four layers; escalate after three repetitions.
  • Keep a friction log with endpoint, root cause, workaround, and hours.
  • Monumint, Bretton AI, and ModernFi postings require feedback-to-product work.
  • Resumes should show a full loop from one customer to platform change.

Key Takeaways

  • At a bank selling APIs to developers, customers integrate directly with the platform, so wherever customers get stuck is a reliable signal about the product.

  • The FDE job postings from Monumint, Bretton AI, and ModernFi all explicitly include the responsibility of bringing customer feedback back into the product.

Imagine you're the first Forward Deployed Engineer (FDE) at a bank that sells APIs to developers. In your third week on the job, you fix the same bug for the third time for different customers: everything works fine in the sandbox, but in production, a transfer gets created twice.

By the third time, you have two paths. The first is to keep fixing it customer by customer, which anyone can do. The second is to treat this third occurrence as data, fix the root cause, and ensure the fourth customer never encounters this bug at all.

This article is about the second path. This capability is what distinguishes an FDE from an excellent technical support engineer, and some financial services companies now write it directly into their job postings.

When the API Is the Product, Customer Friction Is Your Job

Column calls itself the first national chartered bank built for developers and product teams.

Its API is defined by an OpenAPI spec, from which customers can generate SDKs, import into Postman or Insomnia, or run mocks locally.

The bank also provides a sandbox where developers can write software that directly operates bank accounts.

For an FDE, the most important detail lies elsewhere. According to Column's founders, because the bank owns its core systems and developer platform, it doesn't need a middle layer, and developer customers integrate directly with the bank. There's no vendor in the middle to blame, but there's also no vendor in the way when you want to fix something.

So at a bank like this, anywhere customers get stuck should first be treated as a signal about the product. Three FDE job postings from financial companies—Monumint, Bretton AI, and ModernFi—while not stating it this explicitly, all assign the FDE the responsibility of "bringing customer feedback back into the product."

Monumint expects its FDE to turn customer feedback into product improvements, and to grow ad-hoc quick fixes into durable systems serving thousands of customers.

Bretton AI requires building connectors, configuration tooling, and internal frameworks, turning solutions built for a single customer into reusable capabilities.

Which Layer Should the Fix Live In?

The hard part isn't fixing it, but deciding where to fix it. You can think in terms of four layers, from cheap and fast to expensive and durable:

LayerWhen to UseExample with the Duplicate Transfer Bug
On-the-spot fixFirst occurrence, customer is blockedCorrect the retry logic in the customer's code
DocumentationRecurring, caused by a misunderstandingAdd a warning and example to the endpoint description in the spec
ToolingDocumentation is already clear, but it still recursAdd a retry check to the onboarding flow
PlatformMany customers affected, high riskPropose that the SDK automatically attach a deduplication key

Rule of thumb: always fix at the lowest layer first so the customer can keep moving, then let the number of repetitions decide whether to move up.

ModernFi's job posting shows that the middle two layers are real FDE work, not side tasks. The FDE there owns and continuously improves public API documentation, and also works with the customer success team to gather feedback and refine self-serve onboarding tools.

A Complete Worked Example

Back to the opening scenario, now treated as a hypothetical exercise. Suppose your API supports idempotency keys, but the documentation only mentions them on a page few people read.

The first customer is a payroll startup. Its code retries after timeouts without sending an idempotency key, so any network hiccup in production produces duplicate transfers. The sandbox doesn't expose this bug because timeouts rarely happen there.

This is exactly the kind of work Bretton AI describes: edge cases that only surface after systems run in real production environments. In week one, you fix the customer's retry function on the spot. But before closing the ticket, you add an entry to your friction log:

- date: 2026-10-05
  customer: payroll-startup-a
  env: production
  endpoint: POST /transfers
  symptom: 超时后转账被创建两次
  root_cause: 重试时未发送幂等键
  tag: retry-duplicate
  workaround: 修正客户端重试，用 payroll_run_id 生成幂等键
  time_spent_hours: 3

By week three, two more entries with the same tag have appeared. A short script can turn the feeling of "this bug seems to happen a lot" into numbers:

import collections, yaml

entries = yaml.safe_load(open("friction_log.yaml"))
by_tag = collections.Counter(e["tag"] for e in entries)
for tag, n in by_tag.most_common():
    hours = sum(e["time_spent_hours"] for e in entries if e["tag"] == tag)
    flag = "ESCALATE" if n >= 3 else ""
    print(f"{tag:20} {n}x  {hours}h  {flag}")

After three occurrences, move up to the documentation layer. Since the API is defined by an OpenAPI spec, the warning and example should go directly into the endpoint's description in the spec, not into yet another wiki page. That way, the generated SDK, Postman collection, and the customer's local mock all carry the warning, right where developers are looking.

Next is the tooling layer: add a step to the onboarding checklist—"simulate a timeout in the sandbox and verify no duplicate transfers are created."

At the platform layer, you write a one-page proposal for the product team covering the three affected customers, the engineering hours spent, the current workaround, and the option of having the SDK automatically attach idempotency keys.

Bretton AI describes this as: turning how customers use the product into insights for engineering and product teams.

Start Doing This Yourself Tomorrow

Start a friction log from the very first ticket, even before any pattern is visible. Without early data, by the third occurrence all you have is memory. Each entry should include the endpoint, environment, root cause, workaround, and hours spent, because these are exactly what the product team will ask about.

Then set yourself a clear threshold, such as three occurrences, and review the log weekly rather than waiting for inspiration. When the threshold is hit, choose the lowest layer that solves the root cause.

If the customer misunderstood, fix the documentation; if they understood but still got it wrong, you need tooling. If you've done both and the bug keeps recurring, it belongs to the platform.

The step most often skipped is going back to tell the customers who hit the bug that the fix has shipped. This closes the feedback loop and makes customers more willing to keep telling you where they get stuck.

Common Pitfalls

The most common pitfall is staying at the on-the-spot fix layer. The customer is happy and you feel useful, but six months later, you're just a consultant chasing a single customer, and the platform is unchanged.

The opposite pitfall is demanding a platform-level fix the first time you see a problem, before there's any evidence it will recur.

Another pitfall is going into product meetings with scattered anecdotes. "Customers often complain about this" is far weaker than "three customers, nine engineering hours, same endpoint." Also, don't treat a passing sandbox run as done: the hardest bugs at a bank often only appear in production, so the onboarding checklist needs a step that simulates harsh conditions.

Turning This Skill into a Job-Hunting Advantage

When reading FDE job postings, especially at fintechs or companies selling infrastructure to banks, look for phrases like "turn customer feedback into product improvements," "reusable," or "own API documentation."

These phrases mean the company expects you to work across all four layers, not just fix bugs. Monumint also requires integrating with customers' existing systems, such as Salesforce, nCino, Fiserv, or Jack Henry, so candidates with real integration experience have an edge.

On a resume, show the complete closed loop rather than listing tasks. For example: "Discovered a retry bug affecting three customers through a friction log, fixed the spec and onboarding checklist, and proposed an SDK change that was added to the product roadmap." It shows recruiters that you can see the whole platform from a single customer.

If you're not yet an FDE, you can practice this closed loop with any public API: try an integration, document where you got stuck, then submit a documentation fix suggestion.

The fourth customer will never know which bug they dodged. FDEs who do this well leave a bigger mark on the platform than on the support inbox.
