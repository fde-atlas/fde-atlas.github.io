Summary

This article from FDE Soken breaks down the cost of hiring Forward Deployed Engineers in Japan. It argues FDE costs consist of three layers—people, environment, and time—and that budgets built on salary alone almost always fall short. Full-time hires run ¥8-15 million annually, but first-year real cost is 1.4-1.6x stated salary once social insurance, recruiter fees (~30%), and a 2-3 month ramp-up are included. Contracted FDEs cost ¥0.8-2 million monthly; vendor routes reach ¥1.5-3.5 million. The author recommends estimating per-project cost rather than person-months, using a six-step method, allocating 20-30% to PoC, 40-50% to production rollout, and 20-30% to handover. Main overrun causes are scope creep, underestimated data cleanup, knowledge silos, and endless free PoCs. ROI should be judged via time savings, revenue contribution, and retention, targeting payback within 12-18 months, with four exit signals defined upfront.

Key points

  • FDE costs span three layers: people, environment (scales with project count), and time (ramp-up and handover).
  • Full-time FDEs cost ¥8-15M/year; real first-year cost is 1.4-1.6x stated salary.
  • Contractors run ¥0.8-2M/month; vendor-sourced FDEs ¥1.5-3.5M/month but start fastest.
  • Estimate per-project cost, not person-months; ramp-up and handover add roughly 20% annually.
  • Judge ROI with three metrics and a 12-18 month payback target; two or more exit signals mean scale down or switch.

What makes up FDE costs?

FDE costs consist of three layers: personnel costs, environment costs, and time costs. A budget built on personnel costs alone will almost certainly fall short.

FDE (Forward Deployed Engineer) is an engineering role popularized by Palantir. FDEs embed themselves at customer sites, connect their company's product to the customer's operations, and take responsibility through to the point where value is delivered. Although they are development roles, most of their work happens on the customer side, so their cost structure differs from that of typical software engineers.

As a benchmark, FDE costs are roughly ¥8M–15M annually for full-time hires, or ¥800K–2M per month for contracted work. However, the stated amount alone does not determine the total cost.

LayerMain cost itemsHow it growsWhy estimates often miss it
PeopleCompensation, statutory benefits, hiring, trainingProportional to headcountBudgets often treat the stated salary as 1.0x
EnvironmentTravel, security, test environments, licensesLinear, proportional to project countHandled per customer, so costs can't be allocated across projects
TimeRamp-up, handoverIncurred at the start and end of projectsRarely recorded as work hours

Of the three layers, People is the largest in monetary terms. However, the remaining two layers are the most likely causes of estimation errors. Environment costs grow in proportion to the number of projects, while time costs push up the total without ever being recorded as work hours.

Note that the numerical ranges in this article are guidelines based on levels commonly presented in Japanese job postings and contracting agreements. They are not figures from specific companies' track records or official statistics. Always replace them with your own company's grading table and actual quotations.

How much do people-related costs run? Compensation, hiring, and statutory benefits

The largest cost item for FDEs is personnel costs. In practice, it's best to look at the total, including not just the stated salary but also hiring fees and statutory benefit costs.

Cost itemDescriptionImpact on total cost (guideline)
Base compensationAnnual salary / monthly contract feeBaseline (1.0)
Statutory benefit costsEmployer's share of social insurance premiumsAround +15%
Hiring costsAgency fees (typically 30–35% of theoretical annual salary)Around +30% lump sum in the first year
Training and onboardingRamp-up period for product and domain understandingUsable capacity is reduced for the first 2–3 months

With full-time hiring, first-year actual costs tend to reach 1.4–1.6x the stated salary. Organizations that budget at 1.0x will inevitably struggle partway through the fiscal year.

Training and onboarding are especially easy to overlook. Full salary is being paid, yet usable capacity is reduced for the first 2–3 months. In accounting terms, this is buried within personnel costs, but from the perspective of project profitability, it is a definite loss.

What are environment-related costs? Travel, security, and test environments

FDEs work at customer sites. As a result, travel costs, security measures, and test environment maintenance costs grow in proportion to the number of projects.

  • Travel and on-site costs: If on-site presence at the customer several days a week is assumed, transportation and lodging costs accumulate monthly
  • Security measures: Responding to customer security checklists, device provisioning, VPN/dedicated lines
  • Test environments: Environments that mimic the structure of customer data, which must be maintained separately from production
  • Tool licenses: The portion of BI, ETL, monitoring, and cloud costs allocated to customer projects

Although smaller than personnel costs, the defining feature of this layer is that it grows linearly in proportion to the number of projects. When you increase from 3 to 10 projects, this is the layer that bites. Personnel costs can be absorbed to some extent by having staff share multiple projects. However, per-customer security measures and test environments cannot, in principle, be allocated across projects. If you're estimating gross margins as you scale, keep this layer as a separate line item.

What are time-related costs? Ramp-up and handover

Ramp-up takes 2–4 months, and handover takes 1–2 months. These entry and exit work hours change the annual total FDE cost by around 20%.

The quality of FDE deliverables depends on how deeply they understand the customer's business. From the start of engagement to actually delivering value, it's prudent to allow 2–4 months based on field experience. Conversely, at the end of a project, if code or documentation that only the assigned person can handle is left behind, an additional 1–2 months of handover work is required.

The tricky part is that both are hard for anyone to see. The ramp-up period gets dismissed as "they're just not used to it yet." The handover period gets excluded from the budget as "something that happens after the project ends." Unless these are explicitly listed as line items in the quotation, treat them as costs that will surface later as actual expenses.

How do FDE cost benchmarks vary by contract type?

Depending on whether you choose full-time employment, contracting, or going through a vendor, the annual FDE cost for the same work can differ by 1.5–2x or more.

Contract typeCost benchmarkRamp-up speedEase of exitKnowledge retention
Full-time hireAnnual salary ¥8M–15M plus overheadSlow (3–6 months)LowStays in-house
Contract / freelance¥800K–2M per monthFast (2–6 weeks)HighEasily lost when the contract ends
Vendor / consultant¥1.5M–3.5M per monthFastestHigh (per contract)Stays with the vendor

*Note: These figures assume senior to lead-level engineers. Junior engineers and contracts with utilization rates below 50% will fall below these lower bounds.

If you compare only rates, full-time employees appear cheapest. However, the axes you should compare are four: rate, speed of ramp-up, ease of withdrawal, and how knowledge is retained. Which to prioritize depends on how long the project is expected to continue.

How much does a full-time FDE cost? Total cost is 1.4x the offered salary

Full-time FDEs come with recruiting fees and statutory welfare costs added on top. Expect the total cost to be roughly 1.4 to 1.6 times the offered salary.

FDEs are required to be engineers who can negotiate with clients, making this a role with a small candidate pool. It is not uncommon for it to take 3 to 6 months from posting a job opening to a hire being decided. The opportunity loss during that period is also a real cost.

However, in terms of rate alone, full-time employees are the cheapest, and if they will be working for 2 years or more, this form tends to be advantageous. The dividing line is whether you have decided to continue in that project area for 2 years or more.

How much does a contracted or freelance FDE cost? Monthly fixed rates are the norm

Contracted FDEs typically command a fixed monthly rate of 8 to 20 million yen. While you can secure immediately productive talent in a short period, the rate is comparatively expensive.

Rates are generally 20 to 30% higher than the full-time equivalent. Many are quasi-delegation contracts with 3 to 4 days of work per week, making it easy to adjust to fluctuations in project demand.

On the other hand, there is a risk that the client's business logic exists only in the assigned person's head. Whether deliverables are explicitly stated as documentation in the contract changes the cost at contract termination. Write both working days and deliverables into the contract, and confirm the handover of deliverables each month. Doing so can significantly reduce the person-dependency costs discussed later.

Why are FDEs via vendors or consultancies expensive? Management fees are added

Going through a vendor adds management fees and risk premiums, making the rate the highest. In exchange, ramp-up is the fastest, and it is easy to arrange replacement personnel.

When entrusting Palantir-style implementation support to a vendor, in addition to the individual engineer's rate, overhead costs for PM, quality control, and sales are added. Before dismissing it as "expensive," compare it with the total cost if your company were to absorb the risks of hiring, training, and turnover itself.

Even if the assigned person leaves, replacement personnel can be provided. This value does not appear in rate tables, but it directly affects whether you can maintain the team structure.

How should FDE costs be estimated?

FDE costs should not be estimated as "rate × person-months"; accuracy improves significantly when you build them up as the total cost required to run a single project.

What is the procedure for estimating? 6 steps

Building up in order through 6 steps—from project definition to staffing, personnel costs, environment costs, and buffer—greatly reduces omissions in estimates.

  • Define the target project: Decide the number of clients, expected duration per project (e.g., 3 months), and number of concurrent projects
  • Break down work allocation: Assign ratios of expected hours across 4 categories: client negotiation / data preparation / implementation / operational handover
  • Design the staffing structure: Write out the composition, e.g., "1 FDE lead + 1-2 FDEs + 0.5 backend support"
  • Calculate total personnel costs: For full-time employees, calculate salary × 1.4-1.6; for contractors, monthly rate × number of months
  • Add environment and travel costs: Accumulate linearly as number of projects × monthly fixed costs
  • Make the buffer explicit: Account for 2-4 months of ramp-up and 1-2 months of handover on separate lines

For step 6, do not round it into a "buffer"; keep it as an independent line. This allows you to later verify where things deviated when the budget is exceeded. In practice, my impression is that whether or not this single line exists greatly changes the accuracy of next-period estimates.

Why look at cost per project rather than person-months?

FDE outcomes are determined by project success or failure, not hours worked. Therefore, allocating costs on a per-project basis is closer to reality.

ItemExample
Annual FDE team cost30 million yen
Number of projects handled8 projects
Cost per projectApprox. 3.75 million yen

Only by placing this cost per project alongside the contract amount per client (ARR) can you judge "should we have taken on that project?" If you look only at person-month rates, loss-making projects hide in the shadow of profitable ones and become invisible.

When lined up by cost per project, differences become visible. For example, even at the same rate, the cost spikes only for projects where data preparation took extra time. You can then work backward from actual results to determine which variables to consider in the next estimate.

How should costs be allocated by phase?

As a guideline, allocate 20-30% to PoC, 40-50% to production implementation, and 20-30% to operational handover. Leaning too heavily toward PoC delays cost recovery.

PhaseMain tasksCost allocation guidelinePoints to note
PoC / validationData verification, testing value hypotheses20-30%Making it free or low-rate tends to make recovery impossible
Production implementationImplementation, business integration, training40-50%This is the biggest peak. Staff it more heavily
Operational handoverInternalization support, documentation20-30%Cutting it rebounds as next-period costs

In an allocation table this looks obvious, but the phase that actually breaks down most easily is the final operational handover. Since results are already delivered by the end of production implementation, the 20-30% allocated to internalization support and documentation is the first to be cut. And the amount cut comes back the next period as handover costs.

Why do FDE costs exceed expectations?

The causes of FDE cost overruns often lie more in structural factors—scope, data quality, person-dependency, PoC—than in individual ability.

CauseWhat happensAction to take first
Scope creepWork outside the contract scope flows inFix deliverables as a bulleted list, agree to changes in writing
Underestimating data preparationData preparation takes longer than implementationAsk to work with sample data for a week
Person-dependencyHandover costs arise when someone leavesInclude documentation in both the contract and evaluations
Prolonged free PoCCost recovery recedesDecide validation items and judgment dates at the start

What happens when scope creeps?

The closer an FDE works with clients, the more requests outside the contract scope flow in, gradually eroding available hours. Fix the deliverables before starting work.

FDEs work closely with customers, which makes it easy to build trust. The flip side is that requests like "while you're at it, could you also..." tend to arise. The way to prevent this is simple: fix the deliverables in writing as a bullet list before starting work, and handle any changes with a written additional agreement. Precisely to protect the relationship with the customer, you need to draw a clear line.

Why is the cost of data preparation underestimated?

Data preparation tends to take longer than implementation, and the quality of the customer's data cannot be fully known before signing the contract. That is why it gets underestimated.

Common issues include missing column definitions, inconsistent notation, mismatched update frequencies, and access permission barriers. Even if you assume the use of an integrated platform like Palantir, the effort for data preparation does not go away if the input data is messy. Before preparing an estimate, ask to work with sample data for just one week. Whether or not you can insert this short preliminary step will change the accuracy of your subsequent estimates.

What costs does person-dependency create?

A situation where only a specific person can handle the work surfaces as handover costs when they leave. It is hard to see, but it should be considered a de facto liability.

The countermeasure is to include documentation as a "deliverable" in both the contract and the evaluation criteria. While the system is running, no one has trouble, so documentation tends to be put off. Unless you consciously allocate effort to it, it will always be postponed. It is realistic to design on the assumption that "documentation that is not in the evaluation criteria will not be written."

What happens when a free PoC drags on?

For free or low-priced PoCs, the longer they run, the further away recovery gets. Setting the duration and decision criteria at the start is the only brake.

A guideline for cost allocation in the PoC/verification phase is 20-30%. However, verification that starts for free tends to be extended without any criteria for ending it. Decide the verification items and the judgment date at the start, and decide by date whether to proceed to full deployment or stop. This one extra step helps preserve the assumptions of your phase-based allocation.

How do you judge the cost-effectiveness of an FDE?

The cost-effectiveness of an FDE cannot be measured by time savings alone. In practice, it is realistic to judge recovery based on three factors: business reduction, sales contribution, and retention rate.

How should cost-effectiveness metrics be set? Three metrics side by side

With only one metric, you will misjudge. Line up business reduction, sales contribution, and retention rate, and evaluate separately for the short term and mid term.

Metric typeExampleHow to measure
Business reductionHours of manual work saved for the customerMeasure actual work hours before and after implementation (if estimated, state the basis)
Sales contributionWin rate and deal size for projects the FDE was involved inCompare with vs. without involvement
RetentionChurn rate, additional contract rateContinuation status 12 months after implementation

There are also areas that handle real-time field data, as with VACAN Technologies (VACAN, Inc.), which runs the FDE Research Institute. In such areas, there are cases where whether operations have taken hold after six months explains the business impact better than the hours saved immediately after implementation. When designing your metrics, separate the short term from the mid term.

How should you think about the payback period?

In practice, divide the FDE team's annual cost by the gross profit per project, and use whether you can recover it within 12-18 months as a guideline for judgment.

ItemExample
Annual FDE team cost30 million yen
Gross profit per project10 million yen
Number of projects needed to break even in a single year3

This calculation is less a rigorous financial computation than a common language for putting the discussion on the same footing. When the sense of "expensive/cheap" diverges between management and the field, what matters is whether you can talk on the same formula.

How do you decide when to stop or switch?

Withdrawal criteria should be decided in advance. It is precisely the projects that continue without criteria that most damage the cost-effectiveness of an FDE.

No.Signs to consider withdrawal or switching
1Six months after implementation, not a single practice has taken hold on the customer side
2More than half of the operating time is spent maintaining existing features rather than creating value
3No signs of additional contracts or upsells for 12 months
4No handover documentation, so the person in charge cannot be replaced

If two or more apply, consider downsizing the team or changing the contract structure. Since an FDE is a high-cost arrangement, the cost of continuing is also large. Design the exit with the same care as the entry. That, in the end, keeps the overall cost lowest.
