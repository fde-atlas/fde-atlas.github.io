MIIT Issues Directive to Cultivate AI Application Service Providers, Introducing the FDE Model to Solve the "Last Mile" of AI Deployment

Original title: 工信部发文培育AI应用服务商，引入FDE模式破解AI落地最后一公里

虎嗅 · 2026-10-01 · 9 min read

Summary

In August 2026, China's Ministry of Industry and Information Technology issued a notice to cultivate AI application service providers, explicitly encouraging FDE teams rooted at customer sites. The article explains Palantir's FDE model, cites MIT research showing 95% of generative AI pilots produced no measurable P&L impact, and contrasts China's average 5.11 million yuan per LLM project with slow growth in AI services. It compares the US bet on OpenAI's DeployCo with China's administrative approach of service teams, certification and evaluation systems, arguing the AI bottleneck has shifted from models to people and delivery.

  • Both bets assume the gap between models and delivery lasts five to ten years.

On August 27, the General Office of the Ministry of Industry and Information Technology issued a notice, document number MIIT Office Science Letter [2026] No. 414, made public on August 31.

The name is a bit long: the "Notice on Launching a Special Action to Cultivate AI Application Service Providers."

Documents like this are usually never read closely, yet they are actually quite important, so we read it for you—and found that at the end of the fourth task in this document hides a very important sentence: encourage service providers to build Forward Deployed Engineer (FDE) teams, rooted at users' sites, to ensure scenario implementation.

FDE, Forward Deployed Engineer.

These three letters are not a Chinese-made term; they come from an internal job title at Palantir. Think about it: a job title from an American defense contractor (and one that is not friendly to China) appearing in a red-header document from a Chinese ministry does indeed say something.

AI is very hot right now, but there are also many problems. The MIIT's diagnosis of the current situation: unclear resource baselines, uneven delivery quality, broken commercial loops, and an underdeveloped service ecosystem.

For example, "broken commercial loop" means the work is being done, but the people doing it aren't making money.

In fact, at this stage, the bottleneck for AI implementation has shifted from models to people.

I. The 5% Success

MIT's Project NANDA conducted a study analyzing over 300 public enterprise AI deployment cases, 52 executive interviews, and 153 surveys.

The conclusion: 95% of generative AI pilots produced no measurable P&L impact.

Only 5% of integrated systems created significant value.

And the corresponding investment was 30 to 40 billion dollars.

But the point is not that only 5% succeeded; the point is the reason for failure.

And the failure is not that the models are bad—the models are very good. The problem lies in the "learning gap"—general tools work especially well for individuals because they are flexible; but once they enter an enterprise, they get stuck, because they don't learn your workflows or adapt to your processes.

Here's an analogy.

Over the past two years, enterprises worldwide collectively bought a gym membership. The card is real, the gym is real, the equipment is all new, and the trainers can speak eight languages.

The only problem is that it's very inconvenient for you to get there each time, and every time you go in, you have to speak the trainer's foreign language.

This is the "last mile" problem.

In the State Council's August 2025 "Opinions on Deeply Implementing the 'AI+' Action," the official interpretation states in black and white: application implementation faces "last mile" obstacles.

It was also that "Opinions" that first proposed "cultivating AI application service providers" and "building an AI application service chain."

The MIIT's special action this time is the first blueprint for implementing that sentence.

II. China's Average of 5.11 Million

Let's now do the math for China itself.

Third-party statistics show that in 2025, 7,539 large-model-related winning bids were recorded domestically, of which 5,779 disclosed amounts, totaling 29.52 billion yuan.

The number of projects grew 396% compared to 2024.

By rough calculation based on disclosed figures, the average large-model project is about 5.11 million yuan.

What can 5.11 million buy?

In a first-tier city, it's roughly the cost of a 15-person team working for six months—not counting travel, rework, or the client changing requirements three times. And after finishing this project, what do you have left that you can sell to the next client?

Most likely nothing. This is the "broken commercial loop."

There's another data point: in the first half of 2026, 2,770 application-type large-model winning bids were spread across more than 20 industries, with about 73% concentrated in five sectors: government affairs, telecommunications, education/science, energy, and finance. About a quarter of the project names included the words "agent."

Among the top ten vendors, three are state-owned enterprises with telecom operator backgrounds—they win projects as "general integrators."

Now let's cross-reference: as of March 2026, domestic daily token call volume exceeded 140 trillion, an increase of more than a thousandfold since early 2024.

But in all of 2025, China's AI professional services market only just exceeded 4 billion dollars, growing only 4x.

The model layer is riding a rocket.

The services layer is still laboriously riding a bicycle.

III. Understanding Palantir's FDE

Since the document mentions FDE, let's take a good look at Palantir's FDE.

In the early 2000s, Palantir ran into a problem no other software company had encountered: its customers were intelligence agencies.

These customers couldn't articulate what they wanted, couldn't show you their data, and their workflows changed daily.

The traditional software approach of "gather requirements—design a solution—deliver a product" simply failed here.

They realized that when facing sufficiently complex new problems, customers often need to see something that runs before they know what they actually want.

So product discovery cannot be done at headquarters; it must be done on-site.

At the time, only two kinds of people were available in the market:

One was consultants, who could understand the business but couldn't write production code;

The other was sales engineers, who could write code but couldn't modify the product.

Palantir's solution was to embed engineers directly into client organizations—internally called Delta.

For a full decade, Palantir had more Delta personnel than product engineers.

There's an organizational design detail here: a Delta is always paired with an Echo—a Deployment Strategist, a role specifically responsible for adoption, politics, and executive alignment.

Because the technical "last mile" is getting the model to run, while the organizational "last mile" is getting the other side's leadership to sign off—two completely different problems requiring two different talents, so they must be staffed separately.

It's a bit like our regimental commander teaming up with a political commissar.

Even more worth learning: the FDE's task is not to do custom work for clients indefinitely; their real responsibility is to identify recurring patterns from one site after another, then abstract them into platform capabilities.

Palantir's ontology, object model, permission system, and data lineage were all dug out of the mud this way and then encoded into the product.

The essence of the Palantir model is that accumulated capabilities flow back into the product, making the next deal cheaper.

Someone online has already summarized it:

  • Finish a project and leave the client with only a system—that's called outsourcing;
  • Bring back some experience but with no reusability—that's called project-based delivery;
  • Distill industry-general experience into templates and product capabilities—that's what makes an FDE;
  • After distillation, the delivery cost for the next similar client drops significantly—that's what makes a scalable FDE.

Consulting firms sell the same billable hours twice; FDEs turn billable hours into product.

IV. America's Bet

On May 11, 2026, OpenAI founded an independent company called DeployCo.

It launched with over 4 billion dollars in investment, a post-money valuation of 14 billion dollars, jointly funded by 19 global institutions, led by TPG, with Advent, Bain Capital, and Brookfield as co-leads.

The investor list also includes—McKinsey, Bain & Company, Capgemini.

These three are the top tier of global consulting and systems integration, and DeployCo's business model is essentially using engineers plus language models to do what they are doing now—and cheaper.

It's as if the carriage-makers' guild collectively bought shares in Ford Motor Company.

One analysis put it bluntly: look at the capital structure and it's clear—this company doesn't look like an OpenAI subsidiary at all; it looks like Accenture.

What about the real Accenture?

Accenture's generative AI-related revenue tripled to $2.7 billion in fiscal 2025, and bookings nearly doubled to $5.9 billion. In Q1 of fiscal 2026, advanced AI bookings hit $2.2 billion, doubling again year over year, with AI and data professionals reaching 80,000.

Then the company announced: it would stop disclosing this figure separately going forward.

The reason: AI has already permeated almost everything we do.

V. China's Bet

America's answer is one company.

China's answer is a list.

In 2023, the Ministry of Finance and the Ministry of Industry and Information Technology launched pilot cities for SME digital transformation. The approach: market-based open selection of digital service providers, who develop integrated "small, fast, lightweight, precise" products for enterprises to voluntarily choose, then build model cases and replicate and scale them.

Those four words—"small, fast, lightweight, precise"—were carried over verbatim into this AI service provider document. It's a proven administrative tool, just applied to a new track.

The new document also includes a design called "AI Application Service Teams": each team is led by one service provider with no fewer than two upstream and downstream partners, and each locality submits no fewer than ten teams in principle.

This is essentially using administrative power to forcibly assemble Palantir's Delta + Echo.

Because in China, few companies possess all three things at once: model capability, industry know-how, and delivery capability.

Since one company can't cover it all, three get assembled into a team.

Shanghai is leading the way.

On November 29, 2025, Shanghai held the first FDE (Forward Deployed Engineer) training program. It then launched the "Hundred-Thousand-Ten Thousand" initiative: linking a hundred enterprises, building a thousand AI agents, and driving ten thousand developers to transform, while establishing an FDE talent reserve of over a thousand engineers.

The incentive system is also in place: FDE training is tied to intermediate professional title certification from the human resources and social security departments.

And an evaluation system is already growing rapidly.

The CAICT's standard for AI agent application development service capabilities has already classified vendors into five tiers: foundational, enhanced, excellent, leading, and exemplary. An enterprise AI service capability maturity model assessment, led by an institution under the Ministry of Industry and Information Technology, has also been launched nationwide.

Of course, under this incentive, what may grow first is an industry dedicated to evaluating delivery quality.

Organizational issues must also be considered.

Palantir's model works because of an entire organizational system behind it—a flat structure that eliminates internal competition, autonomous mission-driven teams organized around problems rather than functions, and that feedback loop that turns on-the-ground pain points into platform capabilities.

Without that system, rushing to hire a team of FDEs gets us nothing more than embedded consultants.

But from another angle, that may not be a bad thing.

Because the embedded consultant is precisely the scarcest role right now.

Where Chinese enterprises are stuck today is mostly not "lacking reusable platform capabilities," but simply that no one is sitting on the factory floor, running a single task end to end.

First get people on the ground, then talk about accumulation—the order isn't reversed.

More importantly, anything transplanted from outside, once it grows in Chinese soil, will eventually grow into its own form.

Township enterprises aren't the corporate form found in any textbook, and the industrial organization of solar and EV industries wasn't copied from Germany or Japan.

The FDE will most likely be the same. It arrives called Forward Deployed Engineer, but what grows out of it a few years later may no longer resemble Palantir much—yet it will likely fit the clients here, the payment terms here, and the people here better.

VI. A Map for Localities

Finally, something most practical for cities and industrial parks.

The FDE is a profession that must be on-site.

He doesn't sit at headquarters writing code; he needs to sit in the meeting rooms of factories, banks, and hospitals.

So the geographic distribution of this kind of capability will be forced to follow industry, not model companies.

At the 2025 World AI Conference, Shanghai disclosed that the city's AI talent pool had reached nearly 300,000, about one-third of the national total. But factories, power grids, hospitals, and ports are mostly not in Shanghai.

The geographic mismatch between supply and demand starts right here.

The document also contains one more sentence: organize the opening of a batch of real-world scenarios.

Before this, "scenarios" was an adjective in investment-promotion rhetoric; in this mechanism, the scenario becomes, for the first time, an asset that can be traded—because what service providers truly lack is not compute, not models, but a place where they can run their solution once and be allowed to fail while doing so.

Accompanying this, pilot-testing bases and incubators are explicitly written into the document as the foundational conditions for providers' R&D, testing and validation, and pilot maturation.

The role of industrial parks is shifting from "providing space" to "providing validation conditions."

VII. The Wager in AI's Cracks

Historically, every time a new profession was invented, the signal was the same.

After the steam engine came the mechanical engineer; after electricity, the electrical engineer; after ERP, the implementation consultant; after cloud computing, the SRE.

Each time, it wasn't because the technology was inadequate—precisely because it was good enough that the bottleneck shifted from the machine to the human.

This time is the same: models are no longer the problem.

Open-source models are strong enough that many bidders only need to buy compute; everything else is the dirty work of integration, data, processes, and compliance.

But this time is also a bit different.

The very reason the FDE profession exists is that models still can't deploy themselves.

It's a gap-filling profession, and gaps change.

If models keep getting stronger—strong enough to read codebases on their own, connect systems themselves, run validations themselves, write delivery documents themselves—then how long is the window for this profession just named simultaneously by two great powers?

No one knows the answer.

But both sides have already placed their bets.

America is betting on compounding: one company, a corps of engineers, turning every field experience into product, betting it will snowball.

China is betting on scale: three thousand service providers, one set of standards, one professional title track, betting that a sea of people can flatten that last mile.

And the shared premise of both bets is: this gap will exist for at least another five to ten years.
