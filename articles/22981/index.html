Summary

This DeFactory article argues that the '35-year-old retirement' myth for freelance engineers in Japan is outdated, rooted in 2000s-era SI industry culture. The real threat to career longevity is not age but 'shoryu' (commercial chain) depth: a project budgeted at ¥1.5M/month can shrink to ¥500-600K by the third subcontracting layer, forcing full-time work that leaves no time for learning. Data shows freelancers in their 50s polarize—those with direct end-client contracts earn ¥1M+ monthly, while those stuck in downstream implementation exit. The author recommends shortening the contracting chain, shifting to PM, IT consulting, teaching, or hybrid BizDevTech/FDE roles, building T-shaped skills, certifications, and networks. Japan's projected IT talent shortage of 790,000 by 2030 favors experienced engineers.

Key points

  • The 35-year-old ceiling is a relic of 2000s SI industry norms, not a current reality.
  • Deep subcontracting chains cut a ¥1.5M project to ¥500-600K for third-tier engineers.
  • Engineers in their 50s polarize: direct end-client deals pay ¥1M+/month; downstream workers exit.
  • Recommended paths: PM, IT consulting, technical teaching, or BizDevTech/FDE hybrid roles.
  • Japan faces a projected IT talent shortage of up to 790,000 by 2030, favoring veterans.

"Freelance engineers have short careers" "Once you're over 35, projects dry up" — have you ever felt anxious after hearing things like this?

To conclude upfront, the career span of a freelance engineer is not determined by "age." The biggest factor that shortens it is the "depth of the business flow" — being embedded in multi-layered subcontracting where your rate never rises.

In this article, drawing on firsthand information from the front lines of a freelance agency, we cover realistic income data by age group, the reality of being cut at 50 due to age, and business flow and survival strategies for maintaining a long career.

The "career span" of a freelance engineer refers to the period during which you can keep winning projects and working while maintaining an income you're satisfied with. Many people wonder "until what age can I keep working?", but the answer varies greatly from person to person. Let's first clear up common misconceptions and look at the actual situation through data.

Limited to engineers with 5+ years of practical experience

High-rate, direct-contract project information

Many of you have probably heard the phrase "the 35-year-old retirement age theory for engineers." This originally comes from discussions in the SI industry up until the early 2000s.

At the time, IT workplaces were a "battle of stamina" where long working hours were the norm. Under seniority-based HR systems, the typical career path was to move into a management role around age 35, and there was even an image that "anyone still writing code past 35 had fallen off the promotion track."

However, the situation in 2026 is completely different. Remote work has become widespread, greatly increasing freedom in where and when people work. Against the backdrop of the IT talent shortage, it's not unusual to see engineers actively writing code well past 35.

Freelancers, after all, have no company retirement system. As long as you have skills and good health, it's theoretically possible to take on projects in your 60s or even 70s. The 35-year-old retirement age theory can now be considered a thing of the past.

Now let's look at the actual state of freelance engineers by age group. The freelance population, including side gigs, has reached 2.57 million, and freelancing itself as a way of working is spreading.

The following trends emerge when broken down by generation.

What deserves attention is the "polarization" among people in their 50s. Those who have built experience in upstream processes and management continue earning over 1 million yen per month through direct contracts with end companies. Meanwhile, those who stayed in downstream implementation work often get caught up in competition with younger engineers, see their rates keep dropping, and are forced to exit.

In other words, age alone doesn't determine your career span, but the reality is that "if you do nothing, it will definitely shrink."

Looking at data by occupation, about 80% of engineers and technical development freelancers achieve an annual income of 4 million yen or more. Consulting professionals are at a similar level, showing a tendency to earn higher incomes compared to other occupations.

What determines a freelance engineer's career span is not age. It's whether you remain in a position that the market continues to demand. That is the true meaning of "career span."

There are real reasons why people say "freelance engineers don't last long." However, there's no need to feel anxious. Once you understand the true nature of the problem, you can take countermeasures. Here, we organize the structural causes into five points.

The biggest factor affecting a freelance engineer's career span is actually not "age" but the "depth of the business flow."

The multi-layered subcontracting structure remains deeply rooted in the IT industry. Every time the business flow deepens — end company → prime contractor → second-tier → third-tier... — an intermediate margin is added, and the compensation that reaches the engineer actually writing the code keeps shrinking.

For example, it's not uncommon to hear that even when an end company places an order with a monthly budget of 1.5 million yen, by the time it reaches a third-tier engineer, it has dwindled to around 500,000–600,000 yen per month.

At 600,000 yen per month, you would need to work more than full-time just to make a living. There is almost no time left for learning or rest. Your skills stagnate, and your rates drop even further. This negative spiral is the biggest cause of a shortened career lifespan.

On the other hand, an engineer earning 1 million yen per month on a direct end-client or prime contractor project can earn a comfortable income working just 3 to 4 days a week. The spare time allows for skill development and catching up on new technologies, which makes it possible to maintain market value over the long term.

The deeper the commercial chain, the shorter the career lifespan. This is the core point I want you to remember throughout this article.

"It's over when you can no longer keep up with new technologies." Many engineers think this way, but the real danger is not technological obsolescence. It is position obsolescence.

In other words, the risk of continuing to do "nothing but downstream implementation" forever. {{6}High-performing engineers are simply not caught up in price competition with younger workers. People who can handle upstream processes, from requirements definition to architecture design, are in demand regardless of age.

The problem is the case of staying in the "implement the features you're told to" position indefinitely. In this situation, young engineers with the same skills keep entering the market, so competition intensifies year by year.

Shifting upstream, accumulating management experience, acquiring a business perspective. The more delayed your "position elevation," the narrower your career options become.

It is true that areas like COBOL retain demand, but such projects are often "salted-away projects (low rates, high stress)." To sustain a healthy long career, keeping up with modern technologies or shifting upstream is essential.

Here, I will tell you straight, without sugarcoating.

DeFactory actually receives inquiries from engineers in their 50s and 60s. Among them, there are indeed cases where applications do not pass screening due to age alone, regardless of skills or experience.

This is not unique to the IT industry, but it is a fact that client companies care about team age composition and culture fit.

That said, people in their 50s and beyond whose projects never dry up share common traits.

  • They can handle upstream processes (requirements definition and design)
  • They have management experience
  • They can commit to projects long-term

In other words, "people who are doing what it takes" remain in demand even in their 50s. The age barrier certainly exists, but the best countermeasure is to start preparing to overcome it while you are in your 30s and 40s.

Freelancers have no retirement benefits or paid leave. If projects dry up, income drops to zero.

In fact, about 60% of freelancers cite "low or unstable income" as a challenge.

Source: Cabinet Secretariat, "Freelancer Actual Conditions Survey"

Some people think, "I can always go back to being a company employee," but transitioning to full-time employment in your 40s or 50s is far more difficult than you might imagine. In particular, the longer your freelance period, the more likely companies are to worry about "whether you can fit into an organization."

It is important to use tools like iDeCo, small business mutual aid, and NISA (tsumitate investment framework) to systematically build retirement savings while you are still working.

Company employees have systems in place: they receive feedback from bosses and senior colleagues, and skills are naturally shared within teams. Freelancers have none of this.

It becomes difficult to objectively assess your own strengths and weaknesses and your position in the market, and before you know it, you can fall into a state of "just vaguely continuing with the same skill set."

In addition, the loneliness and mental strain that come from long hours working alone can sometimes lead to giving up on a freelance career.

These problems can be greatly mitigated by joining technical communities and using agents. In particular, if you have an agent you can consult with about career planning, they can help you think through "what to learn next" and "which position to aim for."

We have looked at the negative aspects, but concluding that "freelancing can't last long" based on that alone would be premature. There are also considerable advantages unique to freelance engineers.

In the freelance world, your portfolio and skill sheet matter more than your age. What clients look at is "whether this person will deliver results," not your date of birth.

In fact, there are many projects listed as "age not a factor." Especially in legacy system replacement and DX initiatives, veteran engineers with both business knowledge and technical skills are increasingly valued.

Simply updating your skill sheet regularly and describing your recent achievements in concrete terms can dramatically change your odds of winning projects.

As DX promotion and AI adoption advance, the volume of projects itself continues to grow. The "not enough people" situation is a tailwind even for experienced veterans.

Even if you feel your age is a handicap, there are ample opportunities depending on your skill set and position.

If you feel that "competition for projects is fierce," you may be looking at the wrong tier of projects.

Upstream, high-rate projects such as requirements definition, architecture design, and PMO have a limited pool of qualified talent, so competition rates are not particularly high.

According to a survey by the Ministry of Economy, Trade and Industry, from one's 40s onward, "management ability and the track record based on it" tend to be highly valued. In other words, the older you get, the more advantageous you can actually become for upstream projects.

Engineering work is primarily desk work. Unlike construction or transportation, it rarely hits physical limits, and as a freelancer you can even avoid commuting thanks to the discretion that comes with independence.

Of course, long hours at a PC put strain on your eyes and lower back, but this is manageable with proper self-care.

In addition, many freelancers report that interpersonal stress is relatively low. Since the situation of "continuing to work under a boss you don't get along with" basically never arises, mental exhaustion is kept in check.

If you can manage stress and maintain your health, the engineering profession can absolutely be continued into your 60s and beyond.

Company employees have a single track called "retirement," but freelancers do not. There is flexibility to freely change how you work depending on your age and circumstances.

If hands-on development becomes too tough in your 50s, transition to being a technical advisor. Leverage PM/PMO experience to work with multiple companies as an IT consultant. Give back to the next generation as an instructor at a programming school.

Being able to make these "switches" by your own choice is a strength unique to freelancers. It is no exaggeration to say that this flexibility is the greatest weapon for extending your career lifespan.

From here, I will introduce action plans you can start practicing today. What is needed to "extend" your career lifespan is not special talent, but steady accumulation in the right direction.

Saying "I can do anything" is actually the same as showcasing nothing. To survive long-term as a freelancer, the concept of "T-shaped skills"—combining specialized expertise in a specific field with general knowledge in adjacent areas—is effective.

For example, if backend development is your specialty, add knowledge of cloud infrastructure (AWS / GCP). If frontend is your strength, hone your knowledge of UX design and accessibility.

Such combinations not only serve as grounds for raising your rates, but also build client trust: "If we leave it to this person, they can handle everything in one stop."

There are abundant learning resources: online courses, technical books, study groups, and more. What matters is continuously cycling through "learn → try on a real project → get feedback."

"I have experience, but nothing to objectively prove it." This is a common concern among freelancers who struggle to win projects.

Certifications are an effective way to prove your skills from a third-party perspective. In particular, the following certifications are said to directly help in winning projects.

  • AWS certifications (Solutions Architect, Developer, etc.)
  • Information Technology Engineer Examination (Database Specialist, Network Specialist, etc.)
  • PMP (Project Management Professional)

It is true that "you can work without certifications," but they can make a difference at the document screening stage. They also offer the benefit of taking stock of your own knowledge through study, so they are worth trying as part of a career inventory.

According to the Freelance White Paper, the top three channels for freelancers to win work—"past and current clients," "personal networks," and "agent services"—account for about three-quarters of the total.

In other words, the royal road to winning projects is "connections with people."

Sharing information via technical blogs and social media, and participating in study groups and conferences, are not mere networking—they are also activities that make the market aware of your expertise.

Beyond winning projects in the short term, building a position where people think "for this field, it's Mr./Ms. XX" over the medium to long term is extremely effective in extending your career lifespan.

For freelancers, your body is truly your capital. You must handle schedule management, task management, and health management all by yourself.

What you especially need to watch out for is "overworking." If you keep cutting sleep to meet deadlines, your performance drops and the quality of your work suffers as well.

Take one fully off day per week, make 30 minutes of daily exercise a habit, secure 7 hours of sleep. These basics will determine whether you can continue your career 5 or 10 years from now.

Maintain a pace you can sustain long-term. That is the secret to "longevity."

While chasing immediate projects, 3 or 5 years can fly by before you know it—a pattern common among freelancers.

It is important to set concrete milestones—such as "shift to PM projects within 3 years" or "become a specialist in AWS + security by age 40"—and work backward from them.

Management skills in particular, if consciously accumulated from a young age, become a weapon in your 40s and beyond. PM/PL experience, on top of technical skill, creates added value of "being able to move a team," raising the ceiling on your rates.

This is the flip side of the "depth of the supply chain = short career lifespan" explained in Section 2-1. Shortening the supply chain is not merely about raising your rate. It is a strategy of buying "time" to extend your career lifespan.

"Shortening the sales chain" is not just about increasing your income right now. It is an investment in protecting your career 5 or 10 years from now.

Freelance engineers in their 40s and beyond are also at a stage where they should think about a "second career." However, the options are more diverse than you might imagine. Here, we introduce career paths that actually increase in value with age.

PMs/PLs who understand technology are valued on any team. People who "can write code themselves and also manage a team" are in constant demand regardless of age.

According to a survey by the Ministry of Economy, Trade and Industry, PMs have the highest average annual income among IT professionals at 8.915 million yen, and freelancers can aim for even more in this position.

The market for freelance PMs is expanding. The combination of "technical skill" and "the ability to move a team" becomes a stronger asset the older you get.

IT consultants who can leverage not only technical skills but also business knowledge and proposal abilities are a career path well suited to mid-career and senior engineers.

The experience of being able to speak with executives as equals is something young professionals can rarely replicate. By working in parallel as a technology advisor for multiple companies, you can achieve high income while diversifying risk.

For those who think, "I want to step away from hands-on development, but I don't want to leave technology entirely," consulting and advisory roles are an ideal landing point.

Careers in education, such as programming school instructor, corporate training trainer, or online mentor, are also one option.

"Teaching methods based on over 10 years of hands-on experience" offer value that textbooks and online articles cannot provide. It is work where veterans are trusted precisely because of their experience, and many projects can be handled remotely, which is another attractive point.

PM, consultant, instructor—all are strong career paths, but there is one more role we can confidently say has "no expiration date," regardless of age. That is the "BizDevTech" engineer.

BizDevTech is a concept referring to professionals who understand both technology (Tech) and business development (BizDev) and can solve business challenges through technology. DeFactory has adopted this concept in its media name and has a track record of providing development support and business development in an integrated, end-to-end manner.

Now that AI is beginning to replace routine coding, the value of engineers who can decide "what to build" is higher than ever. Professionals who understand both technology and business—not just one or the other—are irreplaceable.

BizDev Tech's projects also include the PM/PMO and product manager domains, making it an ideal platform for those who want to increase their market value through the combination of "technology × business."

What this article has consistently conveyed is that what shortens the career lifespan of freelance engineers is not "age" but "sales chain position."

As long as you remain in the deep layers of multi-tiered subcontracting and keep working at full capacity at low rates, you will have no time to learn, your skills will stagnate, and your career will gradually shrink.

On the other hand, if you shorten the sales chain and win end-client or prime contractor projects directly, you gain room in both income and time. That room makes it possible to acquire new skills and shift to upstream positions, which in turn extends your career lifespan.

People who remain in demand in their 50s are those who, from their 30s and 40s, acted with awareness of their "sales chain position" and "positioning."

What shortens an engineer's career lifespan is not "age," but "an environment (sales chain) where you are bought cheaply and undervalued."
