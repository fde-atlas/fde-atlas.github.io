Implementation to Adoption: A Handoff Playbook for Forward-Deployed Engineers

Quivly · 2026-10-20 · 7 min read

Summary

This Quivly playbook argues that forward-deployed engineer handoffs to customer success, not cutover itself, determine whether implementations get adopted. It identifies structural causes of failure: separate tooling, no definition of done, context that doesn't travel, and unclear issue ownership. It proposes six phases: a shared rollout timeline with gradually shifting ownership from FDE to CS, written exit criteria agreed with the customer before cutover, a concise handoff package covering technical, account, and success-plan context, a hypercare window with shared technical and adoption signals, pre-agreed issue routing rules (single-account vs. platform-wide errors, dual alerts, escalation thresholds), and a proactive agenda that turns adoption gaps into owned actions. Quivly claims teams using its tooling cover 3x more accounts per FDE and resolve 50% of technical issues before customers notice.

Key points

  • Most rollouts fail after cutover through small unowned issues, not at go-live.
  • Handoff should start at kickoff via one shared timeline with shifting ownership.
  • Written exit criteria must cover access, data, integrations, training, and success metrics.
  • Hypercare pairs technical signals (API errors, sync gaps) with CS adoption signals.
  • Post-go-live issues need pre-agreed routing, dual alerts, and escalation thresholds.

For a forward-deployed engineer, go-live feels like the finish line. The integration is live, the data migration passed validation and the customer has signed off on cutover. But for the customer, go-live is the start. Whether they actually adopt what you built depends on what happens in the weeks after cutover, and that is usually when the FDE moves on to the next rollout and the account passes to customer success.

That handoff is where implementation work quietly loses value. This playbook walks through a practical way to run it: one shared timeline, clear exit criteria, a written handoff package, a hypercare window, split ownership for issues and a proactive adoption agenda. For background on the role itself, see what a forward-deployed engineer is.

Why the Handoff Decides Adoption

Most rollouts don't fail at cutover. They fail later, in small ways nobody owns: a webhook starts dropping events, a trained team never logs in, an available integration is never switched on. Each is easy to fix when spotted early and becomes a churn conversation when the customer spots it first.

The underlying problem is usually structural rather than personal:

  • Two teams, two tools. Engineering tracks the rollout in one system and CS tracks the relationship in another, so neither sees the full picture.
  • No definition of done. "Live in production" gets treated as "adopted," even when only part of the scope is in use.
  • Context that doesn't travel. The decisions, workarounds and stakeholder quirks from implementation rarely make it into a format the CSM can use.
  • Unclear issue ownership. After go-live, nobody is sure whether a technical problem goes to the FDE, to support or to engineering.

Quivly's forward-deployed engineers page frames the job as owning "the implementation and ongoing adoption," not just the rollout. The playbook below is built around that idea: the FDE's work isn't finished until the customer is using what was built.

Phase 1: Run the Rollout on One Shared Timeline

A clean handoff starts at kickoff, not at cutover. If CS can see the rollout plan from day one, there is far less to transfer at the end. The simplest way to do that is one implementation timeline that engineering and CS both work from.

Use milestone templates with dependencies

Most rollouts follow a similar arc. Quivly's example rollout plan uses four phases, each with an owner:

  • Discovery: scope and a technical discovery questionnaire, owned by the FDE.
  • Integrate: connectors and field mappings, owned by the FDE.
  • Validate: user acceptance testing and data checks, owned jointly by the FDE and CS.
  • Cutover: production go-live, owned by CS.

Notice how ownership shifts. The FDE leads early, the work is shared during validation and CS owns the cutover. That gradual shift is the handoff, spread across the project instead of crammed into one meeting. Build the phases into a template and link dependent milestones. Quivly's rollout view rolls slipped dates forward to dependent milestones, so the plan never shows a date that is already impossible.

Tie customer readiness to technical checkpoints

Technical progress and customer readiness are different things. Engineering can be 100% done while the customer's team is still unprepared. Track both. Quivly's example tracks readiness separately for engineering, CS alignment and security review, and sets cutover gates such as SSO and provisioning, a migration dry run and a training session. Cutover only happens when every gate is green.

Phase 2: Agree on Exit Criteria Before Cutover

The most useful handoff conversation happens weeks before go-live: what does "done" mean for this account? Write the answer down and share it with the customer. A good set of exit criteria covers more than the technical build:

  • Access is in place. SSO and team provisioning are configured and the right users can log in.
  • Data is validated. The initial migration ran and the customer has confirmed the numbers look right.
  • Core integrations are live. The connections in scope are not just built but sending data in production.
  • Users are trained. End-user training sessions have happened for the teams expected to use the product.
  • Success metrics are set. You and the customer have agreed what adoption looks like and when you will review it.

The last item is the one teams most often skip. Quivly's example onboarding project ends with "conduct end-user training sessions" and "establish success metrics and review cadence." Those two tasks turn a delivered implementation into something CS can manage against.

Phase 3: Write the Handoff Package

A call alone isn't a handoff. The CSM needs something to come back to in three months when the customer asks why a field maps the way it does. Keep the package short and consistent.

Technical context

  • What was built: integrations, data flows, custom configuration and anything outside the standard setup.
  • Known limitations and workarounds, with the reason for each.
  • Integrations that are available but not yet enabled, and why.
  • Who on the customer side owns each system, such as the IT admin for SSO.

Account context

  • The customer's goals and the use cases agreed during discovery.
  • Key stakeholders: the champion, the power users and the executive sponsor.
  • Open risks or concerns raised during implementation.

Success plan

  • The adoption metrics agreed in Phase 2 and their targets.
  • The review cadence and the date of the first review.
  • The next milestones after go-live, with owners.

Much of this can be drafted automatically. In Quivly's Mycroft story, a handoff notebook is built the moment a deal closes from sales calls, emails, recordings and CRM data, so any field engineer can pick up an account already knowing its history. Apply the same idea at the FDE-to-CSM handoff: generate the draft from account data, then have the FDE fill the gaps.

Phase 4: Run a Hypercare Window With Shared Signals

Don't hand off on the day of cutover. Agree on a hypercare window where the FDE stays close to the account while the CSM leads the relationship. Its length depends on the rollout, but it needs a clear end and both people should watch the same signals. The technical ones are those a CSM can't easily read alone. Quivly's FDE page lists signals such as:

  • API call volumes and error rates.
  • Webhook delivery success and failure.
  • Data sync latency and gaps.
  • Feature adoption at the endpoint level.
  • SDK and library version currency.
  • Custom technical metrics from your data warehouse.

Pair those with adoption signals CS already tracks, such as whether trained teams are logging in. When both look steady for an agreed period, hypercare closes and the FDE steps back to an escalation role. Which signals you can watch depends on what your product emits, so confirm this during discovery.

Phase 5: Split Ownership for Issues After Go-Live

Once the FDE steps back, the most common friction is a technical issue nobody knows how to route. Decide the rules before the first incident.

Separate customer-side from platform-side issues

An error on one account usually points to that customer's configuration. The same error across many accounts usually points to the platform. Quivly compares error patterns across accounts and flags the distinction in the alert, so the team knows whether to route to CS or engineering. Even without tooling, make "is anyone else seeing this?" the first question on every issue.

Send two alerts with one set of facts

The FDE and the CSM need different things from the same incident. The FDE needs logs, affected endpoints and a likely fix. The CSM needs a plain-language summary and something to tell the customer. Quivly handles this by creating an FDE alert with full technical context and a CS alert with a plain-language summary and a suggested customer message, both linked to the same incident timeline. The CSM can update the customer while the FDE works the fix, without a coordination call.

Define when to escalate beyond the FDE

Some issues need core engineering, not field fixes. Write down the threshold, such as a platform-side bug or a fix that needs a product change, and route those directly. Quivly's page lists escalation routing to engineering when issues exceed FDE scope as part of the same flow.

Phase 6: Turn Adoption Gaps Into a Proactive Agenda

After hypercare, the FDE's best contribution is no longer fixing what's broken. It's spotting what's missing. Quivly's FDE page puts it as moving from "what's broken" to "what's missing that should be there": endpoints that aren't being called, integrations that are available but not set up and capabilities the account hasn't enabled that would drive value.

Review those gaps with the CSM on a regular cadence and turn each one into an action with an owner:

  • Unused integration: the CSM raises it with the champion, and the FDE scopes the setup.
  • Feature not adopted: CS runs an enablement session or sends targeted guidance.
  • Usage stalls after go-live: an onboarding play triggers a nudge, a check-in or an escalation.

That last point is where repeatable motions help. On Quivly's Plays page, an onboarding stall triggers a nudge, a check-in or an escalation, and renewals get a 90, 60 and 30 day cadence. Defining these once means adoption follow-up doesn't depend on someone remembering. For more on designing them, see what a CS play is.

Quivly's FDE page reports that teams using it cover 3x more accounts per FDE and resolve 50% of technical issues before the customer notices. Whatever your tooling, handoff discipline is what makes that kind of coverage possible.
