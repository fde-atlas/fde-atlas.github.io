How to Prepare for the AWS FDE Leadership Principles Behavioral Interview

Original title: AWS FDE 面试：领导力准则行为面试怎么准备

FDE Times · 2026-11-22 · 5 min read

Summary

This article explains how to prepare for the Leadership Principles behavioral round in AWS Forward Deployed Engineer interviews. It cites an Amazon interviewer's claim that 25% of SDEs who passed technical screens were rejected over behavioral concerns. Because the FDE role involves working inside enterprise customers, behavioral questions mirror the actual job. The author recommends STAR-structured, real customer stories with personal decisions and measurable results, warns that interviewers may probe a single story for 10-15 minutes, and notes OpenAI's FDE interviews test the same trustworthiness with clients.

Key points

  • 25% of technically qualified SDEs were rejected on behavioral grounds.
  • FDE job duties make behavioral stories a direct sample of the work.
  • Bar Raisers independently enforce Amazon's 16 Leadership Principles.
  • Interviewers may drill into one story for 10-15 minutes.
  • OpenAI FDE interviews ask the same question: can you be trusted with clients?

Key Takeaways

  • Amazon explicitly states that its interview process is designed to assess candidates' alignment with the Leadership Principles, and the Bar Raiser is responsible for upholding that standard.
  • The AWS Sr FDE job description describes an engineer embedded inside enterprise customers, so customer stories matter as much as code.
  • Interviewers may dig into a single story for 10-15 minutes; memorized answers won't hold up.

Citing an Amazon interviewer, interviewing.io offers a number that should make many engineers rethink their preparation: among software development engineers (SDEs) who passed the technical assessment, 25% were still rejected over behavioral interview concerns.

This number is anecdotal, coming from one interviewer's account. But it matches Amazon's own description of its hiring.

Amazon states it plainly: its interview process is designed to assess how well candidates align with the Leadership Principles and how well they apply them. A former Amazon Director/GM put it even more bluntly in a Levels.fyi interview: the company hires against the principles, and both positive and negative feedback trace back to them.

If your target is the AWS Forward Deployed Engineer (FDE) role, this number matters even more than it does for SDEs. The reason is written right into the job description: for FDEs, what the behavioral interview asks about is almost exactly the daily job.

For FDEs, the behavioral interview is also a technical interview

The AWS Sr Forward Deployed Engineer job description officially describes an engineer who works inside strategic enterprise customers, responsible for designing, building, deploying, and operating AI systems in production.

This person owns the technical strategy and execution for customers' complex AI projects, including multiple workstreams running in parallel.

Among the basic qualifications, AWS also lists: experience presenting technical concepts to technical and non-technical audiences, including executive leadership. This is a threshold requirement, not a "preferred" item.

Now compare this description with the Customer Obsession principle. Amazon summarizes it in one sentence: leaders start with the customer and work backwards.

When the job itself is carried out inside the customer, the distance between the principle and daily work nearly disappears. Every customer obsession story you tell will be treated as a sample of the very job you're applying for.

Who holds the ruler in the interview room

Amazon includes a Bar Raiser in its hiring process, an interviewer who serves as an objective third party. According to a 2019 Amazon article, the Bar Raiser's job is to uphold the company's 16 Leadership Principles.

This should change how you prepare. The Bar Raiser is not part of the hiring team and is specifically responsible for upholding the principles, so principle-based evaluation has a dedicated guardian in the interview room.

The natural conclusion: even brilliant live coding can hardly compensate for a thin customer story. The two are measured with different rulers, and the second ruler has a dedicated custodian.

Fifteen minutes on a single story

Amazon advises candidates to practice structuring answers with STAR: Situation, Task, Action, Result. For Customer Obsession, the company suggests preparing specific examples of times you put the customer first even when it wasn't the easiest path.

The difficulty lies elsewhere. The interviewing.io guide cites an Amazon interviewer: the questioner may spend 10-15 minutes on a single point, asking follow-up after follow-up, drilling deeper layer by layer. A rehearsed two-minute story falls apart by the third question.

Feel the pressure with a hypothetical example. You say you once delayed a feature promised to a customer to fix a data bug that was distorting their revenue reports. The first question sounds simple: who decided on the delay?

Then come sharper questions: who on the customer's side did you deliver the bad news to, in what words, how did they react, what alternatives did you weigh, and how did you later measure whether the decision was right?

By minute ten, the interviewer can tell whether you were truly at the center of the story or just standing nearby. In this example, the two most likely exposed weaknesses are: saying "we" for ten minutes without being able to point to a single decision made by you personally, and a result with no numbers. Prepare answers for both before entering the interview room.

Read the job description like an exam paper

The most effective preparation is to go through each requirement in the AWS FDE job description and ask yourself where the interviewer will dig deeper. The table below maps the job description to Amazon's guidance on the Leadership Principles round; it is not an official question bank, only a reference for practice.

Requirement in the job descriptionWhere the interviewer may dig deeperStory you need to prepare
Working inside strategic enterprise customersDo you start from the customer's problem or from your own preferred solutionA time you adjusted the technical direction after rethinking what the customer actually needed
Designing, building, deploying, and operating AI systems in productionWhat is your first step when the system fails on real dataA production incident on the customer side and how you handled it, with measurable results
Owning strategy and execution for multi-workstream projectsHow do you prioritize when workstreams compete for resourcesA time you said "no" or "not yet" to a requirement to keep the project on track
Presenting technical concepts to non-technical audiences and executivesHow do you persuade someone who doesn't read codeA briefing to customer executives that ultimately led to a concrete decision

The third column is your homework. Each cell should be a real story: state the role of your customer contact, a decision made entirely by you, and at least one number in the result.

OpenAI asks the same question in different words

If you think this is an Amazon-only practice, look at how an OpenAI FDE interview guide compiled from candidates' experiences summarizes the behavioral interview. What interviewers really want to know is: can this person be trusted in front of customers, and can they be trusted to build the solution that's actually needed.

The two companies use different words. Amazon has Leadership Principles, Bar Raisers, and STAR; according to candidates, OpenAI boils everything down to "trust." What's being measured is the same: the ability to work with customers without needing someone to hold your hand.

So the effort you put into the AWS Leadership Principles round will pay off at other companies too.

What to practice to pass this round

Gather material first, then start rehearsing. Review the past two or three years of work and note every customer interaction: a difficult phone call, a requirement you declined, a failed demo, a time you had to explain a problem to a non-technical person.

If you're an SDE who has never faced customers, internal users or other teams are equally valid sources of stories.

Then, write each story in STAR structure and interrogate yourself like a Bar Raiser: who made the decision, why, what alternatives were given up, how was the result measured. Any question you can't answer yet is something to revisit or verify with former colleagues before entering the interview room.

Finally, put these stories into your resume. An entry like "worked with customers to deploy systems" tells the reader nothing. Rewrite it to specify what kind of customer, what decision you made, and which number changed, and you've effectively shown the recruiter in advance the story they want to hear in the interview.

When reading any company's FDE job description, watch for phrases like "senior leadership," "non-technical audiences," or "working inside the customer." They reveal in advance the other half of the exam—the half that LeetCode practice can't teach you.
