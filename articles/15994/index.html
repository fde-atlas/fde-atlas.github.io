China's Version of the FDE: What the Role Really Looks Like Locally and the "Reskin" Debate

Original title: 中国版 FDE：本土岗位的真实形态与「换皮」之争

Since the concept of Forward Deployed Engineer (FDE) traveled from Silicon Valley to China, a phenomenon unseen overseas has emerged: under the same title, two vastly different kinds of work are mixed together. One is genuine forward deployed engineering—engineers go deep into the customer's environment and use the customer's data to make AI applications production-ready; the other is bluntly called "re-skinned" by the Chinese community—traditional on-site development or staff augmentation relabeled as FDE, with the work content unchanged by a single word. A Zhihu author states it plainly: the FDE boom is real, but under the same name two things are mixed—half is productized forward deployed engineering, half is the old on-site model with a new skin (zhuanlan.zhihu.com). This article's thesis: the confusion around domestic FDE is not a translation problem but a mismatch between two delivery contexts—overseas FDEs grow out of SaaS and the cloud, while domestic FDEs must grow out of privatized deployment, Xinchuang, and the systems integrator ecosystem; understanding this structural difference is more valuable than arguing about "real vs. fake FDE." The discussion unfolds across four layers: job facts, environmental variables, the substance of the controversy, and job-seeking verification.

Job facts: the real name has appeared, but fake names are still the majority

First, the established facts: leading Chinese companies have already listed FDE as an official position on their recruitment sites. ByteDance's Volcano Engine is hiring FDEs covering its full AI product line, with JD bonus points including experience with Agent frameworks such as LangGraph and CrewAI and industry AI solutions experience (jobs.bytedance.com); Tencent's careers site lists an "AI Forward Deployed Engineer (FDE)" position under a group subsidiary, focused on cloud and smart industries (careers.tencent.com); 36Kr reported that Tencent's social recruitment posted multiple Forward Deployed Engineer roles, all requiring three to five years of experience (m.36kr.com). Ant Digital Technologies, Zhipu, and other companies also have same-titled openings; Zhipu's FDE lead role pays 60,000 to 80,000 RMB per month (xhby.net).

But the same analysis also points out: those posting the real name are just the tip of the iceberg. An analysis article on Tencent Cloud's developer community states it directly—the real job titles behind domestic FDE are mostly pre-sales Solutions Engineer, AI implementation engineer, and privatized deployment engineer, with work centered on systems integration and AI model tuning (cloud.tencent.com). This observation mirrors the overseas situation: Google Cloud, Databricks, and other companies cover similar work under different titles; the difference is that in China, "real-name roles" and "same-name fakes" coexist, so job seekers must screen for themselves. The demand-side heat is well documented: one platform's data shows FDE job postings surged 42-fold over the past two years (cited via xhby.net), consistent with overseas tracking. On salary bands, 36Kr reported ByteDance FDEs earn 35,000 to 70,000 RMB monthly with 15 months' pay, up to roughly 1.05 million RMB annually; Alibaba Cloud Intelligence FDEs earn 20,000 to 50,000 RMB monthly with 16 months' pay (m.36kr.com)—domestic FDEs generally earn more than same-level R&D engineers, with salary inversion even appearing (cloud.tencent.com).

Environmental variable one: privatized and intranet delivery reshapes the role

The default environment for overseas FDEs is the cloud: data sits in the customer's cloud tenant, and delivery is mainly integration and tuning. The default environment in China is completely different. Privatized deployment is the mainstream choice—model weights, inference services, and compute all reside in the enterprise's own environment, with input and output data never leaving the intranet (xhby.net); this is especially true for government clients, as government systems across many regions are advancing DeepSeek privatized deployment (nbd.com.cn), and an IDC report shows the LLM privatized deployment market has formed an independent procurement and delivery system (cited via tech.chinadaily.com.cn). In this environment, the FDE's work expands from "calling APIs and doing integration" to "building a full inference and retrieval infrastructure stack inside the customer's intranet"—the engineering depth is actually harder than overseas.

Added to this are Xinchuang constraints: full-stack adaptation of domestic CPUs, operating systems, databases, and middleware has become a hard requirement in central state-owned enterprise scenarios (industry compilation by ai-indeed.com). This means domestic FDEs must master an adaptation matrix that overseas peers never need, and it also gives engineers familiar with the Xinchuang stack unique pricing power in the FDE talent market. For talent transitioning from ERP/MES implementation backgrounds, this is a tailwind—see FDE vs Implementation Consultant for related transition paths.

Environmental variable two: the all-in-one machine boom exposes the deployment gap

Chinese toB delivery has another distinctive form: LLM all-in-one machines. All-in-one machines based on DeepSeek and other domestic LLMs already cover energy, finance, telecom, government, manufacturing, and other industries (news.cn), and broker estimates put the market size above 500 billion RMB by 2027 (cited via sohu.com, third-party estimate). All-in-one machines are meant to be "out-of-the-box" software-hardware delivery, but after surveying 12 listed companies, Leiphone found the real gap in deployment: vendors do not grasp users' business logic, and users' data governance is inaccurate—hardware in place does not mean deployed (m.leiphone.com).

This finding strongly supports this article's thesis: the Chinese market has already paid real money to learn the lesson that "equipment does not equal deployment," and the ones filling this gap are precisely FDE-type roles—people who understand business logic, can do data governance, and can tune capabilities to a usable state in the customer's environment. The all-in-one machine puts the "last mile" problem on the table in its sharpest form; this is the domestic engine of FDE demand, and its necessity argument converges with overseas reports of "95% of pilots having no business impact."

Another unavoidable signal on the demand side: the spread of salary inversion. Multiple 36Kr reports piece together the price curve of domestic FDEs—ByteDance Doubao LLM-related FDEs earn up to 70,000 monthly with 15 months' pay; Ant Digital Technologies B-side FDEs up to 60,000 monthly (m.36kr.com); Tencent Cloud-direction FDE roles have appeared on third-party channels listed at 35-65K with 15 months' pay (as relayed via zhuanlan.zhihu.com; refer to official channels). Price is only the surface; it reflects a supply-demand mismatch: there are plenty of engineers who can write code, but very few who can simultaneously align models, data, and processes within a customer organization. The scarcity premium belongs only to the latter—which is why this article emphasizes "job verification over title judgment": re-skinned roles cannot command this premium and will, over time, be repriced by the market.

The substance of the "re-skinned" debate: the difference in responsibility loops and business models

Now back to the opening question: why is the "reskin" controversy so heated? Because it touches on differences in business models, not differences in job titles. The Chinese-language community has articulated this dividing line quite clearly: the difference between an FDE and ordinary outsourcing lies in the closed loop of responsibility—outsourcers withdraw once delivery is complete, while an FDE must be the first to follow up and take responsibility when problems arise after a system goes live (zhihu.com discussion); FDEs who can clearly judge whether "the business is being done right or wrong" are a scarce asset, with a cultivation cycle of five to ten years (another zhihu.com respondent's view). A comment on Everyone Is a Product Manager takes the more cautious side: the FDE is essentially a hybrid of on-site outsourcing, pre-sales and after-sales, and customer success, and companies should not bet their AI implementation on a single role (woshipm.com).

Both statements are correct, because they describe two halves of the reality in the domestic market. The business model supporting overseas FDEs is "services in exchange for product penetration": on-site losses are recouped through product and renewal revenue (the a16z.com framework; see Business Model Analysis of the FDE for details). But when domestic roles are merely reskinned labor outsourcing, this recoupment mechanism does not exist—clients pay by person-day, vendors earn by headcount, and the FDE's incentive structure is identical to traditional on-site staffing, so a closed loop of responsibility naturally cannot form. Therefore, the key to distinguishing real from fake FDEs lies not in the wording of the job description but in the contract and incentives: is the project priced by headcount or by delivered value? Does the team get a share of renewals and expansions? Who covers failures? The answers to these questions are far more honest than any title.

Another overlooked local variable is wariness on the client side. 36Kr has reported on the "FDE game-playing" phenomenon: behind the high-salary competition, companies worry that FDEs will be poached after being thoroughly scouted during on-site engagements (m.36kr.com). This shows that domestic clients have already begun treating FDEs as a strategic resource that needs to be managed, rather than a plug-and-play service—the scarcity of the role has been confirmed a second time by the demand side.

A Four-Point Job Verification Method for Practitioners

Based on the analysis above, here are four verification points for job hunting or taking on projects domestically. First, verify the closed loop of responsibility: ask clearly who responds when problems arise after go-live and to what depth; if the answer stops at "submitting tickets during the warranty period," it is traditional on-site staffing. See the responsibility delineation method in the Expectation Management Handbook. Second, verify the engineering proportion: ask the other party to describe the actual work breakdown of their last project—what fraction of the time was spent writing code, running evals, and doing data governance? If most of the time goes to configuring mature products and attending meetings, the engineering depth is questionable. Third, verify data access: genuine forward delivery inevitably involves contact with the client's real data and intranet systems; if the role does not even enter the client's production environment, it cannot be called an FDE. Fourth, verify value measurement: ask how project success is defined—are there business metrics? Is the acceptance criterion a feature checklist or an effectiveness baseline? See FDE Delivery Metrics for how to build a measurement system. If a role passes two of the four checks, it is worth serious discussion; if it passes none, no matter how new the title, it is essentially the old job.

Conclusion

Returning to the judgment in the title: the Chinese version of the FDE is not a localized version of the Silicon Valley role, but a role that has regrown within the ecosystems of private deployment, Xinchuang (IT innovation), and system integrators. Its true name has already appeared—the roles on the Volcano Engine and Tencent recruiting sites are landmark events; its false names are still the majority—a great deal of genuine forward delivery work hides under titles like pre-sales solutions, AI implementation, and private deployment engineer, and so does plenty of reskinned on-site outsourcing. The substance of the "reskin" debate is a projection of the fact that the overseas business model of "services in exchange for product penetration" has not yet become widespread domestically: as long as the incentive structure is priced by headcount, a closed loop of responsibility cannot be established. For practitioners, the pragmatic attitude is to abandon conceptual camp-picking and measure every concrete opportunity with the four yardsticks of closed-loop responsibility, engineering proportion, data access, and value measurement; for employers, the domestic lesson is equally clear—hanging the FDE role on the org chart is only the first step; connecting it to real data access, real business metrics, and a real incentive structure is the day the role truly lands in China.
