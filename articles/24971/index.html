Division of Roles Between FDEs and In-House Engineers

Original title: FDEと社内エンジニアの役割分担

日本Fde協会 · 2026-08-11 · 5 min read

Summary

This article by Japan's FDE Association explains how to divide responsibilities between forward deployed engineers and in-house engineers. The author argues the two roles are complementary: internal engineers know the company's systems, data, and domain, while FDEs bring cross-project experience in problem framing and rapid prototyping. A phase-based table assigns leads for scoping, design, implementation, and operations. Case studies from manufacturing and SaaS show that unclear boundaries cause rework, and that concentrating work on one external person creates fragile, person-dependent operations. The key recommendation is to design knowledge transfer from day one through documentation and pairing, so the client can run independently.

Key points

  • FDEs and internal engineers have different strengths, not competing roles.
  • Division of labor should be designed by project phase.
  • Early boundary-setting (internal systems vs. process redesign) reduced rework in a manufacturing case.
  • Common failures: treating FDEs as generalists or internal engineers as mere workers.
  • Handover must be planned from day one to avoid person-dependent knowledge.

When promoting DX initiatives or introducing AI, many companies struggle with how to divide roles between an external FDE (Forward Deployed Engineer—an engineer who embeds themselves in the customer's site and solves problems from both technical and business perspectives) and in-house engineers. At the Japan FDE Association, we frequently receive consultations asking, "What should we delegate, and what should we keep in-house, to collaborate effectively?" By reading this article, you will be able to concretely design the division of roles between FDEs and in-house engineers and build a collaborative framework that prevents over-reliance on individuals. Let's think through this together.

Basic Principles for Dividing Roles Between FDEs and In-House Engineers

The first thing to understand is that FDEs and in-house engineers are not "competing for the same work." Their areas of expertise differ. In-house engineers are deeply familiar with their company's history, data, and operational quirks. FDEs, on the other hand, bring experience gained across multiple sites, including "breaking down problems" and "approaches that demonstrate working solutions as quickly as possible."

Articulating Each Side's Strengths

Most failures in role division stem from allocating work "vaguely" without articulating each side's strengths. The greatest asset of in-house engineers is their knowledge of the business domain and their understanding of integration points with existing systems. The FDE's asset is the ability to handle the entire flow end-to-end, from interviewing stakeholders about business processes to designing improvements and presenting prototypes. Simply sharing these differences at the outset significantly reduces unnecessary turf wars.

Designing a Concrete Division of Roles

In practice, it is easier to organize the division of roles by project phase. The table below shows a typical breakdown that we use in the field.

PhaseFDE LeadsIn-House Engineers Lead
Problem analysisBusiness interviews and prioritizationProviding details of existing systems and data
Design and prototypingPrototype creation and improvement proposalsConfirming internal constraints and integration requirements
ImplementationDesign and implementation support for difficult partsCore implementation and integration with existing assets
Operational adoptionDocumentation and training designOngoing operations and continuous improvement

How to Draw Boundaries: Lessons from a Manufacturing Site

When we handled shipping and receiving management for a manufacturing client, the in-house engineers were extremely knowledgeable about the existing core system, while I (Maekawa) took charge of identifying waste in business processes and automating them. By deciding upfront that "the inside of the existing system is in-house territory, and redesigning business processes is external territory," rework was dramatically reduced. The success of role division depends greatly on how well the boundaries are drawn.

Common Failures in FDE Role Division

A common failure is expecting the FDE to be a "jack-of-all-trades." While FDEs can handle both technical and business aspects, delegating everything to them means internal knowledge never accumulates, and dependence actually deepens. Another failure is relegating in-house engineers to mere "task workers." Assignments that fail to leverage their business domain knowledge squander the strengths of both parties.

The "Fast Only Temporarily" Trap at a SaaS Company

I heard from the CEO of a SaaS company that they had concentrated work on external talent and achieved results in a short period, but operations came to a halt the moment that person left. Behind the speed, knowledge had accumulated in a single individual. This is why, when we design role divisions, we start by eliminating the risk of over-reliance on individuals.

Designing Knowledge Transfer to Prevent Over-Reliance on Individuals

This is a point rarely discussed in typical market discussions of role division. The true value of an FDE lies not only in producing deliverables, but in leaving knowledge behind so the company can operate independently. We view handover not as something to do "at the end," but as something to design from the very first day of the project.

Building Handover into Day One

Concretely, this means documenting the reasons behind design decisions on the spot, involving in-house engineers as pairs, and articulating operational procedures in a form that "works even when the person in charge changes." From my startup experience, keeping records while moving fast feels tedious, but if you try to write everything up later, gaps and omissions are inevitable. Creating a state where operations do not stop even when someone leaves is the goal of collaboration. We also organize perspectives for companies making adoption decisions in The Complete Guide to FDE Utilization for Companies.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q. Are FDEs necessary if we already have in-house engineers?

A. There are situations where they are necessary. If in-house engineers are fully occupied with daily operations, or have limited experience with approaches that span multiple sites, an FDE can accelerate the launch through problem analysis and prototype presentation, while taking on the role of leaving knowledge within the company.

Q. If we delegate to an FDE, won't know-how fail to stay in-house?

A. That depends on the design. We build handover and documentation into day one and involve in-house engineers as pairs. If the framework is designed with the premise of preventing over-reliance on individuals, internal self-sufficiency actually increases.

Q. How can we assess an FDE's practical skills?

A. As a benchmark for learning and evaluation, there is the FDE Certification, a private certification operated by the Japan FDE Association. While it is not a national qualification, it can be used as an indicator of practical competence. For details, please see the certification page.
