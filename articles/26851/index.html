What is an FDE · OpenFDE

Original title: 什么是 FDE · OpenFDE

OpenFDE · 前沿部署工程师社区 · 2026-10-16 · 4 min read

Summary

This OpenFDE community article defines the Forward Deployed Engineer as an owner of the outcome: an engineering role that goes on-site to understand real customer problems, personally builds systems to production, calibrates them against business metrics, and feeds field learnings back into product. Using a four-week bank AI assistant pilot as an example, it introduces the 4C framework—context, code, calibration, contribution—arguing all four must sit in one accountable party. It distinguishes FDE from pre-sales, consulting, and delivery roles, citing OpenAI and Google Cloud job descriptions, and describes “big FDE” as a working style spreading beyond the role itself.

Key points

  • FDEs are accountable for real business outcomes in production, not a single handoff stage.
  • A bank AI assistant case shows one FDE covering research, building, bug fixes, and productization in four weeks.
  • The 4C framework: context, code, calibration, and contribution are all required.
  • OpenAI and Google Cloud definitions support the end-to-end ownership view.
  • “Big FDE” describes this outcome-owning style spreading to other roles.

FDE (Forward Deployed Engineer) is not a new name, but a new boundary of responsibility: putting engineers into the customer's real business environment and holding them accountable, end to end, for "whether the system is actually being used and whether it is actually producing business results."

Many roles work close to the customer, but most are only responsible for their own segment—sales is accountable for signing, pre-sales for the solution, delivery for acceptance, and engineers for the code. What makes the FDE different is that he is accountable for the final outcome, and to achieve that outcome, he can cut across all of these stages.

First, imagine a scene

A bank says it wants to build an "AI assistant to improve account managers' efficiency."

  • In the first week, the FDE is not writing code; instead, he sits next to account managers watching how they handle a day's work, and discovers that the real bottleneck is not "Q&A" but customer information scattered across five systems.
  • In the second week, he connects read-only interfaces to three of those systems and builds a draft assistant that can retrieve by permission level with source citations, piloting it with 10 people.
  • In the third week, he fixes a retrieval bug that would let account managers see data beyond their permissions, while turning the pilot's adoption rate into a chart that customer executives can understand.
  • In the fourth week, he realizes that "filtering customer data by role" is something the next bank will also encounter—so he abstracts it into a reusable module and proposes it to the product team.

Over these four weeks, he did parts of what sales, pre-sales, delivery, engineering, and product management each would do—but he did them not for deliverables, but so that the outcome actually happens. That is the FDE.

A more precise definition

An FDE is the owner of the outcome: an engineering-oriented role who can enter the customer's site to understand real problems, personally build systems to production, and feed field experience back into product capabilities. He is measured not by what documents he delivered, but by whether the business process was genuinely changed.

This is also the consensus in the official descriptions of leading companies: OpenAI defines the FDE as responsible for the entire customer deployment from discovery, scoping, design, and building to production rollout, with success measured by production adoption and measurable business impact. OpenAI Careers↗ Google Cloud emphasizes that the FDE is an embedded builder who codes, debugs, and ships together with the customer, rather than giving advice. Google Careers↗

The intersection of four dimensions: field · code · calibration · feedback

The best way to understand the FDE is not to break down the acronym, but to look at the four dimensions he simultaneously stands on. We call it 4C—a qualified FDE needs all four; none can be missing.

Going deep into the customer's real environment: business processes, where the data lives, who has permissions, who pays, and what the cost of failure is. The question is not "what features does the customer want," but "where is the customer's real business outcome stuck." This dimension determines whether you are doing the right thing.

Personally building the solution to production: connecting data, building retrieval and AI agents, handling permissions and auditing, deploying to production. Not a demo, not a slide deck, not a configuration, but a system that actually runs on the customer's infrastructure. This dimension determines whether you can actually deliver.

Running evals with real business metrics and failure modes to tune the system until it is genuinely useful: accuracy, coverage, unauthorized-access rate, human-handoff rate, adoption rate... Winning is not "it launched," but "it's being used and the metrics actually improved." This dimension determines whether what you delivered is truly effective, or just feels good.

Distilling recurring field friction into reusable connectors, templates, eval sets, and product requirements, so the next customer can land faster. This dimension determines whether you are building a compounding product, or a one-off project.

There are many people who occupy only one dimension: those who only understand the field are consultants, those who only write code are engineers, those who only watch metrics are analysts, and those who only think about productization are PMs. What is truly scarce—and what truly defines the FDE—is holding all four dimensions within a single accountable owner: not a relay of several specialists, but end-to-end accountability for the outcome. In the AI era, that owner can be one person leading a fleet of agents, or a small team leading multiple agents.

What the FDE is not

Precisely because it spans so many stages, the FDE is easily misunderstood as "pre-sales / consulting / delivery with a new name." Let's put it in one sentence up front:

Pre-sales is accountable for the solution, consulting for the advice, delivery for acceptance; the FDE is accountable for business results that actually happen in production.

The boundaries between them, and how to judge whether a role is a "real FDE," are covered in Differences from adjacent roles.

"Big FDE": forward deployed engineering is spilling over

Notably, the FDE is not just a job title, but a way of working that is spilling over. When AI capabilities change too fast and customer problems are highly heterogeneous, "putting the person who owns the outcome into the field and letting them get results end to end" is starting to reshape how sales, pre-sales, delivery, product, and even founders work. We call this broader paradigm "Big FDE" (Forward Deployed-ization).
