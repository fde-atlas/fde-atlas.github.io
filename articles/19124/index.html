Toronto FDE Salaries: What Ontario's Pay Transparency Act Says

Original title: 多伦多 FDE 薪资：安大略薪资透明法怎么看

FDE Times · 2026-11-05 · 5 min read

Summary

The article examines how Ontario's pay transparency law, effective January 1, 2026, turns Toronto FDE job postings into reliable salary data. Employers with 25 or more employees must disclose pay with ranges no wider than C$50,000, while roles at roughly C$200,000 or above are exempt. Using Banyan Software's single-figure posting (C$145,000 or US$170,000, excluding bonus) and Cohere's unusually wide C$175,000–385,000 range, the author shows the law's gaps and warns against anchoring negotiations on third-party estimates like FDE Pulse or ClavePrep, recommending readers build their own dataset from named-employer postings.

Key points

  • Ontario law: employers with 25+ staff must post salary ranges capped at C$50,000 wide.
  • Roles at about C$200,000 or higher are exempt from disclosure.
  • Banyan Software posts a single C$145,000 figure, excluding bonus, with currency ambiguity.
  • Cohere's C$175,000–385,000 range exceeds the legal width cap fourfold.
  • Third-party estimates are market context only; build a dataset from employer postings.

Banyan Software is hiring a Forward Deployed Engineer (FDE) in Toronto, Ontario. The posting states an expected base salary of CAD 145,000 or USD 170,000 per year, excluding annual bonus.

It's a single number, not a range, and it hides three traps for the careless reader: two currencies, a bonus not included in the figure, and the phrase "salary depends on location and experience."

Also targeting Toronto, the tracking site FDE Pulse estimates salaries for Cohere-related roles at roughly CAD 200,000–310,000. ClavePrep's 2026 FDE salary guide, meanwhile, gives its headline numbers based on the US market. These three figures fall into three different categories, and bringing the wrong number to a negotiation could cost you real money.

Since January 1, 2026, the most reliable source of FDE salary data in Toronto has been job postings bearing an employer's name, because Ontario law requires postings to disclose salary and limits the width of the range. But to use this source well, you first need to understand which part of the market the law covers and which parts it misses.

Ontario law turns job postings into data

According to BNN Bloomberg, starting in early 2026, employers in Ontario with 25 or more employees must state an annual salary range or hourly rate in publicly posted job ads. Both the law firm Fogler Rubinoff and Legal 500 confirm the 25-employee threshold. Disclosing salary is now a legal obligation, no longer at the employer's discretion.

More importantly, there's a cap on range width. Fogler Rubinoff, BNN Bloomberg, and Legal 500 all describe the same rule: the difference between the upper and lower bounds of a range may not exceed CAD 50,000 per year. BNN Bloomberg states its purpose plainly: to prevent employers from evading the law with extremely wide ranges.

Suppose a posting lists CAD 120,000–170,000. That range is already at maximum width, with a median of CAD 145,000 and each end just CAD 25,000 from the median. If your experience matches the job description, aiming for the upper half is reasonable; asking above the cap effectively means applying for a different role.

Why do higher-paying roles say less?

The first gap is company size. Because of the 25-employee threshold, if you're targeting a very small startup, its posting isn't legally required to disclose salary.

The second gap is at the high end. Fogler Rubinoff writes that when the salary or the upper bound of the range exceeds CAD 200,000 per year, the disclosure obligation does not apply. Bennett Jones phrases it slightly differently: the obligation does not apply to roles with salaries of CAD 200,000 and above. So when something sits right at the edge of the threshold, don't treat either interpretation as definitive.

Cohere's FDE posting for its Sovereign AI direction, aimed at Toronto candidates, shows what this gap looks like. It lists a Canadian salary range of CAD 175,000–385,000 per year: a span of CAD 210,000, more than four times the CAD 50,000 cap.

Since the upper bound is well above CAD 200,000, this range falls outside the law's tightened scope, and its median of CAD 280,000 says almost nothing about what you'll actually end up with.

So if you see an FDE posting with no salary at all, don't assume the company is deliberately hiding it. The team may have fewer than 25 people, the role may not be in Ontario, the posting may have been published before 2026, or the salary may already exceed the exemption threshold.

For numbers employers do publish, ask three questions

Back to Banyan's posting. This is the best kind of data you can get, because CAD 145,000 is backed by the company itself. Even so, it's still not the amount you'll ultimately take home.

The first question is currency: the posting gives both CAD and USD, and you need to know which currency the contract will pay in. The second is adjustment factors: the posting says salary also depends on market conditions, location, skills, and experience, so CAD 145,000 is an expected figure, not a fixed band.

The third is bonus. The figure excludes annual bonus, so comparing it with an offer that includes bonus means comparing two different things. One more detail: on LegalTech Roles, this posting's header shows "CAD1K - CAD2K Annual," which contradicts the body text. Always go by the posting's body text, not the summary box.

These three questions apply to any FDE posting, and to any country with salary transparency laws. Asking them in the first call with the recruiter is far easier than discovering the answers only when the offer arrives.

Use third-party estimates only to gauge the market

FDE Pulse's CAD 200,000–310,000 for Cohere's Toronto roles is a converted figure, and the page it appears on has no date. It's an inference, not a range published by the company under Ontario law. It suggests the ceiling at large AI companies may be high, but it's not a number to bring into a negotiation.

ClavePrep's problem is different. The guide's headline numbers reflect only US roles at AI companies that have completed Series C funding or later, and have nothing to do with Toronto.

ClavePrep's numbers are taken from public salary aggregation platforms, with no methodology stated, then roughly discounted by 20–40% for early-stage startups or non-AI companies. With a discount range that wide, compounded by market differences, these figures are far less persuasive than a posting backed by a named employer.

Build your own salary dataset

The most reliable approach is to build a table directly from job postings. For every FDE posting in Ontario, record the salary range, its width, the currency, and whether the bonus is listed separately or included. Each row corresponds to one role and one named employer, so when negotiating, this table carries more weight than averages of unclear provenance.

This table also helps you prepare your application materials. When reading job descriptions, note which requirements correspond to the highest ranges, such as experience deploying on real customer data, or working directly with customers. Recurring skills are worth writing into your resume as concrete, quantifiable achievements.

When you come across a posting with no salary listed, or a range wider than CAD 50,000 like Cohere's, don't guess. That means you've entered territory Ontario's law doesn't cover, where the only trustworthy number is the one you go and ask for yourself.
