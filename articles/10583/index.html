The Hottest AI Job, FDE: ¥50K Monthly Salary, and Here's What They Actually Do...

Summary

Based on interviews with six FDE practitioners, this QbitAI piece examines the booming role of Forward Deployed Engineers. Overseas, Anthropic is investing $100M to train 10,000 FDEs, OpenAI launched a $4B deployment company, and AWS committed $1B. In China, Kimi, Tencent Cloud and solo developers are following suit. The classic model splits work into Echo (requirements, design) and Delta (coding) roles, centered on mapping a company's Ontology. Practitioners report communication takes over 70% of their time; the hardest part is organizational friction, not code. Views differ on whether FDE is just outsourcing and whether the role will persist.

Key points

  • Anthropic, OpenAI and AWS are investing heavily in FDE teams; Chinese firms are following.
  • FDE work revolves around building the client's Ontology, with Echo and Delta roles.
  • Communication dominates: roughly 70% of time versus 30% coding.
  • Independent FDEs increasingly act as consultants, teaching employees to use general AI agents.
  • Some see FDE as a transitional role meant to eventually eliminate itself.

What is an FDE? Will it last?

By 听雨, from 凹非寺, Quantum Bit | WeChat account QbitAI

FDE, one of the hottest and most mysterious AI jobs on the internet.

Hot, because many big companies are offering monthly salaries as high as 30–50K, FDEs are all over Xiaohongshu, and job-hunting experience posts are everywhere.

According to FDE Pulse, a recruitment data website, the median base salary for FDE positions with publicly listed pay overseas is about $200,000 a year, roughly 1.34 million RMB.

(Money, money everywhere)

Mysterious, because I interviewed 6 FDE practitioners and found that what they do seems… all quite different.

FDE stands for Forward Deployed Engineer, known in Chinese as Forward Deployed Engineer.

As the name suggests, it's an engineer stationed at the customer's site: deploying software while solving the customer's real problems.

Now, this term has spread from Silicon Valley to China.

Overseas, Anthropic announced a $100 million investment to train 10,000 FDEs by the end of 2027; OpenAI, with $4 billion in funding, set up a company dedicated to deployment; AWS put $1 billion into building an FDE division, sending thousands of engineers to customers' side.

China is following suit, with many big companies posting FDE positions. Kimi announced it would partner with multiple IT service providers to build an FDE team, and Tencent Cloud launched what it claims is the industry's first FDE certification, while recruiting FDE partners.

Meanwhile, many independent developers have emerged, taking on projects single-handedly to do AI transformation for enterprises.

That said, sending engineers to customer sites is nothing new—it existed long before in the internet era.

Deployment engineers, implementation consultants, Solutions Engineers, after-sales engineers… they all did similar work, and were even once seen as "dirty work."

So why, in the AI era, has it suddenly become such a hot commodity? What exactly has changed about this old job?

What exactly is an FDE?

In the classic FDE model, two distinct roles often appear: Echo and Delta.

Echo is responsible for understanding the business, communicating requirements, and designing solutions; Delta writes code, connects data, and actually builds the solution. The two work together on the front line to meet customer needs.

In the back, there's also a group of Devs (regular engineers) responsible for developing the standard platform.

The difference between Delta and Dev can be summarized as: Dev does "one capability serving many customers," while Delta does "one customer, solving many problems."

Notably, some FDE job postings in China have started specifying Echo or Delta tracks. This wasn't the case at least two months ago.

Front-line work revolves largely around Ontology.

Simply put, an Ontology is a "business map" that software and AI can call upon: which employees, departments, and orders a company has, how they relate, and which operations each is allowed to perform.

What an FDE does is organize information scattered across different systems, processes, and employees' experience into this map, then build applications on top of it to solve problems.

When this model crossed the ocean to China, it grew all kinds of variants.

In the AI era, the focus of on-site work has also shifted: companies themselves can't even articulate where AI can be used, so the FDE must first help the customer figure out what to do.

01.AI, led by Kai-Fu Lee, is a typical example.

01.AI has now fully pivoted to enterprise AI, following this path: first find the company's top decision-maker, then send Forward Deployed Engineers on-site to map out the company's Ontology, and finally distill it into products and platforms. In the early days, one project required 5 FDEs on-site plus a 5-person backend team.

In Kai-Fu Lee's view, although their product has been productized and uses a subscription model, "it is not a plug-and-play product." Building a company's Ontology takes one to three months. "The company's databases, roles, and processes all need to be set up before the customer can get full results."

At many other companies, people are doing similar things. For example, Layla and Sonic.

Layla works as an FDE at a SaaS company, though her title is still product manager.

AI-generated

Starting around March–April 2025, she began truly working inside customer projects. Her clients are all listed companies with over 10,000 employees, involving chain stores and manufacturing, and each on-site stint lasts one to two months.

When she started, nobody called this FDE. Later, when the model became popular, she looked into it and "realized, hey, it's basically the same thing."

Her workflow goes like this:

Step one: get on-site and understand the business, sorting out what the customer really wants from a pile of roles like procurement, marketing, finance, and IT;

Step two: propose a solution and build a demo for quick validation;

Step three: once validated, hand it over to the company's existing delivery team for launch;

Finally, abstract out reusable capabilities and iterate them back into the company's product.

This is basically a classic FDE workflow, and also the standard process for enterprise or independent FDEs doing development today.

Layla believes that FDE observations and experience "ultimately need to be distilled into the standard product."

Another enterprise FDE, Sonic, works on review-type AI projects, such as finance/tax review and advertising material review.

AI-generated

Also starting in 2025, his team began studying FDE. In his view, one of the most critical parts of FDE work is mapping out the Ontology.

He gave an example: if a company wants to build an AI assistant for organizational management, it must first clarify how many departments it has and how they relate to each other; beyond relationships, there are also attributes, like a person's job level and salary.

Only by abstracting these into structures an AI agent can understand will it answer questions more accurately, rather than guessing blindly.

This step is often quite laborious, because FDEs frequently run into issues like incomplete historical data from upstream and downstream systems, or legacy systems without open APIs, making it hard to move the project forward.

But Sonic believes this step can't be skipped: "If the quality is high enough, it's very effective—subsequent delivery quality mainly depends on the quality of the Ontology built upfront."

To do all this well, both Layla and Sonic have to be stationed at customer sites.

In Sonic's view, being on-site isn't just about understanding requirements—it's also about aligning the business side's understanding of AI: what can be done and what can't. Only when both sides share the same context can they discuss pain points and solutions.

But Cheng Tianshu from Silicon Valley company Baseten told me that their FDEs basically don't work on-site.

AI-generated

Baseten is an AI inference cloud company. Customers deploy models on their platform, and Baseten makes those models run faster, more stably, and more cost-effectively.

Their customers are almost all engineers, with very specific needs. For example: they used to call OpenAI's API and now want to switch to open-source models—which model should they choose? How to deploy it? How to achieve the expected speed and cost?

The FDE meets with the customer to understand technical requirements, then builds a prototype to validate the solution. Problems may involve cloud infrastructure, model inference optimization, training, or product experience, with FDEs of different specialties handling different directions.

For them, this kind of communication can basically be done online.

The reason is that Baseten offers a standardized, developer-facing product that only covers one clear segment of the customer's business. It isn't highly customized and doesn't require going deep into the customer's organizational fabric to solve business problems.

"Our product isn't tightly coupled with the customer's own business logic," said Cheng Tianshu.

Beyond the big companies, a band of "guerrillas" is growing wild

Outside of companies, a group of "guerrillas" is rapidly expanding: they don't belong to any product company—they find their own clients, take their own orders, and do AI transformations for enterprises.

This itself is a change brought by AI: in the past, customizing a system took a team months; now code is nearly free, and a single person can take on and deliver an order.

This market is extremely wild right now. Lawted, who calls himself "the number one FDE on the entire internet," told me he calls this grassroots FDE.

Big-company FDEs mainly promote their own products, while grassroots FDEs mainly serve "grassroots bosses"—OpenAI or Gemini, either works—and they have no sales quotas: "Whatever you want, that's what I'll give you."

I met an FDE named Zaniel, and after talking with him, I realized this guy's path is seriously wild...

He's a Stanford PhD student who started taking on corporate AI projects solo this April.

Banks, law firms, coal chemicals, postpartum care centers, housekeeping agencies, construction companies... in just over five months, he's completed dozens of projects, delivering all of them himself.

And interestingly, after working across so many scenarios, his focus has shifted from "building AI systems and tools for clients" to "teaching enterprise employees how to use AI."

AI-generated

For example, his first client was a bank, where several employees had to export piles of numbers from a data system every day and compile them into a few reports for leadership.

At first, he built an automatic generator—drag the data in, click once, and the report comes out.

But soon after it was built, nobody used it. Because the report rules changed every day, and the exported table formats weren't exactly the same, the hard-coded program "broke down after a few days of use."

Later he realized there was no need for new software—he could just teach employees to hand the de-identified data to a general-purpose AI for summarization, organize high-frequency workflows into Skills, and connect them to the existing data systems.

That way, employees actually started using it.

Zaniel told me that, at least in white-collar work, most problems can be solved with a general-purpose Agent plus a bunch of Skills and connections to existing systems—"no need to develop other new software."

Another independent FDE taking orders on her own, 软萌孜神, described a similar situation.

She once worked as an FDE for a foreign trade company, discussing with the boss how to increase employees' AI usage—the final conclusion was, just purchase WorkBuddy.

AI-generated

She was responsible for training employees on how to use WorkBuddy, and for connecting WorkBuddy to the group's existing internal systems.

These "guerrilla-style" grassroots FDEs, while transforming enterprises, discovered that in many cases there's no need to build a new product or system, and their business models started leaning toward consulting services.

Zaniel works with enterprises in two modes: one is project-based, the other is advisory-based, and the second is becoming more and more common.

The so-called advisor is, simply put, someone who teaches employees how to use these AI tools. Whenever a new tool comes out, he helps the enterprise figure out how the business should keep up; if a small need arises, he'll build it on the spot, included in the advisory service.

He calls this role the enterprise's external "CAIO" (Chief AI Officer).

Lawted, too, feels his business is starting to resemble a consulting firm.

He runs an FDE community called HA7CH, and through offline meetups and hackathons, he matches enterprises seeking AI transformation with independent FDEs to sign deals.

AI-generated

The process is: the enterprise submits its requirements, and he picks one or two candidates from those who sign up. The candidates spend 48 hours on-site mapping out workflows and building a demo; if the boss is impressed, the deal is signed on the spot, and the candidate handles the subsequent delivery.

After running several sessions, Lawted increasingly felt that FDE work is "very much like consulting": enterprises often only know they want to use AI but can't articulate where the problem is, so the FDE has to first go on-site to complete a diagnosis before producing a solution.

In his view, consultants are actually better suited than programmers to switch to FDE work—they just need to pick up vibe coding skills, and "vibe coding really isn't hard."

Code written in two days; the real trouble is with people

By now, you've probably noticed that in an FDE's work, writing code and doing development takes up little time, while communicating with people takes up the majority.

Several practitioners told me that communicating requirements takes up more than 70% of their time, while development is only about 30%.

And frictions with client employees, inability to get API access permissions, and other difficulties often arise during this process.

While pushing WorkBuddy's integration into enterprise internal systems, 软萌孜神 often had to communicate repeatedly with procurement, IT, ERP product managers, and external engineers. Some systems had no ready-made interfaces, requiring her to push the enterprise to develop them itself.

After more than a month, many APIs still weren't open. She said she'd have to keep haggling with IT the next day.

However, she doesn't think employees were deliberately obstructing. "Nobody intentionally trips you up." It's just that everyone has their own tasks, and AI transformation may not be that high a priority on their end.

AI-generated

Sometimes, not getting permissions is just a process issue.

But the resistance brought by AI has an even subtler layer: in the past, deploying a piece of software rarely touched anyone's position directly; AI is different—it may change a team's original work.

One company wanted to develop an AI system covering dozens of business modules. Zaniel estimated he could finish it in three to four months, but the client required him to work as an advisor with the IT team.

Even after signing an NDA, the other side was unwilling to open the codebase, yet would often have him troubleshoot problems remotely.

Zaniel also suggested that some modules could simply use a general-purpose Agent—no need to build everything in-house.

The leader replied: "If you're using WorkBuddy, what are they supposed to do?"

...After hearing that, all I can say is, this is painfully real.

However, Zaniel doesn't simply attribute this to clients being "uncooperative." Clients may have confidentiality requirements, existing vendor relationships, or perhaps just different departments with their own concerns.

"For an enterprise, the cost of changing how people work is often far greater than changing a tool," he said.

Isn't FDE just outsourcing?

Another frequently discussed question is: Is FDE really just outsourcing?

At least at Baseten, the answer is no.

Cheng Tianshu told me that their FDEs are just like regular engineers—the hiring standards and interview process are exactly the same, they receive the same fixed salary and stock, and they don't rely on commissions.

The services FDEs provide aren't billed separately either. Even if a prototype is built, as long as the client ultimately doesn't use Baseten's cloud platform, no payment is required.

For them, FDEs serve their own product: helping clients get up and running, then bringing field feedback back to improve the product.

AI-generated

Layla and Sonic, who work inside enterprises, also believe that distilling experience back into the product for reuse is a key difference between FDEs and outsourcing.

Layla said that in the past, people distinguished outsourcing by the fact that it "doesn't build its own products and doesn't accumulate its own capabilities."

"If we also don't accumulate our own capabilities or build our own products, and every project has to be done from 0 to 1, then when people call us outsourcing, we really have no way to argue back."

Sonic told me very directly that traditional outsourcing can't do FDE work. "If you don't believe it, try outsourcing and see."

As FDEs, they distill reusable modules from projects, organized by scenario, into an evals and iteration platform, then build a Skill Hub and Agent Hub, where all the FDEs on the team run evals and optimizations.

The result: in a vertical industry, the first scenario takes about a month from onboarding to launch; after that, similar scenarios can be onboarded in just a day or two.

"The longer you do it, the faster you get." Doing it fast means fewer meetings and lower communication costs, so "the business feels the whole process is light and the money is well spent."

It sounds like, as long as experience can be accumulated and reused for the next client, the FDE is clearly distinguished from outsourcing.

At this point, I had already been convinced by the previous speakers, but Zaniel refreshed my understanding again.

What people usually imagine by reuse is first building a semi-standardized software suite, then at the next client, "tweak the rules a bit and deploy it directly."

Zaniel doesn't deny that Skills, code, and industry experience can be reused. But he questions: how much cheaper can these accumulations actually make the next full delivery?

In the past, building an enterprise software system might cost 90 in development, with adaptation for each client costing only 10. Build the software once, sell it to many clients, and that initial 90 gets amortized bit by bit. That's the business logic of traditional software companies.

AI has changed this formula. Now development costs only 1, while adaptation still costs 10.

AI-generated

For example, if you built a customer management system for one client and the next client wants one too, you might as well just build a new one from scratch, "because AI can now produce software so quickly."

Of course, code can be reused, and Skills and industry knowledge can be accumulated. But every time an FDE enters a new company, they still have to re-understand requirements, connect systems, handle permissions, and then push different departments to accept new ways of working.

This part of the investment doesn't decrease along with the cost of code.

Software is getting easier and easier to copy, but whether the business itself can be replicated is another question.

FDEs no longer want to call themselves FDEs

Toward the end of the interviews, I asked almost everyone the same question:

Will the FDE role exist forever?

Cheng Tianshu believes the ultimate purpose of the FDE position "is to eliminate the FDE itself."

Wherever an FDE is needed, it means there's still a gap between the product and customer needs; once the product is good enough, customers can handle things themselves and no longer need an FDE.

In his view, FDEs are better suited to early-stage companies whose products aren't yet mature. Mature companies don't really need this role—it's more like "a transitional function" in the process of a company polishing its product.

But he also admits that AI is developing so fast that "the product you made yesterday can't keep up today." As long as the market keeps changing, this gap will always exist, so this goal "can be approached gradually, but never fully reached."

Zaniel's view is: what FDEs do has been done by people long before AI—it used to be called management consulting, also IT outsourcing; the term has only become popular recently.

"As for whether the term is popular or not, that's just an internet communication problem."

In fact, neither he nor Ruanmeng Zishen cares much about whether they're FDEs. They say so externally only because people are familiar with the term.

"Actually, I don't really like people calling me an FDE, but I have no choice. Because in the current market, you have to tell clients 'AI FDE' for them to pay attention," said Ruanmeng Zishen.

She prefers to call herself an entrepreneur, aiming to transform foreign trade with AI and "make the Chinese supply chain great again."

The three letters FDE have been stuffed with far too many meanings.

Software development, consulting services, employee training, system integration, community matchmaking... all these seemingly different jobs can be related to FDEs.

But whatever kind of FDE they are, they all do the work of actually putting AI into the client's business and winning over the people involved.

If I had to say what has actually changed about this old role in the AI era, I'd say three things:

In the past, the on-site person was responsible for installing finished software; now, you first have to help the client figure out where AI should be used.

In the past, customizing a system took a team; now, code is nearly free, and one person can take the job.

In the past, the hard part was the technology; now, the hard part is people.

It's still the same old role, but in the AI era, it has to know more, move faster, and shoulder more.

The reason FDEs are hot is precisely that technology is changing too fast: companies haven't figured out how to use it, and organizational structures can't keep up, so too many required capabilities are all piled onto one person.

Just as that viral short video ends:

"When companies buy SaaS, they're buying software; when they hire consultants, they're buying advice; when they hire outsourcers, they're buying features.

But when they hire an FDE, what they want to buy is a person—

someone who understands management, technology, human nature, and AI, and ideally doesn't ask for too much money."
