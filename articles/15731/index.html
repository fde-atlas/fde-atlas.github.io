Charter the Lab: When to Fund It, When to Kill It, What Legal Signs

Michael Couch | couch.cx · 2026-10-26 · 10 min read

Summary

Michael Couch argues that an enterprise AI lab is only worth funding as a foundry that turns already-valuable operators into shippers, not as a center of excellence or showcase. The charter requires, before week one: four named operators, a witness (legal/security) who unblocks in hours, one allowed data slice, and a board that labels every loop graduate, library, or sunset within 90 days. Legal signs an envelope covering tenancy, logged writes, classification, and liability splits, not a values poster. Day-30 and day-90 checkpoints are written in so finance cannot move them; missing them triggers hiring freezes or closure. If conditions fail, the adult alternative is running one FDE triad inside a business unit with a skill library and exit conditions.

Key points

  • Fund a lab only as a foundry; a CoE or showcase is theatre.
  • Prerequisites: four named operators, an hour-scale witness, one allowed data slice.
  • Charter covers three jobs only: access, harness, witness; strike a fourth.
  • Day 30 needs a production-shaped path; day 90 needs two labeled loops.
  • If conditions fail, run one FDE triad in a business unit instead.

The manifesto is already on the table. Establish the Lab says what the object is: hunt the operators already pulling, put a harness in their hands this month, treat the lab as the platform behind FDE, not a museum of pilots. This piece is the charter. A sceptical COO can take it into a budget review. If you cannot sign what follows, do not paint the glass.

A lab is worthwhile only as a foundry. A named room, a CoE with nicer chairs, or a 2027 enablement plan is not. The physics did not change: idea-to-artifact collapsed, unpaid shadow speed is now more expensive than sanctioned shipping. What changed is the paperwork. Talent this valuable will not wait for your operating model. Legal will not wait for your values poster. Finance will not fund "explore AI" twice. So you either charter a foundry, or you skip the brand and put a triad in one business unit. Both can be adult. Theatre cannot.

SCQA (at a glance)

Situation: Idea-to-running-artifact collapsed; shadow tools are already faster than IT. Complication: Boards hear "lab" and fund theatre, or they hear "risk" and fund nothing. Both fail. Question: When is a lab the right object, and what must be true on paper before week one? Answer: Fund it only if you can name four operators, one hour-scale witness, one allowed data slice, and a graduate / library / sunset board. Otherwise run FDE in the business and do not paint a glass room.

Worthwhile, under conditions

The economic case is not token savings. It is the conversion rate of people you already pay. Models are rented. Domain is not. The operator who knows which field in the CRM is a lie, who the client actually trusts, and what "done" looks like on a Thursday, that person is a multiplier the moment they have a sanctioned path. Leave them in a queue and you have paid for taste you refuse to use. That is the same compression as The Individual Contributor Age: implementation stopped being scarce. Clarity did not.

Compare the objects honestly.

  • Everyone gets Copilot produces average output. Useful floor. Not a foundry.
  • A CoE owns the narrative, the vendors, and the steering pack. Business units attend. Nothing ships that the business can keep. Usually not worthwhile.
  • FDE only is worthwhile at small scale: one triad, one residency, one named outcome. Skip the lab until pull exists. Do not invent a campus to look busy.
  • A lab that is access, harness, witness is worthwhile when more than one team is already pulling, shadow tools are faster than the ticket, and you can graduate a loop into an owner. That is the empowerment system, not a programme.

If the alternative is operators pasting client work into consumer chat, the lab is cheaper than the incident. If the alternative is two residencies you can name this month, start there. Do not use the lab as a delay tactic for work you already understand.

Do not fund it yet

Kill the lab on paper before you hire, if any of these are true:

  • You cannot name four operators by watching the work. You have a survey titled "AI champions."
  • You cannot name a witness (legal, security, or quality) who will unblock in hours, not quarters. They only attend steering.
  • There is no allowed data product. The lake is "almost governed." Come back later.
  • Tools are a future tense. The 2027 enablement plan is the artefact. Operators are already on personal accounts.
  • The lab is expected to do the last mile for twenty business units. That is a touring band. That is FDE without exit conditions.
  • Success is a showcase, a utilization target, or a vendor coverage map.
  • You are staffing a research bench and you are not actually doing research. Most enterprises are installing known patterns into stubborn workflows. That is foundry work.

If more than one of those is true, do not add headcount. Put skills and a triad in one business unit. Measure a loop. Then talk about a lab.

What must be true on paper in week one

A charter is one page plus names. Not a target operating model. If it does not fit on a page, you are hiding the missing witness.

Mission, three jobs only. Access (identity, model route, allowed slice, sandbox that looks like production). Harness (skills, evals, logging, kill switch, a path that can graduate). Witness (a named adult who can say yes on a Tuesday). If a fourth job appears ("own the company AI narrative," "manage the vendor estate," "run the showcase"), strike it.

People, with measures. Lab lead measured on loops graduated, not headcount. One harness / platform engineer. Four to eight rotating operators with a time-boxed tour and a named outcome in their real job. They do not "go to the lab." They bring the work in. Career path: operator in residence, then technical operator back in the business, then FDE or platform. The lab is not a parking lot for people product did not want.

Envelope of change. You may change these named workflows, inside this classification, with this override. You may ship a skill. You may fail a loop in public as long as the kill switch works. You will not be punished for a prototype that did not graduate, only for a prototype that pretended it was production. That is permission plus a floor, not a speech. Same geometry as walking the work instead of consuming summaries.

The board that kills. Every loop gets one of three labels, in writing, by a date: graduate (running in the operator's workflow, owner named, evals on), library (keep the skill, kill the UI), sunset (delete where people can see the deletion). Ninety days is plenty to know. A lab that cannot kill is a CoE.

Day-thirty and day-ninety trips. Write them into the charter so finance cannot move them. Day thirty: one production-shaped path, logging on, kill switch tested, operator using it on a real case. If you cannot get here, the access story is a lie. Fix that before you hire. Day ninety: two loops labeled. Skills an FDE residency can pick up. A written list of what platform must make boring next. Only then do you add people.

What legal signs

Legal does not sign a values poster about experimentation. They sign an envelope. Keep it boring enough that a partner with hour-scale latency can actually countersign.

  • Tenancy. No secrets in prompts that leave the tenancy. Named model route, not a personal API key. If the site cannot call the API, you already know the air-gap answer. Do not charter a cloud Copilot for that cell.
  • Writes. No unlogged production writes. Identity on every action. A human override the operator will actually hit. A skill without an eval is a style guide; a lab without a stop is how you get a ban in month four.
  • Classification. One data product that is allowed, with a known label, not "come back when the lake is governed." Start with the dataset the operator already lives in.
  • Liability split. Prototype that stays in the envelope is a lab problem. Prototype that ships as if it were production, without graduating, is a conduct problem. Write that down so people stop whispering.
  • Client and works-council constraints where they exist. The charter names the first slice that is allowed to touch client work, and the slice that is not. Ambiguity here is how the lab becomes a waiting room.

Preferences wait: which IDE, which vendor box, which furniture. Non-negotiables live in the path. The harness is the thing legal can see. Governance the FDE and the operator carry, rather than wait on, is the same split as the frontier essay. If legal only attends steering, you have already lost. Empowerment is a latency number.

Who pays, and how you accidentally kill it

Fund the lab as platform opex: identity, routes, evals, logging, a budget for tokens and time. Loops that graduate move onto the business unit. That is how you keep the foundry from becoming a shared service that never exits.

Do not capitalise a campus. Do not bonus the lab lead on utilization. Do not charge back token spend to the four people you just hunted; you will teach them to go back underground. Token spend is a cost, not a KPI. Hours in the lab that do not graduate are inventory. Inventory should hurt.

Vendors will sell you a lab-in-a-box. Sometimes the box is how you get identity and models faster. It is not a substitute for hunting humans, and it is not a line on the charter that says "the vendor owns the last mile." If the only people who can see the work are outside the company, you rented a demo with better lighting.

Where it sits (and where it must not)

Solid line to a platform or deployment lead measured on loops in production. Dotted line into the business units that supplied the operators. Hard contract with security on hour-scale latency. Put it under a transformation office that lives on decks and you reconstituted the CoE. Put it only in IT with no operators and you reconstituted a ticket queue with GPUs. Put it only in the business with no harness and you reconstituted shadow IT.

Lab, FDE, operator are one system. The operator holds the picture. The lab makes it cheap and safe to try. The FDE installs the last mile when trying is not enough. Platform sits behind both. Rival programmes get you two theatres. That is the enterprise triad, zoomed out, same as the end of the monolithic product org: capacity at the edge, center as platform.

The first slice is not a lakehouse

In a marketing or network enterprise the first allowed product is usually ugly and close: the pack, the lockup, the CRM lie, the campaign exception, the onboarding path. Speak the operator's language or they will not come. A foundry that opens on "enterprise knowledge graph" is a campus. The plant version of this argument is already written: the lab sits next to the line, not on the tour. Same OS. Different floor.

Hunt the same way you hunt product-market fit. Who already ships without a ticket. Who is angry with a spec. Who other ICs already ask. Who stayed close to the work after promotion. You will not find them in the all-hands. Spare calendar is not taste.

Cadence over campus

Physical space is optional. Density is not. Change-makers need to see each other's loops. Isolation produces twelve snowflakes. Density produces a library.

The operating rhythm is a Tuesday board, not a quarterly showcase: what graduated, what went to the library, what died, what the witness blocked and unblocked, what inbound pull arrived from the business. If you are still hunting after six months, either the tools are wrong or permission is fake. If shadow IT rises while the lab is "busy," the lab is slower than the street. That is a product failure, not a communications problem.

Score empowerment as an operating system, not as experiments: time to first production-shaped path, change-maker conversion, inbound pull, reuse, shadow down, FDE residencies that start from lab skills. Stop counting demos.

How to kill the lab after you funded it

Write the suicide pact into the charter so pride cannot move it.

  • No production-shaped path by day thirty. Freeze hiring. Fix access. If access cannot be fixed, close the brand and keep the four operators in their units with a golden path and no glass.
  • Zero graduates by day ninety. Sunset the lab. Keep the library if anything in it is real. Do not rebrand as a CoE.
  • Witness latency measured in weeks. The lab is a waiting room. Name a new witness or stop.
  • An incident, then a freeze, then a poster. You had a chance to put governance in the path and chose a ban. Change-makers go underground. That is the failure mode. Recover by narrowing to access, harness, witness, not by adding a steering pack.

Closing a lab in public is a win if the loops graduated or the library is real. Keeping a hospice because the name is on the org chart is how you train the next cohort that programmes are fake.

If you skip the lab

Skipping can be the adult move. One business unit, one triad, one skill library the operator can extend, a residency with an exit condition. That is FDE without a foundry, and it is how you earn the right to charter later. What you may not do is skip the floor: identity, logs, a human override, an allowed slice. Speed without a sanctioned stack is not a morality play. It is a forecast of the next incident.

Small companies often should skip. They already have density. They need a harness, not a campus. Large companies that ration tools through central queues cannot skip forever. The cost of not empowering their own people is now higher than the cost of letting them ship. That sentence is still true. It is not a blank cheque for furniture.

Fund a foundry or do not use the word. Name four operators, one witness, one allowed slice, a kill switch legal will sign, and a board that can sunset in public. If you cannot, run the triad in the business and keep the lights off the glass. Talent has never been this valuable. The charter is how you stop donating it to the street while the operating model is still in draft.
