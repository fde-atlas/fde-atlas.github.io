Enterprise Document Processing Tools: How to Choose

Zarif Automates · 2026-06-10 · 9 min read

Summary

This guide compares five document processing tools—Datalab, ABBYY Vantage, Rossum, Google Document AI, and Azure Document Intelligence—arguing that selection should start from what the business must trust rather than demo quality or a single accuracy number. Key questions include whether fields can be traced to their source page, corrected, and compared across runs. The author matches each tool to a team type: builders pick Datalab, low-code operations pick ABBYY, invoice workflows pick Rossum, and cloud-committed apps pick Google or Azure. It also proposes five vendor questions, an invoice pilot with a deliberately ambiguous total ($1,080 printed vs. $1,008 in line items), and measuring cost per accepted record rather than per page.

Key points

  • Judge tools on traceability, correction, and change visibility, not demos.
  • Five tools fit different team types; none is the default winner.
  • Datalab charges per processor; Team plan is $400/month plus usage.
  • Run an invoice pilot with a deliberately mismatched total to test behavior.
  • Measure cost per accepted record and log failures by error type.

Datalab, ABBYY, Rossum, Google and Azure for pulling data out of documents, and the questions that decide which one fits your team.

A document pipeline can return perfectly valid JSON and still put the wrong amount on the wrong customer.

So when you're choosing a tool, start with the data the business has to trust. A great demo matters less than three plain questions. Can someone trace a field back to the page it came from? Can they fix it? And can they see what changed on the next run?

This guide looks at five options: Datalab, ABBYY Vantage, Rossum, Google Document AI and Azure Document Intelligence. Each fits a different kind of team. None of them is the right answer by default.

How this was researched: this guide is based on the linked vendor material, checked September 17, 2026. The recommendations are editorial judgment. No side-by-side benchmark was run for this article.

Start with the work after extraction

Write down what someone actually needs to get out of the documents. "Read PDFs" is too vague. "Pull out the contract parties and the renewal date, and show where each one is on the page so someone can check it" is something you can build.

Then ask what a mistake costs. A missing heading in an internal research note is a shrug. A wrong payment amount is a real problem. They need different review processes. Judging both by one overall accuracy number hides that difference.

Datalab: for teams building their own pipeline

Datalab treats document handling as a part of your app you maintain, like any other code.

Its platform docs are built around pipelines. You chain conversion, extraction, and your own processing steps together. Every published version is locked and can be called through the API. The docs also cover scoring rubrics and checks that flag when results get worse over time. See the Datalab platform.

For a builder, that answers a specific question: which setup produced this output, and can you go back to the one before it?

That matters for agents that read a lot of documents. Picture a research assistant that reads a stack of company reports. If a report changes, or the extraction setup changes, the team needs a way to see what's different, not just accept a new summary.

Datalab's platform page lists managed hosting and enterprise options, including running inside a customer's own private cloud or on machines cut off from the internet entirely. Confirm availability and pricing for the setup you actually plan to use. See deployment options.

Datalab fits a team that wants to own the app around it and inspect document processing as one of its parts. That's about how it fits the architecture. It says nothing about whether it reads your documents more accurately than the others.

Understand the bill before estimating savings

Datalab charges by processor. Its published pricing page lists a free monthly allowance. The Team plan costs $400 a month and includes $400 of processing, and anything past that is billed by use. Some operations and options add charges of their own, so your estimate depends on your actual pipeline. See Datalab pricing.

Ask what a complete run will cost, including any conversion, extraction, evaluation, or region options. A per-page price only helps when everyone is counting the same work.

ABBYY Vantage: investigate the low-code operating model

Vantage is ABBYY's low-code take on document processing. It comes with pre-trained extraction skills, a designer for building your own, monitoring, and connections to the automation tools an operations team already uses. See ABBYY Vantage.

It's built for a different buyer than Datalab. Instead of a developer wiring a pipeline into an app, it's an operations team putting together document skills with as little code as possible.

The feature list won't decide the fit. What decides it is whether the skill setup, the review screen, and the connections match how the people running it actually work every day.

Ask the vendor to walk one problem document all the way through, from the moment it arrives to the moment someone fixes it. A feature checklist rarely shows how much work that takes.

Rossum: start with the transaction

Rossum aims narrower than Datalab or ABBYY. It's for transaction documents like invoices and purchase orders. The goal isn't to read the text. It's to get a correct number into an ERP or accounts-payable system. See Rossum.

So treat it as a question about process, not features. Here, a document is one step in a business process, not a file waiting to be turned into text.

Map out which system each document has to update, and when a person has to step in. Judge the fit against that map. A general extraction score doesn't tell you whether the transaction comes out right.

Google and Azure: consider the application around the processor

The case for Google Document AI is mostly about where your app already lives. It's a set of document processors, some general and some specialized, meant to plug into whatever you've built on Google Cloud.

So don't judge it on processor quality in general. Check whether the specific processor types you need exist, and how cleanly they fit your setup. See the Google Document AI overview.

Azure Document Intelligence makes the same case for the other cloud. It has ready-made models for common document types, lets you train custom ones, and connects through Azure services a team on Azure probably already uses. Check its supported document types against what you actually receive before you assume it fits. See the Microsoft overview.

Being on the same cloud makes part of the connection work easier. You still have to design the review process, set up access controls, and check the data.

Five questions worth taking to every vendor

  • Can a reviewer trace an important field back to its source? Ask to see the actual output format and how it points to the page.
  • What happens when it can't find a required value? Missing, unsure, and conflicting values shouldn't quietly turn into confident answers.
  • How does a correction reach the system downstream? A review screen only helps if the fixed value becomes the record people use.
  • What can be locked, compared, or rolled back? Find out which changes could alter outputs you already have.
  • What's in the contract and the deployment terms? Confirm how long data is kept, where it's processed, who can access it, and what support you get.

You don't need a research lab to ask these. They just surface the assumptions a polished demo can leave open.

An FDE pilot: reconcile an invoice before exporting it

Use a fake invoice with one field that's ambiguous on purpose. Say its printed total is $1,080, but the line items add up to $1,008.

The right behavior is to keep both numbers and send the mismatch to a person. Valid JSON can't tell you which number is correct.

Before you pick a processor, agree with the business owner on exactly what each output record contains:

  • the source document and its version,
  • the processor or pipeline version,
  • the extracted fields,
  • where on the page each field came from, if available,
  • the results of the validation checks,
  • the review status, and
  • the ID of the record it lands in.

When a reviewer corrects a value, keep the original value too.

Keep the processor's confidence score separate from whether the data is actually valid. The tool can be very sure it read the page correctly, and the invoice can still be addressed to the wrong customer. Check customer identity, currency, required fields and the math separately from the extraction.

One row needs a plain-English version. Say the invoice gets written to the accounting system, but the confirmation never comes back. If you just try again, you might record the same invoice twice. Instead, check whether the first write landed, using the system's status lookup or a unique key it recognizes. That's what idempotency means.

These are proposed test cases, not results from any vendor. Add the document types, languages and scan quality the customer actually has. Keep the documents you tune on separate from the ones you use for the final test, and say how you picked the final set.

Measure the process that reaches the business system

Measure the important fields one at a time. Then measure how many complete records pass the business rules. Report how many documents need review and how long fixing them takes. An overall text-recognition score can hide the one amount field that decides whether the transaction is usable.

During the pilot, keep a log of every failure: the source, the expected value, what came out, what kind of error it was, and what happened to it. Sort extraction mistakes apart from sorting, customer matching, validation and export failures. That tells the forward-deployed engineer whether to change the processor, a rule, or the connections around it.

Work out cost per accepted record, including reprocessing, review, and handling problems downstream. If one processor charges by the page and another charges for several steps, compare the whole workflow on the same mix of documents. A vendor's published accuracy or savings claim is not your test result.

The handoff should name the owner of the source data, the reviewer, the owner of the connections, and the person who runs it. Include how long data is kept and when it's deleted, who can access what, the versioned test set, how to switch off exports, and how to go back to an earlier setup.

Rolling back the processor doesn't undo records already written to an ERP.

Where to start

For a team building an app or agent that runs on documents, test a pipeline product like Datalab next to the cloud processor your app already lives on. For a broader document operation, start with the suite or cloud service the team can realistically keep running.

Make the call based on the whole path, from document in to reviewed record out. Pick the one whose weak spots your team can see and handle. Then run the invoice pilot above with the customer's own document types before you commit.
