Forward Deployed Engineering as a Service

Reveneau · 2026-09-08 · 12 min read

Summary

Reveneau's guide compares three ways to acquire forward deployed engineering capability: building an in-house function, staffing individual engineers through a partner, or engaging a firm that owns the outcome. Building offers the most control but requires a hiring cycle plus two or three engagements of learning; staffing is fastest but leaves delivery risk with the buyer; engaging a firm shifts risk only if the contract names a production outcome and handover date. The recommended sequence is to engage a firm first with capability transfer written in, then build.

Key points

  • Three routes exist: build, staff, or engage a firm that owns the outcome.
  • Building is slowest: a hiring cycle plus two or three learning engagements.
  • Staffing is fastest but delivery risk stays with you; context leaves with contractors.
  • Outcome engagements only work with a production date and handover date in the contract.
  • Recommended sequence: engage first with capability transfer, then hire your own team.

Key takeaways

  • Build: most control, slowest, and you own the structural decisions most companies get wrong on the first attempt.
  • Staff through a partner: the fastest way to get a person working, and the delivery risk stays with you because you direct the work.
  • Engage a firm: risk moves to the vendor, but only if the contract names a production outcome and a handover date.
  • The common mistake is buying an outcome from a vendor while directing the work yourself, which gives you the control of neither model.

This page is the build, staff or engage decision, with what each one actually costs you in control and risk.

Route one: build the function

Hire your own embedded engineers.

What it gives you. Full control, accumulated context that stays with you, and a capability that keeps growing in value. If embedded delivery is core to how you sell, this is the end goal.

What it costs. A hiring cycle for a competitive profile. Across 1,000 postings the role is mostly mid-level, with 60 percent targeting three to five years, and you are competing with companies paying well. Then two or three engagements of learning before the function is good, because the first engagements are where you find out what generalises and how long production takes at your kind of customer.

The hidden cost. The structural decisions. Where the function reports is the one most companies get wrong, and the hiring data suggests over half already have: only 45 percent of those postings sat on a dedicated forward deployed team rather than under sales.

Why building looks cheaper than it is on a spreadsheet. A loaded salary and a hiring timeline are easy to put in a model. The two or three engagements of learning are not, because that cost shows up as slower production dates and rework rather than as a line item anybody tracks. A company comparing "hire two engineers at X" against "engage a firm at Y" is usually comparing a partial cost against a full one, and the comparison favours building every time it is run that way.

When it is right. When this is how you sell, when you have enough accounts to keep engineers busy, and when you can afford the learning period. How to build the team is the step-by-step plan.

Route two: staff engineers through a partner

Bring in individual senior engineers who work under your direction.

What it gives you. A person working quickly, flexibility to add or remove people, and no hiring commitment. You keep control of the roadmap and the approach.

What it costs. You keep the delivery risk. If the plan is wrong, that is yours. And the context these people accumulate leaves when they do, unless you deliberately capture it.

The thing to be clear about. This is staff augmentation, and it is an honest model with honest pricing. It becomes a problem when it is sold as forward deployed engineering, because the words are nearly identical and what you are buying is not. FDE vs staff augmentation separates them.

A worked example of the failure mode. A company hires two senior engineers through a staffing partner, tells them to "own the customer integration," and checks in weekly. Six months later the integration exists but nobody defined what done meant, so it kept growing to match every request. The company paid for two senior salaries and got a system nobody can say is finished, because the judgment about what to build and what to decline was never anybody's job. That judgment is exactly what an outcome-based engagement prices for, and exactly what staffing does not include.

When it is right. When you have senior technical leadership making the decisions and you need capacity. If nobody on your side is making those decisions, more engineers become people who wait.

Route three: engage a firm that takes the outcome

Hand a defined outcome to a firm that works this way.

What it gives you. Delivery risk moved to the firm, a team that has done this before, and no hiring or learning period. The good version also gives you a capability transfer at the end.

What it costs. More per unit of time, less control over how, and a dependency risk if the engagement does not have a fixed end.

The condition. This only works if the contract names a production outcome and a handover date. A firm engaged on time and materials with an outcome-flavoured description gives you the control of neither model, and that is the most common way this route disappoints. How to price and the contract cover the terms.

When it is right. When the deployment matters more than the capability, when you need it now, or when you want to see the model run properly once before building your own.

The dependency risk, named plainly. If the engagement never gets a fixed end, the firm's accumulated knowledge of your system becomes a reason to keep paying them rather than a reason to stop. This is the same failure that shows up in the contract when a handover date is missing. A firm confident in its own work will put a handover date in writing, because handover is what proves the work was actually transferable.

The comparison

Speed to start. Staff fastest, engage close behind, build slowest by a hiring cycle.

Control over approach. Build highest, staff high, engage lowest.

Who carries delivery risk. Build you, staff you, engage the vendor if the contract says so.

Cost per unit of time. Staff lowest, build middling once you account for unbilled time, engage highest.

What you own at the end. Build a capability, staff nothing lasting unless you captured it, engage a running system plus whatever the handover transferred.

Failure mode. Build hires the wrong profile or reports into sales, staff buys extra engineers for a judgment problem, engage never ends.

How to read this table for your own situation. Do not pick the route with the fewest weaknesses in the abstract. Pick the route whose specific weakness you can actually manage. A company with a strong internal technical leader and no budget for a firm's premium should staff, and accept the delivery risk knowingly rather than by accident. A company entering a market it has never sold into before should engage, because the structural mistakes of building are exactly the mistakes it cannot yet see coming.

The combination that works

The sequence most companies should follow rather than choosing one route forever.

Engage a firm for the first one or two deployments, with a handover date and a capability transfer written in. Watch how they run it: the discovery, the production path, the artefacts. Then hire your own, using what you learned about what the role actually needs at your kind of customer.

That reverses the usual order, which is to hire first and learn on your own customers. It costs more per deployment at the start and less overall, because the structural mistakes get made on somebody else's time.

AWS built its partner programme on exactly this pattern: its engineers embedded with partner teams, then support shifting to helping the partner scale their own practice, then the partner working independently. Capability transfer as the design goal rather than a courtesy.

How to write the capability transfer into the first engagement. Name it as a deliverable alongside the production system, not as a hope. Ask the firm to document the decisions it made and why, not only the code it wrote, since the decisions are what your future team needs to repeat. Send at least one of your own future hires to shadow the engagement if you can, even before that person's role exists formally, because watching a real deployment teaches more than any handover document written after the fact.

What this sequencing costs you that building first does not. You pay a higher rate per unit of time for the first one or two deployments, since an outcome-based engagement prices in the firm's judgment and risk. That premium buys you the avoided cost of making the structural mistakes yourself, on your own customers, at your own expense in credibility. Whether that trade is worth it depends on how much a stumbled first deployment would cost you in a real relationship: for a strategic account, usually a great deal.

Common questions

What are the ways to get forward deployed engineering capability?

Three. Build the function by hiring your own embedded engineers, staff individual engineers through a partner who work under your direction, or engage a firm that takes the outcome. They differ mainly in who carries the delivery risk and how much control you keep over the approach.

Which route is fastest?

Staffing gets a person working soonest, with engaging a firm close behind. Building is slowest by a hiring cycle plus two or three engagements of learning, because the first engagements are where you discover what generalises and how long production takes at your kind of customer.

What is the most common mistake when buying this?

Buying an outcome from a vendor while directing the work yourself, which gives you the control of neither model. The related version is engaging a firm on time and materials with a description that sounds outcome-based, so nobody actually carries the delivery risk.

Should we build our own team eventually?

If embedded delivery is how you sell, yes. The sequence that works better than hiring first is engaging a firm for the first one or two deployments with a capability transfer written in, watching how they run it, then hiring using what you learned. The structural mistakes get made on somebody else's time.

Is there a precedent for capability transfer as a model?

AWS's partner programme is built on it: AWS engineers embedded with partner teams, then support shifting toward helping the partner scale their own practice, then the partner working independently. Capability transfer is the stated design goal rather than a courtesy at the end.

When is none of the three the right answer?

When your scope is writable and your technical leadership is strong, in which case you want senior engineers and should pay staffing prices rather than outcome prices. And when your accounts are too small to pay for an engineer's loaded cost, which no vendor arrangement fixes.

When forward deployed engineering is the wrong answer

Forward deployed engineering is expensive and it is often the wrong choice. It does not fit when your contract values cannot pay for an engineer's time, when every customer wants the same thing, when you hold a fixed product opinion you will not change, or when the scope is something you can already write down. Each of those has a cheaper alternative that works better. There is also one failure mode that disqualifies the model after you have already started: the engagement with no end date, which has stopped being a deployment and become a dependency.

How to choose a forward deployed engineering partner

Judge a forward deployed engineering partner on five things, in this order: which of the three jobs their engineers actually do, whether they will put a production date and a handover date in writing, what proves the code correct, how they decline work that only serves one customer, and who is accountable by name. A vendor who will not name a handover date is selling a dependency. A vendor with no answer on declining customisation will say yes to everything, and everything they say yes to becomes something you maintain.

Questions to ask a forward deployed engineering vendor

Twelve questions, each with the answer you want and the answer that should worry you. Five of them come from a16z partner Marc Andrusko, who wrote them as tests for investors evaluating companies using this model; they work at least as well from the buyer's side. The most revealing question on the list is how the vendor systematically declines customisation requests, because a vendor with no answer will say yes to everything and you will maintain all of it.

How to prepare your team for an embedded engineer

Three things have to exist before an embedded engineer starts, and all three are yours to provide: access set up and tested, one named person who can remove obstacles, and one person with the authority to decline scope. The third is the one everybody forgets, and its absence is why engagements grow without control. Beyond that, the difference between a fast engagement and a slow one is mostly about how much context you share and how completely you include the person. Nobody writes this page, and it decides more outcomes than the vendor's methodology does.

Security and access for embedded engineers

Letting an outside engineer into your systems is a real risk and it is manageable with four rules: least privilege by default, repository access scoped to the repositories the work touches, production access as a named exception with an audit trail and an end date, and revocation on the handover date rather than whenever somebody remembers. What changes when a model writes the code is that the review step before production matters more, not less, because generated code is not secure by default.
