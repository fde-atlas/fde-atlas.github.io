FDE: The Structural Role for the Last Mile of AI Implementation—From Capability Model to Platform Practice

In May 2026, OpenAI partnered with TPG and McKinsey to establish an independent Deployment Company with an initial investment of $4 billion; Anthropic joined forces with Blackstone and Goldman Sachs to inject $1.5 billion. Both companies point to the same role—FDE (Forward Deployed Engineer). According to Business Insider, the number of FDE-related positions in the US surged from 643 to 5,330 within a year, a year-over-year increase of 729%.

This is not a cyclical fluctuation in the job market, but a clear structural signal from the AI industry chain: the bottleneck has shifted from model capability to deployment and implementation, and the FDE is precisely the key role bridging this gap.

The FDE role was first created by Palantir Technologies in 2003. Facing customers in US intelligence and defense—where requirements were written in classified documents and processes were hidden behind security clearances—Palantir sent engineers directly to customer sites to work side by side with users. The military term "Forward Deployed" originated from this.

Bob McGrew, former head of product engineering at Palantir and later Chief Revenue Officer at OpenAI, gave the classic definition:

"A Forward Deployed Engineer is a technical person stationed at the customer site, responsible for bridging the gap between product capabilities and customer needs."

Three elements are non-negotiable: on-site embedding rather than remote access; bridging the gap between product and reality rather than selling standard solutions; delivering working software rather than consulting reports. An FDE is not a traditional software engineer, Sales Engineer, or consultant, but a structural fusion of all three in the AI era.

Today, Anthropic's FDE positions require mastery of the agent-era technology stack, including the MCP protocol, sub-agent orchestration, and Agent Skills; OpenAI's FDE team leads strategic customers through end-to-end production deployment of frontier models. The FDE has evolved from an internal role at Palantir into a core role for scaling AI implementation across the industry.

The work of an FDE should be driven primarily by building knowledge and experience and realizing their value, covering at least three dimensions:

  • Process dimension: Mapping out workflows in the business domain to identify opportunities for AI value extraction—end-to-end output enhancement, node consolidation and automation gains, and lowering skill barriers for roles;
  • Data dimension: Building tools, processes, and mechanisms for continuously accumulating explicit knowledge and experience, converting tacit frontline experience into reusable organizational assets;
  • Organization and people dimension: Continuously expanding the AI user base and depth of adoption within the customer organization through influence, education, and advocacy.

These three dimensions interact and iterate, forming a flywheel through which FDEs drive value growth.

However, this capability model spans four dimensions—business domain knowledge, AI technical mastery, knowledge asset construction, and organizational advocacy—and in reality it is nearly impossible for one person to perfectly combine them all. The FDE is essentially a "tip of the spear" role oriented toward a specific business domain—to maximize its effectiveness, it must be systematically supported by a team, platform, and tooling system behind it.

This is exactly the question that current industry practice most needs to answer: What kind of platform and tools does an FDE need to truly become "sharp"?

Elastic supplementation of role capabilities. The platform requires collaboration among four teams: a business domain expert team providing deep industry knowledge, an AI technology expert team handling large model tuning and engineering optimization, a tool customization team responding to frontline personalized needs, and a training enablement team helping customers build AI usage capabilities.

Infrastructure for the toolchain. An FDE needs at minimum: a knowledge base (organizational knowledge asset storage and retrieval), customizable Agent clients (adaptable to different customer scenarios), a skill and Agent repository (reusable agent capability modules), a tool repository (standardized external tool integrations), and an Agent workflow engine (orchestration and execution of complex business processes).

Reaching consensus on the philosophy is easy, but turning these capabilities into an operational platform still requires concrete engineering practice.

Agent MCP Warehouse (mcp.smartmoves.com.cn) starts from answering a fundamental question: What tools should the FDE, this "tip of the spear" role, be equipped with to truly be effective? What it attempts to build is a reusable FDE tooling paradigm—packaging domain expert knowledge into standardized Agent skill assets, distributing them via a universal protocol to various AI clients, so that FDEs can install and use them on the spot at customer sites.

To validate the feasibility of this paradigm, the team chose software engineering as the first practice scenario, building three types of Agents—BA (Business Analyst), PM (Project Manager), and SA (System Architect)—as examples. But it must be emphasized: these specific Agents are merely concrete instantiations of the paradigm in a particular domain, not the platform's goal itself. Switching to other domains such as finance, healthcare, or manufacturing only requires replacing the Agent skill packs for the corresponding domain; the underlying paradigm remains exactly the same.

Under the traditional model, expert experience in any domain—whether a BA's requirements analysis methodology, a financial risk officer's compliance review framework, or manufacturing process optimization knowledge—is essentially an individual skill, dependent on accumulated experience, hard to replicate, and even harder to scale. Agent MCP Warehouse's approach is to structure this domain expert knowledge into standardized Skill definition files (SKILL.md), turning them into organizational-level digital assets.

DimensionTraditional modelSkill assetization model
Knowledge carrierIndividual experience, passed on orallyStructured Skill definition files
Reuse methodDepends on specific individuals' involvementAny AI agent can install and use it immediately
Quality assuranceVaries by person, hard to auditStandardized output templates + gate rules
Evolution pathExperience iterates in individuals' mindsVersioned Skill management, continuous organizational-level optimization

Each Skill definition file is an executable expert knowledge contract, precisely describing trigger conditions (which scenarios activate the skill), execution flow (phased progressive advancement strategy and gate rules), input/output contracts (what context is needed, what standardized assets are produced), and quality standards (built-in validation rules and compliance checks).

This means that a domain methodology accumulated by a senior expert over ten years can be packaged into a Skill, allowing every member of the team to invoke it instantly via an AI agent and produce professional deliverables of equivalent quality. This logic applies to any domain requiring expert knowledge.

Skill definitions are connected to various AI agent tools via the MCP (Model Context Protocol). MCP is the standard protocol connecting AI clients with backend Agent services, enabling package once, distribute everywhere.

No matter which AI client the FDE uses—OpenClaw, Hermes, Workbuddy, Qoder IDE, or others—the same set of Skill assets works plug-and-play. Skills are no longer bound to specific tools or individuals, but become organizational infrastructure. This is especially critical for FDEs: when facing different customers and domains, FDEs do not need to repeatedly set up tool environments; they simply load the corresponding domain's Skill pack in the client to get to work.

As the first implementation validation of this paradigm, Agent MCP Warehouse has built three Master Agents in the software engineering domain, demonstrating how domain expert knowledge can be systematically packaged and distributed:

SA Master AgentSystems ArchitectSystem technical architecture design, interface specification documents, deployment implementation guides, detailed design reviews

Taking the BA Master Agent as an example, it strictly follows gate rules and progresses through four stages: "root purpose discovery → domain knowledge loading → three-dimensional requirements clarification → structured output." During the requirements clarification stage, it conducts multiple rounds of structured dialogue with the client like an experienced BA: first uncovering the project's fundamental purpose, then loading industry domain knowledge, then digging into requirement details scenario by scenario, checking 4A architecture constraints, and sorting out outstanding issues, ultimately producing a complete requirements specification covering scenario analysis, process descriptions, data entities, and system boundaries.

In business process modeling, the BA Master Agent can automatically produce four diagrams/tables for each scenario: swimlane diagrams (role collaboration relationships), state machine diagrams (state transitions), sequence diagrams (interaction order), and decision tables (rule matrices), converting textual rules into visualized processes. On the compliance dimension, it scans the PRD across four dimensions—data security, financial security, business compliance, and technical compliance—and outputs a structured review report.

These capabilities demonstrate how the paradigm works: expert knowledge in a given domain (software engineering here) is standardized and packaged through Skill definition files, enabling an FDE to produce expert-level deliverables with the help of Agents without needing to be a deep expert in every domain.

Chaining these capabilities together, an FDE's workflow with the Agent MCP Warehouse can be simplified to:

  • Load the skill: Enter "please load this skill" in an AI client (such as OpenClaw) and provide the Skill package path, completing the deployment of the corresponding domain Agent;
  • Start working: Enter the corresponding command (e.g., /ba-master) and hand the raw materials provided by the client to the Agent;
  • Progressive advancement: The Agent guides the FDE through the full process according to the domain methodology—uncovering the root purpose, loading domain knowledge, deepening scenario by scenario, checking constraints, reviewing outstanding issues, and more;
  • Structured output: Automatically produce the full set of standardized deliverables for that domain.

The core value of this workflow is: the FDE can focus on business understanding and client communication, while handing structured analysis, modeling, and document production over to Agents. When the domain switches from software engineering to financial risk control, healthcare compliance, or manufacturing process optimization, only the corresponding Skill package needs to be replaced—the workflow paradigm remains entirely unchanged.

The practice of the Agent MCP Warehouse reveals a key design principle for FDE tooling: it is not about teaching the FDE more tools, but about letting the tools carry more domain expert knowledge. When knowledge is packaged as executable Skill assets, the FDE's role reverts from a "do-everything generalist" to the "tip of the spear"—focused on on-site business understanding, requirements judgment, and organizational drive, while standardized analysis and outputs are handed to Agents.

More importantly, this paradigm is universally applicable across domains. The BA/PM/SA Agents in software engineering are only the first validated case; the underlying model of "knowledge packaging → standard protocol distribution → plug-and-play across endpoints" can be replicated to any FDE scenario requiring expert knowledge. This "human + Agent" collaboration model is precisely the necessary path for FDEs to move from individual heroics to scaled delivery.

The explosion of FDE roles marks a paradigm shift in the AI industry from a "model race" to a "deployment race." As model capabilities become homogenized, what truly differentiates players is who can embed AI into business processes and generate measurable value.

The practice of the Agent MCP Warehouse shows that FDE tooling is not an abstract concept but can be implemented as a concrete engineering system of Skill assetization, MCP protocol distribution, and Agent collaboration workflows. Its value lies not only in the BA/PM/SA examples from software engineering, but in validating a universally applicable cross-domain paradigm: packaging expert knowledge as assets, distributing it via standard protocols, and enabling FDEs to use it plug-and-play on the client side—this may well be the most pragmatic infrastructure for the "last mile" of AI deployment.
