AI Adoption at Companies Without an IT Department: Separating the Decision-Maker from the Hands-On Implementer

Original title: 情シスがいない会社のAI導入｜決める人と手を動かす人を分ける

株式会社Sai · 2026-10-13 · 24 min read

Summary

Sai, a Japanese company without an IT department, argues that firms adopting AI should first separate the decider from the doer. The decider—an executive or business owner—sets rules on what information must never be entered and holds final stop authority; the doer can be external. Citing IPA's DX Trends 2026 (IT heads lead AI adoption at 46.0%, only 2.9% of firms have a CAIO) and its own Claude Code website operations, the author says on-site accompaniment-style support (FDE) fits better than fixed-spec contracting, and in-housing should start by naming an operations owner before expanding scope.

Key points

  • The decider must be an executive with authority to halt actions.
  • Only 2.9% of surveyed firms have a CAIO (IPA DX Trends 2026).
  • On-site accompaniment beats deliver-and-done contracting for firms without IT staff.
  • Sai keeps only publishing, sending, retitling, and payment as human checkpoints.
  • For in-housing, name the operations owner first, then widen scope.

Key Takeaways

  • In AI adoption at companies without an IT department, the first step is to separate the decision-maker from the hands-on person: the decision-maker, who is an executive or a business owner, holds the line on what information must not be used and the authority to stop things, while the hands-on person does not need to be in-house.
  • According to IPA's DX Trends 2026, the leadership structure for AI adoption and utilization is led by the head of the IT department at 46.0%, the highest, followed by management at 32.1% and frontline supervisors at 27.5%; companies with a CAIO (Chief AI Officer) account for only 2.9%, and dedicated staff other than a CAIO for 9.8% (accessed October 4, 2026).
  • For the hands-on role placed externally, a model that starts by identifying what to build and accompanies the team until it is used in the field, then hands over to someone who continues operations, suits workplaces without an IT department better than conventional contracting, which ends at delivery against fixed requirements.
  • In our case, we run our website with Claude Code without an IT department, separating rules into instruction files, procedures into operations documents, and decision records into a ledger, leaving only publishing, sending, retitling, and payments to humans; all three production deployments from September 24 to 26, 2026 were blocked by the predefined mechanisms.
  • This approach does not suit companies where no decision-maker is established, or where the IT department's role is entirely delegated to a specific person; the order for moving toward in-house capability is to first decide who will continue operations, then expand the scope of what is built.

Some companies have no IT department box on their org chart, yet the approval process for AI tools moves forward first. What is missing from the approval document is a single line stating the job titles of the person who decides what information must not be used and the person who makes the final stop. This article presents a role table separating the decision-maker and the hands-on person, along with two models for placing the hands-on role externally, accompanied by a record of our own site operations without an IT department.

AI adoption at companies without an IT department means advancing adoption by separating the decision-maker and the hands-on person inside and outside the company

AI adoption at companies without an IT department refers to AI adoption advanced by companies that do not have an information systems department (IT department), separating the decision-maker and the hands-on person inside and outside the company. The decision-maker holds the line on what information must not be used and the final authority to stop things, while the hands-on person handles three tasks: building, fixing, and continuing operations. In companies with an IT department, these two roles are divided within the department, but in companies without one, they start with product comparison before separating the roles, leaving the question of who stops things blank.

The smaller the company and the harder it is to maintain an IT department, the slower the progress, according to surveys. The surveys do not break down results by the presence or absence of an IT department; this is our reading of the figures by employee count. According to IPA's "DX Trends 2026" (FY2025 survey), 75.7% of all companies are working on DX in some form, but only 41.6% of companies with 100 or fewer employees, showing a continuing polarization by company size. In the same survey, 42.3% of companies have "adopted" AI. "Currently conducting trial use" stands at 15.7%, meaning 58.0% of companies in total have adopted or are trialing AI in some form (IPA "DX Trends 2026: Expanding AI Adoption, Can DX Change?" (PDF) / accessed October 4, 2026).

Looking at who drives adoption on the advanced side, the IT department stands out. In the same survey, the leadership structure for AI adoption and utilization was highest for "head of the IT department" at 46.0%, followed by "management" at 32.1% and "supervisors of departments adopting or utilizing AI" at 27.5%. Companies with a "CAIO (Chief AI Officer)" account for only 2.9% and "dedicated staff other than a CAIO" for 9.8%; IPA notes that few companies have a structure that professionally leads AI utilization (IPA "DX Trends 2026: Expanding AI Adoption, Can DX Change?" (PDF) / accessed October 4, 2026). Since the most common model is IT department-led, companies without an IT department cannot simply copy it. Among the remaining models, the one led by management corresponds to what this article calls placing the decision-maker role with an executive or business owner.

The decision-maker is an executive or business owner, and the hands-on person does not need to be in-house

The decision-maker is an executive or business owner, and the hands-on person does not need to be in-house. The basis for this division lies in two things: descriptions in public surveys and the way guidelines require responsible persons to be appointed.

More small and medium-sized enterprises now have policies in place. In the Ministry of Internal Affairs and Communications' "Information and Communications White Paper, Reiwa 8 Edition" FY2025 corporate survey, the combined percentage of respondents in Japan answering that they have a policy to "actively use generative AI" or "use it in limited areas" was 68.9% (49.7% in the FY2024 survey). By company size, it was 74.0% for large companies and 58.1% for SMEs, with a particularly large rise among SMEs (MIC "Information and Communications White Paper, Reiwa 8 Edition, Part I, Chapter 2, Section 1 (1): Policies for Using Generative AI" / accessed October 4, 2026). Even with a policy in place, who decides and who acts does not appear in the policy text.

The same white paper looks at the "who decides" part. Among Japanese SMEs, the most common answer regarding generative AI strategy and policy was "management presents implementation items and achievement targets to each department," and the Ministry of Internal Affairs and Communications sees top-down involvement as the key point for AI adoption by SMEs (MIC "Reiwa 8 Edition White Paper on Information and Communications, Part I, Chapter 2, Section 1 (2) Organizational Initiatives" / accessed October 4, 2026). Across Japanese companies overall, "the purpose and policy of AI use are stated within the business strategy" was highest, while in the US, Germany, and China, answers such as "management presents implementation items and achievement targets to each department" and "the department or person responsible for promoting initiatives is clearly defined" were also prominent (same page / accessed October 4, 2026).

Placing the decision-maker with management or the business owner also aligns with the guidelines' positioning. The MIC and METI "AI Guidelines for Business (Version 1.2)" state that it is important for business execution responsible parties, including management, to consider and practice risk countermeasures when using AI as part of their business strategy, based on the "basic philosophy" (why) and guidelines (what), and to promote the safe and secure use of AI (as stated as of October 4, 2026. MIC and METI "AI Guidelines for Business (Version 1.2), Main Text" (PDF) / accessed October 4, 2026). In the guidelines' "Common Guidelines," 7) Accountability requires, under "③ Clarifying the responsible person," that each entity designate a person responsible for fulfilling accountability, and under "④ Allocation of responsibilities among parties," that the location of responsibility be clarified through contracts between entities, social commitments, and the like (same PDF / as stated as of October 4, 2026). The responsible person is placed internally, and responsibility for outsourced parts is divided by contract. These two points are the premise for whether the hands-on people are placed inside or outside the company.

We can say the hands-on people do not need to be in-house because the "person in charge" is the name of a role, not a headcount. We think of the roles as divided into three: implementation, operations, and security. The implementation role drives everything from concept through requirements definition, design and development, testing, and production deployment, while the operations role handles daily maintenance in addition to creating usage rules and communicating them to users. In the division used in this article, implementation work is the part that can be outsourced, and the operations role is the part that stays in-house.

There are also descriptions of companies progressing without dedicated staff. IPA's discussion paper "Considerations on DX Promotion in Japanese SMEs" states that some SMEs achieve DX results even without executives knowledgeable in IT or specialized departments, and cites, as an example from the 2023 edition of the White Paper on SMEs and Small Enterprises, an SME without dedicated system staff that achieved business efficiency through digitalization in collaboration with a system development company. The paper states that the presence of "executives knowledgeable in IT" and promotion structures such as "a department for DX promotion or strengthening digital business" show no notable relationship with DX results, and that companies may achieve results through measures such as external collaboration even when they cannot establish a sufficient implementation structure (IPA "Survey and Analysis Discussion Paper 2023-02: Considerations on DX Promotion in Japanese SMEs" (PDF) / accessed October 4, 2026, authors' views). The absence of dedicated staff may be a reason to place the hands-on people externally, but it is not a reason to leave the decision-maker vacant.

The first things to decide are the line defining what information must not be entered, and the person who stops things.

The first things the decision-maker must decide are two: the line defining what information must not be entered, and the person who stops things. These two must be decided before choosing a product because neither can be decided on the hands-on side.

Without a line, each user has no choice but to judge for themselves what they may enter. We believe that as a result, incidents such as entering personal information, or giving up on use because the usage is unclear, become more likely. The AI Guidelines for Business, in Part 5 "Matters Concerning AI Users," U-5) Implementation of Security Measures, lists paying attention to comply with security-related points noted by AI providers and ensuring that confidential information and the like is not improperly entered into AI systems and services, and U-2) requires using AI within the scope assumed by the AI provider in its design (as stated as of October 4, 2026. MIC and METI "AI Guidelines for Business (Version 1.2), Main Text" (PDF) / accessed October 4, 2026). Deciding what counts as confidential information is the responsibility of the company using it, and this is the decision-maker's first task.

We narrow the information that must not be entered to three categories: credentials, customer names, and undisclosed deal terms. Credentials means authentication information such as passwords and API keys. The types of things that must not be written are placed in the instruction file, and whether an operation is allowed or requires confirmation is separated by permission settings. How to set the line and where to place it are written in our article on using generative AI for internal knowledge, and how to create internal rules on what may be entered is summarized in how to create internal rules for generative AI use. Even without an IT department, management or the business owner can set this line simply by looking at the types of documents in their company.

The person who stops things is designated by job title. According to the MIC white paper, the most common initiative in Japan to prevent inappropriate use of generative AI was "establishing company-wide policies and guidelines" at 41.1%, while in the other three countries, specialized review systems at the planning and implementation stages and regular post-implementation evaluation and verification tended to be more common than in Japan (MIC "Reiwa 8 Edition White Paper on Information and Communications, Part I, Chapter 2, Section 1 (4) Concerning Risks and Initiatives for Risk Countermeasures" / accessed October 4, 2026). Writing a policy and placing a person who stops things are separate tasks, and a policy alone does not stop operations. In IPA's survey as well, the most common challenge in AI implementation and operation was "lack of specialized personnel" at 50.1%, followed by "insufficient understanding of the effects and risks of generative AI" at 45.8% and "difficulty creating rules and standards for managing appropriate use" at 39.7%. IPA organizes these into three major challenges: personnel shortage, literacy shortage, and delayed governance development (IPA "DX Trends 2026: Expanding AI Adoption, Can DX Change?" (PDF) / accessed October 4, 2026).

In our records, the first line that stopped things was drawn not by a person but by a tool's default mechanisms. The Claude Code permission list counted on September 27, 2026 showed allow 78, deny 0, ask 0, having grown from 9 at the site build in June 2026 with each approval and never having been inventoried. Production deployments, all three times from September 24 to 26, 2026, were stopped by the default mechanisms, and the representative executed them personally (permission design to prevent AI agent runaway). A pattern where permissions only increase without designating a person who stops things by job title occurs regardless of whether there is an IT department. From this record, we learned that we need to decide in advance, under three names, which operations require confirmation: production deployment, external transmission, and operations involving credentials and accounts.

The model of placing hands-on workers externally fits not the build-and-hand-off form of contracting, but the form of entering the field and working alongside the team.

There are two forms of placing hands-on workers externally, and for companies without an in-house IT department, the form of entering the field and working alongside the team fits better than build-and-hand-off contracting. The reason it fits is that in companies without an IT department, the external party needs to see the process through to deciding who will continue operations in-house and handing over after the build.

Contract development takes confirmed requirements, builds on them, and is complete upon delivery. If the requirements are fixed and an off-the-shelf service suffices for the problem, this form is enough. On our FDE Accompaniment Development page, we define FDE (Forward Deployed Engineer) as a form of support in which engineers enter the customer's field and handle everything from problem discovery through implementation, operational adoption, and in-housing as one continuous effort, and we state that we start by identifying "what should be built" and accompany the work until it is actually used in the field. It suits problems whose requirements have not yet solidified; for large-scale development with finalized specifications or problems where off-the-shelf SaaS suffices, conventional options are appropriate.

The working-alongside form fits because we believe implementation and operation are separate jobs. We view documenting the settings and their background, along with usage rules and manuals, as work essential to operations. In the build-and-hand-off form, the ordering side must decide in advance who will keep writing the documentation of settings background and the operational manuals.

On our service page, we state that in the field-entry form, the goal is to build alongside your members, transfer skills, put documentation and an operational structure in place, and reach a state where improvement cycles run on your own. The same page also describes an approach of narrowing the initial target to one workflow and one team and expanding the scope while observing how it is used. This form fits companies without an IT department because, as long as the person responsible for continuing operations is designated by title, the handover process is included in the scope of support even when starting before that person knows how to use it. Even in our published case of supporting a company's business continuity plan (BCP), the sequence was training tied directly to the work, embedding it into daily operations, and accompanying the staff until they could improve usage themselves and spread it. The same service page covers how to sort problems that fit from those that don't, and the in-housing roadmap covers the thinking behind the stage of breaking free from outsourcing dependence.

The conditions that let us continue operating our site without an IT department were putting rules into documents and leaving checkpoints to people

The conditions that let us continue operating our website without an IT department were two: putting rules into documents and leaving checkpoints to people. This record covers a single episode of operating our own site from June to October 2026, and is not a proven track record of effectiveness.

We are also a company without an IT department, and we use Claude Code (an agent-type tool that runs in a terminal) for website operations. What we delegate is seven tasks: article outlines, text checking, structured data audits, search data aggregation, post preparation, drafting of schedule coordination, and video export. The decisions we leave to people are four: publishing, sending, retitling, and payment. The contents of the seven tasks and a two-column table are in our article on improving work efficiency with Claude Code. Mapped onto that article's role table, the tool handles the "build and fix" column, while the representative holds the "line-drawing and stop decisions" column. The stop decisions in the table are publishing, sending, and payment, but we leave four to people by adding retitling of pages that appear in search results.

The first condition was putting rules into documents. For handing over delegated work, we split rules into instruction files, procedures into operational documents, and decision records into ledgers; the instruction files contain only writing-style rules and the types of things that must not be written. In early September 2026, we found that the memory we had Claude Code read for site operations, the operational documents, and conversations each separately recorded a list of the representative's tasks with no authoritative source, so we re-designated the task list and editorial standards as the authoritative sources per topic and changed the memory's role to an index pointing to where the authoritative sources live (using generative AI for internal knowledge). When rules are in documents, the same lines apply whether the hands-on work is done by a person, a tool, or an external party. In a company without an IT department, if the rules exist only in a particular person's head, then on days when that person is absent, no one can consult for line-drawing decisions.

The second condition was leaving checkpoints to people. On September 17, 2026, the representative read a post body that had passed automated checks and held it back for quality reasons, stopping an auto-generated body. The three times the production deployment was stopped by a default mechanism are as written in the line-drawing section, and on a day when images could not be prepared due to the generation service's limits, we stopped publishing and decided thereafter not to publish articles without images. Records of these stops exist because we had placed places where a person looks just before publishing, sending, retitling, and payment.

In our records, the person deciding and the person doing the hands-on work were the same individual; what we separated was not people but where rules live and where things stop. Even if a tool takes on the hands-on role, the two columns of the role table hold as long as line-drawing and stop decisions are placed on the side of documents and settings. When a company without an IT department asks an external party to do hands-on work, writing out these two columns first makes clear what to delegate and what to keep in-house.

What doesn't fit is a company where the decision-maker is not designated, or a form that delegates the entire IT-department substitute to a specific person in charge

In a company where the decision-maker is not designated, or in a form that delegates the entire IT-department substitute to a specific person in charge, the left column of the role table goes empty. In the former, no one owns the line-drawing and stop decisions; in the latter, the owner gets absorbed into the hands-on side.

In a company where the decision-maker is not designated, even if hands-on workers are brought in from outside, things proceed with the line-drawing and stop decisions left blank. In an IPA survey, the reasons companies with 101 to 300 employees were not undertaking DX — "lack of knowledge and information for undertaking DX," "shortage of personnel to plan and oversee DX strategy," and "shortage of personnel to promote and execute DX in the field" — all scored high. IPA organizes this as the problem of having the willingness to act but no personnel to carry it (IPA "DX Trends 2026: Expanding AI Adoption — Can DX Change?" (PDF) / accessed October 4, 2026). The combined share answering "somewhat insufficient" or "significantly insufficient" for the "quantity" of DX-promoting personnel exceeds 90% for companies with 301 or more employees and exceeds 60% even for companies with 100 or fewer, at 85.5% overall (same PDF / accessed October 4, 2026). Since the shortage spans all sizes, if the decision-maker role is left vacant on the grounds of insufficient people, the line-drawing will remain empty until the day personnel are in place.

Once the decision-maker is set, you have a partner for collaboration. According to an IPA survey, the combined share of companies answering "fully achieved" or "somewhat achieved" for collaboration among management, IT departments, and business departments was 72.9% among companies where DX was "producing results" and 37.1% among those where it was not, and IPA states that inter-departmental collaboration is strongly related to DX outcomes (same PDF / accessed October 4, 2026). In companies without an IT department, collaboration becomes a two-party arrangement between management and the business department, and if it is not decided which of the two is the decision-maker, the form of collaboration cannot be settled either. In the Ministry of Internal Affairs and Communications white paper, 27.0% of Japanese companies—about 30%—answered that there were "no organizational initiatives" regarding business transformation through generative AI use, notably higher than in the US, Germany, or China, and by company size the tendency was especially high among SMEs (Ministry of Internal Affairs and Communications, "Reiwa 8 Edition, Information and Communications White Paper, Part I, Chapter 2, Section 1, (2) Status of Organizational Initiatives" / accessed October 4, 2026).

The reason the form of delegating everything to a single person in charge does not work is that the three roles of implementation, operation, and security plus the decision-making judgment all end up in one person's head, leaving no one to confirm a stop judgment. We consider these three to be names of roles, not headcounts. Holding multiple roles can work, but if the person who draws the boundaries and the person who does the hands-on work within those boundaries are the same, no one notices when a line is crossed. Our example in the previous section takes this form, and if one person bears it all, a stopping point must be placed outside the person; what does not work is the form where even that external mechanism is left to the person in charge and management holds no boundaries. For the person placed in place of an IT department, hand over the rows of building, fixing, and continuing operation, while the rows of boundary-drawing and stop judgment should remain with management or the business manager—that is how to read the role table.

The order for moving to in-house development is to first decide who will continue operations, then expand the scope of building

The order for moving to in-house development is to first decide who will continue operations, then expand the scope of building. If you expand the scope of building first, you will need someone to operate everything that gets built.

The person who continues operations is the role that creates rules for use and communicates them to users. Since this role remains even after implementation is complete, decide it first under an internal job title, even if you outsource. In the in-house development roadmap we wrote three stages—co-creation, transfer, and self-reliance—but in companies without an IT department, the name of the person who continues operations comes before these three stages.

The order is to start by picking one task you repeat every week, having existing documents read, and producing a draft; moving to scheduled execution comes only after a single task has run manually a few times (article on improving work efficiency with Claude Code). Continuing operations means continuously adding to the rules; at our company we add only corrections that come up repeatedly to the article's specifications, and update the representative's to-do list as the authoritative source per topic (use of generative AI for internal knowledge). Expanding the scope of building comes after this path of additions starts running.

The same order applies even when you place hands-on people externally. Our FDE accompanying development page assumes an approach that narrows the initial target to one business process and one team, observes how it is used, and then expands the scope, and states that deciding adoption metrics in advance makes it easier to judge whether to continue or expand. Deciding whether to continue or stop on the evaluation date is the job of the decision-maker in the role table, and if the metrics used for that evaluation are decided at the stage of the first business process, the timing of the move to in-house development can also be read with the same metrics.

Frequently asked questions about AI adoption in companies without an IT department

Q. If we have no IT department, should we hire one first?

The order is to decide the decision-maker, the boundaries of information that must not be entered, and the stopper before hiring. Even if you hire an IT department, boundary-drawing and stop judgments remain with management or the business manager, and what passes to the hired person is the hands-on side of the role table. A stopper is needed even before the hire is settled, so either way, the decision-making side's work comes first.

Q. Does the decision-maker have to be management?

A business manager can fill the role, but it must be someone with the authority to stop things right before publication or transmission. If the person who sets boundaries and the stopper are different positions, the place to return to when something stops splits in two. We write that the decision-maker should be management or the business manager because these are the two positions where boundary decisions and stop authority land in the same place.

Q. Can we have the outsourcing vendor decide the boundaries of information that must not be entered?

The judgment of what counts as confidential belongs to the user and cannot be handed to the vendor. What the vendor can handle is limited to the work of turning the boundaries into settings and documents, and the work of counting together what kinds of documents exist in the company. If you hand the boundaries outside, the person who notices when a line is crossed also goes outside.

Q. What does the stopper stop?

Operations that become irreversible the moment they go outside. At our company we keep four things with humans—publication, transmission, retitling, and payment—and place one point where a person checks immediately before each. The same applies immediately before input; the boundaries of information that must not be entered only function in operation when there is a stopper before input.

SAI's perspective: even on an org chart with no IT department box, you can write the stopper's name

Our own org chart has no IT department box either. Even so, rereading our records, the last person to act in each instance where something stopped was the representative, meaning the stopper's name could have been written on the org chart. In consultations with companies without an IT department, the first thing we look at is not product candidates but which box on the org chart will hold the stopper's name.

Even without an IT department box, the stopper's name can be written in one of these boxes.

Look at whether you can write "the last person to stop in AI adoption" into one of your existing boxes, and the first decision comes into view. Even if the box is not yet decided, tell us your situation via contact.

If you want to start from a boundary document, AI adoption support is the entry point; if you want to place hands-on people externally, FDE accompanying development is the entry point, and the choice of entry point is decided after a name is entered in the decision-maker's box.
