What Is the Difference Between a Forward Deployed Engineer, a Software Engineer and a Solutions Engineer?

A software engineer builds the core product for all customers, a solutions engineer helps win the deal before the sale, and a forward deployed engineer makes the product work inside one customer's environment after the sale. The three jobs overlap in skills but differ in where they work, who they talk to and what they are measured on.

This guide compares the three roles side by side, shows how they work together, and helps you decide which one your AI product needs first. If you already know you need a deployment engineer, see our page on hiring forward deployed engineers. If you are still sorting out your core build, start with hiring an AI engineer.

For a plain definition of the role, read what an AI engineering partner is first and then come back here for the comparison.

If you only remember one thing: the roles are separated by the moment in the customer's journey they own. Solutions engineers own the moment before the deal. Software engineers own the product between deals. Forward deployed engineers own the moment after the deal, when value is created or lost.

What Does a Software Engineer Do That an FDE Does Not?

A software engineer designs and builds the product that every customer uses. They think in terms of one codebase, a roadmap, test coverage, performance and long-term maintenance. Their customer is an average of all customers.

A typical week includes roadmap features, code reviews, bug fixes from the support queue and infrastructure work. They rarely meet a single customer, and when they do, it is usually through a product manager.

That focus is a strength. Engineers who are interrupted by every customer request cannot ship a clean product. This is exactly why companies add FDEs: to keep customer-specific work away from the core team.

What Does a Solutions Engineer Do That an FDE Does Not?

A solutions engineer, sometimes called a sales engineer, supports the sale. They run product demos, set up proofs of concept, answer security questionnaires and handle technical questions on sales calls. Their job is to remove doubt before the contract.

Once the deal closes, the solutions engineer usually moves to the next prospect. The customer then needs someone to do the real work of connecting, configuring and rolling out the product, and that is the gap an FDE fills.

Some companies blur the two jobs, and one person tries to demo and deploy. That works for a handful of customers and breaks as volume grows, because pre-sale and post-sale work compete for the same hours.

What Does a Forward Deployed Engineer Do That the Others Do Not?

An FDE works inside the customer's environment and owns the result. They map the customer's data, build the connectors to its CRM or warehouse, set up permissions, run evaluations on real data and train users. They are measured on whether people actually use the product.

They also carry the field back to the product team. When two customers need the same mapping, the FDE flags it, and a one-off becomes a reusable feature. That feedback loop is a large part of the role's value, and neither of the other two roles produces it in the same way.

The Pragmatic Engineer describes FDEs as a mix of platform engineering, software engineering and solutions engineering, which is a fair way to see why the role is hard to hire for.

What Does the Same Customer Request Look Like to Each Role?

Take one request: a hospital group wants an AI assistant that summarizes prior authorization cases for its nurses, using data from its case management system.

RoleWhat they do with the request
Solutions engineerRuns a demo on sample cases, answers the security and privacy questionnaire and helps the prospect trust the product enough to sign
Software engineerReceives a roadmap ticket weeks later to add a generic case management connector, and builds it for every customer
Forward deployed engineerSits with the nursing lead, maps the real case fields, builds and secures the connector, tests on real cases, trains the first nurse group and reports adoption

All three are doing valuable work. Only the third makes the hospital group's nurses use the tool.

How Do the Three Roles Work Together?

In a healthy company the roles hand off cleanly. The solutions engineer helps close the deal and passes a clear scope to the FDE. The FDE deploys and records what the customer needed. The software engineers turn repeated needs into product features.

  • Before the deal: solutions engineer qualifies the technical fit and sets realistic expectations.
  • At kickoff: the FDE agrees what "live" means with the customer and the product lead.
  • During rollout: the FDE builds and releases, and asks the core team for fixes only when the product itself needs to change.
  • After rollout: field notes feed the roadmap, and software engineers build the reusable version.

Problems start when one role is asked to do all three. Core engineers pulled into customer work slow the roadmap. Solutions engineers asked to deploy neglect the pipeline. Customers then wait, and pilots stall.

Which Role Should Your AI Product Hire First?

Hire the role that matches your current bottleneck. Most early AI companies need software engineers first, because there is no product without them. The FDE becomes the right hire when the product works but customers are not reaching production.

Choose a software engineer if:
- The core product is still being built or is unstable
- Requirements are clear and apply to every customer
- You need a reliable foundation more than customer rollouts

Choose a solutions engineer if:
- You have a working product and a pipeline of prospects
- Deals stall on technical doubt, demos or security reviews
- Your founders are doing every demo themselves

Choose a forward deployed engineer if:
- Signed customers are waiting on a rollout
- Pilots work in a demo but stall in the real environment
- Customer-specific integrations are pulling your core team off the roadmap

Can One Person Do All Three Jobs?

In the earliest stage, yes, and it is common. Founders and senior engineers demo, deploy and build at the same time. That is a reasonable start for the first few customers because every conversation teaches you something.

It stops working when customers overlap. Two rollouts at once, plus a new demo, plus a bug in the product, and something drops. The first sign is usually a rollout that slips by weeks because the person who owns it is busy elsewhere.

When that happens, split off the job that costs you the most. Often that is deployment, because delays there hold up revenue and referrals.

Can Engineers Move Between the Three Roles?

Yes, and the best FDEs often have a mix of backgrounds. Many come from software engineering and add customer skills, some come from solutions engineering and add deeper build skills, and a few come from consulting with strong coding.

When you hire, look at the path as well as the title. A software engineer who spent two years on a customer-facing integration team has done more FDE-style work than a senior engineer who never left the core codebase. A solutions engineer who built the proof-of-concept code, not only the slides, is a stronger candidate than one who only demoed.

Moving the other way is also common. Engineers who spend a year at customers often return to the core product team with a sharper sense of what users need. That is one reason companies rotate people through deployment work on purpose.

How Do the Costs Compare?

Compensation varies a lot by company and level, so check current data before you plan a budget. For forward deployed engineers specifically, Levels.fyi reports a median US total compensation of $225,000, with a 25th percentile of $172,000 and a 75th percentile of $320,000.

The bigger cost question is time to hire. A senior FDE can take months to recruit because the skill mix is rare. An outside team can start in days, which is why many companies test the role with a fixed pilot before making a permanent hire. The bottom line: A software engineer builds the product, a solutions engineer wins the deal, and a forward deployed engineer makes it work for one customer after the deal. Hire software engineers first, add a solutions engineer when technical doubt slows sales, and add an FDE when signed customers are waiting on rollouts.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the difference between a forward deployed engineer and a software engineer?

A software engineer builds the core product for all customers and rarely meets any one of them. A forward deployed engineer adapts and integrates the product inside one customer's environment, working directly with that customer's team.

What is the difference between a forward deployed engineer and a solutions engineer?

A solutions engineer supports the sale with demos, proofs of concept and technical answers before the contract. A forward deployed engineer works after the deal, writing production code and owning deployment until users are live.

Is a forward deployed engineer the same as a sales engineer?

No. A sales engineer works before the sale and mostly demonstrates the product. A forward deployed engineer works after the sale, builds in the customer's environment and is measured on adoption, not on winning the deal.

Is a forward deployed engineer a consultant?

Not quite. Consultants mainly advise and deliver documents. A forward deployed engineer writes and ships production code, then stays through go-live. The role has some consulting traits, but the output is working software.

Do forward deployed engineers write production code?

Yes. They write integrations, data pipelines and customer-specific features to production standards, and they own monitoring after release. Coding is only part of the week, with the rest spent on integration, meetings and adoption.

Which role should a startup hire first?

Software engineers first, because there is no product without them. Add a solutions engineer when deals stall on technical trust, and add an FDE when signed customers wait on rollouts that your core team cannot support.

Can one engineer cover all three roles?

In the first few customers, yes. As rollouts overlap, one person cannot demo, deploy and build well at the same time, and splitting off the costliest job, usually deployment, is the next step.
