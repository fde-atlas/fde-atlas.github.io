Backend to FDE: Skill Gaps and Interview Preparation

Original title: 后端转 FDE：技能差距与面试准备

FDE Times · 2026-10-12 · 5 min read

Key Takeaways

  • Two or more years of backend experience in production already covers most of the skills an FDE needs, with debugging and observability the most directly transferable.
  • The three main gaps are customer communication, understanding how AI systems behave, and scoping. Many candidates fail not because their code is bad, but because they do a poor job of technical requirements discovery.

Picture a Forward Deployed Engineer (FDE) interview. The interviewer poses a problem: a logistics company wants to use AI to handle customer complaint emails. If you're a backend engineer, your first instinct is probably to pick up a pen, draw a queue, pick a database, and sketch out the API.

That's exactly where many candidates stumble. A transition guide from fde.academy points out that backend engineers often fail at this stage because they rush into designing an architecture before clarifying the success criteria. Ironically, the same guide also says that anyone with two or more years of production backend experience already has most of the capabilities an FDE needs.

The gap between the two roles is smaller than many people think, and it falls precisely where backend engineers practice the least. Now is also a good time to close it: according to The Pragmatic Engineer, Google, OpenAI, and Anthropic are hiring FDEs aggressively.

You bring more than you think

Paraform groups the core FDE skills into five areas: one backend language, cloud infrastructure, data engineering, applied AI, and customer communication. Anyone with a few years of backend experience has usually already mastered the first three, at least well enough to be useful.

According to fde.academy, the most transferable skills are observability and debugging. On a customer site, you have to debug systems someone else built, with missing logs, restricted permissions, and everyone waiting on you for answers.

Those late-night on-call shifts trained you for exactly this kind of situation.

Read the job postings from Palantir and Anthropic and you'll see the same requirements. Palantir wants its FDSEs to own high-stakes projects end to end.

Anthropic's Applied AI FDE role requires candidates to go deep into customer systems and build production-grade applications on top of Claude models. Both companies want people who can deliver code that actually runs in someone else's environment, which is exactly what backend engineers do every day.

The three gaps to close

fde.academy lists three gaps: customer communication, understanding of AI/ML systems, and scoping the deployment, meaning locking down success criteria before thinking about architecture. Scaler's guide puts it more bluntly: most engineers fail to become FDEs not because their code is bad, but because they do a poor job of technical requirements discovery.

The AI gap only really shows up if you've mostly been building CRUD services. In that case, fde.academy says, you need to understand how AI systems behave differently.

A CRUD API gives the same output for the same input, and when it breaks there's a call stack to trace. An LLM application can be confidently wrong, so you need a set of evals; unit tests alone aren't enough.

Beyond these three points, there's one more requirement in the job postings worth noting. Anthropic asks for candidates with strong initiative who can navigate ambiguity in complex organizations. At a product company, by the time a ticket reaches you, someone has already clarified the requirements; on a customer site, clarifying the requirements is your job.

One project, two ways to tell it

fde.academy recommends rewriting your resume around outcomes rather than features. Scaler offers a narrative framework for interviews: change "I built X" into "I deployed X, which solved Y for customer Z." Try it with a familiar hypothetical project: a service that syncs orders with a partner's system.

Opening lineBuilt an order sync service with Go, Kafka, and PostgresThe ops team was manually entering orders from the partner's system, and the numbers often didn't match

ResultThe service ran stably[Your real numbers: manual entry hours saved, order mismatch rate before and after]

The technology is still there in the right-hand column, but it serves the customer's problem. Don't invent numbers. If nothing was measured at the time, ask former colleagues, or state clearly that it's an estimate.

Never worked with external customers? The internal teams using your systems are customers too; operations, finance, and data teams all count. What the hiring manager wants to hear is: you asked what they needed, and you measured whether they got it.

The first ten minutes decide the problem decomposition interview

According to techinterview.org, in a problem decomposition interview, strong candidates spend the first ten minutes asking questions, about the users and about what "done" means.

This round tests structured problem decomposition, user empathy, reasoning with data, and scoping. The article warns that if you only grind algorithm problems, you're studying for the least important part of this exam.

Back to the complaint email problem. Instead of drawing an architecture right away, start with questions like these:

"Who reads these emails today, and how long does each one take? Roughly how many come in per month, and what types of complaints are they? If the project succeeds in six weeks, which number will you look at to judge that?

What happens if the AI misclassifies a serious complaint? Which system are the emails stored in, and how far can we move the data out of it?"

Each question maps to a technical decision downstream. The cost of a misclassification determines whether human review is needed; where the data lives determines where the model can run. Draw the architecture only after you have the answers, and every box on the diagram will have a reason to exist.

Common mistakes when switching roles

The first mistake is stuffing your resume with technical buzzwords. Listing Kafka, Kubernetes, and gRPC only shows you know how to use these tools; it says nothing about whose problems you solved.

The second mistake is hiding incidents. A story about how you traced a failure to its root cause is the best proof of debugging skill, which fde.academy considers the most transferable skill of all. When telling that story, include the affected users and how you communicated with them.

The third mistake is exaggerating AI experience. If you've only done CRUD, build a small project with proper evals and describe it honestly. One follow-up question about handling unstable outputs, and anyone who hasn't done the work will be exposed quickly.

When reading job postings, watch for phrases like "end-to-end," "customer systems," and "ambiguity," and prepare a real story of your own for each one.

Coding ability gets you a seat at the interview table. What convinces people you can stand in front of a customer is asking "what does done look like?" before you start building.
