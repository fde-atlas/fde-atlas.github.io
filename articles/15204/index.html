ReAct (2022) Explained for FDEs

FDE Interviews · 2026-10-17 · 4 min read

Summary

This piece explains the 2022 ReAct paper for forward deployed engineers. ReAct interleaves reasoning and acting: the model writes a thought, takes an action such as a search, reads the observation, and uses it to plan the next step until finishing. With only one to six hand-written example trajectories, it nearly eliminated hallucinated facts on HotpotQA and FEVER, though combining it with plain chain-of-thought worked best on HotpotQA. On interactive tasks (ALFWorld, WebShop) it beat imitation and reinforcement learning baselines trained on thousands of examples by 34 and 10 points. Failure analysis matters for deployments: errors shifted from hallucinations to thought-action loops (47%) and unhelpful search results (23%), tool quality sets the ceiling, and step budgets plus fallback paths are part of the method, not polish.

Key points

  • ReAct interleaves thoughts, actions and observations in one loop.
  • It nearly eliminated hallucinations on knowledge QA tasks.
  • On ALFWorld and WebShop it beat trained baselines by 34 and 10 points.
  • Failures shifted to loops (47%) and dead-end searches (23%).
  • Tool quality, step budgets and fallbacks are core to the method.

The paper in one minute

Chain-of-thought prompting let models reason step by step, but they reasoned from memory, so a wrong fact early in the chain carried through to a confident wrong answer. Separately, researchers had models generate actions, such as moves in a game or searches, but without any reasoning the models lost track of what they were trying to do.

ReAct puts the two together in one loop. The model writes a Thought ("I need to find who the CFO is first"), then an Action (

search[Acme Corp]

), then receives an Observation from the environment (the search result). The next thought can use that observation, revise the plan or handle a surprise. This repeats until the model calls

finish[answer]

. The prompt contains only one to six hand-written example trajectories in this format.

The authors tested it on two kinds of tasks. On knowledge questions (HotpotQA) and fact checking (FEVER), with a deliberately simple Wikipedia tool, ReAct beat acting without reasoning and almost eliminated hallucinated facts, though on HotpotQA plain chain of thought still scored slightly higher, and the best results came from combining the two. On interactive tasks, a text-based household game (ALFWorld) and an online shopping site (WebShop), ReAct beat imitation and reinforcement learning methods trained on thousands of examples, by 34 and 10 points of success rate.

The loop gives an agent a way to adapt its next action to what a tool returns, rather than committing to a fixed sequence at the start.

Models could reason (chain of thought) or act (plan tool calls), but not both. Reasoning alone hallucinated facts; acting alone lost track of the goal.

Interleave them. The model writes a thought, takes an action, reads the observation, and uses it in the next thought, until it decides to finish.

Better than acting alone on every task, large gains on interactive tasks, and readable traces. On knowledge QA the best results came from combining it with plain reasoning.

Why it matters to an FDE

The ReAct loop is a useful starting point for agents that choose tools as they work. The paper also studies what goes wrong when reasoning and tool results feed into the next decision.

Grounding trades one failure for others. The authors hand-labeled why runs failed. Chain-of-thought failures were mostly hallucinated facts (56%). ReAct had none of those. Its failures were reasoning errors, often the model repeating the same thought and action in a loop (47%), and searches that returned nothing useful, after which the model struggled to recover (23%). These are useful failure cases to test in production: agents that loop, and agents derailed by a bad tool result.

The tools decide the ceiling. The Wikipedia tool in the paper was weak on purpose, able only to fetch a page by exact name or find the next sentence containing a string. Nearly a quarter of ReAct's failures came from it. On a deployment, the quality of search, the shape of tool outputs and the error messages a tool returns matter as much as the model.

Design the exits. The paper's best configurations add a fallback: if ReAct has not finished in 7 steps (5 for FEVER), switch to plain reasoning with voting; or if the votes disagree, switch to ReAct. Step budgets, loop detection and fallback paths are not polish. They are part of the method.
