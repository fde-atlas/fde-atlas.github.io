8 Cases of Information Leaks via Generative AI | How to Prevent Them Based on Where the Leak Originated

Original title: 生成AIの情報漏洩事例8選｜どこから漏れたかで分かる防ぎ方

FDE Consulting · 2026-10-08 · 10 min read

Summary

This article categorizes generative AI data leaks into five exit paths: employee input, service or developer flaws and misconfigurations, account takeover, attacks targeting AI, and AI-driven attacks. It reviews eight public incidents from 2023 to 2026, including Samsung engineers pasting source code into ChatGPT, a Microsoft AI team exposing 38TB via a misconfigured SAS token, DeepSeek's unauthenticated database, the tl;dv meeting-records vulnerability, the EchoLeak flaw in Microsoft 365 Copilot (CVE-2025-32711, CVSS 9.3), and an AI agent-led intrusion into Hugging Face. Each case includes what would have prevented or reduced the damage. The author concludes there are three root causes—inputting what should not be input, unsafe custodians, and overly broad AI permissions—and argues the third will matter most as AI agents proliferate, closing with how FDE practices help implement restricted AI systems in the field.

Key points

  • Leaks are grouped into five exit paths with distinct countermeasures.
  • Eight cases span 2023–2026, from Samsung to Hugging Face.
  • Microsoft's misconfigured SAS token exposed 38TB including keys and Teams messages.
  • EchoLeak let hidden email instructions exfiltrate data with no user action.
  • The author sees overly broad AI permissions as the growing key risk.

Information leaks involving generative AI can be divided into five categories based on where the leak occurred: employees inputting and taking data out, defects or misconfigurations on the AI service or developer side, hijacked AI accounts, attacks targeting AI, and cases where AI itself turns attacker.

The anxiety that "generative AI is scary because of information leaks" often lumps these five together. When the exit differs, the effective countermeasures differ too. Some leaks can be prevented through employee education, some through contracts and settings, and some only through access control design.

Here, we organize eight cases disclosed between 2023 and 2026 by exit, and show what could have been done to prevent each one, or to minimize the damage.

Exit 1: Employees inputting and taking data out

Case 1: Samsung engineer inputs source code into ChatGPT (2023)

In April 2023, an engineer at Samsung Electronics uploaded internal source code to ChatGPT, sending it outside the company. The following month, the company banned the use of generative AI on company-issued PCs, tablets, smartphones, and the internal network (Bloomberg, May 2, 2023).

The entry point of the leak was not an attacker, but an employee who wanted to work faster. This exit remains the most familiar one even in 2026. According to Verizon's "2026 Data Breach Investigations Report", the most common type of data sent to generative AI was source code.

  • How to prevent it: Define categories of information that may be input, and provide an environment contracted by the company. Why bans alone don't stop this is covered in detail in "What is Shadow AI? Risks that bans can't prevent and countermeasures that work in the field"

Exit 2: Defects and misconfigurations on the AI service and developer side

Even when employees use AI correctly, leaks can occur on the AI service side. Companies using the service cannot directly prevent this. What they can do is decide which service to choose and what to entrust to it.

Case 2: ChatGPT bug exposed other users' conversation titles and some payment information (2023)

On March 20, 2023, due to a defect in an open-source library (a Redis client) used by OpenAI, some users could see the titles of other users' conversation histories. Between 1:00 and 10:00 a.m. Pacific Time, approximately 1.2% of ChatGPT Plus subscribers may have had their names, email addresses, payment addresses, and the last four digits and expiration dates of their card numbers visible to other users. Full card numbers were not leaked (The Hacker News).

Case 3: Microsoft AI research team exposed 38TB of internal data (2023)

When Microsoft's AI research team published AI training data on GitHub, they misconfigured the scope of an Azure storage sharing link (SAS token). As a result, in addition to the data intended for publication, 38TB of internal data became accessible, including backups of two employees' PCs. The backups contained secret keys, passwords, and over 30,000 internal Teams messages (Wiz, September 18, 2023).

AI development and testing involve handling and sharing large amounts of data. The same kind of incident can occur when your company becomes the side building AI internally.

Case 4: DeepSeek database exposed without authentication (2025)

In January 2025, security firm Wiz reported that a database (ClickHouse) of Chinese AI company DeepSeek was accessible and operable from the internet without authentication. Logs exceeding one million rows contained users' chat histories, secret keys, and internal system information. DeepSeek stopped the exposure immediately after being notified (Wiz, January 29, 2025).

Case 5: Over 180,000 meeting records accessible via AI meeting notes service tl;dv (2026)

An independent security researcher reported that tl;dv, a service that records, transcribes, and summarizes meetings using AI, had a vulnerability allowing cross-user access to meeting metadata, enabling retrieval of 181,874 meeting records. The researcher began investigating in January 2026 and reported it to the operating company, criticizing that it took six months to fix, while tl;dv explained that there were two separate vulnerabilities (GIGAZINE, August 13, 2026).

Meeting recordings include sales negotiations, recruitment interviews, performance reviews, and management meetings. AI meeting notes tools are also a prime example of tools that field staff can easily adopt on their own judgment.

  • How to prevent Exit 2: Create a list of AI services used internally and understand what data is entrusted to which service. When adopting a new AI service, check where data is stored, the retention period, whether it is used for training, the vulnerability reporting channel, and their track record of response. Limit the information entrusted to the minimum necessary for business purposes

Exit 3: Hijacking of AI accounts

Case 6: ChatGPT login credentials stolen from over 100,000 devices and traded on dark web markets (2023)

In June 2023, security firm Group-IB announced that login credentials for ChatGPT stored on 101,134 devices had been infected by information-stealing malware, and that the records were being bought and sold on dark web marketplaces. By region, Asia-Pacific had the highest number (Group-IB, June 20, 2023).

AI accounts retain a history of past conversations. If an account is hijacked, all the internal information that has been entered into it can be read as well.

  • How to prevent it: Have employees use company accounts (with single sign-on and multi-factor authentication) rather than personal accounts, so access can be revoked upon resignation or transfer. Also decide how long conversation histories are retained

Exit 4: Attacks targeting AI (prompt injection)

Case 7: The Microsoft 365 Copilot vulnerability "EchoLeak" (2025)

In June 2025, it was disclosed that Microsoft 365 Copilot had a vulnerability (CVE-2025-32711, CVSS severity 9.3) that could allow internal data to be exfiltrated without the user doing anything. All an attacker had to do was send an email containing hidden instructions. When a user asked Copilot a question as usual, Copilot would reference that email and, following the instructions, send internal data externally. Microsoft has fixed the issue, and no evidence of exploitation has been confirmed (The Hacker News, June 12, 2025).

Once AI can reference internal emails and documents, text arriving from outside can function as "commands to the AI." This is prompt injection.

  • How to prevent it: Limit the data AI can reference and the operations AI can perform to what is necessary for business. Do not give an AI that reads external documents the authority to send data externally at the same time. Follow update information for AI products and apply fixes promptly

Exit 5: AI turning to the attacker's side

Case 8: Intrusion into Hugging Face by an AI agent (2026)

In July 2026, Hugging Face, which operates an AI model sharing service, disclosed an intrusion into part of its production environment. The intrusion was carried out from start to finish by an autonomously operating AI agent system. The attacker exploited weaknesses in the dataset processing mechanism to execute code, harvested cloud credentials, and infiltrated multiple internal clusters over the course of a weekend. Unauthorized access to some internal datasets and to credentials used by the service was confirmed (Hugging Face, July 16, 2026).

Attacks are becoming faster and broader through AI. Monitoring intervals premised on manual human work are increasingly unable to keep up.

  • How to prevent it: Do not reuse credentials for long periods, and divide permissions finely. Have mechanisms that automatically detect and stop anomalous operations

Three causes of generative AI information leaks, as revealed by the cases

Looking at the eight cases together, the causes can be organized into three.

  • Entering things that should not be entered (Exit 1). This can be reduced through employee education, input classification, and an official environment more convenient than personal accounts
  • The place where data was entrusted was not secure (Exits 2 and 3). Since there are parts that the using company cannot directly prevent, countermeasures lie in how you choose where to entrust data and limiting how much you entrust
  • The permissions granted to AI were too broad (Exits 4 and 5). This is a design problem of limiting what AI can read and what AI can do to what is necessary for business

We believe the third will become the most significant going forward. That is because AI is shifting from a tool that merely answers questions to an agent that reads and writes internal systems to carry out work.

Information that must not be entered into generative AI

Even in environments contracted by the company, decide individually the "places where it is acceptable to enter" the following information.

Implementing countermeasures in a "form that gets used"

The countermeasures for Exits 1 and 4 cannot be implemented just by buying tools. Unless you decide which business processes, which people, and which data will be handed to AI, neither the input classification nor the scope of permissions can be determined.

Our FDE (Forward Deployed Engineer) practice is a way of working in which engineers go into the field, break down business processes, build AI mechanisms that will actually be used, and iterate improvements until they take hold. We work together all the way to building AI mechanisms with limited permissions in a form that the field does not find inconvenient. For FDE, see "What is FDE?
