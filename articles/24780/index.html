Summary

Written by Shohei Tokumitsu, CEO of DeFactory, this Japanese article explains deliverables and documentation across the system development lifecycle. It distinguishes "deliverables" (seibutsu) from "items handed to clients" (nouhinbutsu), walks through four phases—requirements definition, design, programming, and testing—and lists typical documents for each, such as RFPs, requirements specifications, basic/detailed design documents, and test reports. It weighs benefits (shared understanding, measurable progress, quality control) against drawbacks (maintenance burden, change-sync complexity, outdated docs), briefly introduces the Forward Deployed Engineer (FDE) role, and promotes DeFactory's outsourcing services.

Key points

  • Deliverables are phase outputs (documents, programs); client-requested items count as nouhinbutsu.
  • Four phases: requirements definition, design, programming, testing, each with standard documents.
  • Requirements phase must cover non-functional needs like performance, scalability, security.
  • Documenting deliverables aligns stakeholders and clarifies progress but adds maintenance overhead.
  • The article ends by positioning FDEs as engineers spanning requirements through implementation.

In system development, "deliverables" refer to the documents and materials created at each stage of development.

The stages of system development, especially web system development, are wide-ranging. Since web systems can be used on various devices via the internet, all possible cases must be anticipated.

The word "deliverable" may bring to mind the final product, but various deliverables are also produced during intermediate stages of development.

Generally, a deliverable refers to the output produced as a result of completing work or a project.

However, in system development and web system development, deliverables refer to completed programs when some or all of the work at each stage of development is finished, as well as documents and manuals summarizing system specifications and specification methods (specification documents and design documents).

In system development, deliverables are produced at each stage of development, and in some cases, at each task.

"Deliverable" is similar to the term "delivered item," but they are not the same.

Delivered items refer to everything delivered to the client. Many people think delivered items are the final deliverables, but that is not the case.

For example, design documents created at the beginning are also treated as "delivered items" if the client requests their delivery.

In other words, intermediate deliverables and received items—everything delivered to the client upon request—are all "delivered items."

On the other hand, deliverables encompass everything that constitutes the results or evidence of a project. They can be classified into three categories: "final deliverables," "intermediate deliverables," and "received items."

Final deliverables are what the project ultimately aims to achieve. Intermediate deliverables are deliverables produced in the process of creating the final deliverables.

Types of documents in system development include "requirements specifications," "detailed design documents," and "operation manuals." These documents are required at each stage of system development.

Why are they required at each development stage?

By creating and sharing deliverables and documents, smooth information sharing and communication with clients become possible.

Before development begins, the details of system development are finalized during the requirements definition stage. Clearly documenting what is decided at this point helps prevent future troubles. Records are updated as changes occur.

In actual development, it is rare for the same person, or a single person, to build the system from start to finish. The right people are assigned to the right tasks according to each phase, such as design and construction, testing and verification.

Therefore, having well-prepared documents is essential to ensure smooth handovers of work between stages.

Systems are only valuable when operated correctly. However, over long-term operation, the person in charge may change or leave the company.

In such cases, if the flow and history of each stage of the system are documented, it becomes easier to understand the overall flow and past history.

If there are no documents, everything must be reviewed from the system's contents, resulting in enormous costs for maintenance and operation.

Let's take a concrete look at the deliverables for each phase.

This is the phase where the client and the system developers discuss and determine the details of the system to be developed. At the requirements definition stage, you work out the actually required functions, as well as non-functional specs, what the system can and cannot do, and so on.

DeliverableDetails of the deliverable
RFP (Request for Proposal)Purpose of the system development, company overview, organizational chart and business content, current issues and business requirements, desired needs and requests, roles in the project, budget and delivery date, items requested in the proposal
Requirements Definition DocumentCurrent business flow, business flow after systematization, definition of systematization scope, list of business functions, overview of systematized functions, explanation of screen images, overview of batch functions, overview of data layout, definition of non-functional requirements

A business flow is a diagrammatic representation of the flow of work. In the requirements definition phase, you create a document organizing the current business and the business flow after the system is actually implemented.

By presenting this, the business efficiency improvements and reduction of work hours brought about by implementing the system are clearly demonstrated.

In requirements definition, the key point is to discuss not only the system's functions but also the budget and personnel management. Since this affects the entire system development project, it is best to work out the details thoroughly.

Also, what you should pay attention to is non-functional requirements. Non-functional requirements are all requirements other than the functional requirements to be implemented. For example, this includes the processing capacity the system should achieve, the extensibility for adding functions in the future, and security against external threats.

Based on the details determined in the requirements definition, this is the phase where you translate them into design. Depending on the design content, it is divided into various detailed documents such as specifications, diagrams, and design documents.

The following is one example, but the required deliverables vary depending on the project content and the client.

The system development side creates the basic design document and solidifies the details while confirming with the client.

DeliverableDetails of the deliverable
Basic Design DocumentSystem design, screen design, report design, batch design, database design, file design, various configuration diagrams (hardware, software, network), screen transition diagram, various definitions such as screen layout items, batch processing flow, external system definition
Detailed Design DocumentDetailed screen processing definitions, class diagrams, sequence diagrams, status transition diagrams, various definitions such as DB updates and file output
OthersProgramming design documents, design documents used in the testing phase

In basic design, the "system requirements," "functional requirements," and "non-functional requirements" defined in the requirements definition document are developed to a more concrete level. You create a "basic design document," which is a document summarizing the blueprint of the system as seen from the outside.

Following the specifications and design documents created in the design phase, this is finally the phase where functions are built with programs. It is common to divide the system into detailed units by function and have multiple people build them in parallel.

DeliverableDetails of the deliverable
Program Design DocumentComplete set of source code, executable modules
OthersList of system configuration values

In the program development and implementation phase, multiple programmers are each responsible for developing the divided individual modules.

What is important is to create something that is easy for anyone to understand. It is also a key point to establish certain conventions, such as readability and using consistent functions.

The testing phase mainly consists of four types: unit testing, integration testing, system testing, and operational testing.

To check whether the system operates as defined in the requirements, unit tests are performed for each module. Once unit testing is passed, integration testing checks whether multiple programs work together.

Furthermore, system testing confirms that all programs function correctly.

DeliverableDetails of the deliverable
Various test specifications, test results, performance evaluations, quality evaluationsFor each function of unit testing, integration testing, system testing, and operational testing

This is the stage of aiming for completion toward release. You carefully check the overall quality, including durability and actual processing speed.

In the final operational testing, you confirm whether the developed system operates correctly in accordance with the requirements definition in an actual environment.

If there are no problems, you proceed to release.

We have looked at the deliverables for each phase, but what are the advantages and disadvantages of defining deliverables?

It allows for a unified understanding between both the developers and the client.

Having deliverables corresponding to progress also makes it easier to grasp the progress of work as concrete figures.

Also, when deliverables are completed in each phase, the project can proceed while confirming the actual deliverables against the requirements and specifications decided so far.

Even in the unlikely event that a deliverable is not in the expected state, differences in understanding can be identified at an early stage, allowing communication between the client and the development side.

At the start of the project, a rough schedule for each phase is established. Completing the various tasks needed to stay on schedule has clear deadlines when represented as tangible deliverables. When the goal of each task is clear, both the client and the developers can proceed without losing sight of the purpose midway, while maintaining motivation.

Having deliverables defined for each phase allows you to view the whole picture, such as the requirements definition and design of a phase or the system as a whole. Being able to grasp the overall picture makes it easier to notice omissions and oversights, resulting in detailed and thorough responses at an early stage.

Defining deliverables after each task is completed makes it easy to understand the progress and quality of the work from the state of the deliverables.

For example, if completed deliverables can be checked even midway through a phase, the current status can be grasped from both the progress and quality perspectives, beyond simple completion of creation. This makes it possible to foresee and avoid potential troubles at an early stage.

By defining the tasks of each phase in the form of deliverables, what should be done within a task and the content of the work are maintained at a certain level.

Deliverables serve as a guideline, making it easier to produce outputs and carry out tasks with assured quality.

Creating documents that are easy for anyone to understand seems simple but is surprisingly difficult. Documents written by engineers involved in development tend to contain many technical terms, making the content hard to grasp and difficult to understand.

Also, formats may differ for each work phase, or the writing style may not be unified.

② Handling changes and revisions is cumbersome

Since documents are created at each stage, when a change is made, it must be reflected in each of them. Care must be taken to ensure no revisions are missed, and sharing information with the person in charge of each stage is also essential. Making sure everyone is aware of the latest documents must also be done without any omissions.

③ Discrepancy between revisions and the current state

It is not uncommon for development itself to be carried out on a tight schedule. When releasing a system, if actual operations take priority, revisions may be put off.

As a result, document maintenance falls behind, creating a discrepancy with the actual operation of the system, and the documents lose their meaning.

One way to resolve these disadvantages is to outsource the system development itself.

Outsourcing means entrusting in-house work to an external vendor. In system development, there are methods such as "outsourcing almost all stages of system development," "outsourcing only operations," and "having staff stationed at the contracted company."

The benefits of outsourcing system development are mainly the following three:

- Reducing the time and costs of hiring and training personnel

- Saving on labor costs

- Allowing engineers to focus on their core work

However, keep in mind that outsourcing also has disadvantages, such as "know-how and insights not being accumulated" and "the risk of information leaks."
