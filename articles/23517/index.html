What is an FDE: The difference between FDEs at product companies and consulting firms

Original title: FDE 是什么：产品公司与咨询公司 FDE 区别

FDE Times · 2026-11-18 · 5 min read

Summary

The article argues that the FDE title describes two different jobs. At product companies like Palantir, where the role originated around 2013 (internally called Delta), engineers deploy to customers because the software is too complex to self-serve, and their work feeds back into a shared platform. At consultancies like Accenture, FDEs leave client-owned assets such as playbooks and accelerators, and report impact in financial terms to client executives. The author advises reading job postings for clues about ownership and handover, and asking how much time is spent writing production code before accepting an offer.

Key points

  • Palantir created the FDE role because customers could not deploy its software alone.
  • The key difference is whether outcomes flow back to a platform or stay with the client.
  • Consultancy FDEs must quantify impact in terms a CFO understands.
  • Job posting wording about ownership reveals the role type before interviews.
  • Neither model is superior; choose based on skills you want in three years.

A consulting firm's FDE leaves behind client-owned deliverables; a product company's FDE gets the platform adopted. Confuse the two, and your skills, interviews, and career direction will all go off track.

Key Takeaways

  • Palantir created the FDE role because its software was too complex for customers to adopt on their own. There, services are merely a means to get customers onto the product. Accenture, by contrast, uses the same title for its services business.
  • Both put engineers right next to the customer. The difference lies in where the work ultimately goes: does it become a shared platform serving more customers, or an asset left with a single client?
  • Before accepting an offer, ask exactly what the handover at the end of a project includes, and what proportion of your time is spent writing production code.

A Forward Deployed Engineer (FDE) job posting from Accenture in the UK requires candidates to build patterns, playbooks, and accelerators for the client to own after you leave. Palantir, meanwhile, is where the role originated. According to a16z, at Palantir services are merely a means to get customers onto the product, and revenue does not primarily come from services.

So these are two different jobs sharing one name. You might be comparing an offer from a consulting firm with one from a product company, or revising your resume to move into an FDE role.

Conflate the two, and you'll build the wrong skills, tell the wrong stories in interviews, and head in the wrong direction for years. The good news: by reading job postings carefully, you can tell fairly clearly which type you're applying to before you sign.

The role was born at a product company

The Forward Deployed Software Engineer role appeared at Palantir in the early 2010s, where it was internally called Delta. Paraform explains why: Palantir's software was too complex for customers to integrate and run themselves, so the company had to send engineers on-site.

The Pragmatic Engineer defines the FDE as a software engineer who rotates between two places: first sitting with the customer's team, then returning to the core product engineering team. The Pragmatic Engineer also notes that FDEs are often mistaken for consultants. In fact, FDEs are embedded at customers for extended periods and contribute to the product the company sells.

a16z points out why this model works at Palantir: beneath the custom work done for each customer lies a real platform. So in the original model, the work you do on-site makes the platform get used, and what you learn there flows back to the product team.

What do consulting firms use this title for?

Accenture's UK job posting puts the emphasis elsewhere. Engineers work inside the client organization. What remains at the end of a project is a set of patterns, playbooks, and accelerators that the client keeps after you leave.

Accenture also requires FDEs to measure the impact of each deployment with numbers a CFO can grasp at a glance and act on. FDEs report results to the client's CTO, CFO, and CISO. The people you ultimately need to convince are the client's leadership.

At first glance, the two descriptions look very similar—both put engineers right next to the client's team. That's exactly the trap. The real difference is where your work ultimately ends up.

One design decision splits FDEs into two types

An article on Substack argues that the difference between a true FDE and expensive consulting with a new title comes down to a single internal design decision: separating product work from services work.

According to the article, the product team's job is to build something that serves many customers at once—what the author calls the "paved road." Companies that hold this line send engineers to customers to do product business; companies that abandon it are essentially selling labor.

a16z gives the same warning: companies that copy only Palantir's embedded engineering half end up carrying thousands of one-off deployments that cannot be maintained or upgraded.

One incident, two correct answers

Use a hypothetical example to see how this decision plays out in daily work. You're doing a deployment at a bank and discover the data connector can't read its legacy transaction format.

At a product company, you fix the problem to get the customer running, but the work isn't done. You also have to judge whether the fix should go into the "paved road." If other banks use the same legacy format, you bring the fix back to the core product team rather than leaving behind a code branch only this bank uses.

At a consulting firm, the right approach is to build a clean, well-documented module that runs on the bank's own infrastructure, so their team can maintain it after you leave. You also need to explain, in terms the bank's CFO understands, how much money the module saves or generates for the bank.

Both approaches are good engineering; they just measure different things. One asks whether the platform can serve more customers; the other asks whether the client can run on their own after you're gone.

Read a job posting in five minutes

You don't need to wait for the interview—how a company describes the role is itself a strong signal.

Wording (source)MeaningQuestions to ask the recruiter
"Rotates between the customer's team and the core product team" (The Pragmatic Engineer's definition)Product model: customer work flows back into the productWhere do fixes you write on-site go? Who decides whether they go into the product?
"Services are a means to get customers onto the product" (a16z on Palantir)Revenue primarily comes from the productAre FDE hours billed separately to the client?
"Things the client owns after you leave" (Accenture job posting)Services model: the deliverables are the productWhat does a typical handover package include?
"Measure impact in terms a CFO understands" (Accenture job posting)You report to the client's leadershipWho on the client side signs off on your results?

Beyond the questions in the table, the most valuable thing to ask about either type of role is: what proportion of your time is spent writing production code? If all the building is left to others, the skills you accumulate will skew noticeably toward consulting.

Practical step: when reading a job posting, highlight all wording about ownership—such as the company's product or the client's assets—and all verbs about handover. Then classify the posting against the table, and raise these questions verbatim in your call with the recruiter.

Common mistakes when choosing

The most common mistake is treating the title as evidence. Both Accenture's and Palantir's offers say "FDE," but those letters tell you nothing about what your daily work looks like. Product companies differ too: Paraform notes that at AI startups like Greptile, the problem scope is narrower but the pace is faster.

The second mistake is telling the wrong story. In interviews at product companies, tell stories about solving a customer's problem and bringing the lessons back into the product. In interviews at consulting firms, tell stories about what client-owned assets you left behind that teams could run on their own, and quantify the impact.

The last mistake is assuming one type is more senior than the other. The consulting model builds handover skills and the ability to communicate with leadership; the product model builds the ability to turn work for a single customer into something many customers can use. Choose based on the skills you want to master three years from now.

This week's exercise

Find a recent technical incident you handled for a customer; if you're not yet an FDE, use one you handled for an internal team. Write two response plans modeled on the bank connector example: one the product-company way, one the consulting-firm way.

Which one you enjoy writing more is a rough indication of which type of role you should aim for.

The FDE title will spread to more companies, and the more widely it spreads, the less the name itself will tell you. To find out the truth, read the sentence that describes what you leave behind when you walk away from the customer.
