How to Define Acceptance Criteria for AI Systems: LLM Evals and Error Thresholds

Original title: AI 系统验收标准怎么定：LLM 评测与错误阈值

FDE Times · 2026-10-09 · 6 min read

Summary

This guide argues that AI projects fail at acceptance when engineers promise zero errors instead of negotiating error thresholds upfront. Drawing on Hamel Husain, Anthropic, and OpenAI guidance, it frames the acceptable pass rate as a product decision about which errors are tolerable. Standards should be specific, measurable, achievable, and multi-dimensional, covering accuracy, error severity, latency, and safety. Using a logistics email-classification example, the author shows a four-row criteria table with thresholds, sample sizes, and measurement methods, then outlines five implementation steps and common pitfalls such as copying thresholds across projects and relying on a single accuracy number.

Key points

  • LLM evals need not pass 100%; the pass rate is a product decision.
  • Analyze real failures first, starting from about 100 traces.
  • Set criteria across accuracy, severity, latency, and safety.
  • Every threshold needs a sample size and measurement method.
  • Rerun evals after each change and get customer sign-off.

How much error is acceptable is a product decision: agree with the client before writing any code on which errors are acceptable and on which dataset they will be measured.

Key Takeaways

  • LLM evals don't need a 100% pass rate. The right pass rate is a product decision that depends on which errors the client can accept.
  • Good criteria are specific, measurable, achievable with current models, and cover multiple dimensions at once, including error severity.
  • Write tests for failure modes that actually occur in real data, and rerun the evals after every change.

Imagine this scenario: you're in a second meeting with a logistics company. They want to use an LLM to automatically classify complaint emails, and the head of operations sums up the requirement in one sentence: "The classification must be correct every single time. No mistakes."

If you nod and go back to write code, the project is probably already doomed. At acceptance three months later, a single misclassified email lets the client claim the system failed to meet requirements, and you have nothing in writing to push back with.

For a Forward Deployed Engineer (FDE), the hardest part of an AI project is often neither the prompts nor the data pipeline. Systems are bound to make errors; given that premise, the hardest part is reaching agreement with the client on what "success" means. This article is about how to do that before writing the first line of code.

Why "always correct" isn't a criterion

Software engineers are used to unit tests: a red test means a bug, and a bug must be fixed. Hamel Husain, who consults for teams on LLM product evals, points out that LLM evals are different: an eval suite doesn't have to pass 100%. OpenAI's evals guide also notes that it is precisely because AI systems are non-deterministic that evals are needed.

Husain's more important point is that the required pass rate is a product decision, depending on which errors you are willing to accept. So when a client says "no mistakes allowed," the right response isn't "that's impossible," but a follow-up question: which errors are absolutely unacceptable, and which can be tolerated?

Anthropic's documentation on success criteria lays out four requirements: criteria must be specific, measurable, achievable, and relevant to the scenario. Its example is brief: don't write "performs well"; write "accurately classifies sentiment."

"Always correct" fails two of the four: no one can measure it on a finite sample, and no model can achieve it.

Read real failure cases first, then choose metrics

The common approach is to open a ready-made list of metrics, pick out accuracy and F1, and maybe add a "helpfulness" score. Husain warns that this top-down approach tends to make people measure what is easy to measure rather than what users actually care about.

He suggests doing the reverse: first analyze errors in real data, then write tests targeting the specific failure modes you observe. As for scale, he recommends starting with about 100 traces to find errors. When you need to validate an LLM judge, annotate 100 to 200 samples per failure mode.

Applied to this logistics company, that means asking them to provide 100 real complaint emails with personal information removed, running them through your prototype, and reviewing the results together with a customer service agent. Suppose you find three failure modes.

First, "delayed delivery" emails get classified as "general inquiries." Second, damage claim emails get classified as "delayed delivery." Third, emails threatening legal action don't get escalated to a supervisor.

These three failures differ greatly in severity. Confusing "delayed delivery" with "general inquiry" just costs a customer service agent a few minutes to move the email to the right queue. Missing a legal threat email could escalate into a legal incident. A single accuracy number hides exactly this kind of information.

An example acceptance criteria set

Anthropic recommends setting criteria across multiple dimensions simultaneously. One dimension is error severity, for example "90% of errors are merely inconvenient rather than serious."

Latency is also on the list. For safety, Anthropic gives a numerically explicit criterion: fewer than 0.1% of outputs flagged as harmful by the content filter across 10,000 runs.

Note how this criterion is written: it has a threshold, a sample size, and a measurement tool. The email project's criteria should follow the same template. The numbers in the table below are examples only; replace them with the numbers you agree on with the client:

DimensionCriterionMeasurement data
Overall classificationAt least 92% of emails assigned to the correct category500 emails labeled and confirmed by the client
Error severityAt least 90% of errors are merely "wrong queue"Errors within the same 500 emails
Legal threat emailsEvery email in the legal threat test set caught, no exceptions150 labeled legal threat emails
LatencyClassification completed before the email enters the queueStaging environment logs

Now do the math on the first row. At 1,000 emails per day, 92% accuracy means about 80 misclassified emails. If 90% of those are merely wrong-queue errors, about 8 serious errors remain per day.

The question you put on the table is: "Eight important emails will be misrouted every day. Can your team accept that? Or do you need a human review step?"

This question turns a vague worry into a business choice that can actually be discussed. It also aligns with Husain's view of pass rates: the pass rate is a product decision about which errors are acceptable, so the client's product owner must be involved in deciding it.

What about the "no exceptions" row? Absolute thresholds only make sense on a test set that is narrow, fixed, and large enough, so the result isn't due to luck, like the 150 emails in the example table. If the system can't achieve it, the answer is to add human review, not to quietly lower the bar.

Five steps for your next project

Step one: rewrite every vague requirement as a sentence with numbers. OpenAI's guide also starts designing evals from this question: what does success look like?

Step two: get real data and read about 100 traces together with the client's domain expert. Record failure modes in their language, not in machine learning jargon.

Step three: rank each failure mode by severity, then set criteria across multiple dimensions such as accuracy, severity, latency, and safety.

Step four: confirm the thresholds are achievable. Anthropic stresses that metrics should not exceed the capabilities of current frontier models and should be grounded in benchmarks or experiments you have run yourself. Before making a commitment, run your prototype on the test set, so you walk into the negotiation with baseline data instead of gut feeling.

Step five: get the client to sign off on the criteria, then automate the evals. OpenAI recommends setting up continuous evals, running them after every change, and calibrating automated scoring with human judgment.

Common pitfalls

The first pitfall is copying thresholds from one project to another. Anthropic notes that citation accuracy may be critical for a medical application but far less so for a chatbot. A 92% threshold that suits email classification may not suit summarizing medical records.

The second pitfall is relying on a single overall number. Accuracy can look good while hiding serious failure modes. Have at least one criterion on error severity, plus a separate one for the failure the client worries about most.

The third pitfall is choosing the test set yourself. If you did the labeling, the client has grounds to distrust the results at acceptance. Have someone on the client side confirm the labels, at least for the test set covering serious failures.

The last pitfall is treating the criteria as a document you sign once and file away. Prompts change, models change, and input data changes. The criteria only mean something if you rerun the evals after every change.

When a job description mentions phrases like "define success metrics with customers" or "build evals," this is the capability it refers to. Your resume should be specific: which thresholds were agreed upon, how many samples were measured, and what decisions the customer made based on those numbers.

Practice this week: pick an AI feature you're working on and write a set of four-line criteria following the table above, with a threshold, sample size, and measurement method for each line. Then try to answer: "How many critical errors per day are acceptable?" If you can't answer, that's the question to ask the customer at your next meeting.
