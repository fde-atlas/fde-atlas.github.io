Hiring Standards and Interviews ​

Original title: 招聘标准与面试 ​

ai-alchemy-lab.github.io · 2026-09-06 · 14 min read

Summary

This page is an FDE hiring and interview guide that grades every claim by evidence strength (first-hand, traceable second-hand, unverifiable, or missing), then compiles a 30-question question bank organized around four competencies: technical skill, business/domain knowledge, communication, and judgment. Palantir has the most reliable public information: two tracks (FDSE and Deployment Strategist), technical decomposition as the pivotal skill, founder interviews in its early years, and a high-signal behavioral question about the hardest thing a candidate has ever fought for. OpenAI and Anthropic JD requirements are first-hand, but their interview loops come only from prep platforms. The China section summarizes 60+ job postings, four emerging interview formats, and warns that the FDE label has turned negative, so candidates should verify a role isn't disguised on-site outsourcing.

Key points

  • Every claim is tagged by evidence strength, from official JDs to anonymous forum posts.
  • Palantir runs two tracks: FDSE requires full SWE interviews; Deployment Strategist tests reasoning and executive communication.
  • Palantir's key behavioral question judges cost, spontaneity, and detail density, not conclusions.
  • OpenAI/Anthropic JD requirements are first-hand; their interview loops are only prep-platform reconstructions.
  • Chinese postings show four formats, including a 24-hour agent assignment; candidates should screen for disguised outsourcing.

1. Palantir: The Most Stable Format and the Most Public Information ​

1.1 Two Tracks, Two Hiring Bars 🟢 ​

  • FDSE (Delta): Requires passing a full software engineer interview; candidates typically have a formal CS background, because from day one on-site they need to write real code and build real solutions.
  • Deployment Strategist (Echo): The technical interview is lighter, focusing on data reasoning, navigating social dynamics (finding your way in a large organization), and executive communication.

(Source: Nabeel Qureshi's account on Lenny's Podcast, 2025-05-11; he joined as an FDE in 2015, stayed eight years, and was an Enterprise Lead when he left)

A verifiable corroborating detail: as of 2026-09-06, Palantir's public job board lists Internship and New Grad openings for Deployment Strategist, but none for FDSE. DS has an entry point for new graduates; FDSE does not.

1.2 Decomposition Is the Watershed 🟢 ​

In Palantir's official blog, Elisa from New York wrote the first core skill of Delta as:

Technical decomp—taking a high-level business problem and being able to break it down layer by layer and design a solution, understanding all the way from the business problem down to every line of code needed to solve it.

The other two are "autonomy + strong technical intuition" and "critical thinking," where a typical daily decision for the latter is: for a feature a customer wants, should you spend the time building it into the core platform, or build a customized version that ships faster. (Dev versus Delta, 2019-04-08)

This competency was later codified into a question type. Interview prep courses commonly call it the "Palantir-origin decomposition / case round," with the standard moves being: clarify ambiguities → decompose → propose a 4-week scope → name the biggest risky assumption. 🟡 (Interview Kickstart course page, W22 module description, captured 2026-09-01, provider's own description)

1.3 In the Early Days, Every Offer Went Through a Founder Interview 🟢 ​

From Nabeel Qureshi's accounts in "Reflections on Palantir" and on Lenny's Podcast:

  • Alex Karp (CEO) didn't look at resumes; he spent about 10 minutes asking questions completely unrelated to the role, coming out of left field, to see how you decompose problems. In Nabeel's own round, he spent an hour with Karp discussing Wittgenstein.
  • Stephen Cohen (co-founder) would pick a philosophical topic on the spot out of thin air and drill into it deeply, probing the limits of your understanding—impossible to prepare for, essentially a vibe check.
  • New employee onboarding materials included notes from Peter Thiel's Stanford startup course (CS183).

(nabeelqu.co/reflections-on-palantir, 2024-10-15; Lenny's Podcast, 2025-05-11)

1.4 The Behavioral Question with the Highest Discriminating Power 🟢 ​

Nabeel explicitly singled out this question as "extremely discriminating—many people can't answer it":

"What's the hardest you've ever fought for something in your life? Why?"

Scoring doesn't look at the conclusion, but at three things: cost (what was sacrificed: time, health, relationships, money), spontaneity (whether anyone pushed them to do it), and density of detail (people who truly fought for something can describe the specific scenes from those days; those making it up can only offer conclusions and feelings). Answering "I've always worked hard, often staying at the office until 11 or midnight" scores low—overtime is passive; fighting for something is active.

1.5 Recruiting Strategy: The Bat Signal 🟢 ​

Palantir deliberately flew a polarizing flag ("Save the Shire," emphasizing defense, the military, and defending the West), which seemed odd in an era when everyone was building social apps—but it was precisely that "oddness" that repelled the wrong people and attracted three types of talent undervalued by mainstream Silicon Valley: mission-driven aspiring founders, veterans, and patriotic technologists. Nabeel's judgment is that exclusivity is a feature, not a flaw, and he points out that today OpenAI / Anthropic are doing the same thing with "superintelligence is the most important issue facing humanity."

He also offered a judgment on the FDE profile: FDE = a rare combination of political savvy and coding speed. Corroborating evidence: "in every YC batch, ex-Palantir founders typically outnumber ex-Google ones, even though Google has roughly 50 times as many employees as Palantir."

Three preparation priorities inferred from the JDs:

  • OpenAI explicitly states the success metrics are "production adoption, measurable workflow impact, and eval-driven feedback" — the odds of being asked in the interview "how do you know what you delivered is actually useful" are extremely high, so your answer must come down to adoption rates and evals.
  • Anthropic explicitly lists MCP servers, sub-agents, and agent skills as deliverables — you need to have work on these three things you can pull out and talk about.
  • Both companies include "advancing autonomously amid ambiguity" in their JD wording (OpenAI: "scoped and delivered complex systems in fast-moving or ambiguous environments"; Anthropic: "operate autonomously, thrive under ambiguity"), which in interviews maps to situational questions rather than knowledge questions.

2.2 Secondary sources: public breakdowns of the interview loop 🟡 ​

Interview prep platform Aced (formerly Exponent) gives a description of a six-round loop on its OpenAI FDE job page, with a cycle of about 3–4 weeks:

  • Recruiter screen, "focused on FDE-specific motivation"
  • Technical screen or take-home, "LLM-product flavored"
  • Coding rounds, "built on agentic and eval scenarios"
  • System design, with "token-cost, latency, and eval-gate constraints"
  • Customer role-play, "with a simulated non-technical executive", plus behavioral interviews
  • Leadership final

The advice given on that page is: "Practice the customer-facing rounds — they decide FDE offers more often than the coding does."

⚠️ This is a compilation by an interview prep company, not an official OpenAI release. It is consistent with the JD content (the JD does emphasize evals, customer-facing work, and ambiguous situations), but the number and order of rounds are not officially confirmed — ../08-career/interview-prep.md therefore records these companies' interview processes overall as "no first-hand information obtained", keeping only this 🟡 record as reference, not as a conclusion. The salary figures given on the same page (base $153K–$325K) also do not fully match the ranges read directly from the OpenAI job API on 2026-09-06 (FDE $185K–$300K, FDSWE $185K–$325K), suggesting it may mix data from different roles and different points in time. (tryexponent.com/jobs/fde/openai)

Worth noting: Exponent has already launched a "Forward Deployed (Beta)" course, listed in its navigation alongside PM / SWE / System Design, with the copy "Prep for FDE loops at Anthropic, OpenAI, Palantir, and more". This is the first prep platform to make FDE a formal interview category — itself a signal that this role has become standardized. 🟢 (official site navigation, accessed 2026-09-06)

This library has not obtained any verifiable first-hand accounts from OpenAI / Anthropic FDE interviewees in this round. III. Chinese companies: interview formats and common questions ​

3.1 Competency models in client-side JDs 🟢 ​

The six points under "we hope you are this kind of person" in Sperta Group (Nutrend)'s "Forward Deployed Engineer (FDE)" posting form the most complete competency model in any Chinese JD (original text, transcribed from the public recruitment page, 2026-08-30):

1. Not only able to write code, but also willing to understand the business; 2. Not just building demos, but caring whether anyone actually uses it after launch; 3. Not afraid of vague requirements, able to extract structure from chaos; 4. Able to go on-site with business teams rather than sitting around waiting for requirement documents; 5. Has hands-on enthusiasm for AI, but doesn't blindly worship it; 6. Results-oriented, able to drive problems through to resolution.

Two accompanying statements of role boundaries (also from public JDs):

  • Haid Group: "You are not a PM who draws prototypes, nor an algorithm researcher who only writes papers—you are the person who translates business problems into runnable AI solutions on the spot and is accountable for the implementation results."
  • Shanghai Deep Zhilian: "We don't require you to write code yourself, but you must be able to read it, review it, and make the final call."

3.2 Actual distribution of degrees and years of experience 🟡 ​

From this library's statistics on a sample of 60+ AI FDE job postings on Chinese recruitment platforms (2026-08-30):

  • Degrees: The vast majority say "bachelor's degree." A few finance roles, such as Western Securities, require a master's; Fuzhou Gulou Guotou says "no degree requirement"; some vendors accept associate degrees. JoinQuant says "top domestic and overseas university backgrounds preferred," and Shopee says "graduated from top universities at home or abroad."
  • Years of experience: 1–3 years about 20% (mostly vendor-side delivery and customer success roles), 3–5 years about 40% (the main group), 5–10 years about 30%, and 10+ years only a few high-end roles.
  • One state-owned-enterprise quirk: Fuzhou Gulou Guotou says "CPC members preferred."

3.5 ⚠️ An objection that must be addressed 🟡 ​

The term "FDE" has already acquired a negative label in Chinese tech circles. Practitioners' original words in workplace communities include "a pure vendor-side workhorse job," "the worst job there is, bar none," and "just the old Solutions Engineer role." This is one direct reason why client-side internal roles have generally been renamed (to things like AI Application Engineer, AI Enablement Engineer, etc.). (Source: public discussions on Maimai, captured 2026-08; poster identities unverifiable—treat as a reference for sentiment, not as established fact)

Practical impact on interviews: If you are interviewing at a Chinese company that uses the name "FDE," it is worth asking three things face to face—whether the evaluation includes post-launch metrics, whether you can refuse a requirement that shouldn't be done, and whether the people directing you are from your own company or the client. The answers to these three questions directly determine whether this is a disguised on-site outsourcing role (see what-is-an-fde.md for the criteria).

IV. A 30-question interview question bank (organized along four dimensions) ​

Arranged according to the four dimensions in competency-model.md. The parenthetical note after each question is what the question tests. For interviewers: follow up until you get specific times, names, and numbers. For candidates: for each question, first write an outline of a real example.

① Technical (8 questions) ​

  • Describe a time you took over an unfamiliar codebase with no documentation and successfully made changes that went to production. How did you build your understanding? (T1 cold start on unfamiliar systems)
  • What was the hardest data integration you've done? What state were the data sources in at the time, and how did you resolve permission issues? (T2 integration and dirty data)
  • Build an eval set for an AI feature you know well: how many cases to start with? How to stratify? How to find edge-case samples? (T3 eval construction)
  • Have you written LLM-as-judge prompts? How do you verify alignment with human judgment? Where do you set the threshold, and why? (T3 judge calibration—a step almost nobody in the market teaches)
  • Describe a time you deployed a system into someone else's environment (customer cloud, intranet, isolated zone). How did you handle authentication, keys, and data boundaries? (T4 deployment in others' environments)
  • A real production incident: how do you find the root cause, ship the fix, and communicate with three parties at once—the product team, the deployment team, and the customer? (T5 incident handling)
  • An agent is quietly degrading in production (not crashing—its outputs are slowly becoming unreliable). How do you detect it and locate the problem? (T3 + T5, a failure mode unique to AI systems)
  • Under what circumstances should you not use an agent? Give a specific example where you made that call. (Technical judgment; the GSDC syllabus lists "When NOT to use agents" as a standalone teaching point)

② Business and Domain (7 questions) ​

  • Pick a business process you know well and, without looking at documentation, name three exceptions to it, plus who decided to add each exception and when. (B1 business muscle memory—the question this library considers the most discriminating)
  • Now draw the before-and-after difference of this process on a single sheet of paper so a non-technical person understands it at a glance. (B2 process reconstruction)
  • Given the statement "customer service efficiency is low," what checkable numbers would you ask for? (B3 converting goals into data)
  • Describe an experience entering a completely unfamiliar industry: what did you do in the first two weeks, and how did you fill in the industry common sense? (B4 domain cold start)
  • In how many different ways is the same "sales revenue" calculated across your company's three systems? (B5 data definition map)
  • How do you judge whether a pain point a business stakeholder gives you is something they saw firsthand or something they're relaying? (Corresponds to the first gate of the FDE encyclopedia)
  • Describe a time when someone told you to your face that something you built "can't be used." What happened that time? (Business empathy and receiving feedback)

③ Communication and Follow-Through (8 questions) ​

  • Live exercise: I'll describe a business problem in 3 minutes, and you'll compress it into a one-page actionable requirement in 5 minutes. (C1 requirement translation; 53AI's standard is compressing a 30-minute description into 15 minutes)
  • When was the last time you went to a department that wasn't yours to ask people about something you needed to understand? What happened afterward? (C2 organizational navigation)
  • You need data that doesn't belong to you, and the other party says "I can't give you this." What do you do next? (C2 data gatekeeper)
  • Describe a time you showed a half-finished product to a customer. Did anything break during the demo, and how did you handle it? (C3 showing work in progress)
  • How would you explain "model accuracy is 87%" to a non-technical executive? (C1 bidirectional translation; the shape of a good answer is "13 out of every 100 orders need manual fallback")
  • Describe a time you told a customer "this can't be done / it will be delayed / the plan has to be scrapped." How did you deliver it, and what happened to the relationship afterward? (C5 bad news)
  • Can the business side restate the logic of what you delivered in their own words? How do you verify that? (C4 handoff quality; 53AI's ITERATE verification standard)
  • Write a "one-page argument" persuading the product team to include a requirement you discovered in the field in the roadmap. (A standard Palantir DS move, and the entry point of the feedback loop)

④ Judgment (7 questions) ​

  • Given a grand blueprint ("AI fully empowering the production line"), how do you make the first cut? Give quantified reasons: how many people are affected, how frequently, and how long it currently takes. (J1 cut selection)
  • Given a complete business scenario, label each node with its executor: human / AI / automation, with a reason for each label. (J2 human-machine boundary)
  • In your solution, which step did you deliberately not automate? Why? (J3; 53AI's RELATIONSHIP capability, with no counterpart in English-language materials)
  • Should this scenario use a whitelist or a blacklist? What's the fallback strategy? How big is the blast radius? How fast can you roll back? (J3 risk grading)
  • A customer wants a feature: build it into the core platform, or build a custom piece that ships faster? Write out your trade-off reasoning. (J4; Palantir says this trade-off happens every week)
  • Describe a time you made a call with severely insufficient information and a tight deadline. What was your basis at the time, and in hindsight, was it right? (J5 deciding under ambiguity)
  • What's the hardest you've ever fought for something in your life? Why? (A Palantir original question; listening for cost, spontaneity, and detail density)

Tips for Interviewers ​

  • Questions 9, 16, 24, and 30 are the four pillars; when time is short, ask only these four.
  • For question 24, grading doesn't look at the solution—it looks at whether he asks questions first or gives a solution first. Anyone who immediately says "you can use a large model for auto-classification and auto-reply, start with a RAG" is solving the problem he's familiar with, not the customer's problem.
  • Question 30 must be done face-to-face—no video, no written answers. The difference between real and fake is in the pauses.
  • For internal development scenarios, there's an additional one-vote veto: ask "If you were sent to a factory/store to shadow workers for ten days, following the shifts during the day, how would you feel about it?" Anyone who hesitates for more than five seconds is out. This isn't about attitude—it's that his judgment about sources of information is incompatible with the job.

V. Evidence Gaps on This Page (Honestly Noted) ​

  • There are no first-hand sources for the interview loops of frontier labs. The six-round breakdown in Section 2 of this page comes from interview prep platforms; it's consistent with the JDs but unconfirmed officially.
  • No verifiable first-hand accounts of the specific interview formats for FDE roles at Chinese big tech companies (ByteDance, Meituan, Tencent, Didi) were obtained this round. Only the JD requirements are checkable.
  • No public material explains how the "judgment" dimension is scored. Existing certification exams (ADaSci 60 questions, GSDC 40 questions) are all multiple choice, and their validity for assessing delivery-oriented roles is questionable. The judgment questions in Section 4 were designed by this library based on observable behavior and have not been validated at scale.
