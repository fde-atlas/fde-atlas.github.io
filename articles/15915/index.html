The FDE Last Mile: The Real Battlefield for AI Systems Is the Stretch Between Legacy Systems and Production

Original title: FDE 最後一哩路：AI 系統真正的戰場，是舊系統與正式環境那一段

Most AI projects die not in the demo itself, but in the deployment and operations that come after it. Data permissions, security compliance, legacy system integration, production compatibility—this last mile that nobody wants to touch is the FDE's true battlefield, and it's what 91APP deals with every day when implementing CDMP.

The 《The GenAI Divide》 report released by MIT's NANDA research initiative in 2025 ran the numbers: enterprises have invested more than 30 to 40 billion dollars in generative AI, yet 95% of projects have produced no measurable financial return. That same year, Gartner predicted outright that over 40% of agentic AI projects would be canceled by the end of 2027—not because the models aren't smart enough, but because costs spiral out of control, value can't be clearly articulated, and risk management can't keep up.

Put these two numbers together and they point to the same gap: between the scale of enterprise investment in generative AI and the financial returns actually realized, there is a large blank space. Where that gap occurs is the focus of this article.

The scene we see all the time

In helping brands implement CDMP and personalized marketing, we've watched the same scene play out repeatedly: a marketing executive watches the demo, eyes lighting up, and the first question is usually "when can this go live?" What really takes time is the following few months. The member database is a legacy system built ten years ago, the POS and e-commerce backends each maintain their own logic, and the security department has to review everything first before any program is allowed to touch the member data tables. Sometimes we worry that brands place too much hope in that half-hour demo, without leaving time for the unglamorous work that follows.

This is exactly why the Forward Deployed Engineer (FDE) role has been discussed so much over the past two years. A TechCrunch report noted that demand for FDE positions exploded in 2026: at the start of the year only 5% to 10% of companies planned to hire FDEs, by the end of Q2 that had risen to 70%, and the market expects demand to grow by 2,100% by year-end. A report by international media outlet LeadDev noted that FDE job postings grew 729% year over year, a figure drawn from recruiting data that job site Indeed provided to the media; a report by Business Next cited the same numbers. This article is one piece of our FDE series; the other articles in the series cover how requirements can truly be understood, whether speed is really the key to success or failure, the controversy over "reskinned consultants" in the market, and how to tell real FDEs from fake ones. This piece focuses on the most technical part of the series: the deployment work engineers actually handle after the demo ends.

Three layers of breakpoints: what the demo can't show

The FDE's core work is generally divided into three phases: requirements clarification, prototype design, and system deployment and operations. The first two are easy to see and easy to use as selling points; the third is the least discussed, yet the most time-consuming and the most likely to stall a project. This article breaks that deployment work into three layers of breakpoints for comparison, but this is not an exhaustive list—whether business goals are clear and whether the organization is willing to adjust existing processes also determine a project's success or failure:

BreakpointTypical symptom
Data layerERP, POS, and member databases each maintain their own field definitions; the same customer may have three different IDs across three systems
Permissions and compliance layerWhich internal systems the AI can call and which fields it can touch must be reviewed item by item by security and compliance before anything is approved
Environment layerTraffic volumes, edge-case data, and system dependencies that can't be surfaced in the demo environment only reveal themselves once the system runs in production

The Business Next report put it bluntly: "When you have AI call a company's internal systems, who has permission to do what and which data can't be touched—all of this has to be connected one piece at a time." The report also noted that "for many enterprises, the first hurdle in adopting AI is documentation that is messy and outdated." These two statements correspond exactly to the data layer and permissions layer breakpoints; as for the environment layer gap, the report summed it up in one sentence: "These problems are never visible during the demo stage; only when you actually go into the company do they surface."

Looking at these three layers separately, the reasons they stall differ. The difficulty at the data layer is that most brands' member databases, order systems, and POS systems were built at different times, each with its own field naming logic and data formats; the first step is often to reconcile data definitions—confirming whether the "member" in one system and the "customer" in another refer to the same person—before anyone writes code. This sounds trivial, but it often consumes the most time in the entire project. The difficulty at the permissions and compliance layer is that "who can touch what data" ultimately has to be decided by people in the organization; it is rarely a technical specification the engineering team can decide unilaterally. The review process is usually outside the technical team's control, yet it directly determines whether the project can move forward. The difficulty at the environment layer is the most easily underestimated, because the data used in demos is usually a filtered, clean sample and traffic is controlled; production has to face real-world outliers, traffic spikes, and interdependencies between systems. A process that runs smoothly in a test environment may break entirely in production because some legacy system responds slowly or a piece of data doesn't match the expected format. This is also why most FDE teams don't design a perfect solution before going live; instead, they get the system running in a small slice of the production environment first, then gradually expand the scope.

Two case studies: even the people who write the models need a dedicated team for deployment

Case one: the prototype Palantir left behind over a decade ago

The FDE role didn't emerge with this wave of AI hype. A TechCrunch report noted that the practice originated with defense contractor Palantir, which more than a decade ago began sending engineers directly into client organizations to handle data integration, system connectivity, and other work that outside consultants typically don't touch. Palantir's logic was simple: no matter how powerful the software, if no one is willing to spend the time integrating it into the client's existing systems, the software's value can't be realized. This model was later borrowed by the entire industry and became the prototype for today's FDE boom.

Case two: AWS itself established a billion-dollar deployment organization

At the end of June 2026, AWS announced the creation of a dedicated Forward Deployed Engineering organization with a one-billion-dollar investment. According to the official press release on About Amazon, this team will work directly in customer environments alongside customers' business, engineering, and security teams, with the goal of compressing deployment time from months to days or weeks. Among the cases cited in the announcement, the NFL's system went into production within weeks. The announcement also emphasized that these engineers handle "the customer's own data, governance rules, and processes," not a generic demo version. AWS has publicly disclosed partners including the Allen Institute, Cox Automotive, the NBA, the NFL, Ricoh, and Southwest Airlines.

This case is worth examining closely: AWS is one of the top providers of cloud and AI infrastructure, with no shortage of models or compute, yet it still chose to establish a billion-dollar dedicated team to handle "integrating systems into customers' production environments." This shows that even with shared methodologies and reusable working patterns, data structures, permission rules, and existing systems still have to be re-verified for each customer's environment—they can't be solved simply by packaging up generic technical capabilities.

This is exactly what 91APP handles when implementing CDMP

In our work helping brands implement CDMP and personalized marketing, the parts that take the most time overlap heavily with the three layers of breakpoints that FDEs deal with. A brand's member data is usually scattered across its website, in-store POS, and customer service systems. The first step is typically to consolidate this data under a consistent identity-matching logic, confirming that the same person's purchase records across different channels can be viewed as merged — this is the data layer. Next comes determining which roles can see which member tags and which data can only be used in specific marketing contexts — this is the permissions layer. Finally, most automated marketing scripts need to be validated under real traffic before going live, rather than relying only on small-scale test results — this is the environment layer.

The 91APP official blog has also compiled related implementation experience in the past. For example, the gap between online and offline is usually discovered by customers before reports show it discusses how, when ERP inventory figures and e-commerce backends are out of sync, what is truly affected is the consumer's shopping experience, not just a numerical discrepancy in a report. Similarly, many AI applications underperform because the root of the problem isn't the model — it's the tables it can't read, or shouldn't read, which is the same issue as the permissions and data layer breakpoints discussed in this article. We believe the real value of CDMP is not in showcasing how smart AI is, but in having the ability to put a data solution into the real environment where customer member data and order systems are intertwined, and keep it running stably.

Writing implementation capability into procurement requirements matters more than watching a demo

When brands evaluate AI or data implementation solutions, the demo results are usually not the problem; what truly deserves time and verification is the implementation phase. The following steps are recommended to be done before signing the contract, rather than discovered after the project has started.

  • First, conduct a system inventory listing everything that needs to be integrated: including ERP, POS, member databases, and payment systems, checking one by one which systems have open APIs and which field definitions are inconsistent with each other. This list determines how much time will be spent on data integration early in the project; the earlier the inventory, the better you can avoid discovering blockers midway.
  • Move security and compliance reviews up to the requirements interview stage: don't wait until the technical solution is finalized to submit for review. Constraints raised by security teams often change how data access is designed; the later the review, the higher the cost of rework.
  • Design data permissions on a least-privilege principle: don't grant a newly implemented solution access to all fields at once. Start with the data scope that is genuinely needed, then expand based on actual scenarios. This is also the approach most security teams find easier to approve.
  • Schedule production environment validation into the project timeline — don't leave it until the week before launch: a limited launch with monitoring alerts and rollback conditions configured, observed for a period before gradually expanding scope, will catch environment layer issues more easily than a one-time full launch.
  • Choose a technical partner with real implementation and operations experience: asking about their past legacy system integration cases reflects real execution capability far better than judging how polished a demo looks.

Implementation isn't the boring part — it's where value actually happens

The half-hour demo doesn't decide the outcome; what decides it is the months after the demo, doing work no one applauds. Legacy system integration, data permission design, production environment validation — these steps may seem tedious, but they are the key to whether an AI or data solution can truly create value for a brand. For Taiwanese brands, this is actually an opportunity: the technical barrier is being crossed by more and more teams, and what will differentiate them next is who is more willing to spend time on this unapplauded final mile.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q1: What is an FDE (Forward Deployed Engineer), and how does it differ from a typical consultant? A1: An FDE is an engineer who embeds directly in the customer's organization and is responsible for actually getting an AI or software system to launch. The biggest difference from traditional consultants is that consultants usually stop at recommendations, leaving execution to the customer's own team; FDEs personally participate in the implementation work of data integration, system integration, and production environment validation, until the system truly runs stably.

Q2: Why do AI projects with great demos often get stuck at production launch? A2: The data used in the demo stage is usually curated, and traffic is controlled. The production environment faces real data anomalies, peak traffic, and inter-system dependencies — situations that are hard to fully test in the demo stage and only surface once you actually enter production.

Q3: Can small and medium Taiwanese brands without a dedicated FDE team still succeed at AI or data implementation? A3: Yes, but they need to move the three-layer breakpoint checks ahead of project kickoff, including system inventory, the timing of security reviews, and data permission design principles. With limited resources, we recommend starting with a scenario small enough in scope and a single data source, with a fixed budget and a stop-loss point — there's no need to implement company-wide systems all at once. The governance window can also be temporarily handled by an external consultant or a single internal owner; you don't need a full security and compliance organization to get started. Choosing a technical partner with real implementation and operations experience can also fill the experience gap of the internal team.

Q4: How long do system implementation and operations usually take? A4: The duration depends on the complexity of existing systems and the scope of data integration; there is no fixed answer. Taking AWS's publicly disclosed Forward Deployed Engineering model as an example, the goal is to compress deployment time from months to days or weeks, but that pace targets tasks of a specific scope; actual implementation timelines still need to be assessed based on each brand's current systems.

Q5: When should security and data permission reviews begin when implementing a data solution like CDMP? A5: We recommend involving security and compliance teams at the requirements interview stage, rather than submitting for review only after the technical solution is finalized. The earlier the scope and restrictions of data access are clarified, the lower the cost of subsequent rework and adjustments.

Q6: How do the implementation challenges discussed in this article relate to the role of 91APP CDMP? A6: When 91APP helps brands implement CDMP and personalized marketing, it handles exactly the three layers of breakpoints discussed in this article: consolidating member data scattered across different channels, designing data access permission rules, and ensuring marketing scripts run stably under real traffic in the production environment.
