New grad forward deployed engineer: who gets hired

FDE Times · 2026-10-08 · 6 min read

Summary

The article examines whether new graduates can enter forward deployed engineer roles directly. Palantir and Applied Intuition both run new-grad FDE hiring, but only for candidates graduating between December 2026 and spring or summer 2027, with non-skill filters like office location and security clearance. Recruiting platform Paraform argues FDE work requires senior judgement and advises against junior-level postings. The author argues these positions are not truly contradictory: new grads take ownership within small teams rather than alone. For most engineers, especially in Vietnam, the realistic path is 12-18 months of real deployment experience first, choosing customer-facing work over ticket-driven roles.

Key points

  • Palantir and Applied Intuition hire new-grad FDEs, but only within strict graduation windows.
  • Paraform warns FDE work needs senior judgement and advises against junior postings.
  • Non-skill filters include security clearance and five-day office requirements.
  • Small-team structures may compensate for new grads' lack of judgement.
  • A 12-18 month detour with real deployment experience is the realistic route.

Junior FDE roles are real but narrow and tied to graduation calendars; for most engineers the realistic route is a detour they can start preparing now.

In brief

  • Palantir and Applied Intuition are both hiring new-grad FDEs, but only people graduating within a fixed window, from December 2026 to spring or summer 2027.
  • Paraform warns that FDE work needs senior judgement; Applied Intuition puts new hires in small teams, which can be read as a way to fill that gap.
  • For most engineers in Vietnam, the more realistic path is to build 12-18 months of deployment experience before moving into an FDE role.

Palantir, which describes itself as the pioneer of placing engineers inside customer organisations, is hiring Forward Deployed Software Engineers who have not yet graduated. The job post promises new-grad candidates that they will be given responsibility from their very first day.

This year, too, Paraform, a recruiting platform, advised companies not to post FDE roles at junior level. Its reason was brief: FDE work demands senior-level judgement.

The two messages seem to clash, and they raise the question many final-year students and engineers with a year or two of experience in Vietnam are asking: do junior FDE roles actually exist?

They do, but the door is narrow, opens only by season, and appears designed to make up for the judgement new hires still lack. For most engineers in Vietnam, the realistic route is a detour, and that detour can be prepared for now.

The new-grad door is real, and it runs on a calendar

Palantir’s Commercial posting, based in Chicago, describes a job little different from that of an experienced FDE. The hire will sit with customers to design and build software and LLM workflows, end to end. The stated bar is production quality: software customers actually use.

Applied Intuition has also opened a Forward Deployed Engineer New Grad role. The company says new hires will work in small teams and own high-impact projects from start to finish, in its Sunnyvale office five days a week.

The two posts share the most important detail: timing. Palantir only accepts people graduating in December 2026 or spring 2027; Applied Intuition accepts those graduating in December 2026 or summer 2027. If you graduated in 2025 and have worked for a year, you are not in this candidate pool, however good your skills.

The real filter is not the stack

Read the requirements closely and something surprising emerges. Palantir lists a computer science degree only as “preferred”. The hard requirement is proficiency in at least one programming language, such as Python, Java, C++, TypeScript/JavaScript or equivalent.

Applied Intuition puts communication first. Rather than listing frameworks, its post asks for people who communicate well and genuinely enjoy working directly with customers to untangle technical problems.

Picture two candidates. The first has a CV packed with technology names, but every project is coursework. The second uses only Python, but built a tool for the university’s academic affairs office, sat listening to staff describe their process, and revised it three times based on their feedback.

For a post like Applied Intuition’s, the second candidate has a story to tell in the interview. The first does not.

If it needs senior judgement, why hire new grads?

Paraform’s warning is not unreasonable. FDE work forces you to decide things no ticket covers: when to say “no” to a customer, which parts must be solid, and which only need to work for the demo.

Paraform also notes that level and scope explain most of the pay gap between FDEs, meaning the market is paying for exactly that judgement.

So why does Palantir dare to hand out responsibility on day one? One plausible reading is that responsibility does not necessarily mean being left alone. The small-team model Applied Intuition describes suggests new hires own a piece of work in full, but within a group rather than standing alone in front of the customer.

Seen that way, the two sides do not really contradict each other. Paraform is right that FDE work needs senior judgement, and the new-grad programmes do not deny it.

The strict graduation-date conditions show these companies open the door only to a specific cohort each round. For everyone else, Paraform’s warning remains the more accurate picture of the market.

Barriers that have nothing to do with skill

Put the job posts side by side and most of the filters turn out not to concern how well you code.

Palantir’s UK Government version requires candidates to hold security clearance or be able to obtain it, a significant barrier for foreigners that a strong CV can hardly offset. Applied Intuition requires working from its Sunnyvale office all five days of the week.

Given these conditions, if you are graduating within the window, the two posts worth reading closely are Palantir’s Commercial role and Applied Intuition’s; the UK Government version adds the clearance hurdle.

For engineers studying and working in Vietnam, the graduation window and the requirement to work in an office abroad are the two conditions to weigh carefully before pouring effort into an application.

The 12-18 month detour is not the losing route

FDE Academy, a training provider with every reason to paint the entrance as wide, still concedes that the direct door for new graduates is narrow.

In its view, new graduates can still get into FDE work, but usually after 12 to 18 months of employment with real deployment experience. It describes the gap mainly as one of preparation rather than talent.

The 12-18 month figure should not be treated as a rule. The word worth keeping is “deployment”: what counts is experience putting software into real environments for real users, not the number of years spent closing tickets.

In Vietnam, this kind of experience sits in places few people would call FDE work: an outsourcing project where you deal directly with the client, an onsite stint rolling out a system for a bank, or a small startup where you write code and also hear users complain.

Choosing between a prestigious job that only hands you tickets and a less prominent one where you meet customers, anyone aiming for FDE work should lean towards the second.

How to prepare so you don’t miss the next round

If you are still studying, the first task is to check the calendar. New-grad rounds state their graduation windows explicitly, so follow the careers pages of companies with FDE programmes from about a year before you graduate, rather than waiting until you have your degree.

When reading a post, look for phrases such as “embed with customers”, “end-to-end” and “work directly with customers”. They show whether the role is genuinely FDE or just software engineering under a new name. Read the lines about location and security clearance carefully before spending an evening polishing your CV.

On your CV, do not stop at a list of technologies. Describe one project in sequence: who the users were, what decision they needed to make, what you built end to end, and what you changed after hearing their feedback.

That kind of description shows recruiters early signs of judgement, precisely what Paraform worries new hires lack.

The new-grad door will stay narrow. But the people given responsibility on day one tend to be those who practised taking responsibility before anyone handed it to them.
