1,000 Forward Deployed Engineer Jobs, Analysed: What the Postings Actually Say

Vibe Engines · 2026-08-01 · 20 min read

Summary

This article reviews a Bloomberry census of roughly 1,000 forward deployed engineer postings, arguing the title hides three distinct jobs: builder roles (~60%), pre-sales roles (~30%), and internal GTM/RevOps tooling roles (~10%), with postings rarely saying which one you're applying for. FDE-titled postings grew 1,165% year-over-year (Jan–Oct 2025), with a $173,816 median base salary and 68% requiring travel. The author offers practical ways to identify the real job — compensation shape, customer count, product-feedback loop, travel terms, and interview loop — while flagging the census's limits: the 60/30/10 split is judgment, not measurement, growth figures may count renamed solutions-engineering jobs, and pay data covers only postings with published ranges.

Key points

  • One FDE title covers three jobs: builder, pre-sales, internal tools
  • Postings grew 1,165% YoY; median base pay is $173,816
  • Only 8% mention OTE and 0% mention quotas, so pay terms can't identify sales-shaped roles
  • Top skills: Python 66%, TypeScript 35%, AWS 32%, AI agents 35%
  • Interview loops and travel terms reveal the job better than the posting

Reading a thousand job postings is a better way to understand a role than reading a thousand opinions about it. A posting census of the forward deployed engineer market found what anyone who has interviewed for several of these already suspected: the title covers at least three distinct jobs, and the posting frequently does not tell you which one you are applying for.1

What this measures that the rungs below argue about

Rungs 1 and 2 are claims about how value is created. This rung is the only one on the ladder that counts something: a census of roughly a thousand job postings, read as data about what employers are actually asking for rather than as commentary on what the role should be.

The central finding is that one title covers several genuinely different jobs. Roughly a third of the postings are pre-sales-shaped — the work is demonstrating, scoping and winning, and success is measured in deals; others are deployment-shaped, where the work is making the thing function inside one customer's systems; and others are GTM or RevOps internal-tools roles, where the work is automating a company's own go-to-market workflows. The compensation structure, the skills rewarded and what "good" looks like differ across all three, and the title does not distinguish them.

Which turns the census into something directly usable, and this is why it earns a place next to two argumentative pieces. If the variants are distinguishable from the posting's own language — whether it talks about quota, pipeline and demos, or about integrations and production support, or about internal workflows and tooling — then you can tell which job you are interviewing for before the interview, and ask the questions that confirm it. The failure this prevents is specific and common: accepting a pre-sales role while expecting to build, which is a mismatch nobody misled you about.

The three species under one title

SpeciesWhat the posting emphasisesWhat your week looks like
BuilderShip in the customer’s environment; own the full arc of a deployment9Few customers, deep, milestone-driven. Production systems someone depends on.
Pre-salesDemos, proofs of concept, technical qualification, supporting the account teamMany customers, shallow, quarter-driven. Sometimes with a quota or variable pay attached.
GTM / RevOps internal toolsInternal automation and tooling for go-to-market and revenue-operations teams, under an FDE titleInternal customers, workflow-driven, closer to a business-systems team than to customer deployment.

Roughly a third of postings using the title are the pre-sales shape.1 That is not a criticism — it is a legitimate, well-paid career with its own progression — but it is a different rhythm from delivery work, and accepting one while expecting the other is the most common source of unhappiness in this role.

What every posting asks for

Across frontier labs, AI infrastructure companies and vertical startups, the same cluster recurs.

The technical list

  • Strong general engineering — usually Python and TypeScript, plus whatever the customer runs.
  • LLM application patterns: retrieval, tool use, agents, structured output.
  • Evaluation. Rarely a named keyword in the census tally (Python leads at 66%), but asked for in prose across postings, and the least-taught skill anywhere.
  • Integration reality: auth, pagination, rate limits, idempotency, other people’s schemas.
  • Deployment where you do not own the cloud — VPC, on-prem, sometimes air-gapped.
  • Increasingly: MCP servers and agent harnesses named as deliverables.10

The list that decides who is hired

  • Reframing a vague ask into a problem worth solving, before writing anything.
  • Learning an unfamiliar domain in days, well enough to be useful.
  • Saying no to a customer request and keeping the relationship stronger.
  • Judging when to ship something rough and when that would be negligent.
  • Being the only person who understands the system — then engineering your way out of it.

The technical bar is a strong generalist rather than a specialist; the differentiator is the second column. First Round’s account of hiring for this role puts the engineering bar at staff level, plus a temperament most strong engineers have never been asked to develop.11 If you are technically strong and have never sat in front of an unhappy customer, that gap is what to work on — not your algorithms.

Evaluation is not in the census’s keyword tally, which favours named tools such as Python (66%) and AWS (32%), but postings ask for it in prose again and again, and it has almost no training material aimed at practitioners. Turning “it should be accurate” into a golden set, a human-agreement ceiling, a calibrated judge and a CI gate is a learnable, teachable skill — and being able to describe that pipeline in an interview is disproportionately effective. The method is in evals for client work.

Reading a posting properly

Find the compensation shape

A quota, OTE or bookings-linked bonus is a strong hint that the role is pre-sales-shaped, though postings are not consistent about it. Neither answer is disqualifying; not knowing before you accept is.

Count the customers

“Own the full arc of a deployment” is a builder posting.9 “Support the account team across a portfolio” is not. One deep engagement is a different job from six shallow ones.

Look for the product loop

Anthropic’s posting publishes a 40/30/30 split with 30% feeding learnings back into the product.10 A posting with no such loop is describing consultancy, and the work will not compound.

Check the travel line

Some roles are contractually 25–50% travel.15 It is uncompensated cost, and at 40% the effective hourly rate is not what the headline says.

Read the interview loop as data

A decomposition round scored on your questions means a builder role. A live product demo and a mock discovery call means pre-sales. The loop describes the job more honestly than the posting does.

“What fraction of last quarter was spent pre-sale versus post-sale?” and “What did the last deployment contribute back to the product?” Ninety seconds, and they distinguish the three species better than any job description. Both answers are fine; a vague answer to the second one is the informative case.

Why the market looks like this

The posting growth is not a fashion cycle, and it is not really about hiring. It is a response to a measurement problem: capability is broadly available, enterprise outcomes are not, and a widely-reported finding put roughly 95% of enterprise AI pilots at no measurable P&L impact.17 The strategic answer several companies reached simultaneously was to spend margin on deployment depth — a16z’s services-led-growth argument — and the operational expression of that decision is a job posting.8

Hence the capital: OpenAI standing up a deployment organisation,5 Anthropic and Blackstone launching a $1.5B venture,6 Microsoft committing $2.5B to embed engineers with customers.7 A thousand postings is what that strategy looks like from the outside.

Posting counts measure demand signals, not filled roles, and title inflation is real in a hot market — some of those postings are existing solutions-engineering jobs renamed. Treat the census as a map of what employers are asking for, which is genuinely useful for preparing, and not as a headcount. The compensation picture is bimodal and is handled separately in the salary breakdown.

The loop is unusual, and practisable

How the Bloomberry census was assembled

The census is a blog analysis by Henley Wing Chiu of Bloomberry, published on November 18, 2025 and last updated on January 25, 2026.

  • Source the postings. 1,000 job postings for forward deployed engineers, supplied by Revealera, a job-data provider whose clients are financial firms. The post does not say which job boards were covered, when the postings were collected, or how duplicates were handled.
  • Measure growth by title. Postings titled forward deployed engineer from January to October 2025 are compared with the same months of 2024, giving a 1,165% year-over-year increase; October 2025 was the busiest month the author had recorded.
  • Classify each posting by its work. The author sorts postings into three types from what they describe and attaches typical markers to each, such as coding share, travel and posted pay. The split is the author's judgment, not a field in the data.
  • Count requirements. Skills, responsibilities, experience, team placement and industry are tallied as shares, each over the postings that state that item.
  • Read pay from disclosed ranges. The salary median uses only postings that published a range, and the post treats it as base pay.
  • Add a career-path sample. Separately, 100 LinkedIn profiles of people working as FDEs show where they came from; the author presents it as a sample, not a survey of the field.

The three types, as the census describes them

  • Builder FDE (60%). An engineer embedded with customers to build, deploy and maintain production systems, closer to a senior software engineer than to a consultant: 70–90% coding and 30–50% travel.
  • Sales Engineer+ (30%). A solutions or sales engineering job under a new title: demos, proofs of concept and product configuration during the sale, with the build handed to implementation teams; 30–40% coding, under 20% travel, and commission on top of base pay.
  • Internal Tools Builder (10%). A go-to-market engineering or RevOps role building automation for internal sales, marketing and customer-success teams, which the author argues should not carry the title at all; under 5% travel.

How it relates to the other counts on this page

The 1,206 postings across 669 companies and the $185K median base quoted elsewhere on this page come from an earlier snapshot of a different census, Plank's, which uses a stricter definition of the role (its live page has since been updated). Bloomberry's median is $173,816. The two should not be pooled, because different sources, windows and definitions give different denominators.

FDE, solutions engineer, solutions architect

The census draws its sharpest line between the titles by timing: a solutions engineer's work ends when the customer agrees the product will work, and a forward deployed engineer's work begins there. Its Sales Engineer+ type is the overlap, the work long done under sales or solutions engineer titles. Solutions architects usually design reference architectures and advise across many accounts rather than ship production code inside one. What separates the Builder FDE is its coding share and its ownership of a production deployment at one customer.

Two findings change how the rest of this page reads. Most postings are builder roles, not sales ones. And on pay the census contradicts itself: it describes the Sales Engineer+ type as earning commission and carrying a quota, yet counts only 8% of all postings mentioning on-target earnings and 0% mentioning a quota. Even if every OTE mention were a sales-shaped posting, at most about a quarter of them could carry one, so the type must have been recognized mostly from the work described, not from pay terms.

The other limits are partly the author's own. The 60/30/10 split is a classification, not a measurement. Pay covers only postings with a published range and only base salary; experience and industry shares cover only postings that state them, and 79% name no industry at all. The growth figure is by title, so renamed solutions-engineering jobs count as new demand, and one year's count cannot tell a hiring cycle from lasting change. And a posting is what an employer advertises, not a filled role.

The counts make the census's inconsistency concrete. Even if all 80 postings that mention on-target earnings were Sales Engineer+ roles, they would cover 80 of 300, about 27 percent, of a type the census describes as paid on commission, and not one posting mentions a quota. So the type must have been assigned mostly from the work described, not from pay terms.

The growth multiple needs the same care. A 1,165 percent rise is a ratio of title counts, so a team that renamed ten solutions-engineering postings in 2025 adds ten to the numerator while the 2024 count never saw them: renaming and new demand look identical. And the $11,184 gap between the two medians is not a pay trend or a premium for either definition of the role; the two censuses use different sources, windows and definitions, so the difference mixes all three and should not be read as anything until they are put on one denominator.

Common mistakes when acting on the FDE job census

The census is most useful as a way to read one posting well. Most misuses come from applying its aggregates to a single job, or its labels to a different question.

  • Using pay terms to spot the sales-shaped roleOnly 8% of postings mention on-target earnings and none mentions a quota, yet 30% were classed as sales-shaped. So a posting with no commission language can still be a sales job. Read the work described instead: demos and proofs of concept against owning a production deployment, the coding share, and whether the team sits in sales.
  • Preparing for the wrong seniorityFirst Round's account of hiring describes a staff-level engineering bar, while 60% of census postings that state experience ask for three to five years. The two sources describe different slices of the market; prepare for the bar the specific posting and its interview loop set, not for either average.
  • Treating the skill tally as a syllabusA tally counts the words employers write, which favours named tools: Python at 66%, AWS at 32%. Practices such as evaluation or careful integration are usually written as prose, so a low or missing count says little about how much the interview will probe them.
  • Comparing an offer with the medianThe $173,816 median is base pay, from postings that published a range, while 70% of postings mention equity and sales-shaped roles add commission. Compare base with base and the full package with the full package, within the same role type.
  • Hiring under one title for three jobsFor employers the census is a warning: a posting called forward deployed engineer will draw candidates expecting any of three different jobs. State the type, the coding share, the travel and where the team reports, or expect offers declined and early departures from people who wanted a different one.

What a posting census can and cannot tell you

Job postings measure what organisations say they want, which is related to the work but not identical to it. A posting is written to attract candidates and frequently to satisfy an internal process; the requirement list is often aspirational, the seniority label is often negotiable, and the actual day-to-day is set by the team's current pressures rather than by the text. So this is a census of demand as advertised.

It is also a snapshot. A count taken at one moment in a fast-moving market cannot separate durable structure from a hiring cycle, and the growth figures that make the market look dramatic are precisely the ones most sensitive to the window chosen. Postings also over-represent organisations with formal hiring processes, and under-represent the small teams where this kind of work is often most concentrated.

What survives all of that is the taxonomy, and it is the durable contribution. That one title spans a sales-shaped job, a deployment job and a product job is a structural observation, not a statistic — it does not depend on the exact proportions and it explains most of the confusion in how the role is discussed. Read alongside rung 2, the two fit together: a company trading margin for moat needs all three of those variants, values them differently, and has an incentive to describe them with one word.

Questions people ask about 1,000 FDE Jobs, Analysed

What is the difference between a forward deployed engineer and a solutions engineer?

Job postings use one title for both. In Bloomberry's census of 1,000 forward deployed engineer postings, 60% describe builder roles that spend 70 to 90% of the time coding, build and maintain production systems inside a customer, travel 30 to 50% of the time and post about $140,000 to $250,000. Another 30% are what the author calls Sales Engineer+, solutions or sales engineering under a new title: demos, proofs of concept and configuration during the sale, 30 to 40% coding, under 20% travel, and $120,000 to $200,000 plus commission. The author's short version is that a solutions engineer's job ends when the customer agrees the product will work, and an FDE's job starts there.

How fast are forward deployed engineer job postings growing?

Bloomberry's analysis of 1,000 postings from the job-data provider Revealera found that postings titled forward deployed engineer grew 1,165% year over year, comparing January to October 2025 with the same months of 2024, and that October 2025 was the busiest month the author had recorded. A 1,165% increase means roughly 12.7 times as many postings. Because the count is by title, some of the growth is existing solutions-engineering or implementation jobs being renamed, and a count taken in one fast-moving year cannot separate a hiring cycle from lasting demand. Postings also measure what employers advertise, not roles actually filled.

How much do forward deployed engineers earn, according to job postings?

Bloomberry's census puts the median posted base salary at $173,816, computed only from postings that disclosed a range. By type, builder roles post about $140,000 to $250,000, sales-shaped roles $120,000 to $200,000 plus commission, and internal tools roles $100,000 to $180,000. Seventy percent of postings mention equity and 8% mention on-target earnings; the census counts no posting that mentions a quota, although its own description of the sales-shaped type lists one. An earlier snapshot of a separate census by Plank, with a stricter definition and 1,206 postings across 669 companies, reports a median base of $185,000. Both figures are base pay from employers that publish ranges, not a random sample.

What skills do forward deployed engineer job postings ask for most?

In Bloomberry's census, the most-named technical skills are Python in 66% of postings, TypeScript in 35% and AWS in 32%, followed by GCP at 22%, Azure at 18%, Kubernetes at 14% and Docker at 12%. On the AI side, AI agents appear in 35%, LLMs in 31% and retrieval-augmented generation in 12%, with OpenAI, Anthropic, PyTorch and LangChain each named in under a tenth. The most common responsibilities are working directly with customers, in 55% of postings, building and deploying AI or ML systems at 37%, and integrating systems and APIs at 32%. The experience asked for is mostly three to five years.

How senior are forward deployed engineer roles, and where do they sit?

Among Bloomberry's postings that state an experience level, 60% ask for three to five years, 20% for six to eight, 12% for up to two and 8% for nine or more, so the role is mostly mid-level rather than entry-level or staff. Forty-five percent sit in a dedicated FDE team, 38% in the engineering organization and 14% in go-to-market or sales, with smaller shares in customer success and professional services. Fifty-eight percent are at companies with 11 to 200 employees, and 68% of roles require travel. Where a role sits can be a useful clue to which of the three types it really is.

4 questions to test what you just read

8% of 1,000 = 80 postings mention OTE, and Sales Engineer+ is 300 postings, so at most 80/300 ≈ 27% of them. Yet the census describes that type as paid with commission and carrying a quota, which its own 0% quota count contradicts, so pay language is a weak test of the type.

If postings grew 1,165% year over year, how many postings in January–October 2025 correspond to 100 in the same months of 2024?

Show answer

A 1,165% increase means 100 + 1,165 = 1,265, about 12.65 times as many, not 11.65. Because the count is by title, part of that jump can be existing jobs renamed rather than new demand.

Why can't the $173,816 median and Plank's $185K median be averaged into one market figure?

Show answer

They come from different samples with different definitions: Bloomberry's 1,000 FDE-titled postings and Plank's 1,206 stricter-definition postings across 669 companies, each computed only over postings that disclosed pay, and both base salary only. Averaging them hides that the denominators differ.

The Builder FDE, 60% of postings, posted at about $140–250K. Sales Engineer+ is 30–40% coding with under 20% travel, and the Internal Tools Builder is an internal go-to-market role with under 5% travel.
