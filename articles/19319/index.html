Scale AI FDE Interview Questions and Process: Debugging, System Design

Original title: Scale AI FDE 面试题与流程：调试、系统设计

FDE Times · 2026-10-17 · 5 min read

Summary

Drawing on guides from Aced (formerly Exponent), Final Round AI, and techinterview.org, this article breaks down Scale AI's interview process for Forward Deployed Engineers. It starts with two HackerRounds, one being a 60-minute project-style coding test rated hard, followed by onsite rounds covering coding, system design, debugging an unfamiliar codebase in about 45 minutes, and a behavioral round focused on ownership and speed. Communication is scored throughout rather than in a dedicated round. A fictional annotator-agreement script illustrates a debugging approach: reproduce with a small input, then fix a mutable default argument, a wrong denominator, and single-label handling in risk order. The author argues the process mirrors actual FDE fieldwork and recommends timed project practice, recording your explanations, and preparing ownership stories.

Key points

  • Two HackerRounds open the process; one is a 60-minute project-style test rated hard.
  • Onsite rounds: two coding, system design, 45-minute unfamiliar-codebase debugging, and behavioral.
  • Communication is scored in every round; silent debugging reads as guessing.
  • Debug strategy: reproduce with small input, fix mutable default argument first, then denominator.
  • Prep tips: timed project practice, recorded walkthroughs, ownership and speed stories, resume aligned to job posts.

Key Takeaways

  • The process starts with two HackerRank rounds, one of which is a 60-minute online assessment with a single project-style coding question.
  • The onsite interviews include coding, system design, debugging an unfamiliar codebase, and a behavioral round that tests ownership and speed.
  • There is no separate communication round, but communication is scored throughout the entire process, and coding in silence is an easy way to lose points.

You open the HackerRank link, a 60-minute countdown starts, and the question is not "invert a binary tree" but a small project that has to actually run. According to a guide from Aced (formerly Exponent), this is exactly what the online assessment for Scale AI's Forward Deployed Engineer (FDE) role looks like: a single project-style coding question, with the difficulty described as "hard."

That is just one of the two HackerRank rounds you need to pass before the onsite interviews. Final Round AI puts the assessment at 50 minutes and notes that not all candidates go through the process in the same order, so any claims about duration and order should be taken as reference only.

Read the descriptions of each interview round carefully and you'll find they map one-to-one onto an FDE's weekly work: taking over a system someone else wrote, getting it running, and then explaining to others why it broke. Preparing for these tasks is practicing the job itself.

Every Round Simulates a Day at the Customer Site

According to techinterview.org, the virtual onsite consists of four to five rounds: two coding rounds, one system design round, one debugging round, and one behavioral round. In the debugging round, you are given a codebase you've never seen, plus a failing test or a description of symptoms, and you have about 45 minutes to find and fix the problem.

According to Aced, the hiring manager round is a behavioral interview, similar in content to the credo round. techinterview.org says this conversation centers on Scale's values: ownership, and continuing to move fast even when everything around you keeps changing.

The job postings make it clear why these rounds fit the role. Scale's GenAI FDE posting requires rapid experimentation, continuous iteration to meet customer needs, and the ability to explain complex technical concepts to both technical and non-technical audiences.

No Communication Round, Because Every Round Is a Communication Round

Aced states explicitly that communication skills are assessed throughout the entire process, with no dedicated round. Another Aced page advises candidates to clearly explain their reasoning and decisions during technical rounds. techinterview.org puts it more bluntly: debugging in silence looks like you're just guessing.

From this you can infer: a candidate who silently fixes three bugs may make a worse impression than one who fixes two but clearly explains where the third bug is and how they plan to handle it. The latter demonstrates exactly what customers want to see when a system goes down.

Imagine 45 Minutes with an Unfamiliar Codebase

Suppose the debugging question is a script that calculates agreement rates between annotators—the kind of work closely tied to labeled data. The code snippet below is a fictional exercise example, not a real interview question:

def agreement_rate(rows, labels_by_task={}):
    for r in rows:
        labels_by_task.setdefault(r["task_id"], []).append(r["label"])
    agreed = 0
    for task_id, labels in labels_by_task.items():
        if len(set(labels)) == 1:
            agreed += 1
    return agreed / len(rows)

Instead of reading line by line to find bugs, first construct a three-line input: task A has two "cat" labels, task B has one "dog" label. The function returns 2/3, about 0.667. If the convention is to only count tasks with at least two annotators, the correct answer is 1/1; if all tasks count, the correct answer is 2/2. Under no interpretation of the requirements do you get 0.667.

This wrong number points directly to three bugs. The denominator counts rows instead of tasks; tasks with only one label are counted as agreement. Most dangerous of all,

labels_by_task={}

is a mutable default argument: when you call the function a second time with different data, the labels from the previous call are still in the dictionary, and the results get skewed.

The order of fixes is itself part of the answer. The mutable default argument silently corrupts data across runs, so it comes first. The denominator comes second. The single-label case is really a business question—you should ask the interviewer about the convention rather than guess.

While working, narrate your thinking at a steady pace: reproduce with a small input, expected 1.0 but got 0.667, suspect the denominator, fix it, run it again.

If time runs out with a bug still unfixed, state where it is and what test you would add to catch it. That way, "not finished" becomes evidence of ownership.

Where Do LLMs Fit in the Infrastructure?

Scale's GenAI FDE posting requires rapid experimentation for customers and clear explanations to non-technical audiences. So when practicing system design on your own, pick questions involving LLMs and practice explaining your design to both types of audiences.

A common trap is drawing a box labeled "LLM" in the middle and stopping there. Sundeep Teki's FDE interview guide says the most common mistake is jumping straight to a solution without asking a single clarifying question.

Take this question as an example: a customer wants to use an LLM to classify support tickets. Before you start drawing, first clarify whether tickets arrive in batches or as a stream, how costly a mislabeled ticket is, and who consumes the output. Only after these three points are clear should you start drawing.

Tickets enter a queue, workers call the model with retries and timeouts, and low-confidence results are routed to human review. Every time you modify the prompt or swap the model, you run a small eval set with standard labels—which echoes the rapid iteration mentioned in the job posting.

You should also explain the fallback plan when the model API goes down, because that is exactly the kind of incident an FDE working directly with customers will encounter.

How to Practice Over a Few Weeks

Final Round AI observes that the HackerRank assessment does not reward slow, meticulous problem solvers. So swap half of your LeetCode time for timed 60-minute project-style practice: read a CSV file, call a mocked API, write out the results, and include tests.

Practice debugging on unfamiliar repositories, time yourself each session, and record yourself explaining your process. On playback, you'll immediately hear where you went silent for too long.

For the behavioral and hiring manager rounds, prepare two types of stories: one where you proactively took on the hardest part of the work, and one where you had to move fast while requirements kept changing. Each story should include specific decisions and measurable outcomes. Also prepare a clear answer to "why do you want to be an FDE."

Your resume should speak the same language. A sentence like "took over another team's data pipeline, found and fixed a bug causing reporting errors, and explained the root cause to the customer" fits the job posting better than any list of frameworks. Wherever you read FDE job descriptions, watch for whether "strong coding skills" and "communication skills" appear side by side.

Traps That Trip Up Strong Candidates

One easy trap to fall into is trying to fix every bug in the debugging round, running out of time halfway through, and explaining nothing. Another is answering system design questions assuming the LLM is always right and always available, omitting evals and fallback plans. When telling behavioral stories, only saying "the team did X" without clarifying your own contribution will also weaken your answer.

The most insidious trap is treating the hiring manager round as a formality.

At its core, Scale AI's FDE interview is a microcosm of a week at the customer site. Prepare as if preparing for that week, not as if preparing for an exam.
