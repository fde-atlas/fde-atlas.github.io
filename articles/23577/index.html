Thomson Reuters CoCounsel FDE Job Requirements

Original title: Thomson Reuters CoCounsel FDE 岗位要求

Summary

Thomson Reuters posted job ads for its CoCounsel forward deployed engineering team, with the Senior role unusually requiring a JD, formal legal training, or equivalent experience in legal practice. The article argues this reflects how legal AI must be evaluated: outputs are judged on whether practicing lawyers would accept them, covering legal accuracy, citations, and hallucinations. Evaluation appears even at the mid-level and grows in responsibility with seniority, while RAG and agent pipeline work remains core at every level. Stable eval sets also underpin the company's multi-model strategy, letting teams compare new models objectively. The author recommends building a domain-specific RAG project with a citation eval set as portfolio preparation.

Key points

  • Senior CoCounsel FDE role requires legal education or equivalent practice experience.
  • Evaluation of LLM outputs starts at mid-level, not just senior roles.
  • CoCoBench scores outputs by whether practicing lawyers would accept them.
  • Fabricated citations should first prompt a check of retrieval quality.
  • Portfolio projects should include eval sets with per-metric scores, not chatbot demos.

Key Takeaways

  • A Senior job posting for the CoCounsel FDE team on Dice requires candidates to have a JD, formal legal training, or equivalent experience in a legal practice environment.
  • Both mid-level and Senior engineers build RAG and agents, and evaluate LLM outputs across three dimensions: legal accuracy, citations, and hallucinations.
  • To prepare, build a RAG system for a single industry with its own eval set, rather than making yet another chatbot demo.

Thomson Reuters posted a job ad on Dice for a Senior Software Engineer on the CoCounsel Forward Deployed Engineering team. One requirement is unusual for a software engineering role: candidates need a JD (law degree), formal legal training, or equivalent deep experience in a legal practice environment. The company is hiring software engineers, but it's asking about law school.

There's a reason for this. The same posting requires new hires to evaluate non-deterministic AI outputs across three dimensions: legal accuracy, relevance, and reliability. To judge whether a legal answer is right or wrong, you have to know how a lawyer would read it.

As the work of Forward Deployed Engineers (FDEs) goes deeper into specific industries, knowing how to call LLM APIs is no longer enough to stand out. You must also prove that outputs are correct in the eyes of practitioners in that domain. That means writing evals, and this work is just as important as building pipelines.

Pipelines remain core work, and evals start from the very first level

The CoCounsel FDE team has posted openings at two levels. The mid-level role's responsibilities include building and maintaining RAG pipelines, agent workflows, and multi-component AI systems. The Senior posting on Built In NYC, which went offline on August 20, 2026, likewise covers building RAG pipelines and agents.

However, evals come immediately after the building work. Looking at the postings together, it's clear: evals are not just a senior employee's job.

Evals already appear at the mid-level, and the criteria listed there are the most detailed of the three postings. At Senior, the verb shifts from "assist" to "lead," along with benchmarking output quality and measuring hallucination risk.

In other words, the higher the level, the more evals shift from "something to do" to "something to own." And at every level, pipeline work doesn't go away.

Why is scoring legal AI so hard?

Thomson Reuters itself explained this in a 2024 article about CoCounsel benchmarking. Traditional benchmarks rely on multiple-choice or true/false questions, and according to the company, this approach is insufficient for evaluating the open-ended tasks lawyers face every day.

What's harder still is that "correct" sometimes depends on preference. The article notes that two answers may both be right, differing only in which one the user prefers, which makes evals hard to automate reliably.

In 2026, Thomson Reuters settled on its own measurement standard when launching CoCoBench: every output is scored on whether a practicing lawyer would consider it acceptable. The company also noted that systems optimized for generic benchmarks often perform poorly when scored against real workflows.

Here's an example. You build a contract review assistant for a law firm and test it with 20 questions. 18 of the answers are substantively correct, so scored purely on "right or wrong," the system gets 90%.

But checking the citations one by one reveals that 3 of the "correct" answers cite clauses that don't exist in the contract at all. Counting citations, only 15 answers are both correct and accurately sourced: 75%.

A lawyer won't look at the 90% figure. What they see is that roughly one in seven answers fabricates its sources, and they stop trusting the tool.

This example shows why the mid-level posting lists citation quality and hallucination risk as separate criteria alongside legal accuracy: if you lump them together, fabricated sources can easily hide behind a nice-looking number.

There's another layer to this example. Thomson Reuters emphasizes that if initial retrieval quality is poor, LLM performance degrades accordingly. So when facing those 3 fabricated citations, the first step is to check whether retrieval recalled the right clauses, not to blame the model first.

This lesson applies to any client. Before tuning prompts or switching models, agree with domain experts on which errors are unacceptable, then turn each error type into a separate metric, measuring retrieval and answering separately.

Is there still a chance without a law degree?

The legal background requirement appears in the Senior posting, and the wording contains an "or": a JD, formal legal training, or equivalent deep experience in a legal practice environment. The door isn't completely closed to engineers who haven't studied law, but you must prove you understand how lawyers work.

The reason lies in CoCoBench's own measurement standard. If the scoring criterion is whether a practicing lawyer would accept the output, then whoever writes the evals must distinguish between answers that "read fine" and answers a lawyer would sign off on. If the eval set is scored wrong from the start, no pipeline, however good, can save it.

The same logic applies to every vertical industry, from healthcare to finance.

With an eval set, you can switch models

There's also a practical reason evals matter. According to a November 2024 press release, Thomson Reuters' LLM eval program tests new models from multiple AI vendors directly inside its products.

For a multi-model strategy to work, there must be a stable eval set as a baseline. Every time a new model appears, the question "should we switch?" can only be answered one way: rerun the same questions with the same criteria and put the numbers side by side.

On-site with clients, FDEs face the same question, often with nothing prepared in advance. If you set up the eval set in the first week, the model-switching meeting becomes everyone looking at a table of numbers together, instead of arguing by gut feeling.

A portfolio project that tells a complete story

If you want to prepare for roles like this, start with one project. Build a RAG system on a public corpus of legal documents, such as contract templates or statutes, plus a simple agent step, such as automatically breaking complex questions into smaller ones.

Most important is the accompanying eval set. Every question needs a reference answer and source clauses. Score retrieval accuracy, answer accuracy, citation accuracy, and fabricated-source rate separately, and record how the numbers change after each iteration.

These metrics align closely with the criteria listed in the mid-level posting. If possible, run two different models on the same eval set and add a comparison table.

On your resume, don't just write "built a legal chatbot with an LLM." Instead write: designed a citation eval set, found X fabricated-source errors, and reduced them to Y.

In interviews, tell a story about a time you sat down with domain users to agree on which errors were unacceptable. That story shows you understand Thomson Reuters' yardstick: whether practitioners accept the output, not how high the score is on generic benchmarks.

The CoCounsel team's job posting shows that a vertical-industry FDE is an engineer who can both build systems and measure correctness by the standards of industry practitioners. People who have both are at an advantage in any industry where a single wrong citation can cost the client dearly.
