FDE Interview Questions: Common Question Types and How to Answer Them

Original title: FDE 面试题：常见题型与回答方法

FDE Times · 2026-10-19 · 6 min read

Summary

This guide breaks down the interview round that typically decides FDE offers—one that often involves no coding at all. It covers five question types: problem decomposition, system design, debugging, customer role-play, and behavioral questions. The core argument, drawing on sources like techinterview, fde.academy, and Aced's write-ups of Palantir and OpenAI processes, is that interviewers evaluate how you reason toward a solution, not the answer itself. The most common rejection reason is jumping to solutions before scoping the problem. The article recommends spending the first ten minutes asking questions, thinking aloud throughout, and structuring behavioral answers around both technical results and customer impact with real numbers.

Key points

  • The decisive FDE interview round often has no coding.
  • Most common rejection cause: proposing solutions before defining scope.
  • Spend the first ten minutes asking clarifying questions.
  • Debugging rounds reward method: reproduce, state assumptions, change one thing at a time.
  • Behavioral answers need both technical results and customer impact, with numbers.

Key Takeaways

  • The round that decides the FDE offer often has no code: the interviewer evaluates how you move step by step toward a solution, not whether you arrive at one specific answer.
  • The most common reason candidates get rejected is rushing to propose a solution before defining the scope, so use the first ten minutes to ask questions.
  • A good behavioral interview answer has two outcomes: a technical result and an impact on the customer, and both should include numbers.

You walk into the final round for an FDE role and the editor is already open. Instead of giving you an algorithm problem, the interviewer says: a hospital stores patient records in six disconnected systems. What would you do?

There are no test cases and no sample inputs. According to techinterview's analysis of FDE hiring, the round that decides the offer often involves no code at all. The interview tests three groups of skills with roughly equal weight, so don't spend all your prep time writing code.

Many excellent engineers stumble here. fde.academy's prep guide identifies the most common reason candidates get rejected: jumping to a solution before defining the scope. Below, we go through each type of question you'll encounter, along with an approach that works across all of them.

The interview simulates the real job

To predict the questions, look at what FDEs do every day. Google Cloud's FDE job posting describes the role as deploying customized agent solutions with customers, in the customers' own environments. It's looking for "engineers with a founder's mindset who are highly self-driven."

This is also why the technical interview differs from that for product engineers. According to fde.academy, the focus is not on algorithmic optimization but on integration thinking: where the data comes from and where it goes, which system is the single source of truth, and where things can break.

Aced's guide to the decomposition round (Palantir style) puts it even more bluntly: the interviewer evaluates how you move step by step toward a solution, not whether you arrive at one specific answer.

The first ten minutes decide the outcome

techinterview describes strong candidates this way: they spend the first ten minutes asking questions. Using the hospital problem as an example, here's what those ten minutes look like. The dialogue below is an illustrative script, not a real interview transcript.

Your first question should be: "Who will use the merged data: the doctors at the point of care, or the finance team during reconciliation?" The second: "How much time does this cost them today, and what errors has it caused?" These two questions turn "merging six systems" from a project with no boundaries into a problem someone is paying to solve.

Then come the integration questions. Which system is the single source of truth for patient identity? Does every system have an API, or can they only export files? What are the privacy constraints, and who can see what? Ninety days from now, what will make the customer say "this was a success"?

Suppose the interviewer answers: ER physicians need to see allergy histories and prescriptions, and right now they have to open three different interfaces.

Now you break the problem into five parts: identity resolution to match the same patient across systems; connectors to read data from each system; a unified data model for allergies and medications; a single interface for ER physicians; and an access control layer with audit logs.

Then pick the first slice: the two systems holding allergies and prescriptions, one department, one interface. The other four systems come later, and you explain why. Identity matching carries the highest risk, so you recommend validating it on real data before building anything else.

Throughout, think out loud. fde.academy recommends narrating your reasoning while designing, because silence gets interpreted as being stuck. Saying something like "I'll assume these two systems have APIs; if not, the fallback is reading nightly file exports" lets the interviewer see both your assumptions and your fallback plan.

Technical questions: ask about the goal first, then pick the tools

Aced's write-up of OpenAI's interview process includes a system design question that's quite representative: customer traffic grows 10x overnight. How do you keep the system within its latency and cost targets? The wrong instinct is to immediately list autoscaling, caching, and queues.

The right response is still to ask questions first. Is this traffic spike temporary, or the new normal? Is latency measured at p95 or p99, and for which endpoints? How much more is the customer willing to pay?

Each answer cuts off a branch: a temporary spike can be handled with queues and rate limiting, while the new normal means revisiting the architecture and costs together with the customer.

The debugging round demands even more discipline. techinterview notes that randomly changing a few lines of code and hoping for the best gets penalized. First reproduce the bug, read the logs, state your assumptions, then change one thing at a time to verify.

Some companies add a take-home assignment. According to Aced, OpenAI's FDE process includes an assignment of about a week, with runnable code submitted along with a short walkthrough video.

The example prompt is: build semantic search for a product catalog that ChatGPT can query, then explain the system. The video is where you prove you understand why each component was chosen, so invest as much time in it as in the code.

Customer scenarios: the pressure itself is the test

According to fde.academy, the customer simulation round tests communication and ownership. The scenarios are built around real pressures: a frustrated customer, scope creep, unrealistic deadlines, or a customer executive pressing hard while the system is down.

Imagine the interviewer plays an operations lead whose tone turns sharp because the data pipeline stalled mid-peak. A solid answer has four steps: acknowledge the impact—"I understand your team can't process orders right now"; state what's known and what isn't; say what you're doing right now; and commit to a specific time for the next update.

When facing scope creep, neither say "no" outright nor simply nod along. Lay out the trade-off: adding this feature will push back the delivery date, or something else has to be cut. The interviewer wants to see that you can hold the line on the project while preserving the relationship.

Behavioral interviews: two outcomes, one number

fde.academy recommends that every behavioral interview answer cover both the technical result and the impact on the customer, with numbers wherever possible. Engineers often lose points here: they go deep on the architecture and then stop, without saying what the customer gained.

Take an OpenAI interview question recorded by Aced: describe a time you explained a complex technical concept to a customer or a non-expert.

Answer template: the context—who the person was and what decision they needed to make; the analogy you used to explain it and how you confirmed they understood; the technical result—[the system went live, with what accuracy or latency]; the impact—[what decision they made, how many hours or how much money was saved].

Fill the brackets with your real numbers; don't estimate just to make things look good.

The same template applies to your resume. Every bullet should include what you did, the technical outcome, and the impact on users. When reading a job description, if you see phrases like "customer's environment" or "ship with customers," prepare at least two stories about working directly with end users.

Mistakes that trip up even strong candidates

The first mistake is rushing. Engineers used to well-defined problems start sketching an architecture as soon as they see the prompt, but FDE prompts are deliberately vague. Aced describes the decomposition round as: given a deliberately ambiguous real-world problem, you're asked to break it down and explain your reasoning.

The second mistake is thinking silently and then presenting only your conclusion. The interviewer can't hear your process, so they can't score it. The third mistake is treating the customer in the role-play as unreasonable: once you start arguing about who's right, you've forgotten that an FDE's job is to work with the customer to push the project across the finish line.

The last mistake happens during preparation: grinding through another hundred algorithm problems without ever practicing thinking out loud. The decisive round often has no code; it evaluates how you move toward a solution—that is, structured reasoning and how you communicate it. Both only improve with practice out loud.

This week's exercise: take the hospital problem above, set a timer, and record yourself. When you play it back, count how many questions you asked before saying the word "architecture" for the first time.
