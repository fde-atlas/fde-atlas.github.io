FDE Explained: The New Trend for 2026

Original title: FDE一文详解：26年新风口

少数派 - 高品质数字消费指南 · 2026-06-12 · 9 min read

Summary

This piece explains the Forward Deployed Engineer (FDE) role: far from an AI-era invention, it originated around 2005 at Palantir, which embedded engineers on-site with intelligence-agency clients whose data and requirements made remote delivery impossible. The role resurged because enterprise AI adoption is failing at deployment—MIT research found 95% of AI pilots produced no measurable P&L impact. OpenAI built a $14B-valued company around FDEs, Anthropic joined a $1.5B venture, and postings grew over 800% in nine months per FT/Indeed data. The author argues FDEs serve as vendor lock-in tools for model companies and evidence that AI products aren't yet plug-and-play, calling the current hiring boom a window that will close as products mature.

Key points

  • FDE originated around 2005 at Palantir for on-site delivery to intelligence clients.
  • MIT research found 95% of enterprise AI pilots had no measurable P&L impact.
  • FDE postings grew over 800% in nine months of 2025, per FT and Indeed.
  • Overseas FDE total compensation reaches $500K; China pays 3-5x less at 30-50K RMB monthly.
  • The author sees the FDE boom as a window that will close once AI products become plug-and-play.

Last June, a16z posted a tweet with a pretty striking line:

The forward deployed engineer is the hottest job in startups right now.

At the time I just glanced at it. FDE, three letters, just one letter off from FE (Front-end Engineer). I instinctively assumed it was some new variant of front-end work, didn't look closely, and scrolled past.

Then those three letters started acting strange.

The Financial Times reported that this role grew 800% in nine months. Pragmatic Engineer ran a full deep-dive on it. OpenAI announced the creation of a company valued at $14 billion, built specifically around this group of people. Anthropic also teamed up with Blackstone and Goldman Sachs to join a $1.5 billion round.

This was no longer just a new job title.

Then one day, while searching for AI-related jobs on Boss Zhipin, I scrolled through a long list of them, with salaries reaching as high as 100,000 RMB per month.

The wave has already reached China.

But the reason I actually sat down to study it wasn't because it was trending. It was because after digging in, I discovered: this role, which looks like a brand-new invention of the AI era, has actually existed for a full 20 years.

Why did a 20-year-old role suddenly become the job that global AI giants are scrambling to hire for? What problem does it actually solve? And what does the very existence of that problem tell us about what stage the AI industry is in?

What exactly is an FDE

Let's start by unpacking the name.

The F is not for Frontend, it's for Forward. FDE stands for Forward Deployed Engineer, translated into Chinese as Forward Deployed Engineer. The term is borrowed from military language, originally referring to troops deployed on the front line.

One sentence sums up the role: an engineer who moves in with an AI model and lives inside the customer's company.

Note that "moves in" is not an exaggeration. FDEs literally work on-site at the customer's office, join the customer's Slack channels, get access to the customer's internal network, plug into the customer's actual production systems, and stay for anywhere from a few weeks to six months. This isn't the kind of involvement where you hop on a remote call to align on requirements; it's physical, embedded presence.

Anyone who has done B2B work knows this is the hardest part of the entire chain. When a customer says "I want to use AI to optimize our processes," you have to figure out for yourself which process they mean, what "optimize" means, and what counts as "good." Feeding experience back is the fundamental difference between an FDE and outsourcing: outsourcers deliver and leave, while the pitfalls an FDE encounters become the product's next feature.

Second, in the lower half of the diagram comparing FDE and SA, the core difference comes down to one sentence: an SA hands over the architecture diagram once it's drawn; an FDE writes the production code themselves, deploys it themselves, and takes responsibility when things break. From defining the problem on day one to responding to bugs six months later, one person carries it end to end.

There's a saying in the industry that captures it perfectly: one person is an entire special forces unit.

This also explains why salaries can reach this level. Overseas, the median base salary for an FDE is $210,000, with total compensation reaching $500,000, roughly 3.5 million RMB. In China, the current monthly salary is 30-50k, with a few roles paying a million RMB a year, but the job titles haven't even been standardized yet: some are called FDE, some "AI implementation engineer," some "on-site delivery engineer." The same work commands a 3 to 5x price gap between China and overseas.

In one sentence: the Chinese market is still extremely early-stage.

Why did a 20-year-old role suddenly catch fire today, of all times

You might assume the FDE is a new invention of the AI era.

It's not. The role was born around 2005, invented by a company called Palantir.

Palantir is Silicon Valley's most secretive data company; its early clients were the CIA, the NSA, and other US intelligence agencies. Intelligence agencies' data is highly classified and their requirements extremely vague, so traditional software delivery simply couldn't work. You couldn't ask the client to send data over, and you couldn't debug remotely. Even a simple demo required weeks of security review and NDAs.

Palantir's solution was blunt: send engineers directly into the client's office, for a minimum of six months at a time.

This playbook was codenamed Delta inside Palantir. By 2016, Palantir's FDE headcount actually exceeded its traditional software engineers. Alumni who left Palantir went on to found over a hundred companies, including the voice-synthesis company ElevenLabs, whose co-founder Mati Staniszewski started out as a Palantir Deployment Strategist.

The model was validated. But for the next decade-plus, the FDE remained a niche role, circulating only within Palantir and a small circle of government contractors.

Until AI arrived.

Put the dilemmas of the two eras side by side and the structural similarity is absurdly high: can't touch the data, can't articulate the requirements, traditional delivery doesn't work. Twenty years later, a different industry, the exact same dead end.

An MIT study analyzed 300 enterprise AI deployment cases and reached a brutal conclusion: 95% of AI pilots produced no measurable P&L impact.

A caveat on methodology: this figure comes from a 2025 report by MIT's NANDA team, and its methodology is contested within the industry; some argue there are problems with the sample selection criteria and evaluation standards. But the core contradiction it points to is real: the problem isn't the model, it's implementation.

The GPT-4 series, the Claude series, the Gemini series — the models are already strong enough. But between a chat-capable API and a business feature that actually runs inside an enterprise lies a huge chasm. Integrations fail, processes can't be changed, data can't be connected, and no one inside the enterprise knows how to turn a model into an actually usable system.

The sentence customers say most often is: I know AI can do something for me, but I don't know what exactly.

a16z partner Joe Schmidt wrote a precise analogy: enterprises buying AI is like your grandma getting a new iPhone. She wants to use it, but she needs you to set it up for her.

Product-led growth (PLG), which Silicon Valley believed in for over a decade, fails completely in enterprise AI. ChatGPT took PLG to its extreme: sign up and use it, pay with a credit card. But enterprise customers are not individual users. What enterprises want is not a chat box, but a system that plugs into approval workflows, reads internal databases, and runs reliably within compliance frameworks.

That kind of need cannot be met with documentation, APIs, or demos. Only with people.

And so the industry made a huge circle, only to arrive back at the answer Palantir found 20 years ago: send the engineers in.

In early 2025, Colin Jarvis at OpenAI proposed internally forming an FDE team, which initially had just two people. In July of the same year, Oliver Jay, OpenAI's head of international business, officially announced it at the Fortune Brainstorm AI conference. Data from the Financial Times together with Indeed shows that between January and September 2025, FDE job postings surged by more than 800%. Salesforce alone has committed to building a 1,000-person FDE team.

A role that had lain dormant for over a decade has been reactivated by the difficulties of putting AI into production.

One Role, Three Faces

FDEs are hot right now, but different stakeholders see completely different things in the role.

For enterprises: a stopgap tool

The logic is straightforward. An FDE comes in, diagnoses pain points, integrates systems, gets the model working, delivers, and leaves. It's the same logic as hiring a renovation crew: once the house is done, the crew should move on.

In May of this year, Andrew Ng wrote a long piece about FDEs with two clear-eyed judgments. First, enterprises would rather have their own employees do this work; developing internal AI Engineers is more sustainable than relying on external FDEs. Second, FDEs are inherently tied to a single vendor—having someone from OpenAI deeply embedded in your business processes means you've voluntarily given up the flexibility to switch suppliers.

Both of these concerns are valid. In his view, the broader pool of AI Engineer roles will far outnumber FDEs in the long run.

For model companies: a lock-in weapon

Why would OpenAI set up an independent company valued at $14 billion just to house FDEs? Initial investment exceeded $4 billion, with 19 institutions co-investing, plus the acquisition of a deployment company called Tomoro, absorbing 150 FDEs in one go.

Why not just let customers integrate the API themselves?

Because the FDE's real function isn't helping customers build systems—it's using the act of building systems to weld their own models into the customer's business processes.

It doesn't matter when the FDE leaves. What they leave behind is an entire set of workflows, eval systems, and prompt orchestration logic deeply bound to OpenAI's models. Want to migrate to Claude or Gemini? You'd have to tear down the whole tech stack, and the migration cost would be enormous.

Anthropic, together with Blackstone and Goldman Sachs, joined a $1.5 billion joint venture. Same playbook.

What you earn isn't the FDE's service fee—it's the money customers keep spending on tokens after the FDE leaves. It's the same logic as telecom operators sending someone to install broadband for free: the installer leaves, but your monthly internet bill never stops.

This isn't consulting. This is lock-in.

For the industry: evidence that AI isn't mature

If AI products were good enough—good enough that enterprises could buy them and use them out of the box—there would be no need for the FDE role to exist at all. Just like how today you don't need anyone to come to your home to install the OS and tweak the BIOS when you buy a MacBook, and you don't need an engineer on site to set up a database when you use Notion.

But the reality is that today's AI products are far from out-of-the-box ready. Enterprises spend money, buy API credits, and still can't get things working. That's why humans are needed to fill the last-mile gap.

The mass expansion of FDE hiring is itself the most direct proof that AI isn't good enough yet.

I felt this deeply while building Lollipop. Even for a consumer-facing AI interview product, there's a huge amount of dirty work between model capability and a usable product experience: prompts have to be tuned one by one, edge cases covered one at a time, business logic aligned with the model over and over. B2B scenarios are ten times more complex than this.

Who Can Do This Job

Having covered what this role is, why it's hot, and what its essence is, a natural question follows: who is suited for it?

The answer is deeply counterintuitive. When Palantir hires FDEs, it doesn't filter by computer science degrees. They've admitted people who studied mechanical engineering, economics, even philosophy.

What really matters is two things: being able to act with incomplete information, and daring to talk directly to the customer's boss.

To elaborate, a few traits are especially critical.

First, not resisting sales and communication. FDEs spend half their time dealing with people: coordinating with the customer's technical team, reporting to the customer's business leads, giving feedback to their own headquarters' product team. If you just want to quietly write code, this role will make you miserable.

Second, enjoying ambiguity. Having no requirements document is the norm. The input a customer gives you might just be "help us use AI to improve efficiency," and you have to break that sentence down into an executable plan yourself.

Third, being able to run things end to end, engineering-wise. You don't need to be a coding wizard, but you should be able to handle frontend, backend, databases, and deployment yourself—at least to the point of "good enough to use."

Fourth, having a feel for the boundaries of model capabilities. Knowing which tasks are suited for AI and which must be left to humans. There's no shortcut to this feel; it can only come from extensive practice.

Conversely, two types of people are clearly unsuited: pure tech enthusiasts who want to hide in their code, and people who get uncomfortable whenever money or contracts come up. The FDE is essentially a hybrid of a business role and a technical role—you need to touch both sides.

Here's a self-test question for you: With no one writing a requirements document for you, could you produce a prototype worth showing a customer within a week? If your first reaction is "how can I do this without a requirements document," this role probably isn't for you. If your reaction is "let me go talk to the customer first," you already have the makings of an FDE.

The Window Will Close

Back to the fundamental question: Is this wave of mass hiring permanent, or a window of opportunity?

My judgment: the latter.

That doesn't mean the FDE role will disappear. Palantir's FDEs have been doing this for 20 years and still are. But the Palantir model is an elite small team serving highly confidential, highly customized government clients, with contracts worth millions of dollars per deal—a high density of FDEs makes sense there.

What OpenAI and Anthropic are doing now is an industrialized assembly line. A $14 billion valuation, 150 people absorbed in bulk, teams expanding to 1,000—this scale-up is a product of the times sweeping everything along.

When AI products mature to the point where every customer no longer needs a dedicated FDE, this tide will recede.

It's like how, in the early days, every enterprise implementing ERP needed an implementation consultant on site for six months; in the SaaS era, most companies can just sign up for an account and start using it. Implementation consultants haven't disappeared, but their numbers and importance have returned to a normal level.

FDE is just the first to get a name. Looking ahead, there will be forward deployed product managers and forward deployed designers. a16z is already using the term "Agent Product Manager," and even launched an 8-week FDE Fellowship to incubate this group. Decagon, a company building customer service agents, has an entire team of Agent PMs helping customers deploy.

All these newly emerging roles are, in essence, different facets of the same thing:

The final form of AI hasn't taken shape yet.

Models are evolving, products are iterating, and enterprise adoption is climbing. When these three lines converge—the day AI products truly become out-of-the-box ready—these transitional roles will naturally shrink.

But that day hasn't arrived yet.

A Few Final Words

However temporary a bridge may be, someone still has to build it.

But the bridge builder should be clear about one thing: you're building a bridge, not a house. A bridge's value lies in connecting two shores; once the two shores are connected, the bridge's mission is complete.

If you happen to be standing at the entrance of this window right now, an FDE or similar AI implementation role is indeed a good ticket in. But don't treat the window-period dividend as a long-term meal ticket.

In the process of building the bridge, what's truly worth accumulating is not how many agents you helped customers deploy, but three things: a feel for the boundaries of AI capabilities, a deep understanding of enterprise business processes, and the ability to drive things forward in environments with incomplete information.

These things are yours, whether the bridge exists or not.

Models handle the intelligence; humans handle the implementation.

The last mile of implementation is something AI cannot walk on its own. And every step it doesn't complete is your window of opportunity.
