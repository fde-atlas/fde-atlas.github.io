Designing Permission Management for AI Agents | FDE Best Practices

Original title: AIエージェントの権限管理設計｜FDE実践術

日本Fde協会 · 2026-09-29 · 6 min read

Summary

This article from the Japan FDE Association offers forward deployed engineers a practical framework for designing permission management when AI agents start executing real business actions like sending emails, updating data, or creating purchase orders. The author argues that delegation to agents first breaks segregation of duties, and that deferring permission design leads to over-provisioned admin access. Permissions should be split along three axes: read versus write, reversible versus irreversible, and blast radius. Human approval is reserved for irreversible or high-impact actions; everything else runs automatically. Logs must record which user's authority an agent acted under, since agent-named logs fail as audit trails, and design rationale should be documented for handover.

Key points

  • Agent actions first break segregation of duties.
  • Split permissions by read/write, reversibility, blast radius.
  • Reserve human approval for irreversible, high-impact actions.
  • Logs must capture the instructing user, not just agent name.
  • Design permissions before deployment and document rationale.

In this article, FDE refers to Forward Deployed Engineer (a technical professional who works on-site with customers to implement and operate AI and software tailored to their business), not Full Disk Encryption, which means disk encryption. Once AI agents start actually handling work on behalf of people, the first problem that arises in most organizations is permission management. By reading this article, you will be able to determine how to segment permissions for AI agents and which actions require approval, logging, and audit trails, and incorporate permission design into your company's implementation plan. Now, before implementation begins, is the perfect time to think this through together.

The First Thing That Breaks Down in AI Agent Permission Management: Segregation of Duties

If an AI merely answers questions in chat, permissions rarely come up. But the moment an agent starts executing actions like "sending emails," "updating data," or "creating purchase orders," the situation changes completely. The first thing we check when supporting implementations is a single question: whose permissions does the agent operate under?

When a "Human Substitute" Breaks Segregation of Duties

Business processes normally have segregation of duties, separating the person who creates a request from the person who approves it. However, if you delegate everything to an agent, the same entity ends up holding both the creation and the approval-equivalent judgment, and controls collapse all at once. At a manufacturing client, we saw a case where an agent tasked with organizing purchasing data had also been granted update permissions. It was convenient, but there was no record of who approved each change, and when they later did an inventory review, the changes could not be traced.

Excessive Permissions Come from "Just Give It Everything"

When permission design is put off, developers tend to hand over full administrator privileges to keep development moving quickly. We also value showing something working as fast as possible, but permissions are a different matter. An agent only needs the minimum scope required to accomplish its task. Excessive permissions directly expand the blast radius when an incident occurs.

Three Criteria for Segmenting Permission Scope

So what should you use as the basis for segmenting permissions? Here are the three approaches we use in the field.

Read or Write

The first line to draw is separating actions that only reference information from actions that change state. Read operations can be granted relatively loosely, but state-changing actions such as writing, updating, deleting, sending, and ordering should each be evaluated independently. The mindset of "if it can read, it can write too" is the gateway to excessive permissions.

Reversible or Irreversible

Next, consider whether the action can be undone. Things that cannot be reversed once executed, such as sending emails to external parties, moving funds, or placing external orders, should not be left to the agent alone; design them with human approval built in. In business close to the financial domain, isolating these irreversible actions becomes the core of permission design.

How Far Does the Impact Reach?

The third criterion is the scope of impact. Does it complete within a single record, or does it ripple across multiple departments or external systems? In fields like healthcare, where the information handled is highly sensitive, even read operations should be restricted to specific users' specific data, and access beyond that scope should be blocked by design.

Read onlyGrant with a restricted scopeRecord access logs
Reversible updatesGrant with minimal scopeRecord before and after changes
Irreversible executionsAvoid granting aloneHuman approval + audit trail required

How to Implement Approval Flows, Operation Logs, and Audit Trails

Once permissions are segmented, the next step is to create a state where "who did what" can be explained after the fact. This is the part that tends to be missing from typical tool usage guides.

Insert Approvals Only for Irreversible Actions

Adding approval to everything will bring operations to a halt. Build human approval into only irreversible executions and high-impact changes, and let everything else run automatically. Drawing this line is the key point of implementation design. Approval records should capture who approved what and when, so they can be traced in later audits.

Logs Should Record "What Was Done on Behalf of Whom"

What matters in agent operation logs is not simply "it updated something," but recording whose permissions were used and on what basis the action was executed. When we consulted with the CEO of a SaaS company, they had logs, but everything was recorded under the agent's name, making it impossible to trace back to the person who actually gave the instructions. That does not function as an audit trail. We recommend building in, from the start, a design that records the instructor, the action performed, the target, and the result as a set.

Documentation to Prevent Knowledge Silos Is Part of the Design

Permission design is not a one-time task. If you do not document why each permission was set to that scope, the moment the person in charge changes, no one will be able to explain the rationale. We believe that documenting the design intent and making it transferable is part of permission management. Frequently Asked Questions

Q. Should AI agent permission management be designed before or after implementation? We recommend incorporating it into the design before implementation. If added later, excessive permissions become an established fact, and narrowing them afterward faces significant operational resistance.

Q. Should human approval be inserted for every action? No. It is realistic to insert approval only for irreversible executions and high-impact changes, and let everything else run automatically. Make the judgment based on your company's control policies and industry requirements.

Q. What should operation logs contain to serve as an audit trail? The basics are recording the instructor, the action performed, the target, and the result as a set. Logs recorded only under the agent's name cannot trace who gave the instructions, making them ineffective as an audit trail.
