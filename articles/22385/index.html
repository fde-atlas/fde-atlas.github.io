Databricks FDE interview: decomposition round and prep

FDE Times · 2026-10-17 · 6 min read

Summary

Drawing on an Aced.io interview guide and Databricks's own job postings, this article breaks down the FDE interview process, focusing on the roughly 60-minute decomposition round where candidates design a system from a deliberately vague business goal. It argues that LeetCode practice alone is insufficient: the real tests are Spark runtime internals, governance via Unity Catalog, and converting murky requirements into architecture. It also maps the three FDE postings (Seoul, Zürich, AI Engineer) to their distinct emphases and advises tailoring the CV accordingly.

Key points

  • The decomposition round starts from a vague goal; clarifying questions in the first ten minutes matter most.
  • Five rounds exist but only three skills are tested: Spark, governance, and architecture.
  • Data skew answers should follow detection, cause, and fix, backed by measured numbers.
  • Unity Catalog's three-level namespace and lineage are the expected governance answers.
  • The three FDE postings differ in focus, so pick one before writing the CV.

In brief

  • The FDE posting in Seoul demands deep Spark knowledge, including runtime internals; the Sr. FDE (Fullstack) posting in Zürich gives FDEs architecture decisions that must be secure and scalable.
  • The decomposition round of about 60 minutes starts from a vague business goal, so the clarifying questions at the start matter as much as the design itself.
  • Databricks's three FDE postings have different centers of gravity: Spark and MLOps, production architecture, or GenAI. Pick the posting before writing the CV.

The interviewer opens with one sentence: “A retail chain wants to use AI to keep its customers. Design it.” There is no schema, no data, and nobody mentions a deadline.

According to an interview guide from Aced.io, this is what the decomposition round at Databricks looks like: roughly 60 minutes to design a system from a business goal that is still vague.

The round is hard because it resembles the real work. Databricks’s Sr. FDE (Fullstack) posting says engineers will work with customers to build and put into production solutions to their data and AI problems. The responsibilities section states plainly that FDEs lead technical projects that produce production-grade systems.

That is why practicing LeetCode alone will leave a candidate short. Three things are really being tested: a deep understanding of Spark, a grasp of governance through Unity Catalog, and the ability to turn a murky request into an architecture. Each comes with an example below that can be worked through independently.

Five rounds, but only three skills

The Aced.io guide describes a five-round process: 30 minutes with a recruiter, a technical screen, a coding round in a notebook, the decomposition round and a leadership round. This is a third-party account, not official documentation, so the order and length may vary by hiring team.

More reliable is the list of requirements in the postings themselves. The posting for the Seoul role asks for extensive experience with distributed computing in Apache Spark and knowledge of Spark’s runtime internals.

It also asks candidates to understand and use MLOps, ML/AI models and AI APIs, and to code in several languages. The senior posting in Zürich gives FDEs authority over architecture decisions, on condition that the solutions are secure and scalable.

Put the two sources together and the notebook round tests Spark, the decomposition round tests architecture and governance, and the leadership round tests how a candidate works with customers. Prepare around those three skills rather than splitting time evenly across five rounds.

Spark questions are really about what sits under the API

“Runtime internals” means the interviewer wants an explanation of why a job runs slowly, beyond the ability to write a

join

. The classic example is data skew.

Say you join the

orders

table to the

customers

table on

customer_id

, and Spark splits the data into 200 partitions after the shuffle. If one wholesale customer accounts for 40% of the rows in

orders

, all of that 40% lands in a single partition. The result: 199 tasks finish in a few seconds, while the whole job waits on one task carrying nearly half the workload.

A good answer has three steps. First, detection: in the Spark UI, one task’s run time is far longer than the others in the same stage. Next, the cause: the shuffle sends every row with the same key to the same place.

Finally, the fix: broadcast the small table to avoid the shuffle, add a “salt” to the skewed key to spread it across several partitions, or turn on adaptive query execution so Spark splits oversized partitions itself.

Reading this section is not enough. Build a skewed table in a notebook and time the job before and after the fix. In the interview, numbers you measured yourself are far more persuasive than a recited definition.

Unity Catalog is the answer for the person worried about security

Databricks’s documentation defines Unity Catalog as a unified governance layer for data and AI, built into the platform. There are two points a candidate must be able to explain on the spot.

The first is the three-level namespace

catalog.schema.object

. In the retail problem above, the customer feature table could be named

retail.churn.customer_features

. Naming things this clearly on the whiteboard shows you have thought about where data lives and who may access it.

The second is lineage. Unity Catalog automatically tracks how data and AI assets move and connect, from source data to models, services and dashboards. The benefit is concrete: when the marketing director asks where a dashboard figure comes from, you can trace it back to the source table without reading code.

The first ten minutes decide the decomposition round

The Aced.io guide advises spending the opening minutes clarifying the goal, because the round stands in for a real customer meeting. For the “keep customers” prompt, the questions might run in this order:

“How is retention measured: the share of customers who buy again within 90 days, or average order value?” “Who uses the output: the marketing team sending weekly offers, or in-store staff?” “Where does the transaction data live now, and how often is it updated?” “Is there personal data that only some people may see?”

Suppose the answers are that marketing sends a campaign every week, the data updates nightly, and customer phone numbers are sensitive. With those constraints, the architecture almost draws itself.

Data loads every night into the

retail

catalog, Spark computes features, a model scores churn risk, and the results are written to a table the marketing team can read. The phone number column is open only to the campaign operations group.

The drawing is only half the answer; the other half is explaining why this approach beat the alternatives. For instance, batch wins over streaming because campaigns run weekly, so real-time scoring adds cost without adding value.

Model monitoring in the MLOps style goes in because shopping behavior shifts with the seasons. Lineage is the evidence that the system can be audited.

Pick the right posting before applying

Databricks’s three FDE postings have clearly different emphases, and the CV should be tailored to each.

FDE (Seoul) Sr. FDE – Fullstack (Zürich) AI Engineer – FDE
Focus Distributed computing in Spark, runtime internals Production-grade systems, architecture decisions GenAI applications
Experience to demonstrate Deep Spark; MLOps, ML/AI models, AI APIs; coding in several languages 6+ years in data engineering, data platform & analytics or software engineering RAG, multi-agent systems, Text2SQL, fine-tuning
Customer cadence — Leads customers’ technical projects Visits customers every 4–8 weeks as needed

Whichever role you apply for, each CV bullet should state three things: a system shipped to production, a measured number, and how you handled access control or data lineage. “Cut join time from X to Y by fixing skew,” for example, carries far more weight than “proficient in Spark.”

The mistakes that sink strong candidates

The most common mistake is drawing an architecture the moment the prompt ends. The interviewer is playing the customer, and skipping the clarifying questions is like skipping discovery with a real client.

The second is answering Spark questions at the API level when the Seoul posting explicitly asks for runtime knowledge. The third is mentioning governance only when prompted, when the senior Fullstack posting requires solutions to be secure from the design stage.

In the leadership round, a generic story along the lines of “I’m a good team player” loses to one with a specific customer, a specific conflict and a decision you owned.

Nobody needs to memorize every Databricks feature. What matters is showing the interviewer that if they sent you to a customer with a vague request tomorrow, you would ask the right questions, choose the right architecture and leave behind a system others can trace.
