Should an FDE learn evals or fine-tuning first?

Original title: FDE 该先学评测还是微调

FDE Times · 2026-11-13 · 5 min read

Summary

Comparing several FDE job postings and field cases, the article argues that deep fine-tuning skills (SFT, LoRA, DPO, GRPO/PPO) are required almost only by AI infrastructure companies like Together AI, and even there they are bundled with inference optimization (vLLM, TensorRT-LLM, SGLang) and customer project leadership. In OpenAI's Morgan Stanley project, the FDE team focused on retrieval tuning and treated evals as mandatory; OpenAI's own guidance suggests starting with prompting plus an eval baseline before considering RAG or fine-tuning. The author proposes a learning order for aspiring FDEs: evals and prompting first, then retrieval and data skills, then inference serving, and only then post-training techniques.

  • Even at Together AI, fine-tuning is bundled with inference engines and client project experience.
  • OpenAI's Morgan Stanley work centered on retrieval tuning, with evals treated as mandatory.
  • OpenAI's guidance: prompting and an eval baseline come before RAG or fine-tuning.
  • Suggested learning order: evals, retrieval and data, inference serving, then fine-tuning.

Comparing several FDE job postings, only one AI infrastructure company requires mastery of fine-tuning, and whether to fine-tune usually depends on the eval set first.

Key Takeaways

  • The requirement for deep fine-tuning skills (SFT, LoRA, DPO, GRPO/PPO) appears in the FDE job posting of AI infrastructure company Together AI.
  • Even at Together AI, fine-tuning is bundled with inference optimization, vLLM/TensorRT-LLM/SGLang, and hands-on experience leading customer projects.
  • In the Morgan Stanley project, OpenAI's FDE team optimized retrieval and treated evals as essential; OpenAI also recommends starting with prompts plus an eval baseline.

A Together AI posting titled FDE, Post-Training lists a string of acronyms that would send readers straight to a course signup page: SFT, LoRA, DPO, GRPO, PPO. The company wants candidates with expert-level hands-on experience in LLM fine-tuning or reinforcement learning (RL).

At a glance, the posting seems to say: fine-tuning is the ticket into the Forward Deployed Engineer (FDE) profession in the AI era. But when placed alongside several other FDE job descriptions, that conclusion doesn't hold. Across the postings and frontline experience discussed below, the demand for deep fine-tuning skills comes from only one AI infrastructure company, and even there, fine-tuning is not an isolated requirement.

For engineers with a few years of experience preparing to transition into an FDE role, the real question is not whether to learn fine-tuning, but when to learn it and what to learn first, so you don't spend six months mastering a skill customers may never ask about.

At Together AI, Fine-Tuning Is Not an Isolated Requirement

Together AI has two FDE roles involving fine-tuning, and both bundle it with other capabilities. The Post-Training role, alongside that string of algorithms, also requires experience directly leading complex technical projects with customers.

The second role, FDE Strategic Inference, describes its target candidates as senior experts in inference optimization, fine-tuning pipelines, and production deployment. The posting names specific tools: inference engines such as vLLM, TensorRT-LLM, and SGLang. The day-to-day work is selecting, configuring, and optimizing engines based on hardware, model architecture, and workload characteristics.

Judging from how these two postings are written, fine-tuning is just one link in the production chain, not the endpoint. A fine-tuned model still has to run on specific GPUs, handle specific traffic patterns, and serve specific customers. The people Together AI needs are those who can connect the entire chain.

Infrastructure Customers Ask About Latency, Not DPO

An account from an FDE at inference platform Baseten makes this point even more clearly. This engineer said the main goal is helping customers get open-source, fine-tuned, or custom models deployed behind API endpoints.

Note where the word "fine-tuned" sits in that sentence: the fine-tuned model is something the customer brings, and the FDE's job is to make it run. A typical problem cited in the article: how to cut inference latency to one-tenth without increasing cost.

Treat this as a problem to solve. If a request currently takes 2 seconds and the customer wants it down to 200 milliseconds on the same GPU bill, DPO or GRPO will hardly solve it; the answer lies in choosing an engine, configuration, and hardware matched to the model architecture—exactly the work described in the Strategic Inference role.

An Application-Layer Case: The Real Work Is Retrieval and Evals

On the side of companies selling models to enterprises, one case deserves a close look. In the partnership with Morgan Stanley, OpenAI's FDE team needed to develop retrieval tuning techniques so research reports could be accurately found and trusted by users. They tuned the retrieval step, not the model.

The team also treated evals as essential: an LLM-based application isn't finished unless it has an eval set that proves it works.

OpenAI's guide on optimizing LLM accuracy expresses the same view as a sequence: prompt engineering is usually the best starting point; on top of that, you need a reliable eval set as a baseline, and only then consider retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) or fine-tuning.

From these two sides, we can see two types of FDEs: same job title, different skill sets. The right column is based only on the single Morgan Stanley project plus OpenAI's general guidance, and should be treated as a comparison case, not a rule for the entire application layer.

When Fine-Tuning Finally Comes Into Play

Suppose you are an FDE at a bank, and the bank wants an internal assistant to answer questions about analyst reports. You prepare 100 questions with reference answers, run them with prompts only, and get, say, 62 correct. That's your baseline.

Next, examine the 38 wrong answers closely. Suppose 25 of them failed because the system retrieved the wrong report, 8 had correct content but wrong formatting, and 5 stemmed from the model not understanding internal terminology. The first 25 are retrieval problems—exactly the kind of work the OpenAI team did at Morgan Stanley; the latter 8 can usually be fixed with prompts.

Only after handling those two groups and finding the last group of errors still persisting does fine-tuning become a reasonable candidate. And at that point, according to OpenAI, the quality of training data matters more than quantity. So the skill that decides success is selecting and cleaning a few hundred good samples from the questions the system got wrong, not memorizing DPO's loss function.

A Learning Sequence for Aspiring FDEs

From these job descriptions, a sensible sequence emerges. Learn evals and prompts first, because they are the baseline for every decision that follows. Next comes retrieval and data skills: retrieval is the work the OpenAI team had to do at Morgan Stanley, and data quality is the factor OpenAI weighs more heavily than quantity when fine-tuning.

The third step is inference serving: run an open-source model on vLLM or SGLang, measure latency and cost, adjust the configuration, and measure again.

These are exactly the skills named in the Strategic Inference role, and the core of the Baseten account. After that, if your target is an infrastructure company, go deeper into SFT and LoRA, then DPO and RL methods like GRPO and PPO.

When reading job postings, use the table above as a rough screening tool. Postings that name inference engines and post-training algorithms are likely infrastructure-layer roles; there, investing in fine-tuning early is worthwhile.

A posting centered on accuracy and reliability for enterprise customers may be closer to the Morgan Stanley case; there, a solid evals program probably carries more weight than a fine-tuning credential.

Don't just write a single line on your resume saying "has fine-tuning experience." Turn it into a story with numbers: what the baseline was, how many categories the errors fell into, how much the score improved after fixing retrieval, and why you decided to fine-tune or not. If the target is the infrastructure layer, add one more line: latency before and after tuning the inference service configuration.

Don't forget the other half of Together AI's requirement, placed right next to the list of algorithms: leading technical projects with customers. That string of acronyms may be the most intimidating line in the posting, but what really tells you whether customers need any of them is the eval set.
