FDE Customer Needs Research: How to Write a One-Page Research Plan

Original title: FDE 客户需求调研：一页纸调研计划怎么写

FDE Times · 2026-10-08 · 5 min read

Summary

Drawing on a Perspective AI guide, this article argues that FDEs should spend week 0—before writing any code—producing a one-page research plan rather than rushing to prototype. It covers mapping stakeholders (including skeptics, who reveal real failure risks), asking customers about their last actual experience instead of requested features, and applying The Mom Test principles to a 20-minute interview script. FDEs typically run 15–40 async interviews in the first two weeks, then define success with a falsifiable statement, e.g., cutting a dispatcher's route-planning time from 90 to 30 minutes. The article includes a reusable template and three common mistakes: demoing before asking, treating praise as data, and interviewing only supporters.

Key points

  • Week 0 research planning precedes any prototyping or code.
  • Ask about past events, not feature wishes; praise is fool's gold.
  • Interview skeptics first to surface honest failure risks.
  • Plan 15–40 async interviews in the first two weeks.
  • Success definitions must be falsifiable: user, metric, baseline, target.

In interviews, ask customers how they did it last time, not what features they want—that's how you get data instead of a wish list.

Key Takeaways

  • Customer research is the FDE's own job; you can't offload it to a product manager. If you misunderstand the problem in week one, the whole team will build the wrong thing.
  • The best opening question is asking the customer to walk you through the last time they did this task. Customer praise is fool's gold.
  • A success definition needs users, metrics, current levels, and target values—and it must be specific enough that it could be proven wrong.

It's Monday morning, and you're sitting in the customer's conference room. You've been given access to the data warehouse, a Slack channel, and a one-line request from the department head: "We need an agent to handle order dispatch."

A developer's instinct is to open the laptop and start prototyping right away. Wait a few days and start from a blank page instead.

Perspective AI published a guide in 2026 on how FDEs should conduct research. The guide argues that understanding the customer is the core of a Forward Deployed Engineer's (FDE) work, not something to hand off to a product manager. Figuring out what the system actually needs to do is the FDE's responsibility.

So if you misunderstand the problem, the team will likely build the wrong thing—and that mistake may not surface until a lot of effort has already been invested.

Why not just ask customers what they need?

The FDE model originated at Palantir, which understood this early on. Palantir doesn't ask customers what they want; instead, it sends engineers into the customer's own environment. When facing complex, novel problems, customers often don't know what they need until they see a system running.

Interviews still matter, but you have to ask the right questions. "What features do you need?" gets you a wish list. "What happened the last time you did this?" gets you data. The purpose of the one-page plan is to force yourself to ask the second kind of question.

Week 0: What goes on this page?

The same Perspective AI guide lays out a four-week FDE research cycle: Week 0 lays the groundwork, Weeks 1–2 are for large-scale asynchronous customer interviews, and Weeks 3–4 are for designing evals together with the customer's domain experts. Week 0 happens before the first line of code is written, and it must produce two things.

The first is a stakeholder map: the executive sponsor, the person responsible for judging the results (the eval owner), the end users, the system administration contact, and the skeptics. The second is a clear distinction between who judges the system's output and who benefits from that output. These are often not the same people.

The rest of the one-pager below is a suggested structure, not taken from the guide. Limit yourself to one page: if it doesn't fit on one page, it means you don't yet know what matters most. Here's a template you can use directly:

调研计划 — [客户] — 第 0 周
1. 用客户自己的话描述问题：
2. 利益相关方地图（谁能阻止、拖慢、扩大项目）：
   发起人 | 评测负责人 | 最终用户 | 系统管理员 | 怀疑者
   谁评判输出？谁从输出中获益？
3. 最多 3 条待验证的假设：
4. 第 1–2 周访谈安排（访谈谁、何时、同步还是异步）：
5. 成功定义：
   [谁] 把 [X] 从 [Y] 降低/提高到 [N]，衡量方式为 [如何衡量]
6. 待解决的问题：

The fifth line is the hardest to write, so leave it blank for now. Fill it in after you've talked to real users.

Talk to the skeptics first

There's one column on the stakeholder map that's often forgotten: the skeptics. The Perspective AI guide describes this person as someone in the customer organization who doubts the project will succeed—and who can help you discover the failure modes that really matter.

Don't avoid this person. Ask them the two questions the guide recommends: "What conditions need to be met for this to truly succeed?" and "Where have similar projects in this organization failed before?"

Their answers will tell you where they think the project will go wrong. In the first week, this is usually the most honest risk list you can get.

Interview outline: Ask about last week

For end users, the guide recommends opening with "Walk me through the last time you did X." This aligns with two principles from Rob Fitzpatrick's book *The Mom Test*.

First: talk about the customer's life, not your idea. Second: ask about specific things that have already happened, not general opinions or predictions about the future.

A 20-minute outline might look like this:

开场：  “讲讲你上一次给早班规划路线的经过。”
深挖：  “哪一步最花时间？那天花了多久？”
        “当时你开着哪些界面或文件？”
        “上一次出问题时，你是怎么处理的？”
收尾：  “有没有什么我该问却没问的？”
        “接下来我应该找谁聊？”

Both closing questions come from *The Mom Test*. The book also argues that the goal of every meeting is to secure a commitment and a next step, such as scheduling another meeting or getting an introduction.

Ignore comments like "that sounds great." Fitzpatrick calls praise fool's gold in customer learning: shiny, distracting, and worthless.

On interview volume, the Perspective AI guide notes that FDEs typically conduct 15–40 asynchronous customer interviews in the first two weeks, while live interviews, being hard to schedule, tend to stay around 8–10.

Treat that number as a reminder. When you're not sitting next to the respondent, your outline must be clear enough that they can answer on their own—so write it carefully and test it before sending it out in batches.

From one answer to a number that can be proven wrong

Suppose there's a logistics company. You ask a dispatcher to walk you through the last time she planned routes. She says: "Last Monday morning, it took me about 90 minutes. Two trucks reported breakdowns at 6 a.m., I had to call the drivers one by one, then manually copy all the information into Excel, and three deliveries ended up late."

That one sentence already gives you everything you need. The user is the morning-shift dispatcher. X is route planning time. Y is 90 minutes—the current level she stated herself. You propose N of 30 minutes, then verify with her and the skeptic whether that target is realistic.

The fifth line of the one-pager now reads: "Morning-shift dispatchers cut route planning time from 90 minutes to 30 minutes, measured by time logs across 10 consecutive shifts." This statement could be wrong—and that's exactly why it's useful. "The agent makes dispatching more efficient" can never be wrong, so it proves nothing.

Once you have a first prototype, show it to the domain experts. The Perspective AI guide is explicit: don't ask them to score hypothetical outputs—give them real ones. A real route plan for a Monday morning shift will expose unwritten rules that no interview could ever uncover.

Three mistakes that invalidate the one-pager

The most common mistake is presenting a solution before asking questions. If you demo your agent right away, the customer will discuss your agent instead of describing their work. You haven't asked a single question, and the interview has already violated the first principle of *The Mom Test*.

The second mistake is recording praise and commitments as data. "We'd definitely use this" is a prediction about the future. "Last week it took me 90 minutes" is something that already happened. Only the latter belongs in a success definition.

The last mistake is interviewing only the project's supporters. A stakeholder map without skeptics will only show you what you want to see. Imagine this: the real risk lies with the legal team, the IT department, or a shift supervisor who has watched three projects fail in a row—and it surfaces just as you're about to launch.

Take this one-pager to your job interviews

If a job description for an FDE role mentions "customer research" or "requirements scoping," prepare a concrete research story.

Use the same structure as the success definition in your resume: who you interviewed, what you found, and which metric moved from Y to N. If you haven't done a real project yet, writing a one-page research plan for a company you know well is also worth bringing along.

The code you write in week three can only be as good as the one-pager you wrote in week 0. So write that page first.
