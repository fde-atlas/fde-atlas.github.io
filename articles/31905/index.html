FDE vs. Sales Engineer, Consulting, and Implementation: How to Draw the Boundaries of Responsibility

Original title: FDE与售前、咨询、实施：怎样辨认责任边界

pafa.github.io · 2018-08-16 · 8 min read

Summary

This chapter argues that the FDE title overlaps heavily with pre-sales, consulting, and implementation roles, so names alone cannot define responsibilities. Citing the US Bureau of Labor Statistics on sales engineers, Accenture's 2018 service scope, and SAP's Ariba deployment methodology, the author shows these established roles already include hands-on technical work. Instead of labels, the chapter proposes four diagnostic questions: what outcomes the team is accountable for, what it may change, where new knowledge flows, and who bears surprises from exploration. A teaching repair-shop scenario contrasts three arrangements: pre-purchase validation, on-scope delivery, and delivery combined with joint problem discovery. A 2020 Palantir interview with a fully remote forward deployed software engineer shows physical presence is not essential. The conclusion: verifiable commitments matter more than job titles.

Key points

  • FDE work overlaps with sales engineering, consulting, and implementation, so titles alone mislead.
  • Four questions distinguish roles: accountability, permitted changes, knowledge flow, and risk allocation.
  • A repair-shop scenario contrasts validation, standard delivery, and exploratory delivery arrangements.
  • A 2020 Palantir interview shows FDEs can work fully remotely; location and responsibility are separate.
  • Verifiable delivery commitments, not popular titles, should guide hiring and procurement decisions.

When a company says it wants to hire an FDE, it may want a technical consultant who can close a purchase, or it may want an engineer who remains accountable for the customer's production systems. When one title covers different tasks, recruiting, pricing, and acceptance easily end up talking past each other.

The previous chapter traced how the title emerged. This chapter addresses a different question: given a job description or a partnership proposal, how can you tell what it actually entails, without being carried away by the title itself.

Where does it overlap with sales engineering, consulting, and implementation?

As the title gains popularity, the most immediate question is: isn't this just presales or implementation?

The question has value, because it demands that the new title explain its increment. But in answering it, we must not first shrink existing professions into a strawman. The U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics' current occupational description for sales engineers includes not only demonstrations and closing orders, but also confirming technical requirements, arranging installation, modifying products to fit customers, and helping resolve problems; some also participate in new product development. 1 So neither "having customer contact" nor "hands-on technical problem solving" is by itself enough to separate the FDE from sales engineering.

Consulting is not just reports either. In Accenture's 2018 announcement about its software product engineering business, platform strategy, design, and implementation were already placed within the same service scope. 2 This is a service provider's own description of its business and does not mean every consulting contract includes engineering work; but it is enough to remind us that "consulting only advises, while the FDE builds" should not serve as an industry-wide definition.

Implementation can likewise go deep into real work. SAP's public courses on Ariba deployment list requirements and process design, data preparation, system configuration, production migration, and post-go-live support as activities of different phases. 3 This book cites the current methodology description and does not use it to retroactively describe the actual process of any past project. What it shows is this: implementing a standard product may also require joint participation from technology, business, and organization, and cannot be reduced to clicking through a few settings.

Given that these jobs overlap so broadly, what use is a title at all? A title can convey an organization's emphasis, but when judging a specific role, one must still dig a level deeper. Two teams may both write code: one mainly serves to validate a purchase decision, the other owns the outcome of the customer's daily operations; two teams may both implement systems: one is required to deliver as closely as possible to established methods, the other is allowed to bring newly discovered problems from the customer back into product development. What distinguishes them is the combination of responsibility and follow-up action.

Nor do these combinations need to stay fixed forever. A Sales Engineer may continue to participate in key deliveries, an implementation team may develop a sellable industry product, and an FDE may also be pulled by sales targets. If every title is defined as a set of behaviors that never overlap, real projects become hard to fit in. A more practical approach is to look at the work first, then at how the organization names that work.

This is also why this book uses "Forward Deployed Engineer" as the working translation of FDE, without making "on-site" a necessary condition. In a 2020 employee interview, Palantir recorded a Forward Deployed Software Engineer working fully remotely due to the pandemic. 4 "The front line" can mean a physical location, but it can also mean a position where one can access real data, real users, and operational feedback. Sitting in the customer's building every day while receiving requirements only through layers of relay is not necessarily closer to the work than a remote engineer who can directly verify problems.

Of course, remote work is not a free substitute. Being able to observe actual processes, obtain appropriate permissions, and promptly reach the responsible person all require arrangements. The point here is to separate space from responsibility: office location is one condition, and the ability to understand and change real work is another. The former sometimes helps the latter, but it cannot serve as proof of the latter.

Ask a different set of questions, and the differences become clear

A teaching scenario can put these differences on the same table. Consider the teaching repair business from the prologue that wants to shorten customer wait times: customers submit faults, and technicians need to find the corresponding model, applicable manuals, parts inventory, and service records. The team arrangements below are scenarios for comparison, not a record of any real company's project.

One arrangement limits the task to pre-purchase validation. The engineer takes a small batch of approved samples, demonstrates that the product can retrieve applicable manuals, and lists the conditions for integrating with existing systems. It answers well the question "is this product worth continuing to evaluate." As long as both sides know the scope of validation, there is no need to dress it up as a production system with round-the-clock repair commitments.

Another arrangement centers on delivering as contracted. The team confirms fields, migrates data, configures permissions, trains users, and completes the agreed features and acceptance. Its value lies in turning relatively clear requirements into stable delivery. If requirements change significantly, scope, cost, and timeline must be renegotiated. Such scope management protects quality and also prevents ad hoc requests from endlessly swallowing the original tasks.

A third arrangement still delivers, but also takes on exploration: does the repair wait come mainly from not finding information, from parts being out of stock, or from the person who approves replacement parts being unavailable? If on-site evidence overturns the initial judgment, the engineer has the authority to reselect the problem with the customer and bring recurring software gaps back to the product team. Here, discovering the requirements themselves is part of the work, and evaluation cannot rely solely on whether the originally listed features were completed one by one.

All three arrangements may require excellent engineers, and all may appear at different stages of the same project. Calling the third one an FDE does not prove the first two are lesser; what a company needs most may be precisely a well-scoped technical validation or a mature implementation. The distinction here is not about who works harder, but about what the customer and vendor jointly allow to be re-examined.

The first question to ask is: what outcomes is the team accountable for? Is it the customer understanding the solution, the delivery of an agreed feature, continuous operation, or does it also include the advancement of a jointly built product? "Owning customer success" sounds broad, but it is not enough to organize work. When repair times remain long, the team must know whether it needs to keep investigating causes, or has reached the boundary of scope that requires separate negotiation.

The second question to ask is: what can the team change? Can it only modify demo data, can it configure systems, can it write into the customer's business software, or can it also submit changes to the core product? When responsibility exceeds authority, engineers keep passing problems elsewhere; when authority exceeds review capacity, local fixes may affect more users. A useful role design needs the two to roughly match.

The third question to ask is: where does new knowledge go? Does it stay in personal notes, become the customer's operating practice, enter the vendor's delivery toolkit, or change the software platform? These destinations have no inherent ranking, but they correspond to different commercial commitments. If a company wants future deliveries to become progressively easier, it must be able to explain how experience leaves the original project and becomes understood and used by others; merely adding a few success story names does not yet show this process.

The fourth question to ask is: who bears the surprises? New discoveries from exploration change timelines, changes in customer processes bring rework, and maintenance tasks appear after going into production. Billing by hours, billing by project, or funding upfront investment from product revenue each make teams attend to different risks. This book does not treat any particular billing model as the FDE's identity card. How the contract divides responsibility and how the team actually works are more explanatory than whether the invoice says "consulting."

Observing with these four questions, there is no need to rush to rank all roles. A person titled implementation consultant may continuously build reusable software; a person titled FDE may spend most of their time doing presales demos. The former does not need to change titles to gain recognition, and the latter cannot automatically acquire the full meaning of product engineering by title alone.

Clear identity and sufficient mechanism are two different axes

We can now sketch a brief profile of the evidence. It does not judge who is more "authentic," but prevents one kind of evidence from answering for another.

EvidenceWhat identity can confirmWhat mechanism can revealWhat still cannot be inferred
Palantir's remote engineer interviewExplicitly titled Forward Deployed Software Engineer at the timeRemote building and production incident handlingThat every FDE project uses the same division of labor, or that independent revenue proof already exists
IBM's IMS historyNo evidence of an FDE job titleCustomer-specific problems later became sellable softwareThat direct organizational lineage exists between the two companies
An announcement that merely declares the purchase of AI toolsOnly the buyer and vendor can be identified from the announcementThe specific engineering process cannot yet be confirmedHaving bought a tool does not mean an FDE team exists or results have been achieved

The last line is a note on material type, not a new case. It reminds us: the title is an identity clue, the actual change is a mechanism clue, and adoption and outcomes are further evidence. All three need to be found separately.

For example, a job description promising that employees can propose product changes confirms the responsibilities the company expects; only by seeing records of proposals, selection, implementation, and maintenance can we further analyze a genuine backflow. When gaps appear, keep the gaps—do not automatically fill them with a fashionable job title.

Replacing the label with a verifiable commitment

Continuing the teaching scenario of the repair business, the person in charge can ask the candidate team to write the four questions into a single delivery specification: which waiting step to improve, which materials and software may be changed, which problems can be brought back into the shared product, and who decides to increase investment when new requirements arise. Only when these can be written clearly is there a basis for discussing whom to hire and what to buy.

If the proposal only guarantees completing one demonstration, buy it as a proof of concept; if the configuration of a standard product is already sufficient, choose a mature implementation; if the problem still requires joint discovery, and both parties are willing to undertake real engineering and continuous feedback, then consider an FDE arrangement. There is no permanently winning role here, only a combination of responsibilities that better fits the task at hand.
