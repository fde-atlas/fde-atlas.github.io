How to Build RAG: The 5 Steps and Evaluation Design That FDEs Use in Practice | FDE Research Institute

Summary

This Japanese article lays out how forward deployed engineers (FDEs) approach building RAG systems in enterprise settings. Its core argument: fix the target questions and evaluation criteria before touching technology. It details a five-step process (scoping, document preparation, retrieval, generation, evaluation/operations) with deliverables, exit conditions, and timelines, and explains troubleshooting, metrics like Recall@k and faithfulness, and handover to internal teams.

Key points

  • Start by fixing 20–50 recurring questions and an evaluation set, not model choices.
  • Five steps: scoping, document cleanup, retrieval, generation design, evaluation and operations.
  • When accuracy is low, check retrieval before rewriting prompts.
  • Build a 50–100 item eval set including 10–20% unanswerable questions.
  • Handover is complete when internal staff can run one improvement cycle alone.

How should you approach building RAG? What key decisions should be made before starting?

When building RAG, first decide the questions to answer and the evaluation criteria, narrow the scope of documents to search, and then begin.

RAG (Retrieval-Augmented Generation) is an architecture that searches external knowledge such as internal documents and generates answers with a large language model based on that content. As a method, it was formalized in the 2020 paper by Lewis et al., "Retrieval-Augmented Generation for Knowledge-Intensive NLP Tasks" (NeurIPS 2020), and is now the standard architecture for internal knowledge search and inquiry handling.

In my impression, most RAG projects that fail in practice start with model selection or comparing vector databases. When an FDE (Forward Deployed Engineer) enters a company's workplace, the first thing to pin down is not the technical components but "whose questions, answered with what accuracy, would change the work." If this remains vague, the project tends to end up in a state where plausible answers are returned but nobody uses the system.

How do you decide what RAG should solve?

Limit the target to questions that recur in business operations. Extract the top 20 to 50 most frequent questions from inquiry histories to fix the scope.

Three practical criteria for narrowing down are as follows.

  • Recurring — Choose tasks where you can count how many similar questions come in per week. One-off research requests are hard to measure RAG's effectiveness on.
  • The answer exists in documents — Questions whose answers are not written in existing documents are a document organization issue, not a RAG build. If you confuse the two, accuracy will not improve no matter how much you refine retrieval.
  • A human can judge the correct answer — Start with areas where the person in charge can immediately say whether an answer is correct or incorrect. Questions that cannot be judged prevent building an evaluation set, and the improvement loop will not turn.

Should you choose RAG or fine-tuning?

If knowledge is updated frequently and source citation is required, choose RAG; if you want to fix output format or style, choose fine-tuning.

In practice, I feel it is often not an either-or choice, and the first choice is a combination of "RAG for knowledge, prompts to fix output format." If the target documents fit within dozens of pages, a configuration that puts the full text into the context without building RAG is faster and more stable in accuracy. It helps to keep your judgment steady by framing RAG as a mechanism that becomes necessary once the document volume grows and full-text input is no longer realistic.

What non-functional requirements should be agreed on before starting?

The four points to agree on are permission separation, response time, update frequency, and handling of incorrect answers. Adding them later means redesigning.

Permission separation is the top priority in particular. For RAG that includes HR documents or contracts, you need to filter "whether a document may be included in search results" at the retrieval stage. With a design that filters after generation, an accident can occur where information that should not be visible gets mixed into the model's answers. In FDE practice, it is common to incorporate, from the initial configuration, a design that always joins the user's department and role as metadata conditions into the search query.

How should RAG build steps be divided in practice?

In practice, proceed in five steps: target selection, document preparation, retrieval implementation, generation design, and evaluation and operations.

The purpose of dividing into steps is not to measure progress but to make it possible to isolate "where things are stuck." Because RAG is an architecture where retrieval and generation are chained, viewing it as one unit makes it impossible to identify the cause of insufficient accuracy.

What are the deliverables and exit criteria for each step?

Set deliverables and exit criteria for each step, obtain agreement from the business side, and then move on. Duration varies with document volume and the difficulty of permission coordination.

Step 2, document cleanup, is the most tedious part of the process. If you estimate based on the assumption that "internal documents are well organized," issues surface during implementation: old and current versions mixed together, copies scattered across separate folders per department. If search retrieves an outdated version, no matter how good the model is, the answer will be wrong.

How should chunking and embeddings be designed?

Split chunks at semantic boundaries, with overlap between adjacent chunks. For documents with heading structures, splitting by heading is an easy-to-handle approach.

Practical design guidelines are as follows.

  • Chunk size — Rather than fixed lengths, splitting along document structure such as headings, clauses, or Q&A pairs yields more stable accuracy. For documents without structure, aim for a few hundred tokens with a fixed amount of overlap between adjacent chunks.
  • Metadata — Always attach document name, last updated date, department, and visibility scope to each chunk. These are used both for filtering search results and for displaying answer sources.
  • Context completion — On its own, a chunk loses what "this" or "the company" refers to. In "Contextual Retrieval," published by Anthropic in September 2024, adding a short context description of each chunk relative to the whole document and combining it with BM25 reduced the search failure rate by about 49%, and by about 67% when reranking was added (see the official Anthropic blog).
  • Embedding model — For Japanese documents, the effective performance of multilingual models varies greatly by target domain. It is safest to compare two or three candidates on an evaluation set before settling on one.

How far should the first release go?

The first release should be limited to one business process and one document group, with a scope of getting a handful of internal users to use it daily.

If you open it to the whole company as your first move, the distribution of questions becomes too broad and the evaluation set stops working. Running it with limited access for a few weeks and feeding the actual question logs back into the evaluation set ultimately speeds up improvement. In the FDE approach, this "state where usage can be observed" is treated as the highest-priority deliverable.

What should you improve when RAG accuracy is poor?

When accuracy is lacking, suspect the retrieval side first. Before improving generation, check whether the correct documents are ranking highly in retrieval.

The standard practice in the field is: if rewriting the prompt doesn't help, chances are the correct document was never passed to the model in the first place. The diagnostic procedure is simple: for each question in the evaluation set, have a person visually inspect the top k retrieval results and count whether the correct document is included. If it isn't, it's a retrieval problem; if it is included but the answer is still wrong, it's a generation problem.

Where do the causes of retrieval misses lie?

Most causes boil down to three things: notation variants, chunk granularity, and duplicate/outdated documents mixed together.

SymptomLikely causeEffective remedy
Irrelevant documents rank highlyVector search alone is weak on proper nouns and model numbersHybrid search combining keyword search such as BM25
The correct document appears but ranks lowRanking relies only on embedding similarityApply cross-encoder reranking to the top 50 results
Only fragmentary answers come outChunks are too fine, losing contextEnlarge chunks, or pass adjacent chunks together
Answers use outdated informationOld document versions remain in the indexFilter by updated-date metadata; make index rebuilding a rule
Abbreviations and internal terms don't matchInternal jargon isn't captured by embeddingsQuery expansion with a terminology dictionary; synonym expansion

Hybrid search and reranking are improvements that tend to show results relative to the added implementation effort. However, reranking increases inference cost and response time, so decide how many top results to apply it to in line with your response-time requirements.

How do you fix generation that drifts from the facts?

Constrain the model to answer only within the scope of the provided documents, and design it to return "I don't know" when there is no basis.

Specifically, make citations of the reference documents mandatory in the prompt, and have the model refuse to answer when it cannot cite. In addition, always display the source document name and relevant section on screen. Showing sources not only guarantees accuracy but also empowers users to verify answers themselves.

What I've observed in the field is that, more than answer correctness, "whether errors can be noticed" determines whether the system is viable for business use. With sources shown, users can reject suspicious answers on their own. Without sources, a single wrong answer can destroy trust in the entire tool.

How should RAG evaluation be designed?

Measure retrieval and generation separately. Prepare an evaluation set of 50 to 100 questions with correct documents attached, and run it on every change.

A RAG system whose evaluation is postponed makes improvement subjective. You can't judge whether changing chunk size or rewriting the prompt helped or hurt, and the work devolves into trial and error. The evaluation set is the first deliverable created in Step 1.

How do you build the evaluation set?

Extract questions from actual inquiry histories, and have business owners attach the correct documents and model answers.

  • Use the actual wording of questions as submitted, not model-generated ones. Sloppy phrasing and abbreviations are precisely where retrieval struggles.
  • For each question, record "the document and section containing the correct answer." This becomes the basis for measuring retrieval metrics.
  • Mix in 10 to 20 percent "questions that should not be answerable." This detects whether the system is forcing answers to out-of-scope questions.
  • After operations begin, add 10 to 20 items per month from real logs and keep updating the evaluation set.

The scale of 50 to 100 items is a guideline based on what business owners can realistically annotate. Too few and the metrics swing on a handful of hits and misses; too many and updates stall.

Which metrics should you look at?

For retrieval, look at Recall@k and MRR; for generation, look at faithfulness and answer relevance. Judge metrics by the difference before and after changes rather than absolute values.

For evaluating generation, using an LLM as the judge (LLM-as-a-judge) is the practical approach. Open-source evaluation frameworks such as Ragas implement calculations for faithfulness and answer relevancy (official Ragas documentation). However, since the judge can also make mistakes, the safe order is to first compare its judgments against human judgments for the first few dozen cases, understand how the judgments diverge, and only then automate.

How should evaluation be continued after going into operation?

Run evaluations automatically every time documents are updated, and detect drops in metrics. In addition, accumulate user feedback as logs.

RAG accuracy can degrade even without changing the model, because documents grow, the organization changes, and question trends shift. Before handing over operations, build in a mechanism that runs the evaluation set regularly on CI and sends notifications when Recall@k or Faithfulness falls below a threshold. On the UI side, provide simple "helpful / not helpful" feedback plus free-text input, and run a weekly review of negative responses—this is an easy-to-sustain setup.

How does an FDE drive a company's RAG development?

An FDE works on-site, iterating with business stakeholders through every stage, from collecting questions to designing evaluations and handing over operations.

RAG development fits poorly with conventional order-based development because requirements depend on "what will be asked" and cannot be fixed in advance as a specification. Only by looking at actual question logs can you determine the necessary chunk design and permission conditions. Having an FDE stationed on-site and rebuilding in short cycles is a realistic response to this nature of the work.

What does an FDE handle in RAG development?

An FDE consistently handles everything from interviewing business stakeholders, building the evaluation set, implementing retrieval and generation, to creating operational procedures.

  • Collecting questions — Read inquiry-desk and chat histories together with business stakeholders to determine which questions must be answered.
  • Inventorying documents — Identify which document is the source of truth and who updates it. Documents where the "source of truth" cannot be determined require clarifying management responsibility before RAG.
  • Designing evaluation operations — Build the labeling of correct answers for the evaluation set into the design as work performed by business stakeholders, since external engineers alone cannot judge correctness.
  • Implementation and iteration — Show evaluation results to business stakeholders weekly and agree on what to fix next. The key point is not to let engineers alone decide improvement priorities.

The FDE role, as defined by Palantir, is known as a position that embeds itself in the customer's business and handles everything from problem definition to implementation (Palantir's official site). Domains like RAG, where business knowledge and implementation are inseparable, are a particularly good fit for this way of working. At VACAN Technologies as well, when considering RAG for internal documents, we spend time on the step of deciding "which questions will be asked and whose judgment defines the correct answer" before technology selection.

How should the handover to an in-house team proceed?

The goal of the handover is a state where the in-house owner can independently complete one full cycle of "add documents → run evaluation → decide on improvements."

What should be handed over is not code but operational procedures. Specifically: the index rebuild procedure when documents are added or updated, the rules for updating the evaluation set, the troubleshooting procedure when metrics drop (retrieval or generation?), and the response flow for handling reports of incorrect answers.

Involving the in-house owner in evaluation labeling from the construction period onward reduces the disruption at handover. Conversely, if you start the handover only after construction ends, the intent behind the evaluation set fails to carry over, updates stop, and accuracy quietly degrades. RAG is not a build-once-and-done system—it is an operational asset that must continuously adapt to changes in documents and questions. Whether this premise is shared at the time of handover will shape how the system is used over the following year.
