The Four People Who Will Rebuild the Enterprise

Michael Couch | couch.cx · 2026-11-09 · 4 min read

Summary

Michael Couch argues that AI-assisted engineering has destroyed the economics behind forty years of enterprise coordination layers, yet most companies respond by repeating old mistakes—large AI Centers of Excellence, disconnected research pods, or monolithic vendor platforms. Drawing on IDC's 2026 FDE research, the two-pizza rule, and stream-aligned team models, he proposes a minimal frontier unit of four complementary roles: a frontier lead, a creative technical lead, an applied builder, and a domain champion. Removing any role produces a familiar failure mode. The unit's mandate should be to pioneer, prove, and graduate capabilities back into the business rather than accumulate maintenance burden.

Key points

  • AI-assisted engineering removed the software scarcity that justified enterprise coordination layers.
  • Common wrong responses: 50-person AI centers, academic pods, monolithic vendor platforms.
  • The minimum unit combines four roles: frontier lead, technical lead, applied builder, domain champion.
  • Each role's absence maps to a specific corporate failure mode.
  • Success means graduating capability to the business, not permanent maintenance ownership.

How to build the smallest enterprise team capable of turning emerging technology into measurable organisational advantage. The next generation of enterprise technology will not be organised around departments, systems, or specialist disciplines. It will be organised around the continuous discovery, delivery, and compounding of operational leverage.

The enterprise spent forty years constructing organisational machinery around the scarcity of software production.

Because building was slow, expensive, and technically fragile, organisations designed an elaborate liturgy of coordination. Requirements moved from the person doing the work to business analysts, system architects, product managers, designers, engineers, and QA leads—each adding translation, delay, and distance. By the time a system landed, the business had usually asked a different question, and the people on the front line had invented an unofficial spreadsheet workaround to survive.

AI-assisted engineering has dismantled that economic foundation. A capable operator paired with modern tooling can now research, prototype, test, and deploy a working capability in an afternoon—long before an executive steering group has agreed on the terms of reference for a discovery phase.

Yet most enterprises are responding to this shift by repeating their historical errors: hiring 50-person "AI Centers of Excellence," funding academic research pods disconnected from daily business, or buying monolithic vendor platforms that leave them permanently reliant on strangers.

The future does not belong to the largest AI department. It belongs to the smallest effective human system capable of making the enterprise continuously better at transforming itself.

What the research actually tells us: Four real-world precedents

The market is converging on embedded, outcome-driven engineering. IDC's 2026 research distinguishes vendor FDEs, systems-integrator FDEs, and specialist deployment companies, noting that the label has outgrown any single proprietary definition. But four precedents provide the actual architectural blueprint:

Forward-deployed engineers are placed directly into the customer's operational trench. The defining characteristic is not writing code to a client-supplied specification, but holding end-to-end responsibility for solving the actual operational problem.

DeployCo and enterprise FDE practices combine process discovery, production engineering, live validation, and capability transfer. They maintain a strict distinction between frontier engineering (novel capability) and repeatable implementation.

The two-pizza rule was never just about headcount; it was about autonomy. Small, decoupled teams with single-threaded ownership cut through matrixed dependencies and own their trajectory without six layers of permission.

Stream-aligned teams deliver value directly; platform and enabling teams provide reusable scaffolding without becoming approval bottlenecks. Team interfaces are designed explicitly to maximize fast flow.

The opportunity for an enterprise is not to contract out its future to an external consultancy army. The opportunity is to establish its own sovereign operating model: an internal capability that orchestrates employees, contractors, specialist consultancies, and vendor engineers under one cohesive standard.

The core: Four complementary intelligences

The minimum effective frontier unit requires four distinct minds. Each brings a fundamentally different relationship to time, uncertainty, and the business:

The architect of opportunity. Sees the whole system, spots shared patterns across disparate business units, eliminates political friction, and enforces the transition from discovery to delivery.

Makes the improbable possible. Solves the hardest integration joins, establishes reusable technical scaffolding (evals, permissions, observability), and creates momentum without accumulating maintenance debt.

The engine of continuity. Builds, tests, refactors, and hardens systems with AI-assisted speed. Must possess the engineering rigor to debug, test, and take true responsibility for generated code.

The owner of operational reality. An active co-creator on the front line with protected capacity. Supplies real exceptions, validates working prototypes, and ensures the solution genuinely changes the work.

Why this combination compounds

Remove any one of these four intelligences, and the system collapses into a familiar corporate failure mode:

  • Without the Frontier Lead: The engineers build technically dazzling tools that solve problems nobody in the business actually cares about or funds.
  • Without the Creative Technical Lead: The builders produce brittle spaghetti code and unmonitored scripts that collapse the moment they touch enterprise security, compliance, or concurrency.
  • Without the Applied Builder: The leads get trapped in discovery workshops and architecture slideware, producing strategy memos instead of working software.
  • Without the Domain Champion: The team builds for a theoretical, idealized workflow that ignores the messy exceptions of a real Tuesday morning.

Build to graduate, not to accumulate

The central danger of any high-performing innovation unit is that it becomes a victim of its own success. It builds five valuable systems, and within a year its engineers spend 90% of their bandwidth fielding bug reports, answering support tickets, and patching databases.

A frontier pod is not a permanent maintenance department.

Its mandate is to pioneer, prove, and graduate. A genuine transformation unit measures its success by how quickly it transfers capability back into the business, leaving behind clean code, executable skill libraries, and empowered operators who can run without supervision.

The goal is not to build the largest forward-deployed engineering organisation. It is to create the smallest effective human system capable of continuously transforming an enterprise.

Stop hiring monolithic AI teams. Stop issuing 80-page RFPs for multi-year software transformations. Assemble four people who understand the problem, give them an enterprise harness, and put them directly on the line.
