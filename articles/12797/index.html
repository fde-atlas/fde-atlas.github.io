What Does an FDE Actually Do? I've Broken It Down for You

Original title: FDE 到底是干嘛的？我帮你拆清楚了

53AI · 2026-06-04 · 7 min read

Summary

The author argues that an FDE is not an on-site coder but a full-cycle role spanning problem discovery, prototyping, quality safeguards, and driving business adoption, with coding taking only about 20% of the work. The article walks through a 30-day manufacturing engagement and breaks down five capabilities: spotting scenarios worth automating, reframing surface requests into real problems, rapid building with tools like Cursor and Dify, building evals and fallback mechanisms, and organizational adoption. It contrasts FDEs with traditional developers and pre-sales consultants, offers transition advice for programmers, product managers, and operations people, and stresses that FDE work is not exclusive to Palantir—anyone can start by solving a real pain point for a nearby business and turning the case into personal credibility.

Key points

  • FDE is a full-cycle role; coding is only about 20% of the work.
  • Five capabilities: value judgment, need reframing, rapid building, evals and guardrails, adoption.
  • Worthwhile scenarios have high frequency, heavy manual work, and 10x AI improvement.
  • Going live is not adoption; win champions and quantify value.
  • FDE is not Palantir-exclusive; start with real local pain points.

Full text

Key takeaway up front: An FDE is not a "senior programmer," nor is it as simple as "on-site development." It's a well-rounded professional who understands technology, understands business, and can win over customers. In the AI era, the most valuable skill isn't writing code—it's turning AI into systems customers actually dare to use. This article breaks down the five core capabilities an FDE needs.

In my last article, "When Technology Stops Being Valuable, What Do We Rely On to Survive?", I laid out three paths: helping enterprises deploy AI agents, building a personal brand, and combining the two.

The first one is FDE—Forward Deployed Engineer.

After publishing it, I received quite a few private messages. The questions were mostly along these lines:

"What does an FDE actually do?" "Can I do it?" "I'm a product manager / operations person / backend developer—does FDE have anything to do with me?"

Not everyone has a platform like Palantir. But you don't need Palantir—you just need to understand the FDE capability model.

Once you understand what capabilities an FDE actually needs, you'll know where to focus your efforts in the AI era.

When many people hear "FDE," their first thought is "on-site development"—bringing a laptop to the client's office and writing code on the spot.

That's not it.

Let me walk you through a real scenario. Suppose you walk into a manufacturing company:

Day 1-3: Meetings, meetings, and more meetings.

You're not opening your laptop to write code—you're sitting through endless meetings with the boss, the workshop supervisor, and the warehouse manager. You listen to their complaints: production scheduling relies on Old Wang's experience, and Old Wang is about to retire; customer quotes take two days, and customers walk away; inventory data hasn't been updated in three months—items on the books can't be found in the warehouse.

What are you doing? Figuring out where they actually hurt.

Day 4-7: Turning what you heard into a clear map.

Over three days you've heard dozens of problems, but not every one is worth solving with AI. You need to categorize them: Which are high-frequency pain points? Which could AI solve 10x better than existing solutions? Which look like technical problems but are actually management problems?

Then you pick the most painful scenario that's best suited for AI and sketch out a solution.

Day 8-14: Building a prototype.

Using Cursor, Claude Code, or an agent framework, you quickly build a working version. It doesn't need to be perfect—it needs to show the customer results. Then you run and iterate on it together with the business people.

Day 15-30: The hardest part begins.

It's not about "finishing and handing over." It's about making the customer dare to use it, want to use it, and keep using it.

You have to pass the IT department's security review, persuade business staff to change their work habits, and show the boss quantifiable value.

Writing code may only account for 20% of an FDE's work; the other 80% is communication, judgment, and driving things forward.

That's the core of this article.

I've broken down the capabilities an FDE needs into five. Each one can be practiced—you don't need to be a genius, but you do need to practice deliberately.

Many companies say "we want to use AI," but 80% of their use cases aren't worth doing.

It's not that AI can't do them—it's that doing them is pointless. For something that happens only three times a year, even a 10x efficiency gain from AI won't save much money.

How do you judge whether a use case is worth doing? Look for three signals:

Is the frequency high? For things that happen every day, even small optimizations yield big gains.

How is it solved today? If it currently relies entirely on manual work, experience, and veteran know-how, there's room for AI to step in.

Can AI be 10x better than the existing solution? 10% better and nobody switches; 10x better and everyone fights to use it.

Example: customer quotes take two days, happen 50 times a day, and currently rely on Old Wang manually checking inventory and calculating prices. This scenario has high frequency, heavy manual dependency, and AI can respond in seconds—worth doing.

Conversely: quarterly financial audit report generation happens only once every three months and requires manual sign-off. Low frequency, and the manual step can't be bypassed—not worth prioritizing.

A nose for value isn't a gift—it's trained. Look at more companies, listen to more business scenarios, and you'll develop judgment.

This is the most underrated FDE capability.

The customer says, "I want a knowledge base Q&A system."

If you build it directly, what you deliver might be: upload documents → retrieve → generate answers. Technically fine, but nobody on the business side may actually use it.

Because the customer's real problem might be: "The veteran experts are retiring, and 30 years of experience can't be taken with them. New hires take six months to get up to speed, and they make frequent mistakes during that time."

The surface-level need is "knowledge base Q&A"; the underlying problem is "knowledge transfer."

If you reframe the problem as "knowledge transfer," your solution might not be simple RAG, but rather:

Extract the veteran expert's decision logic and turn it into a decision tree. When a new employee encounters a problem, they don't search documents—the system guides them step by step to make the same judgment the veteran would.

The core FDE skill is hearing what's left unsaid and translating surface-level requests into real problems.

How do you practice this? Simple: for every requirement you receive, ask "why" three levels deep.

"Why do you need a knowledge base?" → "Because new hires can't find information." → "Why can't they find it?" → "Because the information is all in the veterans' heads and has never been organized." → "So what really needs to be solved?" → "Enabling new hires to make decisions like the veterans do."

At the third level, the real problem emerges.

In the AI era, you don't build from scratch.

Cursor, Claude Code, and Copilot have driven the coding barrier down to the floor. Frameworks like LangGraph, CrewAI, and Dify have packaged agent architectures into building blocks.

FDE building capability = choosing the right tools + combining and assembling + rapid validation.

You don't need to write underlying frameworks, but you do need to know:

• When to use RAG, when to use fine-tuning, and when plain prompt engineering is enough • Which scenarios suit LangGraph and where Dify is sufficient • How to turn an idea into a prototype the customer can touch within three days

The key isn't "writing fast"—it's "knowing what to write and what not to write."

Often, an FDE's best judgment shows in what they *don't* do. The customer wants 10 features; you know which ones are essential for the MVP and which can wait. Cutting down to 3 core features and shipping a prototype in a week beats building 10 features over three months a hundred times over.

This is the capability technical people most easily overlook.

A demo that runs doesn't mean it's production-ready.

The cost of an agent making a mistake can be greater than having no agent at all. A knowledge base Q&A system occasionally giving a wrong answer might just be embarrassing. But an automated quoting system calculating a wrong price could cost the customer tens of thousands.

An FDE needs to know three things:

How to build an eval set. Once you get the customer's data, first carve out a portion as a test set. Not randomly—cover typical scenarios, edge cases, and abnormal cases. Use this eval set to measure the system's accuracy.

How to set boundaries. Agents aren't omnipotent. Which questions it can answer with confidence, and which ones it should respond to with "I'm not sure—recommend confirming with a human"—this boundary must be defined in advance.

How to have a fallback. What happens if the agent goes wrong? Are there logs? Is there a human review step? Is there a rollback mechanism?

A good FDE, when building a system, should spend as much time on evals and guardrails as on feature development.

This isn't over-engineering—it's being accountable to the customer.

This is the least "technical" capability, but possibly the one that most determines whether a project lives or dies.

System launch ≠ business adoption.

I've seen too many cases: the technical team builds the system, it works well, but nobody uses it.

Why?

Users don't trust the AI's output—they find it unreliable and are afraid to take responsibility. IT blocks it at the security review, and data permissions never get approved. Business processes haven't changed, so the new system clashes with the old workflow. Leadership sees no quantified value, so there's no reason for continued investment.

How does an FDE break through?

Win over the key people. Find the person in the business unit who feels the most pain, and make them your internal champion. Having them push for you is ten times more effective than pushing yourself.

Start small and move fast. Don't launch company-wide right away. Pick one minimal scenario first, perfect it, and let everyone see the results.

Quantify the value. "Improved efficiency" is too vague. Say things like "quote turnaround time cut from 2 days to 10 minutes, saving 200 hours of manual work per month."

Driving organizational adoption isn't about gift-giving and wining and dining—it's about getting the right people into the same boat and letting them see the benefits of change.

One table to make it clear:

DimensionTraditional developmentPre-sales/consultingFDE
Core activityWrite code to specPitch solutions and win dealsEnd-to-end closed loop
Where requirements come fromHanded down by the product managerRaised by the clientDiscovered and reframed by yourself
Delivery standardFeature goes liveContract signedBusiness actually uses it
Technical depthDeepShallowGood enough
Business understandingNot neededImportantMost important

See the pattern?

Traditional developers only deal with code, pre-sales only with solutions, project managers only with schedules.

An FDE has to handle everything, but doesn't need to master each one.

What it requires is a complete closed-loop capability: from discovering problems to defining them, from building prototypes to evaluating quality, from driving adoption to accumulating assets.

Programmers: Your strength is rapid building; what you lack is business instinct and communication skills. Stop burying yourself in code—step out of the tech circle and listen to what business people are complaining about.

Product managers: Your strength is breaking down requirements and user thinking; what you lack is hands-on building ability. Learn to use AI tools to build prototypes yourself—you have an advantage over pure technical people, because from the start you know what to build and what not to build.

Operations / industry professionals: Your strength is understanding the business and people. AI tools can fill the gap—tools like Cursor, Dify, and Coze can be picked up in a week.

What matters most isn't your current title—it's whether you're willing to go to the customer's site and see the real problems.

Here's a concrete action plan for you:

Step 1: Pick an industry you know well and build an agent for a small business for free. It doesn't need to be complex—just solve one specific pain point. Step 2: Write the process up as an article or case study. This is the first brick of your personal IP. Step 3: Use this case to win your next paying client. One case study is worth more than ten pages of slides.

In my last post I said "technical skills are no longer valuable"—what I want to make clear in this one is: so what is valuable?

Translating AI's capabilities into language customers understand, and turning them into systems customers dare to use.

This is something AI itself cannot do. Because it doesn't understand the customer's industry, doesn't understand the people and relationships within the organization, doesn't know what can be said and what can't, and doesn't know how to take a system from "it runs" to "we dare to use it."

These are things only humans can do.

FDE is not Palantir's exclusive domain. You don't need a company with a $330 billion market cap to be an FDE.

You just need to start with the businesses around you. Find a business you understand, identify a real pain point, and use AI to solve it for them.

Then turn that process into your assets—case studies, experience, trust.

This is also the path I'm on. Going forward, I'll continue breaking down real-world FDE cases and concrete methodologies on "WilleAi笔记".
