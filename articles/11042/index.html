FDEs Are Blowing Up: Everyone Has a Different Idea of What an FDE Is

Original title: FDE爆火：每个人心中的FDE各不相同

53AI · 2026-10-30 · 10 min read

Summary

This article offers a sober take on the hype around Forward Deployed Engineers, arguing that companies hiring FDEs are paying for a delivery capability they previously lacked, not a rebranded, pricier engineer. The author stresses that building fast is not the same as solving business problems: a good FDE first judges whether a requested feature addresses the real issue. For vendors, each project should mature reusable tools and methods so implementation costs fall over time, otherwise the model remains expensive human outsourcing. AI that accumulates enterprise context can also leave lasting capability behind after the FDE departs. The piece closes with three questions executives should ask before investing.

Key points

  • A new title without new capabilities is just repackaging.
  • Great FDEs judge which problems are worth solving, not just build fast.
  • Vendors must turn each project into reusable tools and methods.
  • AI-accumulated enterprise context keeps capability after experts leave.
  • Companies should find real bottlenecks before building FDE teams.

Full text

"

When enterprises bring in an FDE, what they are buying is not a more expensive engineer, but a delivery capability they never had before.

Recently, the term FDE (Forward Deployed Engineer) has suddenly become a hot topic.

Some say it is the most valuable new profession of the AI era. Some say every software company of the future will need FDEs. Others put it even more bluntly:

The biggest chaos in the FDE space right now is that a group of people who used to do basic delivery, implementation, and on-site development have simply changed to a trendy job title and become highly paid FDEs.

That remark may be a bit harsh, but it raises a very practical question:

"If it is just a change of name, why should enterprises pay more?"

Last night, I attended an online discussion about bringing FDEs into enterprises. The participants included experts who have long worked in enterprise architecture, digital transformation leaders at large companies, and practitioners currently driving AI project delivery.

Everyone discussed the value of FDEs, organizational models, talent requirements, and problems encountered in actual delivery from different perspectives.

AI has already transformed software development dramatically, so why is it still so hard for enterprises to put AI into practice?

This may be the real reason why FDEs have suddenly attracted so much attention.

LEGACY PROBLEM

Over the past twenty years, enterprise digitalization has always had an old problem:

The business side does not understand technology, and the technology side does not understand business.

Business departments raise requirements, product managers organize them, development teams build the software, and implementation staff handle delivery.

The division of labor looks clear, but at each step, the requirements may drift a little further.

What the business really wants to solve is customer churn, but what gets delivered is a customer management system.

What the business really wants to improve is sales conversion rates, but what goes live is a daily sales reporting tool.

The system gets built and accepted, but the company's operations barely change.

Over the years, we have tried many solutions: business architects, product managers, business-IT integration teams, agile development, digital transformation offices...

Each approach has tried to shorten the distance between business and technology.

The emergence of the FDE is, in a sense, also an answer to the same question.

It emphasizes that engineers go to the business front line, not just listening to requirements and writing proposals, but directly understanding the business, getting hands-on to solve problems, and continuously improving based on real feedback.

This direction is extremely valuable.

But if we understand the FDE as merely a more advanced implementation engineer, we may be underestimating what is really changing in the AI era.

Because today's change is not just that engineers are closer to the business.

It is that AI enables one person to accomplish part of the work that previously required collaboration across multiple specialized roles.

In the past, people who understood the business could not necessarily code, and those who could code did not necessarily understand the business.

Now, a person who truly understands the business can use AI to complete prototype design, process building, code development, and some testing.

This means that work which enterprises previously relied on multiple roles and departments to complete now has the opportunity to be reorganized.

The FDE is just one manifestation of this change.

What truly deserves attention is that the way work gets done is changing.

JUDGEMENT

We often see stories like this:

An FDE arrives at a customer site and, in three days, completes a system prototype that would normally take months to build.

That is indeed exciting.

But I want to ask a question:

Is completing a system in three days the same thing as solving a business problem in three days?

Clearly not.

For example

The boss says they want to use AI to improve the sales team's performance.

An implementation person might immediately start building a sales knowledge base, a customer Q&A assistant, and a sales script generator.

An engineer skilled with AI coding tools might even build these features in a few days.

But the real problem may not be here at all.

Why exactly are sales results poor?

Is the lead quality too low? Are sales responses too slow? Do customer objections go unaddressed? Or does the sales manager simply not know which deals are slipping away?

Without clarifying these questions, how much value can even the fastest development speed produce?

Suppose analysis reveals that the company's real problem is that sales communication records are scattered, so managers cannot spot key deal risks in time.

Then what is more worth building may not be a sales Q&A assistant, but something that connects customer communications, deal identification, risk alerts, and next-step actions.

Both use AI, but the business value of the two solutions can be completely different.

The former adds another tool.

The latter has the chance to change how the sales team works.

This is the first capability I believe an excellent FDE must have:

"Not quickly implementing what the customer asks for, but judging whether what the customer asks for will actually solve the problem."

Today, AI is lowering the barrier to technical implementation.

But the barrier to problem judgment has not disappeared because of it.

It may even have become more important.

Because when development gets easier, the most common mistake enterprises make is to build a pile of things they don't actually need, faster.

NEW DIVISION

In last night's discussion, one point resonated with me deeply.

AI is increasingly concentrating capabilities that were once scattered across different specialized roles into a single person's workflow.

In the past, for an enterprise to complete business architecture, data design, and application development, multiple specialists had to participate separately.

Now, AI can already assist with a large portion of that work.

So a new question arises:

If AI can perform many specialized tasks at once, must the person using AI possess the ability to understand and judge that work?

I believe yes.

But this does not mean everyone in the future must become a super expert who understands business, architecture, programming, and data all at once.

That is almost unrealistic.

The more likely change is this:

In the past, an engineer relied on the professional skills they personally possessed to do their work.

In the future, a person, in addition to their own professional abilities, can draw on the many professional capabilities AI provides.

Their focus will gradually shift from personally completing every task to judging problems, organizing capabilities, reviewing results, and taking responsibility.

Two types of people, two paths

For example, a consultant with over a decade of experience in business operations consulting could diagnose operational problems and design processes, but had to rely on development teams to implement systems.

Now, with AI, they can complete some prototype development, data analysis, and business automation themselves.

Conversely, an excellent software engineer can also use AI to more quickly understand business materials, analyze processes, and form solutions.

Both have the potential to grow into excellent FDEs.

So I disagree with judging whether someone is qualified to be an FDE based on whether they were previously an implementation engineer or held a basic-level role.

"What really needs to be judged is: has AI given them capabilities they did not have before, and have those capabilities created new business value?"

If only the job title has changed while the capabilities remain the same, then it really is just packaging.

But if someone uses AI to genuinely cross their original professional boundaries and can independently solve problems that previously required a whole team, that is a real capability upgrade.

What bosses need is the latter.

LEVERAGE

During last night's meeting, I strongly agreed with a point raised by a teacher from Dell:

For a software company, every completed FDE project should make its tools, platforms, and methodologies more mature.

This point is very important.

Because it concerns whether the FDE business model can truly hold up.

Suppose an AI company has ten excellent FDEs.

Each of them can go deep into the customer site, understand the business, develop systems, and solve problems.

That sounds great.

But if every project requires re-understanding the customer, re-organizing knowledge, re-developing processes, and re-connecting systems, then the more projects the company takes on, the more people it may need.

In the end, it is still a software project company dependent on high-end labor.

Only now, instead of selling ordinary engineer man-days, it sells senior FDE man-days.

A truly valuable model should be different.

With the first customer, the FDE solved the problems of sales communication and opportunity identification.

In this process, they not only delivered an application, but also built up a knowledge structure for customer communication, methods for identifying opportunities, business rules, AI workflows, and evals standards for effectiveness.

By the second customer, these capabilities can be reused.

Of course, every company's customers, products, organization, and sales approach differ, so understanding and adaptation are still needed.

But you should not start from scratch every time.

Further on, as customers grow in number and the products and methods keep maturing, the implementation cost for new customers should gradually decline.

That is the business value I believe FDE should most pursue:

"An excellent FDE should not only solve one customer's problems, but also make similar problems easier and easier to solve."

The same logic applies to client companies.

If a company spends hundreds of thousands of yuan to bring in an FDE, and after the system goes live and the experts withdraw, the company still cannot maintain, improve, or use it, then what has this investment left behind?

A system? A few documents? Or a business capability that can keep running and improving?

I believe bosses should care most about the last question.

CONTEXT

When discussing FDEs, there is another thing that is easily overlooked.

Why does an excellent FDE need to spend so much time at the customer site?

A very important reason is that companies have too much important information that is never fully documented.

Why a certain customer matters—perhaps the sales director knows best.

Why a quote cannot go any lower—the boss has a set of judgment criteria in mind.

Why a certain customer must be handled by a particular salesperson may involve partnership relationships built over the past few years.

Why a certain project is worth doing despite low profit may be tied to the company's long-term strategy.

This information does not all live in the ERP, CRM, or enterprise knowledge base.

It exists in people's experience, relationships, judgment, and daily work.

To understand this information, an FDE usually needs extensive interviews, meetings, research, and on-site observation.

But in the AI era, we may be gaining another way.

What would happen if AI could continuously participate in a company's real work processes, be authorized to record important meetings, decisions, actions, and outcomes, and steadily build up its understanding of the company?

It would then have the opportunity to gradually develop an understanding of the company's business context.

This includes key internal and external relationships, the leadership's judgment principles, as well as historical decisions, important commitments, risk matters, and domain expertise.

None of this can be fully captured in a single interview.

It requires continuous accumulation, verification, and correction as business activities proceed.

Of course, what AI records is not necessarily all factual, and the judgments it forms may not be correct. Key business information still needs human confirmation, and anything involving permissions and privacy must be constrained.

But the direction is worth attention.

In the past, companies had to keep explaining to newly arrived engineers who they were, how they operated, and why they made certain decisions.

"In the future, could we let AI gradually build up an understanding of the company, and then help engineers solve problems faster?"

If this can be achieved, the way FDEs work could change dramatically.

They would no longer need to spend large amounts of time repeatedly learning about the company, and could devote more energy to problem assessment, process restructuring, and validating results.

More importantly, when the FDE leaves, the business knowledge and working capabilities the company has accumulated will not easily leave with them.

What the company truly retains will not just be a software system, but a set of business capabilities that can continuously accumulate and improve.

This is also a direction I have been paying increasing attention to in my recent research on enterprise AI implementation.

ACTION

I do not recommend that companies, just because FDEs have become popular, immediately hire FDEs or pay a high price for a set of FDE services.

Especially for growth-stage companies—many do not even have a complete IT department, let alone a mature data team.

Copying the FDE organizational model of large enterprises may actually increase management and delivery costs.

I would rather suggest that bosses first answer three questions.

Do not start with whether to build an FDE team. First look at where the real bottlenecks affecting results lie in your customer acquisition, sales conversion, customer service, delivery, or business management. Pick one problem, rather than launching ten AI projects at once.

If the goal is to improve sales conversion, you need to know what the current conversion rate is, at which stage the problem occurs, and what you intend to improve. If the goal is to improve delivery efficiency, you need to know how much time and manpower it used to take and how the quality performed. You do not need to perfect all your data governance at the start, but you must be able to judge whether improvement has genuinely happened.

After the project ends, has it left behind knowledge, processes, tools, and maintenance methods? The next time a similar problem arises, can the company solve part of it on its own? If you switch to another department or another customer scenario, is the past experience still applicable?

These three questions matter far more than giving the role a new name first.

Companies can solve problems through their own employees, external consultants, FDE teams, or standardized AI products.

Which approach is adopted does not really matter.

What matters is: who is truly accountable for the implementation process, who keeps using it, who validates the results, and in whose hands the capability ultimately resides.

THE END

I believe the further fusion of business and engineering that FDE represents is a direction worth taking seriously in the AI era.

But I also do not believe that it is inherently more valuable than past consultants, product managers, or implementation engineers just because it has a new English name.

For software companies, the real opportunity is not to repackage their existing engineers and sell them at a higher price, but to use AI and product platforms so that delivery capabilities can be continuously replicated.

For business owners, the real opportunity is likewise not to hire a few composite talents worth millions, but to let AI gradually enter the real business, giving the company capabilities it never had before.

For digital transformation leaders, it may also be necessary to rethink their own role:

Future value lies not just in how many systems were built or how many agents were deployed, but in whether you can organize business, technology, data, and AI together to continuously improve the way the company works.

I don't know whether the FDE will become a long-term hot role.

But I'm increasingly convinced of one thing:

"In the AI era, what's truly scarce isn't the people who can build the software, but the people who can judge what's worth building and help enterprises continuously capture value."

That's what really deserves serious thought from companies behind the FDE hype.

We no longer need to keep debating whether someone is or isn't an FDE.

Because what companies truly care about has never been the name of the role.

Rather, it's:

After spending this money, what new capability has the enterprise actually gained that it didn't have before?

This is also the question I most like to ask when judging whether an AI investment is worthwhile.
