Is FDE Tough? A Practical Guide to the 4 Factors of Difficulty, Rewards, and Who It Suits

Original title: FDEはきついのか？大変さの4要素とやりがい・向き不向きを実務解説

Summary

This article from FDE総研 explains why Forward Deployed Engineers (FDEs) are considered demanding, arguing that hardship and fulfillment are two sides of the same responsibility. It breaks the workload into four factors: ambiguity, customer interaction, travel, and breadth of technology. Why is FDE said to be "tough"?

FDE is considered tough because you define problems with no ready-made answers alongside customers, and shoulder everything from implementation to adoption on your own.

FDE (Forward Deployed Engineer) is a role established by Palantir and also recruited for by companies like Anthropic. You embed yourself at the customer's site and take on everything from deciding what problem to solve, through implementation, to making sure the solution is actually used in the field. The broader the scope, the broader the responsibility, and that weight of responsibility is exactly what fuels the "tough" reputation. Put another way, the real source of the difficulty is not the sheer volume of work, but the breadth of judgment entrusted to you. Getting this wrong leads to a misreading of what the FDE role really is. For an overview of the role, also see What is FDE? The Definition of a New Role Born at Palantir.

Why are "difficulty" and "reward" two sides of the same coin?

The toughness and the reward of FDE are two sides of the same responsibility, and whether you're suited to it depends on whether you experience that load as autonomy.

It is not an easy job. However, that weight of responsibility translates directly into a tangible sense of achievement. FDEs carry concentrated load precisely because they are the ones who first carve out problems with no defined answers, and whether you're suited to the role depends on whether you experience that load as the appeal of autonomy. The same role—"defining problems with customers and owning them from implementation to adoption"—appears as pressure to one person and as fascination to another. Toughness and reward are two sides of the same coin.

That's why, when evaluating FDE as a career, you must look at the load and the reward together, not separately. Ruling it out based only on the "it's tough" reputation means seeing only one side of the same fact—the size of the autonomy. Conversely, entering the role based only on "big autonomy sounds fun" means burning out when you're forced to make judgments amid ambiguity.

What can you decide once you understand what the load consists of?

Breaking the load down into ambiguity, customer interaction, travel, and technical breadth lets you calmly judge whether you can handle it.

If you judge based on a vague sense that it "seems tough," you'll misread the reality. The load of an FDE can be broken down into concrete elements: ambiguity, customer interaction, travel, and technical breadth. Knowing what each involves lets you determine whether it's a load you can't bear, or one you might actually enjoy.

For example, some people struggle with ambiguity but excel at customer interaction, and others are the opposite. Not all four are equally tough, and which ones hit you hardest varies from person to person—that's the fork in the road for your decision. Rather than avoiding the role out of vague anxiety, checking your compatibility element by element leads to a choice you won't regret. The breakdown is laid out below.

Where does the "tough" reputation come from?

The source of the "tough" reputation lies in the role definitions that Palantir and Anthropic themselves publish.

The toughness of FDE isn't just someone's subjective impression. Anthropic's job posting explicitly states that FDEs need "high agency to navigate ambiguity," and that visiting customer sites accounts for 25-50% of the role (Anthropic official job posting). Palantir describes FDEs as owning high-difficulty projects end-to-end in small teams, a role close to that of a startup CTO (The Pragmatic Engineer).

In other words, the high load is something companies disclose upfront as a requirement. If you read the conditions in the job posting as a "preview of the load," you can significantly reduce the gap after joining. If you want a sense of the actual daily load, What Does a Day as an FDE Look Like? is also helpful.

What exactly is hard about being an FDE?

The sources of difficulty can be organized into four: ambiguity, customer interaction, travel, and technical breadth. The table below shows what each looks like concretely.

AmbiguityDefining problems yourself when specifications aren't settledEarly project stages and when assumptions shift
Customer interactionOn-site negotiations, expectation management, accountabilityRequirement agreements, progress reports, and when trouble occurs
Travel and fieldworkThe weight of travel, such as 25-50% customer site visits (Anthropic job posting)On-site interviews and adoption support
Technical breadthWorking hands-on broadly, from implementation to LLM applicationsDesign decisions and technology selection throughout the project

Why is "ambiguity" the biggest factor?

The biggest factor is dealing with "ambiguity"—redefining the problem yourself from a state where specifications aren't settled.

The biggest reason FDE feels tough is having to define the problem yourself when specifications aren't settled. Anthropic's job posting also explicitly states that FDEs need "high agency to navigate ambiguity" (Anthropic official job posting). You aren't given what to build; you start by reframing the question itself at the customer's site, so a wait-for-instructions mindset simply doesn't work.

Unlike development where requirements documents come down from upstream, FDEs start from "what actually needs to be solved to help the customer?" Assumptions often shift midway, and you must make the call yourself to redo designs you've already settled. Moreover, since no correct answer is provided for those judgments, responsibility for the decisions themselves comes back to you. This uncertainty is the core of what makes people call FDE "tough."

How much of the role do customer interaction and travel take up?

Anthropic's job posting puts customer site visits at 25-50%, meaning the work doesn't stay confined to your desk.

Customer interaction and travel also push up the load of FDE. On-site negotiations, expectation management, and accountability all converge on the FDE, and the job can't be completed at a desk alone. Being technically correct isn't enough; you need to put into words what the customer expects and where they feel anxious, and align on it.

Anthropic's job posting states that customer site visits account for 25-50% of the role, showing how much weight travel carries (official Anthropic job posting). That much less time is available for settling in and focusing on code alone. Conversely, this means that securing blocks of implementation time is itself a challenge that FDEs must design for themselves.

How broad are the technical skills required?

FDEs handle everything from implementation to LLM usage on their own, so the breadth of technology itself becomes a burden.

In addition to ambiguity and customer interactions, the wide range of technologies involved is another burden for FDEs. From implementation to LLM usage, one person covers a large territory, which makes for a different working style than specializing deeply in a single area. Since you own projects end-to-end, you can't simply hand off intermediate steps to someone else and be done.

These four factors—ambiguity, customer interactions, travel, and technical breadth—overlapping simultaneously is the true nature of what makes the FDE role demanding. While any one of them alone might be bearable, the difficulty lies in all four occurring at the same time within a single project. What makes the FDE role rewarding?

The rewards lie in two things: the great autonomy of owning projects end-to-end, and how visible the results are. Behind the burden lies a sense of fulfillment that's hard to find in developing generic features.

What does the autonomy of end-to-end ownership mean?

By Palantir's definition, the FDE is a role that owns high-difficulty projects end-to-end within a small team.

The core of the FDE's reward is the autonomy to own a project entirely yourself. By Palantir's definition, the FDE is a role that owns high-difficulty projects end-to-end within a small team, similar to a startup CTO (The Pragmatic Engineer). Because you handle everything consistently from problem definition through implementation to adoption, your scope for decision-making is large, and your own judgment directly shapes the results.

In large organizations with division of labor, one person's involvement tends to be limited to a portion of the process. FDEs are the opposite: precisely because the team is small, you can decide for yourself what to build, how to deliver it, and how to make it stick. The situation of "nothing moves forward unless I decide" is a burden, but at the same time it's also the freedom to proceed exactly as you've decided.

Where do the results become visible?

You can see what you designed and implemented being used at the customer's site, and feedback comes back quickly.

The other reward is that results are immediately visible. Something you designed and implemented is actually used at the customer's site—this sense of fulfillment is hard to come by when developing generic features (Palantir's official blog). Unlike development where you stack up features without ever seeing who uses them or how, as an FDE you can witness, on the spot, the moment your customer is helped.

Precisely because travel and customer interactions keep you close to the field, feedback comes back quickly. If you're heading in the wrong direction, you notice early; if you're on target, you feel the payoff right away. The very thing that was a source of burden—"going to the field"—is what underpins the speed of learning.

How do the burden and rewards balance out?

The ambiguity that was a source of hardship is, viewed from the other side, the freedom to choose your own direction—the two are two sides of the same coin.

The four burdens—ambiguity, customer interactions, travel, and technical breadth—map directly onto autonomy, trust, field awareness, and breadth of coverage. Because things are ambiguous, you can choose for yourself what to solve; because there are many customer interactions, the other party's reactions reach you directly; because you go to the field, you can see the moment your work is used; and because your technical range is broad, you can assemble the whole with your own judgment.

Therefore, the option of "reducing only the burden while keeping only the rewards" doesn't exist. When considering the FDE role, the question to ask is not the total amount of burden, but whether you genuinely want to take on what that burden consists of.

What kind of person is suited to being an FDE?

FDEs suit people who want to carve out problems together with customers and carry the work through to results with their own hands. Conversely, those who want to concentrate on fixed specifications will find it a heavy burden.

What kind of person is suited?

The best fit is someone who can frame their own questions amid ambiguity and, working alongside the customer, see things through to adoption.

The people suited to FDE work are those who can frame their own questions when faced with ambiguity and carry projects through to results while working alongside the customer. If you find situations without predetermined answers interesting rather than daunting, and can take on not just implementation but also negotiations and adoption, the large autonomy translates directly into satisfaction.

Beyond technical skill, it's also important to enjoy the communication involved in articulating and adjusting customer expectations. The role suits people who can embrace a high-agency environment as an opportunity for growth rather than a burden. If you want to examine your fit in more detail, see Who is suited to being an FDE?.

What kind of person should avoid it?

For those who want to settle in and concentrate on fixed specifications, the ambiguity and heavy customer interactions tend to be a burden.

Conversely, for people who want to focus on development within fixed specifications, the FDE's ambiguity and heavy customer interactions tend to be a burden. If situations where design assumptions shift frequently, or field work involving 25-50% customer site visits, would stress you out, there's no need to force the choice.

This is not a matter of ability but of compatibility. People who excel at deeply focused development will produce better results by choosing environments where they can leverage that strength, while those who enjoy ambiguity choosing the FDE path lets both sides perform at their best.

How can you assess your own fit?

Fit is a matter of compatibility, not superiority—you should judge based on which kind of burden you're willing to take on.

The assessment process is simple. First, for each of the four criteria in the table above, check one by one which side you lean toward. Next, if your leanings skew toward the "better to avoid" side, treat that as a mismatch with the environment rather than a lack of ability. Finally, weigh the burden you can accept against the rewards you can gain.

What matters is not separating the two aspects—difficulty and reward—but determining which kind of load you can take on. If you don't stop your judgment at the single word "tough" and instead break it down into the four factors, you can reach a conclusion you're satisfied with, whether or not you choose to become an FDE.
