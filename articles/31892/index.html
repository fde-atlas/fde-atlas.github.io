Portfolio: When to Add Headcount, and When to Turn Down the Next Client

Original title: 项目组合：何时增员，何时拒绝下一个客户

pafa.github.io · 2025-09-01 · 7 min read

Summary

This chapter argues that FDE teams must manage commitments as a portfolio: signed contracts, sales opportunities, product investment, and operational support all compete for the same people and cannot share the same guaranteed capacity. Using a repair-service teaching scenario, the author shows why a paying customer may still be deferred when prerequisites like local ownership and capacity are unconfirmed. Loss-making projects should be judged on concrete evidence of learning—such as when a capability enters a maintained product—not vague strategic value. Before hiring, teams should distinguish whether capacity shortages stem from growing valid work, excessive manual maintenance, or blocked collaboration. Rejections and stop decisions should leave explicit conditions for reconsideration and proper handover.

Key points

  • Signed contracts, sales opportunities, and product work must be estimated separately, not charged to the same capacity.
  • Shared dependencies across projects should drive planned improvements and drills, not just reported reuse savings.
  • Continued funding for loss-making projects requires specific conditions: maintained product entry, a named next user, observable cost decline.
  • Before hiring, distinguish three causes of shortage: growing valid work, manual toil, or blocked collaboration.
  • Rejections should state what cannot be taken on and offer smaller scope, later dates, or alternatives when possible.

Every project claims to be valuable, yet together they may fail to deliver. Several customers request go-lives at the same time, and a single product engineer owns all the critical interfaces—and is the only one who can recover when failures occur. Each contract is profitable on its own, but the team's shared calendar can no longer honor them.

Portfolio management means choosing among existing commitments, new opportunities, product investment, and operational work as a whole. Customers face the same problem: multiple departments want to do AI, competing for the same business experts, data engineers, and security staff. A single project passing approval does not mean all projects can start at once.

See all commitments first, then new opportunities

The UK government project delivery standard brings existing project performance, dependencies, funding, and capability constraints into portfolio selection; when resources are insufficient, plans should be adjusted. 1 The inspiration for FDEs from this management principle is not to let each field commitment stand on its own while failing in aggregate. It does not prescribe a uniform intake ratio for FDEs.

A supplier can first divide work into committed delivery and support, approved exploration and product investment, and opportunities still under discussion. All three need estimation, but they cannot draw on the same kind of "committed capacity": a sales opportunity is not a signed contract, and a signed contract does not prove the customer's prerequisites are in place.

Incident response for existing services, vacation coverage, and handovers must come into view first. Otherwise new customers fill the schedule, and any issue with an old customer can only be handled by interrupting new projects. What looks like an occasional delay actually comes from the same time being sold twice.

Cross-project dependencies also need checking. Several projects sharing one interface does not necessarily mean fixing it once; they may use different versions with different go-live windows. Several customers depending on the same model service may also encounter changes at the same time. Shared dependencies should help schedule improvements and drills, not merely be recorded as reuse gains in reports.

Why a revenue-generating new customer may still be deferred

The following continues the book's repair-service teaching scenario, without introducing real customers or industry intake metrics. The existing team is completing a manual-confirmation pilot in one region. Three new requests then arrive: a second region wants to replicate quickly, a public product needs a capability added, and another customer is willing to pay for custom format work.

RequestWhat is already knownWhat is currently missingDecisions that can be made this round
Second-region replicationExperience from the first region existsThe local lead's time, difference verification, and added capacity are not yet securedThe supervisor approves limited observation only after agreeing on staffing and observing conditions are met; no commitment to simultaneous go-live
Public product improvementThe product owner has received the issueRequires maintainers, implementation resources, and confirmation from the next userSchedule protected product investment once conditions are met
New customer's custom formatThe other party is willing to discuss paymentScope, maintenance service, and available staff not yet confirmedOffer a feasible start date or a partner arrangement; cannot sign for immediate delivery

These choices do not permanently rank product improvement above revenue. If the public feature has only a vague vision and no one to maintain it, while the custom service has a clear scope, a reasonable price, and available deliverers, the latter can take priority. Conversely, a high-priced request that would crowd out existing recovery capacity cannot be accepted just because the revenue is large.

How Teams Are Staffed, Hired, and Evaluated already calculated the second region's conflicts using a one-week schedule. This chapter does not add those hours to a new ledger, but changes the object of comparison: do new demand, fulfilling current contracts, fixing shared capabilities, and selling custom services actually compete for the same people? Which can be deferred, and which requires others' agreement first?

A new opportunity with only commercial intent can start later, have a smaller first phase, or be declined. Signed commitments must be handled according to the actual agreement and cannot be unilaterally canceled after internal re-prioritization. Portfolio decisions first constrain what you commit to afterward; they are not a reason to escape existing obligations.

What kind of learning deserves more budget

Once the numbers are clear, the discussion can actually become more open. A pioneering project may receive strategic budget, while a highly repetitive mature project should be required to deliver steadily. Stuffing both into the same average gross margin means managers see neither the value of exploration nor the cost of repetitive work.

Suppose both projects lose money. One uncovered a limitation many customers share, the product team has taken it over, and future deployments can validate the improvement; the other keeps adding custom branches that cannot run once the customer leaves the original team. Their current-period reports may look similar, but their future paths differ.

But the former also needs evidence; a moving "strategic value" narrative cannot justify indefinite extension. Conditions for continued investment should be concrete: which capability enters a maintained product by when, who is the next clearly identified user, and how the decline in effort will be observed. If these conditions repeatedly fail, the team needs to re-estimate rather than repeat the vision in every retrospective.

Product budget should protect explicitly approved shared work, not serve as a temporary shelter for project overspending. If an interface fix is required to fulfill the current customer's contract, it must first be honestly recorded in the corresponding cost; only separately approved general development counts as product investment. Project Economics: Getting Both Sides' Numbers Straight keeps worked examples on how to separate hours and avoid double counting.

For customers, shared capabilities also need receivers. When every department is willing to showcase pilots but no one is willing to maintain the underlying data, the organization cannot build a platform by counting projects. Whether a shared service forms depends on follow-up budget, maintenance responsibility, and whether users actually adopt it; it cannot be achieved by merely putting several departments' needs on the same demo page.

When to add headcount, and when to change the work first

When a team is persistently short on capacity, distinguish three causes first. First, confirmed, valid work keeps increasing. Second, the organization leaves a large amount of repetitive manual maintenance in the field instead of moving it into product improvement or a formal operational service. Third, the work could be done but stalls for long periods waiting on permissions, materials, and decisions.

The first may support adding headcount; the second requires comparing fixes, automation, and service configuration; the third calls for fixing collaboration conditions first. They can also appear together. If not separated, new engineers may just add more people waiting, or make more customers depend on the same fragile practice.

When deciding to hire, you should at least be able to articulate the ongoing work the new hire will carry, the mentoring time existing members must invest, which budget covers salary and associated costs, and how to allocate the person if the new business is slow to arrive. Unvalidated opportunities can support limited exploration, but should not be written up as guaranteed revenue; nor should a company mistake late cash collection for a staffing shortage.

Temporarily moving people from one project to another requires the same specificity. Who is moved, which existing work pauses, who takes over their operational duties, and do the affected customers or business owners accept the impact? Without these answers, "flexible staffing" likely just hides this week's delay in another plan.

Using external partners is likewise not automatic added capacity. Whether the partner can deliver to the required standard, understands permissions and failure recovery, and whether the customer accepts the arrangement all need confirming. The core team still must spend time on interfaces and acceptance. For work with clear boundaries, partners can be an effective choice; for problems the team does not yet understand itself, handing the task off does not necessarily reduce uncertainty.

Declining and stopping should also leave a next step

Stopping a project does not require exhausting every possibility. When core data cannot be legally or reliably obtained, the customer cannot cooperate, results cannot be accepted, or the cost of solving far exceeds the obtainable value, narrowing scope or exiting may be the responsible decision. The service continuity and documentation handover required for exit must still be done properly.

Here, distinguish declining a new request, pausing an action, narrowing an agreed scope, and ending an entire project. The first two do not necessarily change all existing work; the latter two usually require both parties to arrange remaining obligations. Unfinished items do not disappear because of an internal decision to stop; specific exit conditions are examined in Boundaries, Risks, and Exit.

For deferred requests, conditions for reconsideration can be left, such as the local lead securing time, key data becoming available, or the product interface entering a maintained version. The basis for revisiting is that conditions have changed, not that the customer followed up again. For declined requests, state which parts cannot currently be taken on, and when confident, offer a smaller scope, a later date, or an alternative path.

A combined review does not have to become a long meeting in a fixed format. A major deviation in an existing project, the departure of key personnel, a change in cash plans, or the completion of improvements to a public product are all reasons to re-rank. The person in charge needs to see the actual choices made this time: which work continues, what is added, what is postponed, and who will explain things to those affected. Without these decisions, a mere ranking by scores still leaves the team unsure of what to do tomorrow.

From this perspective, the business capability of an FDE is not just about selling complex projects at high prices. It also includes distinguishing learning investment from recurring costs, distinguishing customer value from vendor revenue, and ensuring that every expansion of commitment is backed by corresponding evidence.

The next time someone presents a successful project, you can first look at the results, then follow up on those results by asking: what did the customer actually change, where did the investment come from, who will bear the follow-up, and how much can the next customer reuse. Only when you have asked this far does what you are looking at begin to be a business.
