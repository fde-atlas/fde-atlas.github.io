How FDEs Should Answer Customers Asking "When Will This Feature Ship?"

Original title: FDE 如何回答客户“功能什么时候上线”

FDE Times · 2026-11-17 · 6 min read

Summary

This guide explains how forward deployed engineers should answer the inevitable customer question of when a feature will ship without creating unintended commitments. The core rule: only promise delivery for items locked and confirmed by the product team, since dates are the product team's to commit. Before answering, FDEs should probe the real need behind a feature request, which often differs from the request itself. All requirements should be routed to the owning product team via tickets that capture the actual use case, not bypassed. Temporary custom work must have explicit scope and maintenance ownership, or customers will assume permanent support. The article includes suggested phrasing by roadmap status and advice on using these stories in FDE interviews and resumes.

Key points

  • Only items locked and confirmed by the product team can be promised; dates belong to product.
  • Ask what the customer actually needs before checking the roadmap.
  • Match your answer to the item's real roadmap status; avoid vague reassurances like "soon".
  • File tickets describing the true need and route them to product rather than bypassing it.
  • Scope temporary scripts clearly so customers don't assume permanent support.

The safe answer depends on the item's actual status on the roadmap, and only the product team has the authority to commit to dates.

Key Points at a Glance

  • Only say "it will ship" when the item is locked in and the product team has confirmed it; in all other cases, never say "soon"
  • Before answering, ask the customer what they need the feature for—the real need often differs from the request itself
  • Route all requests to the product team that owns the area; for custom work done for a customer, clearly flag that it won't be maintained indefinitely

A single sentence like "we expect it in Q2," even said offhandedly, becomes a customer expectation. Rework learned this lesson while writing a guide for customer success teams on how to discuss the roadmap without overcommitting.

This guide is written for customer success (CS) teams, but it applies equally well to Forward Deployed Engineers (FDEs). When you're sitting next to a customer fixing their data pipeline and a request comes up during debugging, the customer will naturally turn to the technical person beside them. An offhand answer at that moment may stick with the customer longer than any slide deck.

Committing too easily has real costs. Umbrex's FDE Playbook flags a specific business risk: customers will assume that custom work done for them will be supported forever.

Meanwhile, many vendors state in writing that they make no commitments at all. Optimizely's roadmap disclaimer says the roadmap is not a commitment to deliver any code or features, and items on it may not ship as planned or may not ship at all; DocFusion has nearly identical wording.

So an offhand "it'll be there next quarter" on-site creates exactly the kind of expectation these documents work hard to avoid.

The item's status determines what you can say

ProductPlan points out that sharing a roadmap does not constitute a commitment—implied or explicit—to deliver specific features on specific dates. A roadmap shows customers the company's strategy and direction; it does not guarantee delivery dates.

The safest approach is to answer according to the item's actual status, no matter the mood in the room. The table below lists reference phrasing for each status, along with wording to avoid.

StatusWhat you can sayWhat to avoid
Locked in, confirmed by the product team"This one is confirmed for Q2—I can tell you it will ship."Adding a specific date the product team hasn't set
Planned, not locked in"This is on the roadmap for the second half of the year. I won't give you a date I can't personally stand behind, but it is a prioritized item.""Soon" or "almost done"
Under consideration"The product team is evaluating this request. It represents our current direction and could still change, so there's no milestone yet.""Soon" or "probably next release"
Not on the roadmap"This isn't on the current roadmap. I can walk you through what we're focused on right now.""Let me fight for it" or "it shouldn't take long"

The second row is the hardest to say, because the customer wants a number. Aha! recommends sharing only timelines the company is willing to commit to externally, to avoid creating a false sense of certainty. Mind the Product offers an alternative: use "Now/Next/Later" labels to show priority order without tying anything to a date.

Giving a broad time frame while stating clearly that you won't give a date is better than giving a specific date and later having to walk it back.

Before answering, ask what the customer actually needs

The table above is only useful once you understand what the customer really wants. Feature requests are often solutions customers have invented for a different problem. So the FDE's first step is to uncover the underlying need.

Consider a hypothetical example. A customer's operations lead asks: "When will the system have a button to export to Excel?" Instead of rushing to check the roadmap, you ask back: "What do you do with the export?" He explains that every Monday he compiles the previous week's data and pastes it into an email for his director.

You follow up: "Which numbers does the director look at? Might she need them midweek too?" It turns out there are only three metrics, and the director wants them regularly. The real need is a recurring report delivered on time to the right person; the export button is just the implementation he happened to think of.

Only now do you check the status. Suppose the export feature isn't on the roadmap—you use the phrasing from the last row of the table, then pivot to what the customer actually needs: "Export isn't in the current plans. However, if what you need is for these three metrics to land in the director's inbox every Monday, we can look at whether that's achievable within the project scope."

If you write a temporary script to send the reports, be clear from the start about which engagement it belongs to and who maintains it after handoff. Umbrex writes that custom development with no owner should not be treated as supported.

The "supported forever" misunderstanding usually starts exactly with these kinds of temporary solutions: built with care, but never explicitly flagged as time-limited.

Umbrex also draws a memorable line: without review, an FDE should not create long-term obligations to a customer. Verbal commitments like "we'll maintain this script for you long-term" require approval from someone with the authority to make that commitment.

Route requests to the product team—don't go around it

When the meeting ends, your job is only half done. AgileLab's FDE handbook puts it bluntly: FDEs are not a shortcut around the product roadmap. PostHog's handbook describes a similar practice: when a customer is stuck on a problem only the product team can solve, the FDE hands the work to the team that owns the area.

So the ticket you file should state the real need, not a verbatim copy of what the customer said. In the example above, a good ticket would spell out: who needs it, what they currently do manually, how often, which three metrics, and the current workaround.

A PM reading such a ticket can compare it against requests from other customers; "Customer X wants an Excel export" can't do that.

Don't expect a single ticket to change priorities. Your value lies in accumulating evidence: only when several different customers describe the same underlying need does your ticket start to carry weight.

Common mistakes that turn a sentence into a commitment

A common mistake: the item isn't locked in yet, but to keep the meeting pleasant, you say "we're already working on it." This is really a variant of "soon"—and as Rework notes, the customer will remember it as an agreed milestone.

Guessing at dates you overheard at standup and presenting them as if the product team had set them likewise turns internal rumor into a commitment to the customer.

A subtler mistake is using executive pressure to force the product team to take on your customer's request. You might win once, but you'll lose the trust of the people who will have to maintain that feature later.

The final mistake is building a temporary solution without saying how long it will last, so the customer treats it as part of the product. Insight Partners notes that at its best, FDE work compounds in value over time; at its worst, it creates technical debt.

Bringing this skill into interviews and your resume

If you're preparing for an FDE interview, have a story ready about a time you had to decline or narrow a customer request.

Tell it in four beats: the original request, the question you used to uncover the real need, the answer you gave based on the roadmap status, and how you routed it to the product team. A story like this shows you can hold boundaries while keeping the customer satisfied.

On your resume, don't just write "worked directly with customers." Be specific: describe how you turned raw requests into tickets that captured the real need, or how you scoped a temporary solution before handoff.

When reading job postings, check whether the description mentions bringing customer feedback back to the product team; if it does, lead with this story.

The next time someone asks "When will this ship?", ask the customer a question before answering. That question can often reveal a path to meeting the customer's needs without making any commitments.
