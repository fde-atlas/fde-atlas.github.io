What is FDE? The role of engineers who build things that work in the field alongside customers.

The term "Forward Deployed Engineer," abbreviated as FDE, is appearing more and more often on recruiting sites and in overseas tech news. OpenAI and Anthropic alike are recruiting engineers under this job title, and it is sometimes introduced as a way of working pioneered by the company Palantir. A search in Japanese already turns up several explainer articles written in the context of job changes and careers. On the other hand, there are still few articles that, from the standpoint of executives or marketing leaders deciding whether to implement AI in their own company, organize what this term means and why it is spreading now. In this article, after verifying the definition and origins of the FDE from primary sources, we explain what Japanese companies should take away from this trend from the perspective of those who run businesses.

What Is an FDE?

A Forward Deployed Engineer (known by the initials FDE; in Japanese the job title is often translated as "frontline deployed engineer") is an engineer who, rather than building their own company's products, goes directly into customer companies' operations and assembles software that solves problems specific to that customer. Palantir Technologies (a US data analytics company), which first systematized this role, describes its own FDEs (formally, Forward Deployed Software Engineers) on its official blog as follows: whereas a conventional software engineer "builds a single feature for many customers," an FDE "provides many features for a single customer"—a way of working with the focus reversed (see: Palantir, "A Day in the Life of a Palantir Forward Deployed Software Engineer" (2020)).

The same article also touches on the difference between FDEs and consultants. Whereas a consultant builds proposals and implementations from scratch for each customer, an FDE is in a position where "most of the existing product suite can be used as-is in combination" (see: same source). In other words, an FDE can be described as a technical professional who uses general-purpose products as a foundation while handling implementations tailored to customer-specific requirements on top of them. In addition to software development skill, this role requires the ability to understand the customer's business and to work hands-on while making decisions in the field.

This point—that both technical ability and interpersonal skills are required—is consistent with analyses in other media as well. LeadDev points out that, assuming technical competence, interpersonal skills and domain knowledge are the biggest differentiators (see: LeadDev, "The rise of the forward-deployed engineer (FDE)" (2026)). Given the nature of the role—breaking down problems on the same level as the customer's staff and driving implementation while managing priorities—writing code alone is not enough.

Why OpenAI and Anthropic Are Adopting It

The FDE way of working is no longer Palantir's alone. Companies providing generative AI have begun recruiting engineers under this job title.

OpenAI is publicly recruiting Forward Deployed Engineers in multiple locations. On the Tokyo job posting, the role is described as one that "leads production deployments of frontier models end to end with our most strategic customers," stating that the FDE handles requirements definition, technical scoping, system design, building, and production operation, working directly alongside the customer's engineering team (see: OpenAI, "Forward Deployed Engineer - Tokyo" (as of August 2026)).

Anthropic likewise recruits FDEs as members of its Applied AI team. On its official recruiting page, the FDE is described as a role that goes directly into strategic customer companies and builds generative AI applications that solve real business problems together with the customer's team (see: Anthropic, "Forward Deployed Engineer, Applied AI" job listing (as of August 2026)).

This trend is also showing up in hiring market statistics. Tech media outlet TechCrunch, based on a survey conducted in the first half of 2026 by executive search firm Christian & Timbers, reports that the share of companies planning to hire FDEs jumped from 5–10% at the start of 2026 to 70% by the end of the first half (see: TechCrunch, "Forward-deployed engineers are the AI industry's latest talent obsession" (2026)). LeadDev, a media outlet for engineering leaders, also independently cites data showing that FDE job postings increased 729% year over year as of April 2026, and multiple reports from different sources corroborate the same trend: demand for the FDE role is expanding rapidly over a short period (see: LeadDev, "The rise of the forward-deployed engineer (FDE)" (2026)).

How Does It Differ from SIers, Contract Development, and On-Site SES?

When this term is introduced in Japan, a common reaction is, "Isn't that the same as an SIer or SES?" It is true that there are similarities. The structure of entering the customer's site and engaging with the customer's problems is shared.

The difference lies in how deliverables are handed over and how deeply one engages in the field. Conventional SIers and contract development typically treat requirements definition through design, development, and delivery as a single contract unit, with delivery marking the end of the engagement. An FDE, by contrast, does not stop at delivery; the premise is continuously updating working software, rebuilding implementations each time field priorities change. The Palantir description's point that "existing product components can be used as-is in combination" also indicates a position distinct from contract development that builds from scratch.

Another commonly confused concept is resident-type SES (System Engineering Service). SES refers to a contract form concerning employment agreements and work locations, whereas FDE is the name of a way of working and a role, and does not necessarily involve physical on-site presence. What separates the two is not the place of work, but a difference in the quality of engagement: how far one is involved as a stakeholder in the customer's business outcomes.

For example, even in the same situation of "working at the customer's site," there is a difference in how responsibility for outcomes is held between an approach of quietly completing assigned tasks and an approach of building priorities together with the customer and rebuilding working deliverables based on one's own judgment. What the term FDE refers to is the latter—that is, a position involved in the field not as an executor of tasks but as a maker of deliverables. In this respect, it draws a clear line from the former image, which the word "resident" (jōchū) tends to evoke in Japan.

What This Means for Japanese Companies

Why is discussion of this role meaningful for Japanese companies now? Behind it lies a common wall that many companies introducing generative AI are facing.

Multiple survey results show that while many companies have started using AI in their operations, only a small fraction are actually seeing results that translate into profit. A McKinsey survey shows a similar trend: while the share of companies regularly using AI has reached 88%, only about 40% can report an impact on profit, and only about 6% are achieving significant results (see: McKinsey "The state of AI" (2025)). What is pointed out here is not the performance of the tools, but the fact that the process of rebuilding the business itself is missing.

Behind the spread of the FDE way of working across companies lies precisely this wall. Simply distributing generic AI tools does not fit into frontline operations. Only when someone enters the customer's site, engages with actual business data, and iterates the cycle of building working deliverables and refining them do the tools turn into results. The reason OpenAI and Anthropic maintain FDE teams in-house can also be read as stemming from the same recognition of the challenge: simply handing over model performance to customers does not translate into production-level results.

What this means for Japanese companies is that they can bring back the very idea of a "specialist role responsible for implementation." Whether hiring in-house or receiving external support, advancing AI adoption requires designing the people who will enter the field and keep building working deliverables—before selecting tools.

Another point Japanese companies cannot afford to overlook is the fact that this job title itself has not yet fully penetrated Japanese business settings. As mentioned above, the rapid expansion in the hiring market is a movement originating in the US, and Japanese-language explanations are mostly in the context of job changes and careers. Put the other way around, the sooner a company adopts this mindset in its business operations, the better positioned it is to be the first to establish a continuous implementation system for AI adoption—one that does not end with "build it and be done." The point is not to chase the job title itself, but to consider how to bring into one's own company the mindset of "continuously building in the field" that this role embodies.

Summary

FDE is a way of working for engineers, systematized by Palantir and increasingly adopted by generative AI companies such as OpenAI and Anthropic, in which they embed themselves in the customer's site and keep building working deliverables. It can be distinguished from SIers, contract development, and resident-type SES by how deliverables are handed over and how deeply they engage with the field. What matters for Japanese companies is not memorizing this job title, but bringing back the idea that turning AI adoption into results requires designing the people who will "continuously take charge of implementation in the field."
