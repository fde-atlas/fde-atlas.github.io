FDE application rejected with no interview? What to change

FDE Interview Prep · 2026-09-25 · 12 min read

Summary

This article offers a four-part self-check for FDE candidates rejected before any interview: verify the posting's hard gates (years of experience, work authorization, clearance, sponsorship), confirm form answers don't contradict the posting or resume, ensure the top third of the resume shows the evidence the posting asks for, and check whether the wrong title or level was targeted. Since employers like Anthropic state they give no feedback, the author advises against guessing at keyword filters and recommends reapplying only after a visible change, such as new shipped work or a rebuilt resume header.

Key points

  • Employers rarely explain rejections; Anthropic's FAQ states no feedback is given.
  • Check four things: posting gates, form consistency, resume top third, title and level.
  • If a hard gate excludes you, apply to a different posting rather than rewriting the resume.
  • The resume header should show production work and a customer who depended on it.
  • Reapply only with visible new evidence; Anthropic allows reapplication after 12 months.

You read the posting twice. You have the stack, you have shipped to real users, and the job sounds like the one you already do on your best weeks. A few days later an automated email thanks you for your interest, and no human has spoken to you. (For the resume itself, line by line, see the FDE resume guide.)

The short answer: in our method, a rejection before any interview gets checked against four things you can see yourself. Is there a gate in the posting you don’t meet? Does a form answer contradict the posting or your resume? Does the top third of your resume fail to show what the posting asks for? Or did you apply at the wrong title or level? Don’t expect to be told which one it was, so check all four, fix what you find, and apply again only once something a reader can see has changed.

Why you won’t get a reason

Some employers say plainly that they will not explain. Anthropic’s careers FAQ says it is not able to provide feedback on resumes or interviews. Source 1Careers \ AnthropicPublisherAnthropicSource typecompany hiring page

So plan as if no reason is coming. You can still send a short, polite note to a recruiter you have already spoken to, but don’t wait on it, and never send the next application on the strength of a guess.

Don’t read the rejection email for clues, either. One Blind poster wrote, in February 2026, that the Greenhouse rejection they received after Anthropic’s FDE technical round was the same template they had received for roles where they never entered the interview loop. Read these two ways.

When a gate is against you, change the posting, not the resume. If you need sponsorship and the posting says it cannot sponsor, the application is spent before anyone reads it. If you are a new graduate, Databricks’ AI FDE posting says in its own words that it is not for you. Source 6AI Engineer – Forward Deployed Engineering (AI FDE)PublisherDatabricks (careers site / Greenhouse)Source typecompany job posting Apply where the posting is written for you instead: Palantir, for one, runs a separate New Grad application. Source 12Apply: Forward Deployed Software Engineer, New Grad - Commercial (New York, NY) - Palantir on LeverPublisherPalantir Technologies (Lever)Source typecompany job posting

When a gate is in your favor, put it where a skimmer sees it. “US citizen, active TS/SCI with polygraph” belongs in the header of a government application, not in a line on page two. “Authorized to work in the US without sponsorship” belongs there too, when it is true and the posting raises it. If clearance is your gate, the FDE security clearance post covers it in depth.

Years of experience sit between the two. We treat a clear shortfall as a gate, and a near miss as something to argue from the scope you have owned. Second check: the title and the level

The same kind of work goes by different titles. Palantir posts it as Forward Deployed Software Engineer Source 9Palantir Technologies - Forward Deployed Software Engineer (New York, NY)PublisherPalantir (Lever job board)Source typecompany job posting and Cognition as Deployed Engineer. Source 13Cognition job boardPublisherCognition (Ashby job board)Source typecompany job posting Search only for “forward deployed engineer” and you may miss the posting written for you.

Level matters as much as title. We treat a level mismatch like a gate: a strong resume sent to the wrong level is still a mismatch. If one company has an associate posting and a senior one, apply to the one whose stated years and scope you meet. Third check: when the form contradicts the posting or your resume

The application form is part of the application. Palantir’s US Government FDSE form, for example, asks whether you support the travel percentage and are comfortable with the pay range advertised on the posting. Source 14Apply: Forward Deployed Software Engineer - US Government (Washington, D.C.) - Palantir on LeverPublisherPalantir Technologies (Lever)Source typecompany job posting Hold every answer to three tests:

  • It is true. A yes to travel you can’t do can come back to you in the recruiter screen.
  • It matches the posting. Read what the posting advertises before you tick.
  • It matches your resume and what you will say out loud. If you confirm FDSE over software engineering, expect to be asked why.

Fourth check: the first third of your resume

This part is our method, not a rule any employer publishes. The top of the first page is what a fast reader, or a screening tool, sees first. If the evidence a posting asks for is not there, assume it was not seen.

The first third should answer four questions without scrolling:

  • Can this person take the job? Location, work authorization, citizenship or clearance, when the posting raises them.
  • Which job is this for? The posting’s exact title in a one-line summary.
  • Do they ship production code? The first bullet of the latest role names a system that ran in production.
  • Did someone outside their team depend on it? The same bullet names that person or group by role, and what changed for them.

Here is a fictional top third, before and after.

Before:

Senior Software Engineer. Passionate about AI and building scalable systems. Skills: Python, Kubernetes, LLMs, React, Go, Terraform.Responsible for backend services and collaborated with cross-functional stakeholders.

After:

Forward Deployed Engineer | Authorized to work in the US without sponsorship; open to customer travelSoftware engineer who builds and runs integrations inside customers’ systems, from discovery call to pager.Senior Software Engineer, Acme Freight Software (fictional), current role Built the carrier-email classifier our largest customer’s support leads use to route inbound mail; ran discovery with their team lead, built an eval set from the emails they had misrouted, carried the pager after launch, and they retired their manual triage rotation.

The “after” answers all four questions before the reader reaches a second job. The “before” answers none, and its skills list claims things the page never shows. If you have no customer to name yet, what to do with no customer-facing experience shows where that evidence hides in work you have already done. The same line, said aloud, is the opening of your answer to tell me about yourself in a minute.

A checklist of what you control

Run this before you apply again, whether to the same employer or the next one. It is our checklist, built from what postings and forms state.

Before you apply again

  • I meet every gate the posting states: years, level, work authorization, citizenship, clearance, location and travel.
  • The gates that help me are in the header, not buried.
  • I found the posting under every title the company uses, and picked the level I match.
  • The summary line uses the posting’s exact title.
  • The first bullet of my latest role shows production code and a person who depended on it.
  • The posting’s own nouns (discovery, evals, production rollout, data pipelines) appear where they are true of my work, and nowhere else.
  • Every yes-or-no answer on the form matches the posting, my resume and what I will tell a recruiter.
  • I can say why this role and not software engineering in two sentences.
  • I saved the exact resume and answers I sent, with the posting’s text.

That last item matters more than it looks. Postings change or close, and when you apply again you need to know what the reader saw last time. Keep one row per application: the posting title and link, the gates, which resume version you sent, and the outcome.

Worked example: diagnosing one rejection

Here is the method run on one fictional rejection. Priya is a backend engineer. She applied to a US FDE posting that says it cannot sponsor a visa and states slightly more years of experience than she has, and got an automated no a few days later.

  • Gates. She needs no sponsorship, but her header didn’t say so. Fixed: it is now the second line. Years are a near miss, so she argues scope instead: she owned one customer integration end to end.
  • Title and level. The same company has a second FDE posting at a lower level, and she meets its stated years outright.
  • Form. She ticked yes to the posting’s travel requirement, but her resume said “remote only”. That is a contradiction a reader can see. Fixed: she is open to travel, so the resume now says so.
  • Top third. A skills list and no customer. Rebuilt in the “after” shape above, with the integration she owned as the first bullet.

Decision: don’t reapply to the same posting yet. Apply to the lower-level posting with the new top third and the corrected form answers.

Notice what she didn’t do: reword her bullets, guess at a keyword filter, or send the same file to the next ten postings.

Common mistakes that feel like fixes

  • Rewording the same bullets. Swapping “led” for “drove” changes nothing a reader checks. Change what the bullet proves: add the customer, the outcome or the production run.
  • Sending the same application to the next posting. Each posting states its own gates and nouns. A resume tuned to an AI lab’s FDE posting and one tuned to an enterprise data company’s should differ in the summary and the first bullets.
  • Stuffing keywords. A term you can’t discuss for five minutes is a follow-up question you will struggle with. List only what the bullets already prove.
  • Answering the form fast. The form is the first thing you write for this employer. Draft it in a document, reread it against the posting, then paste it in.

When and how to apply again

Check each company’s own rule before you reapply. Anthropic’s careers FAQ says candidates may re-apply after 12 months, or sooner if something materially changes. Source 1Careers \ AnthropicPublisherAnthropicSource typecompany hiring page Other employers may say something different or nothing at all, so read their careers pages rather than assuming.

What counts as a change is the employer’s call, but here is how we would judge our own. Apply again when you can point to at least one of these:

  • New evidence. A project shipped to a real user, a new role, or a production system you now own.
  • A changed gate. Your work authorization, clearance or location is different from last time.
  • A rebuilt top third. Not new adjectives, but new proof in the header, summary and first bullet.

A different posting is a new application, not a change in you. If a company sets a waiting period, check whether it covers every role or only the one you applied to.

Then change the whole application, not only the resume: rewrite the form answers against the new posting, so the page tells the same story you will tell on the first call.
