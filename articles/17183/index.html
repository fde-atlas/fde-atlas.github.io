A Week in the Life of an FDE | Shipping Code on Day One and Returning It to the Product by Friday: The Implementation Process

Original title: FDEの1週間｜初日にコードを出し、金曜に製品へ返すまでの実装プロセス

TIMEWELL · 2026-09-15 · 13 min read

Summary

TIMEWELL's Hamamoto walks through a week-long FDE implementation cycle: Monday involves interviewing management, division heads, and frontline staff separately, identifying data owners, and deploying a first version running on anonymized real data by evening. Tuesday through Thursday focus on shadowing users, collecting exceptions, shipping one version per day, and running preliminary effect measurements. Friday uses KJ-method clustering to update a domain dictionary and split findings into reusable patterns (returned to the product) and customer-specific branches. The author argues speed comes from shortening the listen-build-feedback loop, not coding speed, contrasts this with sequential custom development measured by acceptance rather than business metrics, and honestly lists what cannot ship in a week, such as core-system write integrations and security-reviewed connections. Engagements run roughly 45–90 days, ending when the customer can self-sustain.

Key points

  • Speed comes from shortening the listen-build-feedback loop, not from faster coding.
  • Monday: three-layer interviews, identify data owners, ship a first version on anonymized real data.
  • Tuesday–Thursday: gather exceptions via observation, one version per day, preliminary measurement on Thursday.
  • Friday: KJ-method clustering, dictionary updates, patterns go to product, branches stay customer-specific.
  • Engagements last 45–90 days; success means the vendor becomes unnecessary.

Hello, I'm Hamamoto from TIMEWELL Co., Ltd.

This is the seventh article in the FDE practice series. In the previous six articles, I wrote about the techniques of listening, observing, being present, structuring, recording and counting, and handing the dictionary to the machine. This time, I'll write about how I arrange these across a single week.

Our company says we can deliver the fastest implementation in an FDE style. Honestly speaking, this phrasing leaves room for misunderstanding. It doesn't mean our engineers' hands are fast. It means the round trip of listening, building, capturing reactions, and feeding back into the product fits within one week. In this article, I'll write out that week from Monday to Friday, what differs from the process of contracted development, and what cannot reach production in a week, without hiding anything. If you want to check in advance how far your own team can run this round trip, please use the AI literacy diagnostic.

Premise: the source of speed is not fast hands, but short round trips

First, I'll write about what speed really is.

A person who served as an FDE at Palantir for eight years writes that when a small team in their twenties showed up at the customer's site and built real, usable software in one to two weeks, the customer was astonished. That's because the existing system integrators they dealt with operated on multi-year waterfalls 1. The FDE organization AWS launched in June 2026 states its aim as compressing deployment from months to days 2. The implementation-focused company OpenAI launched in May describes its typical approach as diagnosing value, selecting a small number of priority workflows with both management and the field, and designing, building, and deploying within the organization 3. What they have in common is the order: don't wait for requirements to solidify, put something working in place early, and learn after it's there.

There's one more reason for our speed: we are a small company. In large FDE organizations, the person who listens in the field, the person who builds, and the person who feeds back into the product are different people. At our company, it's the same person. The person who finds an exception in the field on Friday builds it the following Monday and decides that Friday whether to make it a standard feature of the product. What would need to be communicated in meetings if people were separated is completed within one person.

And then there's the existence of coding agents. Previously, only skilled engineers could produce code on day one. Now, if you hand over nouns and verbs, a provisional screen appears within hours. However, there's a pitfall here. As I wrote in the article on abstraction and concretization, there is research showing that even when the speed of writing code increases, the increase in value is smaller than that. Being able to write fast and being able to write the right thing are different. The FDE week is designed to shorten round trips in order to find the right thing, not designed to write fast.

Monday: listen across three layers, identify the data owner, and put down a first version by evening

Monday morning is a day for listening. We listen separately to three layers: management, the division head, and the field. If you gather them in the same room, only management's voice survives. We ask management what they want to change in the industry, the division head about this quarter's numbers, and the field about the screens they opened yesterday afternoon. The goal of the week is to find a single point that satisfies all three simultaneously. We call this single point what OpenAI calls "a small number of priority workflows."

Within the morning, we decide one more thing: the owner of the data. "Which system does that number come from?" "Who is the administrator?" As a former Palantir FDE wrote, 8-12 week pilots can be consumed by negotiations for data access 1. Our arrangement is to decide the anonymization procedure at the first meeting, run the security review in parallel, and operate on anonymized data while the review is in progress. If the owner is identified by Monday morning, anonymized real data is in hand by the afternoon.

The afternoon is time for observing. We sit diagonally behind the person in charge and watch the flow of their day through shadowing. We don't interject. We record movement and waiting. This is where the five-column record begins.

In the evening, we put down a first version. On top of our product's standard components, we load the nouns and verbs heard in the morning, copy the screen flow observed in the afternoon, and run it on anonymized real data. The coding agent writes it, a human reads it, and it's placed in front of the person in charge. I wrote the details of how we build it in the live mock article. The question we ask after placing it is one sentence: "What's different?" The differences that emerge here are what we build on Tuesday.

The first version carries no write operations. Read and suggest only. Processing that writes back to the production system is not included until four things are decided: permissions, prior simulation, human involvement in critical decisions, and auditable records. This is a condition that comes before speed.

Tuesday to Thursday: gather exceptions through observation, layering one version per day

Tuesday through Thursday are days for building while watching. In the morning, using the contextual questioning method, we sit at the place of work and ask, "Why did you do that just now?" while watching the work. The exception notebook grows: "When it's urgent, the section chief pushes it," "This report is used only at month-end," "This check stalls when the manager is away." In the afternoon, we load the morning's exceptions into the version.

The version is built spec-driven. We write one line of the exception notebook as a short specification, have the coding agent implement it, a human reads it, and it's placed in front of the person in charge. The reason we write the specification first is so we can later trace what we had the agent build; I've summarized the method in the spec-driven development article. One version per day. Even if there's room for two, we do one. The number of times the person in charge can respond is limited to once a day.

On Thursday afternoon, we do a provisional effect measurement. We have them do the same work using the working version and count time and the number of screen switches. If consent for recording has been obtained, we count from video. The difference—"approval waiting went from 47 minutes to 12 minutes"—becomes material for Friday's decision. The provisional measurement is a deliverable for the person in charge, and at the same time, evidence for our own question: "Is this pattern worth feeding back into the product?"

One thing our engineers are careful about during these three days: don't overbuild. If there are 10 items in the exception notebook, you'll want to build all of them. But 7 of the 10 are branches unique to that company, and they only happen once a month. By Thursday, we build only the exceptions that happen every day. The rest are decided on Friday, after sorting into patterns versus branches.

Friday: make bundles, update the dictionary, and feed patterns back into the product

Friday is a day for thinking away from the field. In the morning, we turn the fragments from Monday to Thursday—the verbatim transcripts, the five-column records, the exception notebook, and Thursday's measurements—into cards using the KJ method way of thinking, make bundles, and name them. I wrote this procedure in the KJ method article.

In the afternoon, we pick five categories from the bundle of names and update the dictionary: nouns, verbs, relations, states, and exceptions. The definition of "approve" changes this week to "the section chief decides and the manager stamps," and the version goes up by one. As written in the ontology article, the dictionary is written on the assumption that the agent will read it the following Monday.

Then we split the bundle of names into patterns and branches. "Approvals have two tiers" is a pattern we have seen at other companies too. Work to let the product configure the number of approval tiers and decision-makers goes into the product backlog. "That paper is held by Tanaka-san in accounting" is a branch. We record it and do not put it into the product. One of the pressure tests a16z organized in January 2026—whether you can say "No" to customization 4—is something our company can answer because of this Friday.

At the end of Friday, we write two documents. For the person in charge, a paragraph: "Next week we will build the approval path for urgent cases on screen. The reason is that the current paper path assumes section-chief decisions." For our own company, a decision: "The alternate path for urgent cases is a pattern; the holding place is a branch." With these two documents written, the week ends.

Repeating weeks. In 45 to 90 days, our company becomes unnecessary

A week is this repetition, but as the weeks accumulate, the content changes. In its January 2026 essay, a16z organized the pattern of a real FDE as time-boxed deployment, citing a sprint that reaches production in 90 days as an example 4. AWS places at the center of its design the state in which the customer can run on their own at the end—that is, leaving behind something that works, documentation, and trained internal staff 2. We also decide the on-site period at the start: roughly 45 to 90 days.

Weeks 1 and 2 are the back-and-forth described above, covering reading and proposing. In weeks 3 and 4, we phase in write-backs. We decide four points—permissions, pre-simulation, human intervention, and audit records—and first write back just one process, watching it in production for a week. Around week 4, we start having the person in charge touch the dictionary. "The decision-maker for the urgent category changed" gets fixed by the person in charge, not by us. In weeks 6 to 8, we create days when our engineers are not on site. If the back-and-forth keeps running on those days, we are ready to leave.

There are three conditions for leaving. The person in charge can update the dictionary themselves. The effect of the working system is visible to the person in charge through business metrics. And the patterns have returned to the product, so the same learning is no longer needed at the next customer. The person who led Palantir's commercial business called the FDE the "zero-authority CEO" of the customer's business, demanding accountability for outcomes 5. For us, the outcome is that we become unnecessary.

Differences from the contracted development process

We line up the structure of a week alongside the contracted development process.

PerspectiveContracted developmentAn FDE week
Order of processRequirements definition, design, implementation, testing, acceptance in seriesListen, build, capture reactions, return—in parallel every week
Day-one deliverableMeeting minutes and a draft of requirementsA first version running on anonymized real data
Handling of changeRequirement changes mean additional cost and re-estimationChanges go into Tuesday's version. On Friday, sort into pattern or branch
How results are measuredAcceptance (does it work as specified)Business metrics (did 47 minutes become 12)
Where learning goesThe customer's assetsPatterns to the product, branches to the customer's extensions
How it endsAccept and withdrawThe person in charge touches the dictionary, and we become unnecessary

The biggest difference is not arranging the process in series. Contracted development does not write code until requirements are fixed. It designs the fixed requirements, implements, tests, and accepts. This order is the least wasteful if the requirements are correct. The FDE abandons the series because requirements are never correct from the start. The person in charge cannot write, on a blank specification sheet, the exceptions the section chief will hit. They can only say it after seeing something that works. That is why we put listening and building in the same week, and capture reactions every day.

Another difference is how results are measured. Contracted development measures by acceptance: if it works as specified, it is done. The FDE measures by business metrics: even if it works as specified, it is not done unless the person in charge's pending approvals have decreased. That is why we take a provisional measurement on Thursday and retake it the following week. This difference in measurement changes the meaning of "fast." Contracted development's speed is the number of days to acceptance; the FDE's speed is the number of days until the person in charge's day changes.

Our practice and its limits. What does not reach production in a week

We will be honest. There are things that do not reach production in a week.

Write-back integration with core systems does not happen in a week. Because we decide permissions, pre-simulation, human intervention, and audit records, and phase in starting from a single process, it takes several weeks. Connections requiring security review also depend on the review period. We proceed with the review in parallel and run on anonymized data during the review, but we cannot make the review itself faster. Processes that only appear at month-end or busy seasons cannot be seen until that time comes, so we listen to them as events, build provisionally, and fix them when the time arrives.

We will also state the conditions for speed: the person in charge's time and access to the data. On sites where the person in charge keeps saying "I'm busy, later," the back-and-forth does not run. On sites where the data owner cannot be identified on Monday, there is nothing to build on Tuesday. At the first meeting, we agree on these two as conditions of the engagement. On sites where we cannot agree, we do not promise a one-week back-and-forth.

Finally, we will note the danger of the phrase "code on day one." What appears on day one is an incomplete first version. It is a question device for drawing out the answer to "What's different?" from the person in charge. If this is taken as a finished product, disappointment arrives on day two. That is why, whenever we place a first version, we always say: "This exists so you can tell us the differences." The value of speed lies not in day-one completeness, but in how quickly we approach the right thing by Friday.

Summary

The basis for our claim that we can implement fastest is that the back-and-forth of listening, building, capturing reactions, and returning to the product fits within one week. On Monday we listen across three layers, identify the data owner, and by evening place a first version running on anonymized real data. From Tuesday to Thursday we gather exceptions with contextual questioning, stack one version per day with spec-driven development, and take a provisional measurement of the effect on Thursday. On Friday we form bundles with the KJ method, update the dictionary, return patterns to the product, and record branches.

Over repeated weeks, in 45 to 90 days, the person in charge updates the dictionary themselves and we become unnecessary. The differences from contracted development are not arranging the process in series, measuring results by business metrics rather than acceptance, and learning returning to the product. Some things do not reach production in a week, and the conditions for speed are the person in charge's time and access to the data.
