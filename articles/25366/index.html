On-Site Debugging as an FDE: How to Reproduce Field Bugs

Original title: FDE 现场调试：如何复现现场 bug

FDE Times · 2026-10-25 · 6 min read

Summary

Using Saronic's autonomous surface vessels as a backdrop, this guide explains how forward deployed engineers should debug field-only bugs: reproduce before fixing. A hypothetical scenario of a vessel veering off course near port illustrates the method. Key practices include recording everything locally with a ring buffer, sending only small summaries over low-bandwidth satellite links, replaying snapshots to isolate whether the fault lies in software, hardware, or the network, fixing nondeterminism in the software stack, and converting triggering snapshots into permanent regression tests. It also advocates deliberately injecting faults like GPS loss in controlled trials with objective pass/fail criteria, and lists common mistakes such as debugging only your own layer.

Key points

  • Reproduction comes before repair; without it, no fix is verifiable.
  • If replay reproduces the bug, it's in software; otherwise suspect inputs or nondeterminism.
  • Record everything locally; send only small summaries over slow satellite links.
  • Run replays multiple times with fixed seeds and simulated clocks.
  • Inject faults deliberately in controlled trials with objective pass/fail criteria.

Key Takeaways

  • In the field, reproducing a bug is often harder than fixing it. Record data first, then fix.
  • If a replay reproduces the bug, it's in the software; if not, suspect the inputs (hardware, network) or nondeterminism in the software stack.
  • Proactively inject faults in controlled trials, such as GPS loss or engine failure; don't wait for the real environment to trigger them for you.

Imagine an unmanned vessel that holds its heading steadily offshore all morning. In the afternoon, as it enters port, it starts to drift off course, corrects itself, then drifts off again. Back at the office, replaying the logs shows everything looks normal.

Handling failures like this is written right into the job description of a Forward Deployed Engineer (FDE) working on autonomous systems. Saronic builds autonomous surface vessels, and describes its FDEs as the people who integrate, deploy, and improve autonomous systems in real-world environments.

According to the job description, FDEs troubleshoot software, hardware, and network issues simultaneously during deployments, and spend 50% or more of their time traveling domestically and internationally.

You may never board a ship. But nearly every FDE has encountered bugs that only appear at the customer site: no debugger to attach, slow networks, and no one who can recreate that exact moment for you. The open sea is the harshest version of this problem, which makes it the best setting for learning.

In the field, the hard part is reproducing the bug

A moment at sea happens only once. The waves, wind, nearby vessels, and satellite signals will never align the same way again. If you patch things on the spot without recording anything, you can neither prove the fix works nor recognize when the bug returns.

At Saronic, the role responsible for planning sea trials tracks defects through a structured process, recording reproduction steps, severity, and corrective actions for each defect. The principle worth remembering: until you can reproduce a bug, you have nothing reliable to fix.

Reproduction requires data. According to Saronic, endurance trials of its Corsair vessel produced roughly 500 hours of video from multiple cameras, totaling about 17 TB.

The company says it uses this data for model development, replay, and regression testing. The trial was designed to run continuously to assess reliability and endurance — in other words, to surface the failures that short tests miss.

Slow links force you to decide what to send back

A study on autonomous vessels notes that at sea, ships are constrained by low-bandwidth satellite links. No one would push 17 TB over such a connection. So separate two things: record all data on the vessel's onboard machines, and send ashore only enough information for people to know "something happened."

Here's a simple sketch of this idea. The thresholds, cooldown periods, and field names are just examples:

import collections, json, time

def send_over_satlink(msg):
    """桩函数：替换为真实的卫星客户端，并加上队列和大小限制。"""
    payload = json.dumps(msg).encode()
    assert len(payload) < 512, "摘要必须足够小"
    print("SATLINK", payload)

class FieldRecorder:
    """在本地机器上记录全部数据，经慢速链路只发送摘要。"""
    def __init__(self, window_s=120, rate_hz=50, cooldown_s=60):
        self.buf = collections.deque(maxlen=window_s * rate_hz)
        self.cooldown_s = cooldown_s
        self.last_snap = {}  # 原因 -> 最近一次快照的时间

    def record(self, s):  # s：包含 t、heading_err_deg、gps_fix、link_ok、sw_version 的 dict
        self.buf.append(s)
        if abs(s["heading_err_deg"]) > 5:
            self.maybe_snapshot("heading_error")

    def maybe_snapshot(self, reason):
        now = time.monotonic()
        if now - self.last_snap.get(reason, float("-inf")) < self.cooldown_s:
            return  # 仍是同一次航向偏离，不再额外保存快照
        self.last_snap[reason] = now
        path = f"/data/snap_{int(time.time())}_{reason}.jsonl"
        with open(path, "w") as f:
            for s in self.buf:
                f.write(json.dumps(s) + "\n")
        # 经卫星只传几百字节，完整文件留在船上
        send_over_satlink({"reason": reason, "file": path, "n": len(self.buf)})

The key is the ring buffer: when an anomaly is detected, you already have the previous two minutes of data — the segment leading up to the failure. The cooldown period matters just as much. Without it, a heading deviation lasting a few minutes would generate thousands of snapshots, filling the disk and clogging the satellite link.

Also write the state of all three layers into the same record: software version, GPS status, and link status. That way, when it's time to isolate the fault, you have evidence in hand.

Example: heading deviation in port

Back to the ship from the beginning. This is a hypothetical scenario for practicing the method, not an incident Saronic has published. On paper there are three classes of hypotheses: the navigation logic doesn't handle conditions that only appear near shore; sensors read differently in a port environment; or a connection interruption causes the system to switch modes.

As for the positioning layer, this hypothesis has grounding. Research on autonomous vessels warns that when GPS loses signal, is jammed, or is spoofed, unmanned vessels can end up in dangerous situations. This is exactly why the

gps_fix

field in the record matters so much.

The decisive test is replay. Feed the recorded snapshot, exactly as captured, into the navigation software stack on an office machine. If the virtual vessel deviates just like the real one did at sea, the bug is in the logic, and you now have a test case to fix against.

If the replay looks perfect, there are two possibilities. The first is that the data you fed in differs from what the system actually received at sea — in that case, suspect timing, hardware, or the network.

The second is that the software stack itself is nondeterministic: thread ordering, processing latency, or some random step can make the same input produce different results.

So run the replay multiple times, fix the random seeds, and use a simulated clock. If the runs disagree, resolve the nondeterminism in the software stack before drawing conclusions about the hardware.

Write the findings into the bug ticket like this:

FieldHow to fill it in for the heading deviation bug
SymptomHeading error repeatedly exceeds the threshold when approaching port
ConditionsTime, location, GPS status, link status, software version
Reproduction stepsReplay a specific snapshot; record the number of runs and how many of them showed the bug
SeverityWhether it affects operational safety
Suspected layerSoftware, hardware, or network, with evidence from the replay attached
Corrective actionThe fix, plus a regression test based on this snapshot

Many people skip the last row. The snapshot that triggered the bug should become a permanent test, so the next trial doesn't have to go back to sea to hunt for the old bug.

Trigger failures before the real environment does

The best way to avoid surprises at sea is to create failures yourself in controlled trials. In a 2013 sea trial, GPS was deliberately jammed, and a prototype system automatically switched to eLoran.

That test targeted positioning systems rather than autonomous vessels, but the method transfers directly.

In a recent autonomy demonstration, the program included an emergency response drill for a simulated mechanical failure, with the vessel monitored remotely from shore.

Such tests are only valuable when the criteria are defined in advance. Saronic organizes testing around a test matrix, where every requirement maps to an objective pass or fail criterion.

"The degraded mode worked well" is just an impression. "When GPS is lost, switch to the backup system with deviation no greater than the allowed value" is a testable criterion.

There's also the safety question. At Saronic, the on-site test lead directs trials in real time and is responsible for their safety. To deliberately inject a fault, you must agree on it with this person beforehand; no one may unilaterally cut off a running system.

Common Mistakes

The most common mistake is debugging only the layer you're familiar with. The software engineer dives into the code, but the evidence points to the antenna or the link. That's why the FDE job description puts software, hardware, and networking on the same line.

The second mistake is trying to send all the data back over a slow link, clogging it at the very moment you need it most. The third is fixing things on the spot without saving a snapshot, so the fix can never be proven. The fourth is testing without pass or fail criteria, then arguing afterward about whether the results were any good.

Shortening the loop between the field and the code

Saronic's test planners work daily with the software team, vessel operations, and suppliers to resolve every issue quickly and thoroughly. A good bug ticket keeps that loop turning: people in the office get the snapshot and reproduction steps and can figure out what happened without traveling to the site.

On the career side, when reading FDE job postings, watch for phrases like "software, hardware, and networking" or "real-world environments": they signal that the job requires field debugging. And don't just write "handled production incidents" on your resume.

Tell the story of one specific bug: how you collected data, how you reproduced it, which layer you isolated the problem to, and what regression test you turned it into. In interviews, a story like that is far more persuasive than a list of tools.

The sea doesn't give you a second chance. A good engineer is someone who can bring that one moment of failure back to the office and replay it as many times as they want.
