Background: The private deployment wave after DeepSeek

In the first half of 2025, domestic large model private deployment entered an explosive growth phase. According to a May 2025 Xinhua Net report, over a hundred companies are competing in the large model all-in-one machine space, with the direct trigger of the boom being DeepSeek's rise; all-in-one machines based on DeepSeek and other domestic large models already cover energy, finance, telecom operators, government, manufacturing, and education/research sectors. A Cailian Press report describes the product form: DeepSeek all-in-one machines released in rapid succession, surging demand for private deployment, and training-inference all-in-one machines delivering "out-of-the-box" software-hardware integrated delivery. Taken together, the two main storylines show a market window where supply-side product form (all-in-one machines) and demand-side compliance motives (data staying off the intranet) have arrived simultaneously.

First, let's clarify what "private deployment" means. According to the definition on Xinhua Daily's website, private deployment means the model weights, inference services, and compute are all deployed in the enterprise's own environment (on-premises data center, private cloud, or dedicated zone), with input and output data never leaving the intranet. This definition makes it a completely different delivery form from API procurement: what the customer buys is not model capability, but an entire engineering system that must run in their own data center and be continuously maintained. This form has become the "preferred solution" in China; according to an IDC report cited by China Daily Net, private deployment as the preferred solution has driven the formation of an independent procurement and delivery system in this field. There is a dedicated in-site analysis of this form's relationship to job structures, see FDE Localization in China.

The problem: hype first, deployment lags

Demand-side hype is backed by market estimates: according to brokerage estimates cited by Sohu (third-party estimates, note these are predictive in nature), the domestic large model all-in-one machine market will exceed 500 billion yuan by 2027, and over 30 companies have already launched all-in-one machine products. 21st Century Business Herald also observed that demand for DeepSeek private deployment is surging, G-end (government) large model deployment is becoming a trend, and related concept stocks in the secondary market have moved accordingly. But the gap between hype and deployment capability is equally evident: National Business Daily observed that AI all-in-one machines are mostly "jointly produced" and assembled by several vendors—broad industry coverage but uneven single-point depth, with supply-side product form ahead of delivery capability.

For buyers, the question thus becomes: the hardware and models are all in place, so why can't the business actually use them? This question is not hypothetical—it is a phenomenon-level problem directly confronted by Lei Feng Network's survey, which the evidence chain below answers.

The solution: software-hardware integration is only the starting point; delivery capability is the product

The industry's answer can be summarized in three layers. The first is the product layer: all-in-one machines bundle compute, models, and foundational software, compressing "deployment" into a nearly out-of-the-box action and lowering the entry barrier—this solves the "can it be installed" problem. The second is the service layer: taking the iSoftStone-Huawei partnership as a sample, the two parties jointly released the Ascend DeepSeek professional services solution, covering one-stop large model delivery capability from data processing, model development, and optimization to deployment and inference services—note this coverage chain: from data processing to inference services, almost no link is "pure hardware"; the service layer solves the "can it be used" problem. The third is the market structure layer: according to an IDC report cited by China Daily Net, China Telecom's AI company has entered the top three in the large model private deployment market, with leading players entering this market on the strength of telecom-grade service networks—the structure layer answers the "who is qualified to deliver long-term" question.

The judgment from combining the three layers: in the private deployment market, the product (the all-in-one machine) is only the delivery vehicle; what is actually being purchased is full-chain delivery capability. This judgment mirrors the in-site analysis Build or Buy Your Delivery Team—vendors are competing on delivery capability, while customers are selecting delivery capability.

Implementation: bottlenecks concentrate in two "soft" links

The implementation-level evidence comes from Lei Feng Network's survey of 12 listed companies, whose conclusion is worth quoting verbatim: the real gap in all-in-one machine deployment lies in vendors not mastering users' business logic and users' inaccurate data governance—domestic empirical proof that "hardware in place does not equal deployment." This is corroborated by analysis from the Tencent Cloud Developer Community: the bottleneck for enterprise AI often lies not in the model itself, but in deployment links such as requirements definition, data permissions, and system integration.

Combining the two pieces of evidence, the bottleneck profile of domestic private deployment becomes clear. First, business logic: vendors arrive on-site with generic all-in-one machines, but the customer's industry workflows, metric definitions, and approval chains all live in the customer's head, requiring someone on-site to translate business language into system design—this is precisely the core work of the Forward Deployed Engineer (FDE), and also what domestic counterpart roles (private deployment engineers, AI implementation engineers, pre-sales solutions engineers) actually do. Second, data governance: customers' existing data is often not organized into a usable state, and issues with data permissions, quality, and labeling erupt during the deployment phase; if data governance is inaccurate, model output cannot align with business definitions, and scenarios naturally fail to land.

The common feature of these two links: they cannot be solved by piling on hardware or upgrading models—only by "people who understand the business being on-site." This explains a seemingly paradoxical phenomenon: the more widespread all-in-one machines become, the greater the demand for on-site delivery personnel, because "out-of-the-box" only standardizes the installation step, while the longer customization phase that follows is actually exposed.

Results: delivery capability enters the revenue structure

The hardest evidence for whether delivery capability holds up is financial data. According to Cailian Press, iSoftStone's 2025 revenue was 35.090 billion yuan, with AI-related business contributing more than half—financial validation of the Huawei-affiliated integrator's transformation into "compute, models, applications, terminals" full-stack delivery. This shows that in the domestic market, "getting large models running in customer environments" is no longer just a concept, but a revenue structure supporting tens of billions in revenue.

The result on the other side is the supply-side competitive landscape: according to IDC, the private deployment market has formed an independent procurement system, with leading players (such as China Telecom's AI company) entering the top three. Putting the two results together: the market has voted with real money for "full-stack delivery capability," not for models alone or hardware alone. For practitioners, this means private deployment project opportunities will continue to flow to organizations that possess compute, model, and application capabilities simultaneously—and the scarcest resource inside such organizations is FDE-type roles capable of end-to-end delivery.

Constraints: the triple boundaries of Xinchuang, intranets, and requirements definition

The constraints on domestic private deployment differ from foreign cases. First is Xinchuang adaptation: industry-compiled materials show that domestic AI agent service providers' core capability lists generally include full-stack Xinchuang adaptation (domestic CPUs, operating systems, databases, middleware), already deployed at central state-owned enterprises, requiring delivery teams to have cross-stack adaptation experience; Orient Fortune Securities research also notes that system integrators have gained incremental Xinchuang integration business through customer resources, with some companies entering the private cloud market via the Huawei Kunpeng ecosystem. Second is the intranet constraint: data staying in-domain means cloud collaboration tools and online evals platforms cannot be used directly, and delivery processes must be rebuilt in offline environments; for security-related checklist items, see the in-site Data Compliance Checklist.

Third is the difficulty of defining requirements: the demand side of private deployment projects is often a mix of business and technical departments, and unclear requirement definition is the most common cause of project delays and rework. The quality of scoping during the pre-sales stage directly determines the fate of delivery. Fourth is vendor-side organizational constraints: the drag that on-site delivery places on gross margin traps many vendors in the dilemma of "taking on projects that can't sustain the team, but not taking on projects means the team can't be sustained"—an old business-model problem replayed in the AI era.

Reusable Lessons

First, selling an all-in-one machine is not the same as delivering AI: hardware arriving on site is only the beginning of delivery; sorting out business logic and data governance account for most of the work hours, and pricing and scheduling should be structured accordingly. Second, pre-sales must include an inventory of the current state of data governance—for projects where the data is unusable, the faster the deployment, the more the rework; this inventory should become a standard step in pre-sales for private deployment projects. Third, the delivery team needs "business translation" capabilities; on-site staff with purely technical backgrounds cannot handle private deployment projects, which corresponds to the composite skill requirements of the FDE role—see the on-site comparison of FDE and implementation consultants. Fourth, when observing the market, track "procurement heat" and "deployment depth" separately: the former from tender announcements, the latter from scenario runtime data; the two often diverge.

For deployment depth in large-scale scenarios such as banking, see the on-site review of scenario deployments at ICBC and China Merchants Bank; for an international comparison of delivery organizations and models, see An Analysis of the Palantir FDE Model and FDE Business Model Analysis.
