Design Procedures for Business Workflows and Data Integration

Original title: 業務ワークフローとデータ連携の設計手順

日本Fde協会 · 2026-09-17 · 6 min read

Summary

A practical guide from the Japan FDE Association on designing business workflows and data integration after requirements are set. It explains how to break processes into steps described by owner, input, output, and whether judgment is involved; to color-code which steps to automate versus leave to humans; and to choose between API, batch, or manual-entry-replacement integration based on immediacy, frequency, and data volume. It also stresses building access control, logs, and failure paths into the design from the start, citing cases from manufacturing, SaaS, and financial clients.

Key points

  • Describe each workflow step by owner, input, output, and judgment required.
  • Keep human judgment for steps with many exceptions, accountability, or money involved.
  • Choose integration by immediacy, frequency, and volume: API, batch, or replacing manual entry.
  • Over-connecting via real-time APIs caused maintenance pain at a SaaS firm; daily batch fixed it.
  • Design permissions, logs, and failure paths upfront; retrofitting governance is costly.

The requirements are set. Next comes the task of turning those requirements into an actual working blueprint of business workflows and data integration. If you stumble here, all your hard-won requirements become a "pie in the sky." We at the Japan FDE Association, a neutral organization of practitioners of FDE (Forward Deployed Engineer—engineers who embed themselves in customers' operations and solve problems with technology), have tackled this design phase head-on. By reading this article, you will learn to break down business workflows into individual processes and produce a blueprint that covers data integration between systems, including "what to automate and where to leave human judgment." We also organize how to choose integration methods and identify fragile points, in a form you can use directly in your next steps.

Breaking Down Business Flows into Individual Processes

The starting point of design is dividing the field-level flow gathered through interviews into "processes." Field explanations tend to be given in chunks, like "when an order comes in, we process it and ship." Only by breaking this down into units such as input, judgment, transformation, recording, and output does the feasibility of automation become visible.

Write It as "Who," "What Input," and "What Output"

Describe each process using four points: the person in charge, input data, output data, and whether judgment is involved. This format clarifies the nature of the data passed between processes. In a manufacturing client's shipping/receiving management, a process the field considered a single task was actually split into three: inventory checking, slip issuance, and shipping instructions. Only after decomposition did it become clear that only slip issuance could be automated, while inventory checking required human judgment.

Color-Code Automation and Human Judgment First

Once processes are laid out, color-code which ones to automate and which to leave to human judgment. Processes with many exceptions, those involving accountability, or those tied to amounts or credit should not be forced into automation—design them to retain human judgment. In FDE practice, it is common to ask at the outset, "Do you really need to automate all of that?" It is not rare that inserting a single human checkpoint makes the field run more comfortably than full automation.

Criteria for Choosing a Data Integration Method

Where data moves between processes is "integration." Choosing the wrong method here yields a system that runs but cannot be maintained. Integration methods broadly fall into three categories: API integration for real-time connections, batch integration that processes data together at fixed times, and automation of manual transcription that replaces human re-entry with a mechanism.

Judge by Immediacy, Frequency, and Data Volume

Selection should be based on the nature of the business, not on memorized performance figures. If immediate reflection is needed or frequency is high, API integration fits. If nightly batch processing suffices or large volumes of data must be handled at once, batch is the solid choice. Here are the decision guidelines.

Integration MethodSuitable BusinessPoints of Caution
API integration (real-time)Inventory, reservations, and other cases requiring immediate reflectionVulnerable to specification changes and outages on the other side
Batch integration (scheduled)Daily aggregation, bulk processing of large dataDelay before reflection / requires retry design for failures
Replacing manual transcriptionTasks where humans are transcribing right nowRisk of automatically carrying over the original input errors

The "Over-Connecting" Trap Seen at a SaaS Company

A certain SaaS company connected sales data across multiple tools in real time via APIs. It looked smart, but every time one tool changed its specifications, integrations broke one after another, and they were consumed by maintenance. When they reverted the parts that did not need immediacy to daily batches, breakages dropped dramatically. So that connecting does not become an end in itself, the designer's role is to ask, process by process, whether that integration truly requires immediacy.

Make Integrations Resilient and Traceable

What generic explanations of integration design overlook is the perspective of governance. Being able to trace later who touched which data and when. Whether this is built in from the start makes a world of difference in peace of mind after production rollout.

Build Access Control and Logs into the Integration Path from the Start

Along the paths where data moves between processes, build access permissions and processing logs into the design stage. Who can execute this integration, when and how many records were processed successfully, and where failed data is quarantined. Trying to add these later forces you to rebuild a running system. In a financial institution project, designing each integration to record processing counts, the executor, and failed records from the start made agreement with the audit department proceed remarkably quickly (FDE here refers to the job role, not Full Disk Encryption).

Identify Fragile Points Early

Integrations tend to break in predictable places. Connection points where external specifications change, transformation points where data formats don't align, and waiting behavior when the other side goes down—mark these on the blueprint first, and decide in advance how failures are detected and re-executed. Only when you draw not just the "path that works normally" but also the "path when something fails" is the blueprint complete. For tips on the requirements-gathering stage, see Practical Techniques to Avoid Failure in FDE Requirements Definition, and for the overall approach to improvement, see How to Approach AI-Driven Business Improvement.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q. How finely should business workflows be broken down? The guideline is: down to the granularity where the boundary between automation and human judgment becomes visible. Too fine makes the design heavy; too coarse leaves the data handed off between integrations ambiguous. Using as a criterion whether each process can be written as "person in charge, input, output, and presence of judgment" helps you settle on an appropriate granularity.

Q. Should all integrations be real-time via APIs? No. Make real-time only the processes that truly need immediacy; for the rest, batch is often sufficient. Over-connecting raises maintenance load and increases the number of breakage points. It is realistic to ask, process by process, whether that integration needs immediacy.

Q. Can governance and log design be added later? Technically possible, but rebuilding a running integration becomes costly. Records that let you trace who processed what and when are, in the end, faster and safer when woven into the paths at the design stage.
