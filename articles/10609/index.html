Part 1 | You Know How to Use AI—So Why Can't You Deliver Projects? Understanding the FDE Through Palantir

Why does an AI demo look smart, yet the business owner doesn't dare let it actually execute?

Palantir's FDE (Forward Deployed Engineer) practice offers an entry point for understanding this question: engineers work close to the real business, connecting user needs, data, constraints, and software delivery. OpenAI has also set up similar roles to help customers turn research capabilities into production systems.

In today's article, we'll continue using a repair work order to break down this way of working: what's still missing between "the model can answer" and "the business can use it"?

Scenario recreation: Why is your AI demo perfect, but the boss doesn't dare use it?

Imagine this familiar scenario: you give the AI a piece of equipment repair request information, and it instantly produces a fault summary and thoughtfully recommends a repair engineer.

The demo is perfect, and the whole room applauds. But the business owner immediately fires off a series of soul-searching questions:

  • "Is this engineer available today?"
  • "Are his qualifications sufficient to repair this machine?"
  • "Who has the authority to approve this dispatch?"
  • "If the work order has already been taken by someone else, how does the system handle it?"

Once these questions come out, the project instantly falls from the show-off stage of "what can the model answer" into the deep waters of "does the enterprise dare to use it, and can it use it sustainably?"

Generating an answer in a chat box is easy, but completing a real dispatch requires connecting data, rules, permissions, and actual operational results behind the scenes—engineering problems far more complex than a demo.

While recently re-reviewing Palantir's materials, I've been pondering a core question: how do you turn an AI demo that looks smart into software that people actually use and that produces real monetary results?

What does an FDE actually deliver? Starting from "understanding human language"

FDE stands for Forward Deployed Engineer—let's consistently call it "Forward Deployed Engineer." Within Palantir, this role has an even more imposing name: Forward Deployed Software Engineer (FDSE).

The official definition is plain: work with customers to understand problems, process business data, build custom applications, and push projects from "I want to build something" to "this thing actually runs." [1]

Note the words "forward deployed"—they mean you must be closest to the front lines of fire.

As an FDE, you can't just sit in the office writing code. You need to know why frontline operators secretly bypass a certain system, figure out what a supervisor actually looks at when approving an action, and understand which data can truly be trusted. Only by figuring these things out can your technical solution take root.

Let's take the work order dispatch example again.

A layperson might say: "I want to build an intelligent scheduling AI agent."

A qualified FDE would translate the requirement into: "Let the dispatcher select candidates from available and qualified personnel, confirm, complete the dispatch, and keep an operation record."

See the difference? The latter sentence contains the user, the action, the constraints, and the result—this is the kind of "human language" that can actually be implemented and accepted.

FDE delivers through collaboration

In Palantir's system, the FDE is by no means a lone hero. [1][2]

  • Deployment Strategist: Like a "scout," they dive into workflows, dig into data, and work with engineers to find solutions.
  • Forward Deployed Software Engineer (FDSE): Like a "special forces soldier," they focus on engineering implementation, turning solutions into code and pushing projects forward.
  • Core engineering team: Like an "arsenal," they absorb frontline feedback, standardize effective tools, and continuously upgrade the product.

These roles are tightly interwoven. Strategists need to understand data and products, and engineers need to be able to debate directly with users.

Understanding them as "comrades-in-arms who iterate together around customer problems" is far more accurate than the outsourcing model of "one person gives requirements, another takes the order and writes code."

For those of you who want to learn FDE, this point is crucial: you don't need to shoulder all the professional work alone, but you do need to see the big picture and know when to call for help—whether to bring in the business owner to make the call, or to have the security team review it.

First understand the foundation: what is Palantir's technical base actually solving?

In Palantir's official architecture, there are three core components: Foundry, AIP, and Apollo.

  • Foundry: An enterprise-oriented data operations and application platform.
  • AIP: Integrates generative AI into business processes, essentially giving the enterprise an "AI brain."
  • Apollo: Supports continuous software delivery, essentially an "auto-update butler."

And at the core of it all is something called Ontology. It's like a giant map connecting the enterprise's data, logic, actions, and security policies. [3]

Let's use dispatch as an example again:

If you want the AI to dispatch work, it first needs access to personnel scheduling and work order data, right? (That's Foundry's job)

Then, you need to clearly define what an "engineer" is, what a "work order" is, and what the "assignment relationship" between them is. (That's Ontology's job—it's the core!)

Next, the AI can help interpret requirements and generate suggestions. (That's AIP's home turf)

Finally, when actually submitting a change, it still needs to check whether permissions are sufficient and whether it's compliant. (That's Ontology gatekeeping again)

After going live, how does the software get upgraded and how is its status monitored? Leave that to Apollo.

Start practicing FDE thinking with three questions

For us ordinary learners, don't be intimidated by those lofty concepts. The best entry point for learning this methodology is the business scenario you're most familiar with: whether it's expense approval, order anomalies, or customer lead assignment.

Try rewriting vague ideas like "I want to add AI" into: "Which person, under what conditions, needs to better complete which action."

Today's homework: Pick a scenario and answer these three soul-searching questions:

  • Who uses it every day? (User profile)
  • Where is the most time-consuming, most error-prone step currently? (Pain point identification)
  • What results will you use to judge whether the improvement is successful? (Effect validation)

If you can't answer these three questions yet, don't rush to write code—first go chat with real users. This kind of conversation will directly determine how you select data, design the interface, and evaluate the model later.

Zero-One Notes

I'd rather understand the FDE role as a way of sequencing work: first look at real users and constraints, then write code; first define an action that can be executed and verified, then talk about "AI agents". You don't have to copy the job titles of big companies, but you can borrow this habit of iterating right up against the problem.
