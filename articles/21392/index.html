Handshake FDE Role Requirements and Interview Preparation

Original title: Handshake FDE 岗位要求与面试准备

FDE Times · 2026-10-24 · 5 min read

Summary

This guide reads Handshake's three FDE job levels as a de facto interview prep checklist. Mid-level roles require 2–4 years' experience with ReactJS/TypeScript and PostgreSQL; the Enterprise AI role centers on designing and running agent evals and iterating until metrics improve; the closed Staff role demands leading ambiguous projects from scratch with client executives. The author advises backing every requirement with real evidence—production deployments, eval pass rates (e.g., 22/40 to 31/40), schemas, and playbooks—and warns against pitching prototypes as production, framing yourself as a consultant, or discussing evals without numbers.

Key points

  • Handshake explicitly states its FDE role is not consulting or implementation.
  • Mid-level roles require 2–4 years' experience, ReactJS/TypeScript, and PostgreSQL skills.
  • Enterprise AI roles center on designing, running, and iterating agent evals.
  • Staff-level roles demand leading ambiguous projects and working with client executives.
  • Common mistakes: dressing up prototypes as production and citing evals without numbers.

The job descriptions for the three levels are best read as interview questions, and every answer should be backed by something you've actually run.

Key Points at a Glance

  • Both the mid-level and Enterprise AI roles require you to personally push solutions into production, not just finish a prototype and hand it off to someone else.
  • The Enterprise AI role puts agent evals at the core: design tests, run them, and iterate repeatedly until the results improve.
  • At the Staff level, the requirement shifts to leading projects from scratch in ambiguous environments and working with clients' VPs or C-level executives.

Open the Forward Deployed Engineer (FDE) job posting at Handshake AI Enterprise, and among a pile of familiar bullet points, there's one sentence that's easy to skim past: this is not an implementation or consulting role. If you're looking to transition into an FDE role, don't skip that sentence. It makes it quite clear what kind of engineer Handshake is looking for.

Handshake is worth studying because it has posted FDE roles at multiple levels, each written with considerable specificity, though the Staff role is now closed. Its AI business grew out of a network of academic experts, which is exactly the resource that top AI labs urgently need.

As a result, FDEs there face two types of clients: the mid-level role serves partner AI labs, while the Enterprise AI role serves enterprise customers.

Reading these job descriptions thoroughly will give you a clear prep checklist, whether or not you apply to Handshake.

The most effective way to read them is to treat every requirement as an interview question you'll encounter, and prepare evidence from things you've actually run to answer it.

Three roles, three rungs of the same ladder

The earlier mid-level FDE role had engineers collaborating with partner AI labs and internal teams, owning the full process from concept to deployment.

According to the summary on the job page, it requires 2–4 years of experience, ReactJS/TypeScript, and a solid understanding of relational databases like PostgreSQL and data modeling. It also requires rapidly building prototypes, testing and fixing them based on direct feedback, and then writing playbooks so the next deployment can be reproduced by following them.

The Enterprise AI role shifts the focus to agents. Engineers work on-site at enterprise customers as small deployment teams, designing and running evals to measure agent performance, then improving step by step until the results genuinely go up.

The job description states plainly that it wants people who have pushed AI applications into production, not just fine-tuned models or built prototypes; it also requires knowledge of agent architecture and how to systematically measure and improve AI.

The Staff role, posted in San Francisco on February 9, 2026 and now closed, added two more layers of requirements. Candidates must have led ambiguous, high-risk projects from scratch with almost no guidance. Experience working directly with client VPs or C-level executives is listed as a plus.

How does "not consulting" change the day-to-day work?

A consultant can write up a plan and hand it to another team to build. An implementation engineer can install a product following an existing script.

So here, communication isn't a peripheral skill. The mid-level role explicitly requires communicating and collaborating well with both technical and non-technical people. When you're sitting inside a client's team, a prototype demo that fails because you didn't explain it clearly costs as much as a bug.

Imagine your first week at an enterprise customer: they want an agent that answers internal questions. A consultant would come back with an architecture document. The FDE as Handshake describes it would come back with a prototype, a set of evals showing what percentage of questions the agent answers correctly, and a plan to push that number higher.

Example: one project, four pieces of evidence

Suppose you built a chatbot at your current company that answers questions about internal documents. On a resume, that line usually reads "built a RAG chatbot using LLMs." It doesn't answer any of the questions in Handshake's job descriptions, because it doesn't say whether the bot actually went live and how well it performed.

Break the same project down against each requirement. The table below shows one way to map them; the numbers are illustrative only, so replace them with your own.

Item in the job descriptionThe real questionEvidence to prepare
Pushing AI into production, not just prototypesAre there real users?User count, uptime, an incident you handled
Designing and running evals, iterating until results improveWhat did you measure with?A benchmark question set, pass rates before and after each change
PostgreSQL and data modelingHow was the data organized?The schema you designed and why you chose it
A playbook for reproducible deploymentsCould someone else rebuild it?A one-page document with a checklist at the end

Evals are the most worthwhile investment, because they turn a claim into a number. Suppose you write 40 questions with reference answers, run the chatbot, and it passes 22. You change how documents are chunked and rerun to get 27; then you add a source-filtering step and reach 31.

That sequence of 22, 27, 31 is exactly what the Enterprise role calls "hill-climbing." Your new resume line could read: "Took an internal chatbot to production; built a 40-question eval set and, over two rounds of improvement, raised the pass rate from 22/40 to 31/40." The interviewer will dig into those two rounds, and you'll already have the story ready.

What order should you prepare in?

Start at your own level. If you have 2–4 years of experience, first solidify the mid-level fundamentals: be able to write a clean React/TypeScript app connected to PostgreSQL, and explain why the schema has the shape it does.

Next come evals, the AI-specific skill the Enterprise role mentions in both the responsibilities and the qualifications. Take any agent or chatbot you've built, write a benchmark question set, run it, record the numbers, change one thing, and run it again. Repeat a few rounds, and you'll have a real measurement story instead of a vague claim of "understanding evals."

Then write a playbook. The mid-level role treats writing documentation for reproducible deployments as part of the job, not an extra burden. A good playbook spells out prerequisites, setup steps, how to verify everything is working, and where things tend to go wrong.

Finally, if you're aiming for a higher level, gather the experiences where you took on a problem no one had defined and drove it all the way to a result. The Staff role doesn't ask whether you're good at executing against requirements; it asks whether you've personally written the requirements amid ambiguity, and which client executives you've persuaded.

Mistakes that sink applications

The most common mistake is dressing up a prototype as production. Handshake says clearly that it isn't looking for people who have only done fine-tuning or experimental versions, so a portfolio stuffed with notebook demos gives the wrong answer on exactly the question that matters most.

The second mistake is introducing yourself as a consultant. Phrases like "solution consulting" or "acting as a bridge" sound nice, but they collide head-on with the line "this is not a consulting role." Tell your story with a builder's verbs: built, shipped, measured, fixed.

The third mistake is talking about evals without numbers. "Experienced with LLM evaluation" proves nothing; a round-by-round sequence of pass rates does. Don't neglect communication either: prepare an example of explaining a technical decision to non-technical people, because the job description puts it on par with PostgreSQL.

This week's exercise

Pick an AI project you've worked on, however small. Write 30 questions with answers, run them once, note the pass rate, change one thing, and run them again. By the end of the week, you'll have two numbers and a reason explaining the gap between them.

Every FDE candidate claims to be detail-oriented and customer-focused. What truly sets you apart from them is these two measured numbers.
