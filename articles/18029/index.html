FDE Client Communication: How to Respond When You Don't Know the Answer

Original title: FDE 客户沟通：不知道答案时怎么回答

FDE Times · 2026-11-14 · 5 min read

Summary

This guide teaches forward deployed engineers how to respond when they don't know an answer in front of clients. It argues that guessing is riskier than admitting uncertainty, since clients can tell when you're improvising. The author proposes a four-sentence structure: state what you know, define a narrow gap, describe your next step, and commit to a realistic date for an answer—illustrated with a Japanese invoice recognition example. Credibility comes from a track record of kept promises, so commit only to what you control, like an answer date rather than a feature delivery date. The article lists common mistakes (gaps too broad, vague promises, polished answers, overuse) and recommends rehearsing the skill and demonstrating it measurably on resumes.

Key points

  • Guessing damages trust more than admitting you don't know.
  • Use four sentences: known facts, narrow gap, next step, answer date.
  • Commit only to what you control, like a reply date.
  • Avoid vague promises, overly broad gaps, and overusing the phrase.
  • Rehearse with a colleague and show the skill measurably on your resume.

Key Takeaways

  • Clients can sense when you're guessing about what the system can do, so guessing is riskier than admitting you don't know yet.
  • A good "I don't know yet" states what you do know, where the gap is, and a date for an answer you're certain you can deliver.
  • Credibility comes from a long track record of keeping your word, so deliver on both the dates and the content you promise.

In a conference room at the client's office, the head of operations asks you: can the agent your team is deploying recognize scanned Japanese invoices as accurately as English ones? Six people turn to look at you, and you don't know the answer.

Those few seconds are the hardest test a newly hired Forward Deployed Engineer (FDE) faces. The instinctive response is often to say "yes" to sound competent, or to throw out a number that sounds plausible. The skill you actually need to practice is saying "I don't know yet, I'll get back to you by Thursday" in a way that raises your credibility rather than lowers it.

Fde.academy points out that experienced FDEs say "let me confirm and get back to you" far more often than new engineers feel comfortable with. In front of a room full of stakeholders, it may seem weak, but it is almost always the more trustworthy answer.

Why does guessing cost you?

The fastest way to lose trust is to focus on yourself. When you're busy worrying about how you look, you stop truly listening to what the person in front of you is saying. Guessing to seem smart serves the speaker, not the client.

Conversely, when clients see that you genuinely care about their problem, they are more willing to trust you. You think you've saved face, but what the client sees is someone who cares more about their own image than about the client's stack of invoices.

Amit Koth, who has written about the technical depth the FDE role requires, puts it even more bluntly: clients know exactly when someone is guessing about what might work versus what actually works. So guessing wrong in front of the people who operate the system every day is never a safe bet.

Replace a guess with four sentences

Honesty alone isn't enough; the answer also has to be useful and point toward a solution. So "I don't know" can't stand alone—it must come with what you already know, what you'll do next, and when you'll return.

Applied to the Japanese invoice example, your answer could go like this:

"We've already tested scanned English and German invoices on real data, and the results have been solid."
"For Japanese, I haven't seen anyone run it on scanned documents like yours, so I don't want to give you a number without evidence."
"I'll run a test on 50 of your sample invoices and check with the model team about the limitations for vertical Japanese text."
"I'll send you the results Thursday morning, along with a recommendation for next steps."

The 50 invoices and the Thursday deadline are just examples; what matters is the structure. The first sentence proves you have real knowledge. The second draws a clear line around what you don't know, narrow and specific. The third shows the client what you're going to do. The fourth is a commitment.

This rhythm isn't unique to FDEs. The Influential PM gives product managers almost identical advice: "I don't know, but I'll find out and get back to you by end of day." Even in financial advisory, practitioners have found that few clients expect their advisor to be an all-knowing encyclopedia.

The commitment matters most

Note the fourth sentence: you're committing to a date for an answer, not a date for delivering a feature. Fde.academy has criticized FDEs who promise clients things before checking with their own team. "I'll get back to you by Thursday" avoids that trap, because you're only committing to something within your own control.

So work backward when setting the date. If the model team usually takes two days to respond, don't promise tomorrow. Thursday is a good commitment only if, even when the results are incomplete, you still have something to report, such as "we ran 50 samples, here are the most common failure cases, and we're still waiting on the model team."

The attached condition is simple: if you promise an answer on time, deliver it on time. Trust is built on character and intent; credibility is built on evidence and a long-accumulated track record. Every commitment you keep adds another line to that record.

From the conference room to Thursday morning

That same afternoon, send a meeting summary email with the Japanese invoice question listed as its own line item, along with the Thursday date. What you said in the meeting becomes a written commitment visible to both sides.

On Tuesday, send a brief message: "Received the 50 samples, running them now; the model team responds today."

Thursday morning, the most important thing is to deliver on the commitment made in the meeting, both the timing and the content. Don't start by apologizing for not knowing sooner; going off to find out and coming back is simply what this job looks like.

Mistakes that make "I don't know" backfire

The first mistake is skipping the first sentence. Without it, the client hears only a gap and starts doubting even the parts you actually know well.

The second is a vague commitment: "Let me look into it and get back to you." No date, no owner, and no way for anyone to follow up.

Worse still is keeping the date but bringing back an answer that's been "polished" to save face: the timing is right but the content is wrong, which just means falling into the guessing trap again three days later.

The last mistake is using the phrase too often. If every question gets pushed to later, clients will wonder why you're there in the first place. The four-sentence script is only persuasive if you can answer most questions on the spot.

Practice before you walk into the room

This skill is hard to use well in front of a client the first time, so rehearse it. Ask a colleague to play the head of operations and fire five questions at you about your product—some you know, some you don't.

For each question you have three seconds to decide: answer on the spot, or use the four-sentence script. Your colleague scores you on four criteria: did you state what you know, was the gap narrow enough, was the next step specific, and was the date realistic.

The most common lost points come from the second sentence. "I'm not sure about Japanese" is still too broad; "I haven't seen anyone run it on scanned vertical Japanese text" is the gap you can actually close before Thursday.

Showcasing this skill to employers

If an interviewer asks whether you've ever been unable to answer a question in front of a client, don't dodge it. Prepare a real story with all four sentences, including the date you set and what happened when you came back.

Don't write "strong client communication skills" on your resume. Write one concrete line: how you tracked open questions owed to clients and answered them on time. Measurable skills are always more credible than adjectives.

The next time you're asked a question you can't answer, don't treat it as an embarrassing moment. It's a chance to make a small commitment and keep it.
