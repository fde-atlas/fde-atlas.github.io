Forward Deployed Engineer vs Consultant: What Actually Differs

A forward deployed engineer writes the production code themselves and is judged on whether the system works. A consultant writes a recommendation and is judged on whether the client liked the deck. That is the whole difference, and almost everything else follows from it.

Both roles show up when a company wants outside help with a hard problem. Both roles can be smart, senior, and expensive. But they are structured to produce different things, and picking the wrong one for the job wastes months.

TL;DR

  • A forward deployed engineer (FDE) embeds inside your team and ships production code, owning the outcome, not just the recommendation.
  • A consultant advises. The deliverable is usually a report, a roadmap, or a set of slides, and the client's own team implements it.
  • FDEs are paid to make something work in production. Consultants are typically paid for time or a project fee, regardless of whether the recommendation gets built.
  • The model comes from Palantir, which embedded roughly 120 forward deployed engineers inside JPMorgan starting in 2009. OpenAI adopted the same structure for its enterprise AI work, growing its FDE team from 2 to 39 people in a single year.
  • Hire an FDE when you need working software inside your stack. Hire a consultant when you need a strategy, an audit, or a second opinion before you commit resources.

What a forward deployed engineer actually does

The clearest description of the difference comes from how OpenAI built its own forward deployed engineering practice. According to a case study on the team's approach, FDEs write production code themselves, embed on-site with the client for weeks at a time, and use evaluation-driven development instead of shipping on faith. The team is kept deliberately small, "not a cast of thousands," and explicitly not optimized around billable headcount the way a traditional consulting engagement is.

That last point matters more than it sounds. A consulting firm's business model often rewards more staffed hours on an account. An FDE model rewards the opposite: the smallest team that can make the thing work, because the team is judged on the outcome, not the hours logged against it.

The FDE method, as practiced at Palantir and OpenAI and codified in detail in Varick Agents' FDE-in-30-Days guide, follows a three-phase loop that repeats:

  • Audit. Learn how the work actually happens, not the documented version of it. Sit with the people doing the job and map every step, exception, and handoff.
  • Evals. Build a golden dataset of real examples with hand-labeled correct outputs, then measure pass rates and failure categories against it. This is how trust gets earned instead of asserted.
  • Deployment. Ship on top of the client's existing systems, start in a sandbox, and increase autonomy gradually while monitoring everything.

A consultant's engagement usually stops before step three. The audit and the recommendation are the deliverable. Someone else, often the client's own engineering team, has to build and run whatever comes next, which is where a lot of consulting recommendations quietly die.

Forward deployed engineer vs consultant, side by side

Forward Deployed Engineer Consultant
Deliverable Working, deployed software A report, roadmap, or recommendation
Outcome ownership Owns whether it works in production Owns the quality of the advice, not the build
Embed duration Weeks to months, on-site or close to it Often shorter, workshop or interview-based
Code ownership Writes and ships production code personally Rarely writes code; may direct others who do
Incentive structure Tied to the outcome, kept small on purpose Often tied to billable hours or project fee
Best fit You need something built and running now You need a second opinion or a plan before committing
Risk if wrong hire Overkill for a problem that just needs advice A polished plan nobody has the capacity to build

Neither role is strictly better. A company that has clear plans but no execution bandwidth needs an FDE. A company that has plenty of engineers but no clarity on what to build needs an outside strategist first. The mistake is buying a consulting engagement when what you actually need is someone to sit down and build the thing, or the reverse: handing a build to someone who has never shipped it before and calling it consulting.

Why the incentive difference matters more than the title

Reports suggest FDE compensation at frontier labs can run well above typical engineering pay, tied to the size of the problems FDEs are trusted with. Whatever the exact number, the structural point holds regardless of who is doing it: when someone is paid based on whether the system works in production, they build differently than someone paid for hours of advice. They write the evaluation set before they write the pitch. They start in a sandbox instead of a slide. They plan the handoff instead of hoping the client's team can execute the recommendation on their own.

That is also the reason "we do FDE-style work" has become a claim worth being skeptical of. Anyone can call an engagement forward deployed. The tell is whether the person actually writes and ships the code, embeds long enough to see it run against real data, and is accountable if it breaks, or whether the engagement quietly reverts to advice with extra steps.

FAQ

Is a forward deployed engineer the same as a consultant?

No. A forward deployed engineer embeds inside the client's team and writes production code, owning whether the system works. A consultant typically delivers advice, a roadmap, or an audit, and someone else builds it.

Is a forward deployed engineer more expensive than a consultant?

It depends on scope and duration, not the title. An FDE engagement is priced against a working outcome; a consulting engagement is often priced against time or a project fee regardless of whether the recommendation ships. Compare scope, not just day rate.

Can a consultant become a forward deployed engineer on the same project?

Yes, if the engagement shifts from advising to actually building and shipping the code, and the person is accountable for the production result rather than just the recommendation. The title matters less than what they are actually on the hook for.

When should I hire a consultant instead of an FDE?

Hire a consultant when you need an outside opinion, a feasibility study, or a roadmap before you commit engineering time. Hire an FDE when the plan is already clear enough and what you need is someone to build and ship it.

Do forward deployed engineers replace an internal engineering team?

No. They typically embed alongside your team, transfer the working system and the knowledge behind it, and hand off ownership once the engagement ends. The goal is your team ending up more capable, not permanently dependent on the embed.
