How FDE teams are evaluated: the time-to-value metric

Original title: FDE 团队怎么考核：价值实现时间指标

FDE Times · 2026-11-18 · 6 min read

Summary

This article argues that hours and project counts are the wrong metrics for FDE teams, proposing time-to-value instead: the time from contract signing to a customer's first real, measurable outcome. Drawing on Waydev, a16z, Rocketlane, and DORA, it contends this metric must shrink with each customer cohort or the team is drifting into a services business. It also recommends tying FDE compensation to post-signature delivery outcomes rather than sales-style quotas, and suggests engineers track their own deployment dates as career evidence.

Key points

  • Hours measure input and projects measure output; neither shows whether customers got value.
  • Time-to-value should run from contract signing to first production use, shrinking per cohort.
  • a16z's engineer-months metric reveals whether connectors and eval templates are actually reused.
  • DORA research shows speed and stability aren't tradeoffs, though it wasn't validated on FDE teams.
  • Compensation should follow post-signature outcomes; paying for signings or hours distorts incentives.

Hours and project counts can't answer that question. Only by tracking time-to-value per customer cohort can you tell whether an FDE team is compounding gains or sliding into a services business.

Key Takeaways

  • Hours measure input; project counts measure output. Neither tells you whether the customer actually received value.
  • Time-to-value must be viewed as a trend: every customer should be faster than the last, otherwise there's no compounding to speak of.
  • FDE compensation should be tied to post-signature outcomes, not copied from sales quota design.

"If the 30th deployment takes as long as the 3rd, nothing is compounding." That line comes from a Waydev analysis of the FDE model published in August 2026. It's the fastest way to tell whether a Forward Deployed Engineer (FDE) team is building a product or simply selling hours.

Waydev also issued a more unsettling warning: an FDE model can look healthy on both revenue and customer satisfaction while quietly turning into a services business. Waydev argues that this problem only shows up in engineering data, which most organizations don't look at.

This matters to every engineer, because the metrics a team chooses determine which parts of the work get recognized. Teams measured on hours reward whoever spends the longest at the customer site; teams measured on project counts reward whoever takes on the most work. Only teams that measure time-to-first-value reward the engineers who make each customer faster than the last.

Hours and Project Counts Measure the Wrong Thing

Software delivery faced this problem long ago. The Accelerate/DORA methodology was designed from the start to measure what actually happens, not inputs like hours worked. Lines of code were likewise excluded, because they are output, not outcomes.

Applied to FDE work, hours are input and projects are output. A team can report a 100% full calendar and 12 projects running live, with not a single customer seeing results in production. Both numbers show the team is busy; neither shows that anyone benefited.

Time-to-value points directly at the outcome: how long it takes the customer to see what they paid for. More importantly, as Waydev puts it, this number must keep shrinking with every customer cohort. If the curve is flat, frontline experience isn't flowing back into the product.

Start the Clock at Signature

Waydev and a16z both set the starting point at contract signature and the endpoint at real results. Rocketlane only specifies the endpoint, the first measurable outcome; DORA in software delivery starts the clock at code commit. The table below compares these four approaches by starting point, endpoint, and the problems each can surface.

In an FDE playbook aimed at professional services leaders, Rocketlane sets the KPI for Deployment Engineers as: first measurable outcome within 90 days. a16z poses a diagnostic question instead: how many engineer-months does it take from contract signature to first production use?

a16z argues that enterprises want to choose AI vendors whose deployment process is predictable and whose time-to-value is fast.

a16z's metric is the most practical because it accounts for both time and headcount. Take a hypothetical example: serving the 3rd customer, two FDEs spent three months, i.e. 6 engineer-months.

If that number is still 6 at the 30th customer, everything is being rebuilt from scratch. If it has dropped to 2, connectors, eval templates, or launch processes are genuinely being reused.

Timesheet reports cannot distinguish between these two scenarios. In both, two engineers are working at full capacity.

Starting the Clock at Signature Changes How the Team Works

Once the clock starts at signature, the first thing to get cut is the weeks spent writing proposals. An FDE playbook published by a practitioner on dev.to argues that a demo running on real data after 2 weeks beats proposal slides that took 6 weeks to produce.

The same author uses production time-to-value to describe FDE economics: 5 months, not 15.

5 months versus 15 months is personal experience, not an industry benchmark. But the direction is clear: connect to real data early, and let customers give feedback on something that runs, not on a document.

Rocketlane adds a habit to keep this metric from being measured once and forgotten. It recommends tracking, for each in-flight project, the value newly delivered within each 30-day window, and comparing it against time invested at the customer site.

A project in its fifth month whose last three windows delivered no new value needs to be re-examined, no matter how satisfied the customer is.

Does Faster Mean Sloppier?

This is the most common objection: reward speed, and engineers will push half-finished work into production. DORA's research on software delivery offers an answer: its studies have repeatedly shown that speed and stability are not a trade-off.

DORA defines lead time for changes as the time a change takes from commit to version control to running in production, which can be seen as the software delivery counterpart of time-to-value.

However, DORA's conclusions come from software delivery teams and have not been specifically validated on FDE teams. Treat it as a well-grounded analogy, not proof.

Quality is safeguarded by the endpoint of the clock. Rocketlane says "measurable outcome" and a16z says "production use," so demos don't count.

If first value is defined as a metric the customer agreed to in advance, such as the number of files automatically processed per day, cutting corners cannot stop the clock any earlier.

Compensation Must Follow the Same Clock

Metrics only carry weight when tied to compensation. Rocketlane advises against designing FDE variable pay the way sales quotas are designed. It argues that such a design optimizes for winning deals rather than delivering outcomes, so bonuses should be tied to post-signature outcome metrics.

Put this together with Waydev's warning and the conclusion is consistent. Teams paid on deal count will look great on revenue; teams paid on hours will look great on utilization. Only teams paid on shrinking time-to-value have the incentive to bring what they learn at customers back into the product.

Turn Your Team's Metrics Into Your Own Advantage

You don't have to wait for your manager to change the KPIs. Record three dates on your current project: contract signature day, first demo on real data, and the customer's first production use. After serving two or three customers, you'll have a dataset of your own showing whether you're getting faster.

That dataset is the most powerful line on a resume. A sentence like "cut time from contract signature to first production use from 10 weeks to 4 weeks by building a shared connector" shows a hiring manager both deployment ability and product thinking. "Worked on 8 projects" only shows you showed up.

When reading job postings or attending interviews, ask how the team measures FDEs. If the answer is billable hours or utilization, you're applying to a services team. If they can tell you how long the most recent customer took to go to production and how that compares to the first customer, that team is building something that compounds.

The most valuable question to ask your own team is simple: Is the 30th customer faster than the 3rd? Teams that can answer with data are building a product; teams that can't are selling time.
