How to Assess Forward Deployed Engineer Skills

FDE Council · 2026-10-25 · 4 min read

Summary

This FDE Council article argues that assessing Forward Deployed Engineer candidates should start from the outcomes a specific role owns, not the job title. It recommends mapping responsibilities to observable competencies, gathering evidence through structured past-work conversations plus bounded work samples, and having interviewers record evidence against a shared rubric before calibrating disagreements. It stresses that the Council's public v0.1 competency framework is a draft reference, not a validated hiring test or certification.

Key points

  • Define role outcomes and boundaries before writing interview questions.
  • Translate competencies into observable evidence tied to the actual job.
  • Combine past-work interviews with realistic, bounded work samples.
  • Use a shared rubric, record evidence first, then calibrate interviewer disagreements.
  • The FDE Council framework v0.1 is an unvalidated draft, not a hiring standard.

In brief: assess a Forward Deployed Engineer against the work your team needs done, not the job title alone. Use a role-specific competency map, ask candidates for evidence across technical delivery and customer work, and have interviewers apply the same observable rubric. The FDE Council framework can help organize that map, but its current v0.1 draft is not a validated hiring test or certification standard.

1. Define the outcomes before writing interview questions

“FDE” can describe different jobs at different employers. Before screening candidates, state what the person will own: for example, turning an unclear customer need into a scoped problem, building and deploying a solution within real technical constraints, and leaving the customer able to operate it.

Make the boundaries explicit. Is the role responsible for production code, customer discovery, adoption, handover, or product feedback? Which responsibilities are shared with product, sales engineering, customer success, or implementation teams? A precise scope helps candidates understand the role and helps interviewers evaluate relevant evidence.

The Council’s role definition describes FDE work across discovery, design, building, deployment, adoption, handover, and feedback. Use it as a starting point, then adapt the scope to the actual job.

2. Map the role to competencies and evidence

Competencies are useful in hiring when they are translated into behaviors an interviewer can observe. The open FDE Council Professional Capability Framework groups the work into 11 core domains. A hiring team can choose the domains relevant to the role and define what strong evidence would look like at its expected level.

Examples of job-relevant evidence for FDE competency areas
Area Evidence to explore
Discovery and problem framing How the candidate clarified an ambiguous request, identified stakeholders and agreed measurable success criteria.
Architecture and integration How they chose an approach that fit existing systems, identity, data and operational constraints.
Engineering and evaluation How they built, tested and improved a solution, including how they identified failures and decided whether it was ready.
Deployment and operations How they handled security, reliability, monitoring, support and production ownership.
Adoption and handover How they enabled users and operators, transferred knowledge and checked that the customer could sustain the solution.
Delivery judgment How they managed scope, communicated trade-offs and recommended stopping or reshaping work when assumptions failed.

These are prompts, not a universal scorecard. Select the areas that matter for the actual role and be clear about what is essential on day one versus learnable after hiring.

3. Gather evidence in more than one way

A single coding exercise or polished project presentation may miss important parts of FDE work. A balanced process can combine a structured conversation about past work with a realistic, bounded work sample. Use the same core questions and evaluation criteria for each candidate applying to the same role.

  • Past work: ask what the candidate personally owned, what constraints they faced, what changed in production and what they would do differently.
  • Problem framing: give a short ambiguous scenario and ask what they would clarify before proposing a solution.
  • Technical judgment: explore architecture choices, integration risks, evaluation, reliability and the reasons behind trade-offs.
  • Customer communication: ask how they would explain a risk, a delay or a change in scope to technical and non-technical stakeholders.
  • Handover: ask how they would verify that the customer can run, support and evolve the delivered system.

Keep work samples proportionate to the role and the time asked of candidates. Explain the task, evaluation criteria and any constraints in advance. Do not ask candidates to provide unpaid production work for a real customer problem.

4. Use a shared rubric, then calibrate it

Interviewers should record evidence before discussing an overall impression. A simple internal rubric might distinguish between no relevant evidence, evidence with substantial guidance, independent performance on a defined problem, and sound judgment in complex or ambiguous situations. Define each level for the specific competency; do not treat this illustrative rubric as equivalent to the Council framework’s proficiency levels.

After interviews, compare the evidence against the agreed role criteria. If interviewers disagree, identify whether they saw different evidence or interpreted the standard differently. Revise unclear questions and rubrics before the next hiring round. Review outcomes over time for patterns that may indicate irrelevant barriers or inconsistent evaluation.

5. Be transparent about what the process can establish

Interviews provide limited evidence. They cannot prove that someone will succeed in every customer environment, and a framework does not replace role-specific judgment. Tell candidates what the job involves, how the interview stages work, and how their information will be used.

The FDE Council framework is a public draft, open for comment, and has not yet been validated through a formal job task analysis. It can help teams discuss the role and identify coverage gaps. It should not be presented as an official hiring threshold, credential, or assessment.

Frequently asked questions

What should an FDE interview test?

It should test the responsibilities of the role: typically problem discovery, engineering judgment, delivery in constrained environments, communication, and whether the candidate can bring a solution through production and handover. The mix depends on what the job actually owns.

Should every FDE role use the same competency rubric?

No. A shared framework can provide common language, but interview criteria should reflect the role’s scope, level, and operating context. Make the expectations explicit and apply them consistently to candidates for that role.

Is the FDE Council framework a hiring test?

No. The framework is an open draft reference. It is not a validated assessment, certification, or required hiring standard.
