After Delivery: Real Usage and Exit

Original title: 交付之后：真正的使用与离场

pafa.github.io · 2026-02-03 · 19 min read

Summary

This chapter examines what happens after an FDE delivery: going live is not the same as being used, delivering business results, or enabling customer autonomy. The author distinguishes four kinds of "done" — handover, adoption, measurable improvement, and independent adaptation — using the Sun Finance project with AWS (four dated milestones, with headcount reduction still only projected) as an anchor. Cases like CoreWeave's use of Cohere North illustrate that adoption should be measured by whether real tasks enter the new workflow. The chapter advocates diagnosing where employees abandon a system, separating improvement causes, gradual handover modeled on Google SRE practices, and isolated rehearsal exercises, while noting that ongoing collaboration can be a legitimate alternative to full exit.

Key points

  • Handover, adoption, results, and autonomy are four distinct "dones" needing separate evidence.
  • Sun Finance's four milestones show stage gaps; headcount savings remain projections.
  • Adoption should be judged by tasks entering the workflow, not logins or user counts.
  • Capability transfer is tested through gradual handover and isolated rehearsals, not training hours.
  • Exit is not the only good outcome; sustained joint engineering can be a rational choice.

On October 9, 2025, a generative AI project for Sun Finance completed its final demonstration. It was built to extract identity document information and assist fraud detection, and was carried out jointly by the fintech company and AWS's Generative AI Innovation Center. Reading this far, it is tempting to mentally draw a line under the project.

A technical article later co-authored by the customer and AWS lists two more dates: a technical handover on November 14, and production go-live on January 22, 2026. Adding the kickoff on August 26, the same project left behind four milestones. Demonstration, handover, and production launch are each different things. 1

This record does not call the collaborating team at the time FDEs, and this chapter will not retroactively assign them that title. What it documents is a joint engineering practice closely related to forward deployment, and, unusually, it does not compress the whole process into a single sentence like "launched in a few weeks." The four dates let us see that between the technical deliverables handed over by the external team and the work the customer must take on next, there is a distance that has to be fully traveled.

That distance deserves its own chapter. The previous chapters covered requirements, data, engineering, and launch validation; what now decides the project's fate is whether the customer will keep handing real tasks to the system, and, as the external engineers gradually step back, whose hands the work will fall into.

One Delivery, Four Kinds of Completion

Handing a system over to a customer can first be understood through four questions.

First, was the thing delivered? Can the receiving party obtain the code, configuration, and runtime environment, and are the agreed features in place? Second, has it entered real work? Are staff using it to handle tasks they would have to complete anyway? Third, has the work improved as a result? What changed in waiting time, rework, service quality, or economic outcomes? Fourth, when circumstances change, can the customer respond appropriately? Do they know how to diagnose, when to stop, whom to contact for support, and can they modify the parts they are responsible for?

These four kinds of completion can happen at different times, and only some may occur at all. A system that is live but that no one wants to use is one state; employees use it daily but spend just as much time double-checking its output is another. The business gains benefits, but every update requires the original engineers is yet another. Giving all of them the same "done" label only makes it impossible to plan the next step.

Sun Finance's four milestones prove that there are indeed gaps between stages, but they do not prove that all the later questions have been resolved. The original article, published on April 30, 2026, states the reduction in manual intervention and staffing needs as projections. The production launch has a definite date, while the operational staffing changes remain expectations. 1 If a reader merges the two into "headcount was saved after go-live," they will end up with an unproven conclusion sitting next to an accurate timeline.

For business leaders, this distinction determines when budgeted benefits can be realized; for technical leaders, it determines whose responsibility the follow-up work is; for FDEs, it affects whether they are actually completing engineering work or underwriting business outcomes that have not yet happened. There is no need to deny the project's value because of this, but different kinds of completion do need different kinds of evidence.

Only When the System Has an Entry Point Can Work Flow Into It

Sustained adoption does not always begin with better training. Sometimes the first thing to change is where employees start their work.

A CoreWeave case study shows such a change. According to a record published by Cohere on February 3, 2026, the collaboration involved its Solutions Architect, and the enterprise AI work platform North was integrated into the customer's existing Slack support workflow. Previously, support engineers had to piece together issue information and route it through a separate support portal. After the change, engineers trigger information gathering in Slack, review and supplement it, and confirm; downstream internal automation then creates the ticket and assembles the relevant engineering teams. 2

Slack here is simply the work software the team uses every day to communicate and handle issues. What deserves attention is not the addition of yet another chatbot, but who still has to make judgments: the customer raises an issue, the support engineer initiates the information gathering; the system provides material, and the engineer decides whether it is accurate and whether anything is still missing; only after confirmation does the issue continue to move forward. The AI takes on a clearly defined slice of preparatory labor, while the original professional responsibility retains its place.

This material explicitly says Solutions Architect, not the FDE title, and it does not publish any operating record from after the external team left. What we borrow it for is to observe how adoption lands on concrete work actions. Placing the system at the existing entry point does indeed reduce the need for employees to start a separate process just to use it; but a connected entry point, on its own, does not prove that employees trust it, let alone that the problem is solved.

Following this distinction, we can ask questions more useful than "how many monthly active users." Of the tasks that need this kind of help, how many enter the new workflow? Of the material the system prepares, how much is directly usable, how much needs editing, and how much is abandoned? After confirmation, does the issue reach the right person to take it over? Are employees finishing work faster, or have they traded scattered time gathering material for concentrated time checking it?

These are the observation angles this book recommends, not a reconstruction of CoreWeave's internal metrics. They are especially useful for roles where task frequency varies widely. An employee who encounters only two unusual issues per month and completes both smoothly may have adopted the tool more fully than someone who opens the system every day without processing any real work. Headcount and open counts look intuitive, but they still need the right denominator.

Adoption also cannot be proven by forcing employees to click. If the old entry point is shut down and people can only pass through the new system, usage rates will naturally rise. At such times, it is more important to watch for workarounds, rework, and requests for help: is anyone redoing the work outside the system, passing unchecked output to the next colleague, or has the old waiting time simply moved into a group chat that is hard to measure? Entering the workflow should mean the issue is properly carried forward, not merely that it passed through a screen.

When Employees Stop Using It, First Find Which Step Got Hard

When adoption falls short of expectations, vaguely saying "the organization resists change" is easy, but it rarely leads to improvement. More helpful for a field engineering team is to take one specific instance of abandonment and take it apart.

Here is a teaching scenario. An equipment service department introduces a repair knowledge assistant. Employees are willing to let it search manuals, but rarely adopt its automatically generated handling recommendations. An observation that followed tasks found that the recommendations themselves were mostly readable; the problem was that employees had to enter the equipment model, repair history, and site photos separately into three different inputs, and the output could not be attached to the work order. When help was needed, preparing the inputs was more troublesome than the familiar manual search.

This situation calls for reducing information shuffling. Producing a batch of prettier training materials does not touch the point where employees give up. Another situation is the exact opposite: all the material arrives automatically, but the recommendation does not indicate which equipment version it applies to. Employees cannot judge whether to follow it and must go back to the manual. In that case, the priority is to restore the basis for judgment, not to keep shortening input time.

A third situation is also possible: the assistant works well, but employees must spend their own performance-measured time helping another department verify material. An action that benefits the overall process becomes, for the individual, an added burden. Only when engineers surface this labor can the process owner allocate it. Otherwise, handing the adoption problem to the technical team will only produce more reminders, pop-ups, and user manuals.

These possibilities show that adoption is an ongoing design judgment. Whenever a certain type of task bypasses the system, it is worth asking: is this workaround compensating for a system defect, or preserving necessary professional judgment? The former should be gradually eliminated; the latter may be exactly part of safe operation. The fact that a flowchart does not draw a manual channel does not mean the channel itself is resistance.

The FDE's value here also needs a defined scope and time limit. Working close to the job helps find blockers quickly, but if every process discussion is intelligible only to the external engineers, the customer still has not gained the ability to improve continuously. Jointly observing one problem takes more time than delivering a report explaining it, but it lets the people taking over also learn how to discover the next problem.

A September 2026 internal transformation review from Microsoft also offers an adjacent observation: after broad tool rollout, adoption hit a plateau, and teams then mapped out what account managers actually do in a week and organized peer learning around job tasks. 3 This is not an explicit FDE program record, nor does it separately prove how much benefit peer learning brought; it makes "getting employees to learn AI" more concrete — first find out where employees need help, then bring improvements into that step.

When work improves, how exactly does it improve

After sustained use, the person in charge still faces a judgment call: does the change come from the system, or from other adjustments made around the system? In explaining the improvement in handling time, the CoreWeave case mentions both internal process improvements and North's automation. 2 This is a valuable restraint. Entry reorganization, clarified responsibilities, and automatic document organization all happened together, and the public materials do not separate their individual contributions.

Project teams need not stop working just because they cannot obtain perfect causal proof, but when deciding on the next investment, they should retain the strongest alternative explanations. For example, the first users may be more experienced and more willing to cooperate; during the pilot there may be dedicated staff watching for anomalies; after launch the business may happen to enter a slow season. Only when the effect carries over to other shifts, ordinary employees, and more complex tasks does the original judgment undergo a new test.

The following numbers are for illustration only. A group of people originally took twenty minutes per task on average, which became fifteen minutes after using the system. If, over the same period, another group dropped from twenty to seventeen minutes after process simplification, all five minutes saved by the first group cannot be credited to AI. Whether the two groups' work is similar enough requires further comparison; the purpose of this small calculation is to separate "there was improvement" from "who caused the improvement."

For businesses where a control group is impractical, you can also keep task records of the same caliber from before and after the change, group them by complexity, and note which conditions changed over the same period. Low-frequency tasks may require longer observation, and a few successes cannot be packaged as stable gains. For high-frequency tasks, watch for long waits beyond the average: whether a small number of anomalies exhausted the labor saved.

Such records should serve decisions. If the improvement mainly came from process simplification, the simpler practices can be extended to teams without AI deployed; if automation is especially effective for a certain type of task, that category can be expanded first; if it only works with an external expert present throughout, maintenance and capability issues should be addressed first. Acknowledging that the sources of benefit are complex can help the project find the next cheapest, most reliable improvement.

Knowing the answer and knowing how to change it are two different capabilities

Capability transfer has become a goal some FDE vendors publicly emphasize. Cohere's FDE authors proposed in August 2026 to run co-building and internal customer mentoring throughout delivery, so customers can continue to run, evaluate, troubleshoot, and extend the system. AWS's FDE announcement also lists internal champions, operational documentation, and autonomous operation capability as delivery goals. 45

The public originals can currently support these capability-building goals, as well as the earlier handover and production dates; the public materials used in this book cannot yet reconstruct the full process of "after a named FDE leaves, the customer independently completes a change." Therefore, this chapter does not treat departure success as a settled conclusion, and instead uses the following exercises to examine what evidence the promises require. Understanding how a system works and having the authority and conditions to fix it are separated by several steps.

On October 28, 2026, Google Cloud provided an example closer to a takeover process, though still with boundaries: it said its FDEs worked with internal engineers at an unnamed financial institution on architecture reviews, pair programming, and voice workflow rehearsals; within weeks, 25 internal developers obtained platform certifications. The vendor said the institution's voice system went into production in late September, built and operated by the customer's engineers. 6

This includes more actual work arrangements than a future training goal, but it is still a one-sided vendor disclosure. The customer is not named, and the materials do not say when external staff withdrew or how the customer independently completed its first change after departure; certification and production operation do not automatically prove long-term autonomous maintenance. Readers can use this to ask more specific questions: which operations did internal engineers actually take over, and when the model or platform changes, which decisions still require vendor involvement?

Engineering practitioner Chris Hughes, in a September 2026 retrospective on FDE work, described an opposite anonymous project: an external team rebuilt the customer's existing proof of concept and arranged for customer engineers to participate by module; the code submitted by the customer still needed substantial refactoring. The two sides each handled different modules, with no cross-team review of each other's work, and only when integrating and comparing overall performance did they assess whether the customer could truly take over independently. Months later, the project sponsor judged the existing team unable to carry it and instead purchased a third-party product. In another piece of work, Hughes recalled a system that ran for years and was then maintained by a partner. 7 Both are one-sided practitioner retrospectives; the customers and project dates are not public; they cannot prove a failure rate, but they show that "the customer maintains it themselves" and "someone takes over operations" are not the same choice.

Still using the explicitly constructed maintenance assistant from earlier as an example. A business expert being able to point out that a manual is outdated does not mean he should be granted permission to modify the running program; an engineer being able to update the document index does not mean he has the authority to decide whether a maintenance rule applies. Effective takeover requires the two kinds of knowledge to converge when needed: the expert judges content, technical staff implement changes, and the person in charge decides whether to open it to users. Each person masters their own segment; only the organization possesses the complete capability.

This distinction prevents a common handover illusion: training one smart, enthusiastic internal contact and then treating him as evidence the whole customer organization has learned. One person knowing how to use it does not mean other shifts do too; one person remembering the principles does not mean someone will catch things when he is on vacation or transfers. Rather than requiring everyone to understand all details, it is better to break key actions down into replaceable positions, then have another person complete one pass following the documentation.

Documentation also serves different purposes. A system diagram for managers answers where money and responsibility are invested; a handling guide for those on duty answers what to look at first right now; a change log for maintainers answers why a special rule cannot be casually deleted. Packing all of them into one massive slide deck is easy to deliver in form, but users may not find the entry point when they need it.

More helpful documentation is organized around specific actions. When a certain type of anomaly appears, first confirm who it affects and whether it is spreading, then judge whether it is a business data change, a connectivity issue, or a model output problem; for parts that can be handled independently, spell out the steps; for parts that must be escalated, list the recipient and the information needed. The person taking over need not memorize the entire system, but should be able to follow a path to the next step.

Reasonable escalation should be preserved here. Customers need not replicate the vendor's foundation model research team, nor should they be asked to fix platforms they have no permission to enter. What needs to stay with the customer is control over its own business scope, permitted changes, risk consequences, and escalation channels. A zero escalation rate is not proof of capability maturity; concealing problems that should be escalated may instead prolong a fault that was originally recoverable.

Slowly handing over the driver's seat

Google's public SRE practices offer a clear takeover approach. SRE is an engineering team responsible for reliable system operation. Its takeover mechanism includes documentation, hands-on training, and the gradual transfer of operational responsibility, changes, and access permissions; during the transition, the original development team continues to provide support. 8 This is Google's internal engineering methodology, not an FDE customer outcome case, but it points to a very practical tension in handover: responsibility must be handed over, yet all help cannot be withdrawn the first time the taker encounters an anomaly.

Applying this to FDE delivery, this book suggests treating "who does the work" as a more direct observation target than training hours. At first, external engineers do it while the customer observes and asks questions; next, both sides complete it together; then the customer executes while external engineers only observe or prompt within an agreed scope. Finally, the external team no longer participates in daily actions but still provides support for product issues beyond the customer's responsibility.

The key to this gradual approach is not rigidly stipulating how many weeks it takes, but choosing the right actions to hand over. For a low-risk internal retrieval tool, updating documentation and identifying outdated answers may matter most; for a system that can modify business records, stopping execution, determining which operations were completed, and restoring a verifiable state may matter more. Rehearsals should be chosen around consequences, rather than fitting every project with the same thick curriculum.

The isolated drill for the maintenance assistant can be filled in like this. The business has decided to retire the old teaching manual V1; the retrieval library needs to reflect this change, and the new manual V2 applies only to the X2 model. The customer maintainer is responsible for updating the retrieval materials, the business expert confirms the scope of applicability, and the release owner decides whether to open it up. The external FDE observes and may only point to existing operating instructions; once the exercise crosses out of the isolated environment, they must intervene immediately. The manuals and models here are synthetic data and provide no real repair instructions.

No real training was held, and the fictional pass was not written up as a customer capability outcome. A reader can have a colleague redo it with the same materials; whether the second person can find the scope of applicability and the stopping point tests true replaceability better than the thickness of the training materials.

Handover also includes transferring permissions and responsibilities. If a team is to be responsible for restoring service but has no permission to view the necessary records or execute the restoration, it has only nominal responsibility; if it holds broad modification permissions but no one confirms the consequences of changes, that is another kind of gap. Permissions need not all be opened at once, but every transferred action should have matching authorization, materials, and support arrangements.

After going live, the world keeps changing

Sun Finance's technical record offers another insight for handover: extending to new documents or languages requires adjusting prompts and validation rules, checking with validation data, and redeploying. 1 Even after the code from the first launch has been fully handed over, new business inputs will still bring the engineering work back.

You do not need to know the internal names of these tools to understand this chain of actions. First describe the new material the system will encounter, then modify how it is understood and checked, validate with material of known answers, and finally send the change to the actual running environment. Merely being able to open the existing system is not enough to complete this chain. Being able to explain errors also does not mean being able to safely release a fix.

The public article does not list who ultimately bears each action long term, so we cannot conclude from it that Sun Finance has fully exited external help. It is enough to remind readers: "scalable" does not only mean the software allows adding a configuration; it also means someone is responsible for deciding when to add it, who validates it, and who handles problems when they arise.

Placing this back in the project's first week, handover is no longer a task that appears only in the final days. The customer can designate future maintainers early and involve them in key trade-offs; jointly record the cause the first time an anomaly is handled; and retain materials that allow re-verification the first time a change is made. Knowledge accumulated this way carries provenance, and the successor knows what specific problem an apparently redundant constraint was meant to solve.

But we must also avoid enshrining all temporary workarounds forever. A shortcut left behind by a rushed effort may have been only a product limitation at the time; once the product improves, keeping it adds maintenance burden. Handover materials should also state why it exists and under what conditions it can be reconsidered. Inheriting the basis for a judgment is more valuable than inheriting only the previous engineer's habits.

Long-term use also changes the shape of costs. During the build phase, development investment is conspicuous; after going live, more attention is needed for the labor of updating materials, troubleshooting errors, reviewing outputs, and supporting new users. Some costs fall on the business team, some on the platform or security team. If only the model bill is recorded, the project may look increasingly cheap while the organization invests more time every day keeping it usable.

This is also why sustained adoption and business outcomes must be viewed separately. Heavy usage means more opportunities to create value, and also more opportunities to expose errors and consume resources. Completed real tasks, quality changes, and maintenance investment should be placed in the same observation window, rather than substituting cumulative call counts for outcomes. How long the window is depends on the task cycle; one lively launch week cannot substitute for long-term judgment.

Watching business results and dependence on individuals at the same time

There is another kind of progress that is easy to misjudge in handover: the business runs ever more smoothly while the original FDE grows ever busier. Looking only at task success rates, one would think the system is maturing; but including the time spent answering questions privately, patching data ad hoc, and making decisions on the customer's behalf may reveal another curve.

This book recommends observing two things simultaneously within the same task scope: whether service quality is maintained, and whether the frequency and time cost of a particular external person having to intervene personally is declining. Only when quality is maintained or improved and personal intervention declines does it come close to the goal of capability transfer. If quality improves while personal intervention also increases, one must distinguish whether business volume is growing, difficult tasks are increasing, or every success still depends on the original engineer's rescue; conclusions cannot be drawn from consultation counts alone.

Building on the manual drill above, also observe whether the customer can independently complete the update, retest, and handover of the release decision under the established permissions. If the original FDE only needs to handle platform defects, the internal team has already taken over daily responsibility; if every time it is he who decides which manual is valid, what was transferred may be only the operational entry point. This is precisely what gives the isolated drill long-term observational value, rather than being a one-time performance on the day training ends.

Departure is not the only good ending

When the business changes quickly and the product is still evolving frequently, keeping a joint engineering relationship may be more cost-effective than full internalization by the customer. A small team may also lack enough workload to support a complete set of maintenance roles. Long-term cooperation can be a business choice made after comparison; the question is whether the customer knows which part of ongoing support they purchased, which capabilities remain in-house, and how they can respond when support changes.

Capabilities can be discussed in three tiers. Can daily work be carried out internally as usual; can small in-scope changes be judged and handled internally; and for changes involving the underlying product or new risks, can the customer clearly articulate requirements and validate the other party's solution. The third tier does not require the customer to write all the code alone, but it does require the customer to be able to tell whether the problem has truly been solved.

This division also makes exit more pragmatic. Exit can mean a resident engineer no longer attends daily meetings, the customer takes over one workflow, or both parties switch to on-demand support. Contracts, technical replacement, and commercial lock-in will be discussed specifically later; here, the first thing to confirm is whether operational responsibility has genuinely moved from one person to another sustainable arrangement.

The customer's first choice is whether to purchase and begin building. The second choice happens far more diffusely: employees decide whether to use it for the next order, maintainers decide whether a small change can be done themselves, and owners decide whether to keep investing or narrow the scope. These choices may not appear at the project celebration, yet they come closer to the system's actual fate than the "delivery complete" date.

Therefore, the FDE can treat departure as a test opportunity rather than a predetermined endpoint. When the external engineer steps back, the customer should still know what the system is doing, where it needs to change, and whom to turn to when something happens. Only then has what was handed over become not just something that runs, but a piece of work that can keep running.
