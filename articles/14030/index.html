Forward-Deployed AI Engineering Services

Galactus · 2026 · 3 min read

Summary

Galactus, founded by Colin Burke in 2018, offers forward-deployed AI engineering services. Its core argument: AI pilots often prove the concept but fail in production because they lack real data, permissions, integrations, evaluation, and a place in users' daily work. Galactus embeds engineers who learn a workflow from the people doing it, then build within the client's existing codebase, tools, and systems rather than replacing them. One small team owns scoping, engineering, integrations, evaluation, and rollout, avoiding the split between strategy firms and implementation teams. First releases typically ship in 4–8 weeks; handover includes production code, versioned evaluation cases, docs, and a runbook, all owned by the client. Pricing ranges from a $5,000 workflow mapping sprint to $75K–$250K for a dedicated team on multi-system work.

Key points

  • Founded by Colin Burke in 2018; sells embedded FDE services, not consulting.
  • Pilots fail without integrations, evals, and real user adoption.

  • First releases go live in 4–8 weeks; clients own all code and docs.
  • Pricing: $5,000 mapping sprint, from $20K per workflow, $75K–$250K dedicated team.

A forward-deployed engineer starts with the work. They follow real cases, find the rules and the odd exceptions, and build with the data, permissions, and systems you already have.

One small team owns scope, product, engineering, integrations, evaluation, and rollout. You don't pay one firm to write a strategy and another to learn the problem again.

The workflow is worth doing and someone owns it. Nobody on your team has time to take it to production.

Staff copy the same details between the CRM, spreadsheets, email, payments, support tools, and internal databases.

The workflow has an owner and a business case. Your product and engineering teams can't step off the core roadmap to build it.

Someone has to approve the big decisions, handle unclear cases, and see why the system did what it did.

Each step removes one risk before we give the system more to do.

  • / 01

    Follow the work

    Walk a real case from start to finish with the people who do it. Note every decision, exception, delay, and handoff.
  • / 02

    Draw the line

    Pick one path. Name the systems it touches, where a person stays in control, and what success looks like.
  • / 03

    Build in your stack

    Work in your repository, connect the real tools early, and test normal, hard, and unsafe cases before launch.
  • / 04

    Launch and hand over

    Release to a small group, fix what they find, document the system, and hand it to your team to run.

It depends on the workflow. A typical handover includes:

  • A workflow map with scope, exclusions, and success measures
  • Production code in your repository
  • Integrations with the systems and data you already use
  • Versioned evaluation cases, deterministic checks, and error handling
  • Review, exception, and escalation paths for your team
  • Monitoring, deployment notes, and a runbook
