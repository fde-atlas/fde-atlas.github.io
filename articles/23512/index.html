Summary

Written by Shohei Tokumitsu, CEO of DeFactory, this Japanese article explains the freelance consulting market for IT engineers. Monthly rates typically run 1–1.5 million yen, above the 600,000–1 million yen range for freelance engineers. The author argues that in IT strategy, DX, and PMO work, hands-on engineering experience—especially requirements definition, estimation, and feasibility judgment—is often valued more than a consulting-firm background. IPA's DX survey (73.7% of companies pursuing DX in FY2023) supports growing demand. Key risks include income gaps and ~45-day payment terms, so 3–6 months of savings is advised. The article stresses that agency layering matters: a 1.5-million-yen project can lose roughly 3.6 million yen per year in take-home pay through subcontracted channels. It recommends agents with transparent margins and direct-client deals, and positions FDE (Forward Deployed Engineer) as a realistic career path bridging engineering and consulting.

  • Freelance consultants earn 1–1.5 million yen monthly, above typical engineer rates.
  • Engineers' practical experience is highly valued in IT strategy, DX, and PMO roles.
  • IPA data shows 73.7% of companies pursued DX in FY2023, boosting consultant demand.
  • Deep subcontracting chains can cost about 3.6 million yen per year in take-home pay.

Do you think "consultants are people who come from consulting firms"?

In fact, hands-on experience as an IT engineer is now one of the most sought-after skills in the freelance consulting market. As consulting projects paying 1 to 1.5 million yen per month continue to increase, transitioning from engineering has become a realistic career option.

In this article, we explain the reality of freelance consulting from an engineer's perspective, covering project rates, required skills, and how to choose an agent.

The jobs of "consultant" and "engineer" may seem completely different at first glance. However, in actual practice, the areas where their skills overlap are rapidly expanding. Let's first clarify the definitions and then look at the key transition points for engineers.

Limited to engineers with 5+ years of practical experience

High-rate, direct-contract project information

A freelance consultant is a professional who is not affiliated with a specific company and contracts directly with clients as an individual to support problem-solving and strategy development.

The basic working style is to handle an entire project alone, from current-state analysis and issue identification to proposing solutions, implementation support, and project management. The fields vary widely, including strategy, business reform, and IT, but in recent years, demand for experts in IT and DX has surged in particular.

Compared to being a company employee, the biggest attractions are "high income" and "flexibility in how you work." Since you can choose your own projects and working hours, it's not uncommon for freelance consultants to adopt a lifestyle of "working full-time for six months and taking the next six months off."

On the other hand, if projects run out, your income drops to zero. There is no fixed salary or employee benefits, and everything from taxes and contracts to health management is your own responsibility. High income comes at the price of being prepared to make every decision yourself.

If you've been thinking, "I'm an engineer, so consulting has nothing to do with me," you might be missing out.

It's true that pure strategy consultants and development engineers have different roles. However, in areas such as IT strategy, DX promotion, PMO, and data utilization, their skill sets overlap significantly.

Comparing the two makes the differences and commonalities clear.

The more experience an engineer has with PMO or upstream processes, the lower the barrier to entering consulting projects. Rather than learning new technologies, honing your "upstream mindset" and "communication skills" is the shortcut to making the transition.

The overlap between "engineers and consultants" has been formalized as a job role in the FDE (Forward Deployed Engineer), which has been rapidly gaining attention in Japan in recent years.

FDEs work for vendor companies with SaaS or AI products while embedding themselves at client sites, handling everything end-to-end from building data platforms to implementing business processes and ensuring operational adoption. "Organizing issues like a consultant, then completing the implementation yourself as an engineer"—this combination is the defining characteristic of an FDE and has become one of the most realistic career paths for freelance engineers transitioning into the consulting domain.

The percentage of companies working on DX reached 73.7% in fiscal 2023 (55.8% in fiscal 2021). This figure, from "DX Trends 2024" by the Information-technology Promotion Agency (IPA), also confirms the rapidly growing demand for outsourcing to consultants with IT knowledge.

Source: IPA "DX Trends 2024"

New consulting projects in areas such as generative AI strategy development and LLM implementation support are also appearing one after another. The market has grown to the point where more than 50 agent services for freelance consultants compete with each other, and the numbers show that "now is the chance" for IT engineers.

The overall volume zone is around ¥1–1.5 million per month. However, at the manager level and above, staying at a major firm and earning compensation including incentives can sometimes result in higher income.

At levels below manager, going freelance tends to increase take-home pay, but the risk of "no project = zero income" is always close at hand.

Monthly rates for freelance engineers vary depending on skills and experience, but ¥600,000–1 million is the mainstream range. For PM- and PL-class engineers, rates can reach ¥1–1.4 million.

Compared to consulting projects, the rate level rises by a step. Especially in IT strategy, DX promotion, and PMO areas, many projects directly value hands-on engineering experience, and because the barrier to crossing over is lower, the rate increase tends to be larger.

For engineers with experience in upstream processes, consulting projects are by no means out of reach.

You may see figures like "¥20 million annual income as a freelance consultant." While this is a realistic number, not everyone can achieve it.

If you can work at least 10 months a year at ¥1.7–1.8 million or more per month, you approach ¥20 million pre-tax. In reality, however, you need to account for gaps between projects and payment terms (e.g., end-of-month closing with payment two months later, meaning funds arrive roughly 45 days after).

A reasonable career plan is to first aim for the manager level (¥1.5–2 million per month) and set an annual income of ¥15–18 million as a realistic landing point.

"What kinds of projects are out there?" is one of the biggest concerns for engineers considering a career crossover. Consulting projects span everything from strategy to IT, and there are more areas where engineering experience proves valuable than you might expect.

Among freelance consulting projects, IT, DX, and PMO areas are in especially high demand.

As companies accelerate their DX initiatives, projects such as "PMO for core system renewal projects," "formulating AI adoption strategies," and "supporting the establishment of data management frameworks" are rapidly increasing. These projects require not only pure business strategy knowledge but also a feel for the realities of IT implementation. This is the area where consultants with engineering backgrounds can shine the most.

The PMO area is particularly rich in project volume, and demand for those with PM/PMO experience remains high for positions managing progress and issues across large-scale projects.

With 3–5 years of hands-on experience as an IT engineer, entering DX or PMO projects is entirely realistic. Experience with requirements definition or coordinating multiple stakeholders will further broaden your options.

Freelancing has an image of being unstable, but for consulting projects, long-term engagements of 6 months to over a year are the norm.

From the client's perspective, external consultants are brought in as specialists with know-how the company lacks internally, so there is little benefit to switching them out on short notice. It is also common to see consultants continue on to the next project with the same client.

The strategy of "secure your first project as a long-term engagement and build your next network of contacts during that time" is a realistic approach to stabilizing income as a freelance consultant.

On the skills side: "what should you hone?" On the risk side: "what should you prepare for?" Honestly understanding both is a prerequisite for a successful career crossover.

There are three essential skills for succeeding as a freelance consultant.

① Specialized skills and practical experience Without specialized knowledge and a track record in the area you offer, you cannot expect to win projects or deliver results. For engineers, the key is whether you can explain, in your own words, the connection between your IT implementation experience and "business challenges."

② Sales ability (self-marketing) As a freelancer, you yourself are the product. Even when using agents, creating a profile that makes clients think "I want to work with this person" and demonstrating strong proposal skills in meetings directly affect your rates.

③ Communication skills Consultants enter client sites as outsiders, so they need the adaptability to earn trust even from people they have just met. For freelancers, "reputation is everything." Being overly picky about projects or repeatedly declining after applying to multiple ones will damage your reputation among agents. Honest, professional conduct underpins a long-term career.

Credentials such as an MBA or Small and Medium Enterprise Consultant certification are useful as trust enhancers, but practical experience and personal networks are what truly matter. What you have solved in the past matters more than whether you hold certifications.

To engineers who feel, "I understand technology, but consulting is a different world," here is something worth saying.

The "requirements definition experience," "ability to see the system as a whole," and "on-the-ground feel for DX promotion" that engineers possess are highly valued in consulting projects. In particular, the "ability to coordinate multiple stakeholders" that PM and PL veterans have is an area where pure strategy consultants often struggle.

What a career crossover requires is not learning new technologies, but acquiring a "strategic thinking framework for upstream processes" and "the ability to translate into business language." Being able to articulate "why we are building this" and "which business challenge this solves" in the client's language will accelerate your shift from engineer to consultant.

📌 Pro Tip The most highly valued engineer skills in consulting engagements are "estimation ability" and "judging feasibility" While pure strategy consultants offer "pie in the sky" ideas, consultants with engineering backgrounds can make technically grounded proposals like "With that spec, you could implement it in 3 months using AWS's XX" or "That would create technical debt and should be avoided." This is precisely the biggest reason clients pay engineer-turned-consultants over 1.5 million yen per month.

One of the things you first notice after going independent is the cash flow problem.

Freelance consultant compensation is often set with payment terms of around 45 days, such as "end-of-month closing, payment two months later." In other words, compensation for work performed in October may not be paid until December. In the first few months after going independent, you can easily run short on cash on hand.

You also have to handle schedule management, health management, tax processing, and contract management all by yourself. Many people only realize after going independent how much the "back office did everything for them" when they were a company employee.

As countermeasures, it is effective to secure 3 to 6 months' worth of living expenses in advance and to choose an agent with short payment terms (payment on the 15th of the following month is about the fastest standard).

Even with strong skills, choosing the wrong agent can significantly reduce your compensation. Correctly understanding "transaction layers" and "margin rates" is the most important factor in maximizing your take-home pay.

"Transaction layers" refers to the structure of how a project order reaches the freelancer.

  • Direct (first-tier): End client → Agent → You
  • Second-tier and beyond: End client → Consulting firm → Agent → You

The more companies in the middle, the more margins each takes, lowering your take-home pay.

Let's look at how take-home pay changes depending on transaction layers, using a 1.5 million yen per month project as an example.

Transaction layer patternMargin rateMonthly take-homeAnnual take-home (12 months)Annual loss
Via BizDev Tech (direct project)Approx. 15%Approx. 1.27 million yenApprox. 15.24 million yen―

Even for the same 1.5 million yen project, deeper transaction layers alone create an annual difference of 3.6 million yen. Just as important as "which project to join" is "which transaction layer to join through."

Freelance agent fees (margin rates) are generally considered to be around 20-30% across the industry. However, since most agents keep their margin rates hidden, it is difficult to judge whether the compensation offered to you is fair.

Source: consulfree.com, "What is the intermediate margin of freelance agents?" https://consulfree.com/blog/freelance-agent-margin/

Choosing an agent with low fees does not automatically guarantee a benefit, but choosing an agent that publishes its margin rate makes it easier to judge whether your share is fair.

When choosing an agent, it is important to look comprehensively not only at the margin rate but also at "the proportion of direct projects," "the speed of payment terms," and "the account manager's understanding of the consulting industry."

Its biggest feature is a lineup of projects focused on direct transactions with end clients and prime vendors. By keeping transaction layers shallow, it achieves a structure that maximizes take-home pay for engineers and consultants.

In addition, the support staff themselves have hands-on experience as PMs and engineers. Because they propose projects after understanding their content, you experience less stress from "not being understood" or "only mismatched projects."
