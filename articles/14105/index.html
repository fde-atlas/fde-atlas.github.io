Forward deployed engineer security clearance: who asks

FDE Interview Prep · 2026-09-25 · 18 min read

Summary

This article explains when FDE job postings require a security clearance and how to read the exact wording. The requirement follows the customer, not the company type: postings serving government, defense and intelligence clients ask for clearances in three ways — active (already held), eligible (obtainable), or preferred. Based on September 2026 postings, Google, AWS and Salesforce demand active TS/SCI (sometimes with polygraph), Databricks asks only for US citizenship and secret-clearance eligibility, while OpenAI, LangChain and Cohere treat clearances as preferences. The piece also decodes US, UK and NATO clearance levels, flags citizenship requirements, and advises copying the clearance line verbatim when saving postings.

Key points

  • Clearance requirements follow the customer, appearing across AI labs, cloud platforms and consultancies alike.
  • Google, AWS and Salesforce postings require an active TS/SCI, some with polygraph.
  • Databricks requires only US citizenship and eligibility for a secret clearance.
  • OpenAI, LangChain and Cohere list clearances as preferred, not required.
  • The status word (active, eligible, preferred) matters more than the clearance level.

The short answer

A security clearance is not a requirement of the FDE job as such. The postings that ask for one are for government, defense and intelligence customers, and they ask in one of three ways: an active clearance you already hold, eligibility or willingness to get one, or a clearance that is only preferred. As of September 2026, Google’s Public Sector FDE postings in Reston list an active TS/SCI as a minimum qualification, while Databricks’ US Public Sector FDE posting asks for US citizenship and eligibility for a secret clearance. Source 1Forward Deployed Engineer IV, Google Public Sector — Google CareersPublisherGoogleSource typecompany job postingSource 2Forward Deployed Engineer, GenAI, Google Public Sector — Google CareersPublisherGoogleSource typecompany job postingSource 3Forward Deployed Engineer (FDE) - Public SectorPublisherDatabricks (careers site / Greenhouse)Source typecompany job posting

So read three things in the clearance line before you apply:

  • Status. Active, obtainable or preferred.
  • Level. Secret, Top Secret, TS/SCI, a polygraph, or in the UK and Europe, SC, DV or NATO.
  • Citizenship. Whose citizen you must be, and whether dual citizenship or residence history matters.

Then, when a recruiter asks “do you hold a clearance?”, answer with facts, not hope. The last section has the words.

Active, eligible or preferred: read the exact words

The status word matters more than the level. Here is how we read each one.

Active, current, must possess. You hold the clearance now. Nothing you do between application and offer changes that, so treat it as a gate. Salesforce, AWS, Accenture Federal Services and Palantir’s Intel posting are written this way. Source 4Forward Deployed Engineer, FDE US Government - Job ID: 10517550PublisherAmazon.jobsSource typecompany job postingSource 6Missionforce - Associate Forward Deployed Engineer (FDE), Public SectorPublisherSalesforce (Workday careers site)Source typecompany job postingSource 7Forward Deployed Software Engineer - Intel (Washington, D.C.) - Palantir on LeverPublisherPalantir Technologies (Lever)Source typecompany job postingSource 8Forward Deployed Engineer - Palantir Foundry (TS Cleared) (AFS, 4708356006)PublisherAccenture Federal Services careers (Greenhouse)Source typecompany job postingSource 9Forward Deployed Engineer - Palantir Foundry (TS Cleared) (AFS, 4708356006)PublisherAccenture Federal Services (Greenhouse job board)Source typecompany job posting

Eligible, ability to obtain, willing to obtain. You don’t need to hold one today, but you must be someone who could be granted one. For a US clearance, that starts with US citizenship, which is why Databricks puts the two side by side: “U.S. citizenship and eligibility for a U.S. government secret clearance are required to access classified information.” Source 3Forward Deployed Engineer (FDE) - Public SectorPublisherDatabricks (careers site / Greenhouse)Source typecompany job posting

Both at once. Some postings accept either. Copy the line, word for word

When you save a posting, paste its clearance sentence into your notes exactly as written. “Active”, “eligible” and “preferred” read alike when you skim a dozen postings, and they lead to different decisions. The levels, in plain terms

You’ll see these abbreviations in the clearance line. Here’s what each one means, in plain terms; the primary sources are linked, and none of this is advice on the vetting process itself.

United States

  • Secret and Top Secret (TS): levels of access to classified information. Top Secret is the higher of the two, and Confidential sits below Secret, as the US executive order on classified national security information sets out.
  • SCI (Sensitive Compartmented Information): not a higher level but controlled access to certain intelligence information, granted on top of a Top Secret clearance. That is why postings write “TS/SCI”.
  • Polygraph: a lie-detector examination some agencies add. A CI (counterintelligence) polygraph covers matters such as espionage and foreign contacts. A full-scope polygraph (FSP, or “Full Scope Poly”) covers the counterintelligence topics and more personal-conduct questions besides.

When Palantir’s Intel posting asks for a “Full Scope Poly level” clearance, it is naming the broadest polygraph. Source 7Forward Deployed Software Engineer - Intel (Washington, D.C.) - Palantir on LeverPublisherPalantir Technologies (Lever)Source typecompany job posting

United Kingdom

  • BPSS (Baseline Personnel Security Standard): the baseline pre-employment check of identity, right to work, employment history and criminal record. The UK government’s guide to vetting levels says it is not a formal security clearance, but it underpins SC and DV.
  • SC (Security Check): for long-term, frequent and uncontrolled access to secret material, or occasional supervised access to top secret, in the words of the same guide. Microsoft’s UK posting names the issuer: the United Kingdom Security Vetting unit (UKSV). Source 15Microsoft Careers: Sr Data Scientist - FDE - Security Check (SC) ClearancePublisherMicrosoftSource typecompany job posting
  • DV (Developed Vetting): for frequent and uncontrolled access to top secret material, per the guide’s DV section. Some posts need an enhanced version above it, eDV.

NATO

A NATO clearance lets you handle NATO classified information. It is granted by your own country’s national security authority, not by NATO. Palantir’s London NATO posting and C3 AI’s London posting for defense customers both ask for one, held or obtainable. Source 13Forward Deployed Software Engineer - NATO (London) - Palantir on LeverPublisherPalantir Technologies (Lever)Source typecompany job postingSource 14Forward Deployed Engineer - Defence | C3 AIPublisherC3 AISource typecompany job posting

How to answer the clearance question

The question can arrive before any call. Palantir’s US Government FDSE application form asks about clearance status, alongside whether you support the posting’s travel percentage and pay range. Source 29Apply: Forward Deployed Software Engineer - US Government (Washington, D.C.) - Palantir on LeverPublisherPalantir Technologies (Lever)Source typecompany job posting Whatever you tick there, say the same thing on the phone.

Here is the structure we use: what you hold, whether it is active, and whether you are eligible. Three facts, no adjectives. Pick the case that is yours.

If you hold an active clearance

“Yes. I hold an active TS/SCI with a CI polygraph, through my current employer. Happy to give your security team whatever details they need.”

Say the level, the status and the polygraph type. Don’t describe the programs or customers you worked on; the level is the answer.

If you held one and it has lapsed

“I held a Secret clearance at my last job, until I left in March. I don’t believe it’s active now. I’m a US citizen and I’m willing to go through the process again.”

Never round a lapsed clearance up to “active”. Give the facts and let the employer’s security team work out the status.

If you have never held one

“I don’t hold a clearance. I’m a US citizen, and I’m willing to go through the process. I saw the posting says ‘ability to obtain’, so I wanted to be clear about where I stand.”

State the facts that bear on eligibility, like citizenship, and your willingness. Don’t promise an outcome: “I can definitely get one” is a claim no candidate can make.

If you can’t meet this posting’s requirement

“I’m not a US citizen, so I can’t meet this role’s requirement. Is there a team doing similar work that doesn’t need one?”

This answer costs you one role and keeps you in the recruiter’s pipeline for others. Hiding it costs you the role later, after everyone has spent time.

Mistakes to avoid

  • Treating “preferred” as a gate. If the line says preferred and you meet the citizenship rule, apply.
  • Treating “active” as negotiable. If it says active and you have none, that posting is not for you yet.
  • Skipping the mission half. We prepare for a second question after the clearance one: how do you feel about working for this customer? Draft one honest sentence that names the mission, for example: “I want my code in front of analysts who make real decisions, and I’m comfortable with the constraints that come with that.” Then pressure-test it with the clearance and mission question.
  • Forgetting travel. Cleared roles can carry heavy travel, so have your number ready. Practice with the travel question.

Before you apply to a cleared FDE role

  • Copy the clearance sentence word for word.
  • Mark it active, eligible or preferred.
  • Note the level, and whether a polygraph is named.
  • Read the citizenship and residency lines, including dual citizenship.
  • Read the location and travel lines.
  • Write your answer in the three-fact structure, and say it out loud once.
