Behind the FDE Boom: How Can Enterprises Turn Individual Capabilities into Organizational Capabilities? _CNR (央广网)

Original title: FDE热潮背后：企业如何将个人能力转化为组织能力？_央广网

Summary

This article examines how enterprises can turn individual FDE experience into organizational capability. It cites Indeed data showing US FDE job postings up over 700% year-over-year, and Maimai data showing Chinese FDE-related postings up over 1,500% from January to July 2026. Full text

As the large-scale adoption of artificial intelligence in enterprises accelerates, the FDE (Forward Deployed Engineer), a new type of core role capable of bridging the gap between technology and business, continues to spark heated discussion and attention. Statistics from the overseas recruitment platform Indeed show that the number of FDE job openings in the US market has grown by more than 700% year over year compared to last year. Data from the Chinese professional community Maimai shows that from January to July 2026, FDE/on-site deployment engineer positions grew by 1522.73% year over year.

Policy signals are also clear. In August this year, the Ministry of Industry and Information Technology issued the "Notice on Launching the Special Action for Cultivating AI Application Service Providers," proposing to cultivate versatile application talent who "understand business, know models, are versed in security, and can deliver," and explicitly encouraging service provider companies to build FDE teams, embed themselves at customer sites, and ensure scenario implementation.

However, the rapid expansion of FDE teams does not mean the challenges of AI adoption across industries will be easily solved. Engineers entering customer sites can help customers complete AI projects, but after project delivery, can the experience accumulated in the field in turn drive product improvements and be reused in more projects? A forecast released by Gartner in September this year pointed out that by 2028, fewer than 20% of FDE collaboration projects are expected to convert customers' recurring needs into core product capabilities. Discussions have also emerged in China's industry about whether FDEs will degenerate into "high-end outsourcing."

Against this backdrop, the recently announced tenth Ram Charan Management Practice Award provides a window into observing Chinese management practices. This year's list of winners has a high "AI content," with many industry leaders sharing their practices and reflections on AI-driven transformation. Among them, an organizational case from a veteran technology company is particularly representative. "Building a New Army for the AI Era with the Dao, Fa, and Shu of AI for Process—Digital China's Path to Reconstructing Organizational Capabilities" won the "2026 Ram Charan Management Practice Award · Excellence Award," and the case was included in the Harvard Business Review Chinese case library. Rather than simply discussing model performance and application results, what makes this case more noteworthy is how the company transformed individual experience from practice into sustainably accumulated organizational capability.

FDEs should never be reduced to a job description

Judging from most companies' current job postings, FDEs are often defined as technical backbone talent who "understand AI technology, can write code, and have strong communication skills." But treating it merely as an individual position to be fought over with high salaries risks overlooking its true value in the AI implementation chain.

The real pain points of enterprise-level AI implementation are concentrated in areas such as vague requirement boundaries, fragmented business knowledge, and the disconnect between technical solutions and actual business. Under the traditional remote development model, technical teams struggle to capture the implicit needs of frontline business scenarios. Engineers need to go deep into the field, build prototypes together with frontline users, and continuously adjust based on actual feedback, so that AI can truly be embedded in business processes rather than remaining at the demo stage.

This "forward deployment, iterate while using" working model is not a "new species." The globally renowned data analytics company Palantir established FDE teams early on; its core platform development team emphasizes "one capability serving multiple customers," while the forward deployment team operates on "one customer invoking multiple capabilities." The two work in coordination, bringing technical capabilities into customers' actual business while feeding problems and experience discovered in the field back into product development. But for this working model to keep running, relying on a few outstanding engineers is clearly not enough. How customer needs are conveyed to product and technical teams, and how field experience is used in subsequent projects, all require coordination among different departments within the company. This also means the value of an FDE lies not just in an individual engineer's personal ability, but extends to the organizational mechanism behind them.

And the arrival of the AI era is further putting engineers' personal abilities and the organizational mechanisms behind them to a sterner test. On one hand, large model technologies are far from truly mature, with capability boundaries constantly shifting through rapid iteration; on the other hand, customers often have not yet figured out their own needs, while simultaneously harboring extremely high expectations and urgent anxiety—fearing they will miss this wave while eager to see quantifiable results. The combination of these two forces has further amplified companies' demands and expectations for FDEs: no longer satisfied with "having one capable person" or "setting up a collaboration process," they now require the entire organization to quickly understand new technologies, accurately translate customer intent, and convert scattered field experience into reusable, accumulated capabilities. In other words, the challenge behind FDEs is no longer just individual combat effectiveness or the refinement of organizational mechanisms; it points to a systematic upgrade of the organization's overall cognition, coordination, and evolutionary capabilities.

Digital China's exploration follows exactly this line of thinking. In its own practice, Digital China has tried to build its FDE team from the perspective of organizational collaboration. People from different backgrounds—sales, solutions, technology, delivery—can all become part of the FDE team; the key is to form a shared language, a shared technology platform, and a shared way of working.

"We may not be AI experts in front of customers, and we are certainly not experts in our customers' industries, but Digital China's FDEs are definitely process experts in AI for Process." Guo Wei, Chairman of Digital China, once emphasized in an internal speech that the unique value of FDEs lies in a systematic method for precisely aligning AI capabilities with customers' business processes. Such capabilities are difficult to acquire solely through external recruitment; they must be continuously accumulated at customer sites and through project practice.

The Puzzle of Scale: From "Headcount Expansion" to "Capability Diffusion"

If the FDE represents a working mechanism and organizational effectiveness that connects customer sites, technical development, and product improvement, then building it at scale involves at least two layers: the first is scaling people—through recruitment and training, enabling more people to master on-site AI deployment capabilities; the second is scaling capabilities—allowing the experience one person accumulates through projects to be preserved and passed down, internalized as the organization's underlying capabilities and common standards.

For companies, adding headcount is relatively easy; the hard part is turning the working methods that a few key people developed at customer sites into capabilities that more teams can master.

Since 2025, Guo Wei has been pondering this question. Through customer visits, internal and external exchanges, and internal organizational discussions, Digital China drew on the "small-station training" approach: first train a team with new methods and new standards, then gradually roll it out to the larger organization.

In 2026, Digital China launched FDE-themed workshops, with participants from different roles including marketing, pre-sales, delivery, product, and R&D, all of whom had frontline experience with AI projects.

Unlike typical technical training, the workshops did not rely on external instructors or generic textbooks; instead, real customer projects served as the teaching content. Participants not only had to explain what technologies were used and what features were developed, but also reconstruct how the project was advanced: how requirements were initially discovered, what obstacles were encountered, why the solution was adjusted, and how resources were coordinated to drive implementation. Which practices were worth learning from, and which lessons must not be repeated, were equally key points of the review.

Beyond case discussions, Digital China also introduced the "hexagon maturity model," originally used for evaluating leadership among cadres, into FDE development, observing the team's organizational capabilities across six dimensions: strategic management, shared beliefs, consensus alignment, incentive mechanisms, team building, and self-learning. It looks not only at whether technical development can be completed, but also at whether one can judge business value, coordinate across departments, and keep learning through project practice.

However, establishing training and evaluation systems does not mean organizational capability has already formed. Whether new teams can use existing methods to solve problems and reduce dependence on a few key individuals must ultimately be tested in subsequent projects. This is the key step for FDE development to move from headcount growth to capability diffusion.

After Project Delivery: Turning Judgment into Organizational Assets

After a project is delivered and the contract payment is successfully collected, where do the truly valuable lessons from the project process go? When facing new customers and new projects again, can these lessons still be drawn upon and applied? For companies, if every project depends on frontline engineers figuring things out from scratch, existing successful experience can hardly be converted into sustained delivery capability. But accumulating practical experience cannot be solved simply by writing a few more documents. The most valuable information is often scattered in the details of requirement discussions, the process of solution revisions, and records of repeated trial and error—and such tacit experience is difficult to fully convey through standardized documentation.

The development of generative AI has created new conditions for organizing this scattered information. McKinsey's Lilli platform consolidates consulting knowledge and project materials accumulated over many years, helping employees find and use existing experience. The platform's adoption has also prompted employees to rethink how knowledge should be recorded and organized so that it can be put to use by those who come later.

Digital China's weekly report system offers another angle of observation. According to Guo Wei, since rolling out weekly reports in 2017, Digital China has accumulated about 2.172 million records covering client visits, project progress, technical breakthroughs, and business reviews. Unlike assessment metrics that focus only on work outcomes, these weekly reports also capture how things actually got done: how client needs were judged, how adjustments were made when encountering resistance, and how different teams were coordinated.

After integrating AI capabilities, this process data accumulated over many years is beginning to be explored for assisting talent identification and cross-project experience reuse, opening up new possibilities for organizing experience. Digital China has started trying to use this long-accumulated unstructured information to conduct correlation analysis on project experience, work behaviors, and professional expertise.

In building FDE capabilities, these materials can first provide richer observational evidence for talent development. Beyond looking at how many projects someone has completed, one can also draw on their long-term work records to understand their performance in requirements judgment, client communication, cross-departmental coordination, and continuous learning.

However, being recorded does not mean it can be directly replicated. Different clients have different conditions, and existing methods still need to be tested and adjusted in new projects. For Digital China, weekly reports provide the raw material, project reviews distill the methods, and the Digital China Wenxue platform supports knowledge accumulation and reuse. Only by connecting these links can individual experience have the chance to become a capability the organization can use sustainably.

Testing the Effectiveness of FDE Development with the Next Team

Returning to the question at the start of this article: after a complex AI project is fully completed, what does the enterprise actually retain? Beyond the delivered results, what matters more is whether the judgments, methods, and experience from the project can be used by subsequent teams.

This also means that today's discussion around FDEs, while on the surface about how to cultivate and scale a new role, is at a deeper level really about: in the AI era of rapid technological iteration and constantly changing client needs, can enterprises establish an organizational mechanism for continuous learning and rapid evolution?

In this sense, the FDE may merely be the first visible entry point in this round of organizational transformation. The enterprises that will truly build differentiated advantages in the future may not be those with the most FDEs or the most resources invested, but rather those able to establish a complete closed loop: letting the front line continuously generate experience, letting the organization identify and distill that experience, letting the technology platform carry that experience, and then letting the next team build on the existing accumulation to keep moving forward.

The case for which Digital China won the Ram Charan Management Practice Award is valuable to observe precisely for this reason. It does not treat the FDE merely as a new role, but instead attempts to connect talent development, project reviews, knowledge accumulation, organizational evaluation, and AI tools, exploring how to let frontline experience flow and be reused continuously within the organization. Whether this mechanism can ultimately translate into a stable, scalable, and replicable business capability still needs to be tested by more projects over a longer period, but it has at least raised a direction worth continued discussion across the industry.
