AI implementation: in-house or external FDE? Criteria to check before the approval meeting | Rubber Mari

Summary

This article, aimed at information systems, corporate planning, and DX staff preparing internal proposals, argues that choosing between in-house AI implementation and external FDEs is not about which is better but about company conditions. It proposes three decision axes: deadline for the first measurable result, ability to hire or train implementation talent, and the number and continuity of target workflows. It notes FDE hiring is highly competitive, with Japan's market only forming as a category in 2026. In-house suits large organizations with continuous AI work; outsourcing suits those needing fast results on a few workflows. The author recommends a hybrid: external FDEs deliver initial implementations plus a documented methodology, then internal staff take over operations and expansion, with self-sufficiency written into delivery requirements.

Key points

  • The build-vs-buy decision rests on three axes: timeline, talent, and workflow volume.
  • FDE hiring is intensely competitive; in-house means competing for talent or training novices.
  • Outsourcing fits when few workflows exist and fast measurable results are needed.
  • The recommended hybrid starts external, then internal teams take over using documented methods.
  • Delivery requirements should include mechanisms and documentation enabling internal self-sufficiency.

Should you develop in-house talent to implement AI into business operations, or turn to an external FDE (Forward Deployed Engineer)? From the standpoint of someone who works as an FDE and does the implementation, this article organizes the realities of the hiring market, the conditions under which in-house or external approaches each fit, and the design of a hybrid that combines both. It can be used as material for approval decisions.

Whether to implement AI in-house or outsource it is determined not by "which is better" but by your company's own conditions. There are three decision axes: time (by when do you need the first results), talent (can you hire and develop implementation talent), and the nature of the target operations (continuous and repetitive, or a one-time pass). This article organizes the decision criteria across these three axes and the design of the hybrid most common in practice: "start externally, then bring it in-house." The intended readers are information systems, corporate planning, and DX promotion staff who will put an AI implementation structure through an approval process.

Premise: Hiring implementation talent is now the most competitive

The role of implementing AI into business operations and running it through to results is called an FDE (Forward Deployed Engineer) (What is an FDE). This job category is expanding hiring at major overseas AI companies, and in Japan it has only just begun to form as a category in 2026, so hiring experienced people means competing with the majors (Japan FDE Market Map 2026). Choosing "in-house" effectively means either "joining this hiring competition" or "developing people with no prior experience." If you choose in-house without factoring this premise into your approval, the plan will stall at the stage of building the structure.

Conditions where in-house fits

In-house is rational when all of the following conditions are met.

  • The target operations for AI implementation are on a scale where new ones will continue to emerge going forward
  • You have a track record in engineer hiring and can make yourself attractive to candidates
  • And you can tolerate the time needed to build the structure before the first results

For a large organization planning to make AI utilization a permanent function, in-house is the right investment.

Developing people from scratch is also an option. As implementation patterns have become established, the FDE is becoming a role that can be trained. Our own company publishes hiring premised on training. However, training requires experienced people who can teach the patterns, and real business operations in-house to serve as practice grounds.

Conditions where external fits

External is rational under the opposite conditions.

  • First, you want to produce tangible results quickly in one operation
  • You cannot spare the time to hire and develop implementation talent
  • The target operations are still only a few, not enough volume to justify dedicated staff

In this case, bringing in an external FDE who has established patterns shortens the time to the first measured results.

When choosing external, the "criteria for calling oneself an FDE" written in the FDE Market Map can be used as-is. The key points are: whether they can show, with actual measurements, the operations they implemented and the time saved, and whether they leave behind a form (mechanisms, tools, procedures) that lets the front line run on its own after delivery.

Outsourcing that leaves only work reports stops entirely the moment the contract ends.

Practical answer: Start externally, then bring it in-house

The most common approach in practice, with the fewest failures, is the hybrid. The design has two phases. In the first phase, an external FDE implements the first few operations and leaves behind tangible results and implementation patterns (how to break down operations, verification procedures, and the form of operations). In the second phase, in-house staff use those patterns as teaching materials to take over operations and horizontal rollout, while the external party shifts to reviewing the difficult parts.

The key point of this design is to include "articulating the patterns" in the first-phase deliverables. If you receive only a working mechanism, you will end up learning from zero at the handover stage. Conversely, whether or not you include "a state where in-house staff can run on their own" in the requirements definition when outsourcing is what first determines the success or failure of internalization.

Next step

Before the approval meeting, write down answers to three questions: "By when do you need the first results?", "Can you hire or develop implementation talent?", and "How many target operations are there?" That will settle most of the direction.

How to structure the approval document itself is organized in How to Write an Approval Document for AI Agent Adoption. If you want to start externally, please consult us via AI Implementation FDE.

FAQ

Frequently Asked Questions

If we outsource AI implementation, won't the know-how fail to stay within our company?

It depends on how the contract is designed. Include in the deliverable requirements not only the system itself but also documented formats for breaking down, validating, and operating the business processes. We define delivery as reaching a state where the client can operate independently.

For AI implementation, which is cheaper: in-house development or outsourcing?

It depends on the number of target business processes. For a few processes, outsourcing is cheaper; if you run dozens of processes on an ongoing basis, the fixed costs of in-house development can be recovered. Since the break-even point depends on your estimate of your own workload, the safe sequence is to first outsource one process and measure actual results, then use those figures to make the investment decision on in-house development.

What should be outsourced first for AI implementation?

Business processes where data has already been accumulated and the judgment criteria can be articulated. The selection method is organized in Why AI adoption ends with "zero results".
