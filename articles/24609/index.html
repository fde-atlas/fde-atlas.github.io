Avoiding Failure in Requirements Definition: 5 Things FDEs Actually Do

Original title: 要件定義で失敗しない｜FDEがやっている5つのこと

日本Fde協会 · 2026-07-18 · 7 min read

Summary

The head of the Japan FDE Association argues that requirements definition fails when teams chase a perfect document, since customers usually cannot articulate what they truly need. Drawing on a ¥100 million project with 8 developers and 20+ stakeholders, he contrasts traditional waterfall-style definition with the FDE approach of building prototypes alongside customers, and details five practices: observing the workplace first, using prototypes to elicit honest feedback, narrowing scope aggressively, designing for change, and declaring completion jointly with the customer.

Key points

  • Perfect requirements documents are a fantasy; customers often don't know what they need until they see something working.
  • FDEs start by sitting beside users and observing operations, not by filling in document templates.
  • A same-day no-code prototype once locked in about 80% of requirements on the spot.
  • Asking "would work stop without this feature?" cut scope to 40% and hit a 180% profit target.
  • Completion is confirmed by customers using the prototype, not by stamping a document.

The FDE approach to requirements definition is fundamentally different from the traditional waterfall model. Rather than "creating a perfect requirements document before starting development," it's about "defining requirements together with the customer while showing them something that works." As the representative director of the Japan FDE Association, I am convinced from my experience organizing requirements for 8 developers and over 20 stakeholders on a 100-million-yen system project that the most dangerous thing in requirements definition is "spending time aiming for perfection."

This article explains the 5 techniques FDEs practice in the field. By the time you finish reading, you should be able to put into practice concrete approaches for preventing misalignment with customers. For an overview of the skills required of FDEs, see "5 Essential Skills for FDEs."

Why Requirements Definition Fails

The Illusion of the "Perfect Requirements Document"

The biggest cause of failure in traditional requirements definition lies in the very assumption that "if the requirements definition is perfect, development will succeed." In reality, however, in most cases customers themselves don't accurately know what they want to build. A requirements document written in words alone cannot align the mental images of the customer and the engineers.

A manufacturing client once asked us to "overhaul our inventory management system." We could have built the system exactly as specified in the requirements document. But when we walked the shop floor, we found the real problem wasn't inventory management—it was that the warehouse, sales, and accounting departments were each looking at separate data. A requirements document accurately records "what the customer said," but it doesn't contain "what the customer actually needs."

In a nutshell, the traditional approach is "write first, then build," while the FDE approach is "build while deciding."

5 Things FDEs Practice in Requirements Definition

1. Enter the shop floor without waiting for a requirements document

FDE requirements definition doesn't start with writing a requirements document. It starts by going to the customer's shop floor, sitting next to the staff, and observing their work. At one small-to-mid-sized company, the accounting staff spent two hours every morning manually transferring data from three systems into Excel. They call it "just my usual work," but from the outside it's clearly a process that should be automated. No matter how much you fill in a requirements document template, this kind of issue will never surface.

2. Use prototypes to draw out "the customer's real thoughts"

FDEs use prototypes, not documents, to align on requirements. When you show them something that works, customers start speaking concretely: "I want this changed like this," "We don't need this feature." Meetings with words alone tend to become abstract battles, but in front of something that works, everyone speaks their mind.

On a SaaS company project, we organized the issues in a morning interview and built a simple no-code version to show them that same afternoon. The moment they asked, "Can we start using this tomorrow?" about 80% of the requirements were locked in on the spot.

3. Narrow the scope by asking "Do you really need that?"

When I served as PM on a 100-million-yen system project, over 20 stakeholders each raised different requests. Implementing everything would have exceeded both budget and timeline. I went around to each department asking, "If this feature didn't exist, would your work come to a stop?" Keeping only what they answered "yes" to as mandatory requirements narrowed the development scope to 40% of the original, and we achieved a profit target of 180%. The skill of "subtracting" requirements rather than "adding" them is what determines success or failure in requirements definition.

4. Design with change as a given

We enter design on the assumption that requirements will change. From the very start, we consciously aim for "a structure that minimizes the impact of changes when they come." In AI product development, it was standard practice to build the first prototype as a Python script of a few dozen lines, get customer feedback, and then rewrite the production code from scratch. Clinging to the first code ends up costing more in the long run.

5. Declare "completion" of requirements definition together with the customer

In the traditional approach, requirements definition is marked "complete" once the customer stamps the document, but in the FDE approach, you have the customer try the prototype and obtain verbal agreement that "we can proceed in this direction." What matters is not how polished the document is, but whether the direction matches "what the customer truly wants." Getting them to touch something that works and say "this is fine" is far more reliable agreement than a stamp on a document.

What FDEs Can Do When Companies Struggle with Requirements Definition

"Our requirements definition isn't going well" is one of the most common topics of consultation from companies that have outsourced to SIers. Issues such as misalignment surfacing after development starts despite a requirements document being created, scope and costs ballooning with every change, or being unable to articulate requirements in the first place—FDEs address these by embedding themselves in the customer's shop floor and taking an approach of "finding and defining requirements together."

From my experience building an organization from zero to 60 people at a startup, I have seen firsthand that "moving small and getting feedback" ultimately delivers both better speed and better quality than "making a perfect plan before acting." The same applies to requirements definition. For more on how companies can leverage FDEs, also see the "Guide to FDE Utilization for Companies."

Frequently Asked Questions

Q. Can FDE-style requirements definition be used in waterfall projects?

A. Yes. Using prototypes within the waterfall requirements definition phase to improve the accuracy of the documents is effective. If you build consensus with the combination of "requirements document + working prototype," you can greatly reduce the risk of rework in later stages.

Q. Won't showing a prototype lead to the misunderstanding that "this is the finished product, right?"

A. This is a common concern, but it can be resolved simply by saying in advance, "This is a prototype for confirming direction." In fact, showing something rough reassures people that "it can still be changed," making them more willing to share their opinions.

Q. What is the approximate cost of requesting requirements definition from an FDE?

A. It depends on the scale of the issue, but the requirements definition phase—from field observation to prototype—typically takes a few weeks to a month, with costs ranging from several hundred thousand to several million yen. Since the direction can be solidified before entering large-scale system development, it helps reduce total development costs.
