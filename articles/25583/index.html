What Is an AI FDE? An Article to Help You Understand (With Learning Resources)

Original title: AI FDE是什么？一篇文章带你了解（附学习资源）

腾讯新闻 · 2026-09-17 · 9 min read

Summary

Drawing on a talk by Kevin Bai, an Anthropic engineer who previously built FDE teams at Palantir and Rippling, the author explains what Forward Deployed Engineers are, when they are needed, and what they earn. FDEs are customer-facing software engineers suited to selling technically complex products to non-technical buyers. With AI making software highly customizable, OpenAI, Anthropic, and Amazon are investing heavily in the role. The author argues the trend is real but warns that lasting value lies in feeding field learnings back into the platform, not just on-site service.

Key points

  • FDEs are software engineers trusted to face customers directly, a model originating from Palantir.
  • FDEs are only needed when selling technically complex products to non-technical buyers.
  • Palantir's $4M average contract value far exceeds typical SaaS peers.
  • US FDE pay averages around $200K total; Chinese big tech offers roughly RMB 600K–1M.
  • Gartner predicts 70% of enterprises will abandon purchased agentic AI by 2028.

Full text

Hello friends, I'm Simonlin, using plain and simple language to walk you through AI step by step and boost your efficiency 10x!

Recently I watched a 17-minute talk by Kevin Bai, an engineer at Anthropic who spent many years as an FDE (Forward Deployed Engineer, an engineer role stationed on-site at customers—more on that below) at Palantir, then went to Rippling to build an FDE team from scratch, growing it from 1 person to 25 in a year.

The talk is called Forward Deployed Engineering 101, and it's probably the clearest introductory lesson on this role out there right now.

After watching the talk, I dug through official materials from Anthropic, Palantir, and OpenAI, plus reports from several media outlets, and that's how this article came together~

In this article, I want to tell you: what an FDE is, what an AI FDE is, what they actually do, how much they earn, and some of my own judgments.

We've prepared a transcript of the talk—grab it at the end of the article.

01 PART

What is an FDE: Definition

VIEW / Scroll down for my take

FDE stands for Forward Deployed Engineering, usually translated into Chinese as Forward Deployed Engineer.

Kevin's definition is just one sentence: "An FDE is simply a customer-facing software engineer." You would hire them into your team as a software engineer, and at the same time, you'd dare to put them in front of customers.

It sounds unremarkable, but the origin of this term is anything but simple. Back when Palantir was selling its data platform Foundry, it hit a dead end: what customers were buying was neither software nor people's time, but an outcome. An oil boss cares about pipelines, not data pipelines; a consumer goods company cares about getting more shelf space. How the data is organized is an implementation detail—customers don't care, and they shouldn't have to.

So Palantir's solution was to send engineers straight to the front line, understand the essence of the customer's business, and then build the solution on the platform. What the customer receives is that outcome.

In startup lingo, FDE is scaling the company's early "design partners" to enterprise level: I don't know what the product should look like, you don't know what to buy, so let's build it together.

This definition now has official backing.

Anthropic's FDE job posting says: embed directly with the most important customers, build production-grade applications with Claude, deliver technical artifacts like MCP servers (a standard interface for connecting AI models to external tools and data), subagents, and agent skills (pre-installed skill packs for AI), then feed reusable patterns back to the product and engineering teams.

Palantir's official blog puts it even more bluntly: a traditional engineer builds one feature for many customers, while an FDE unlocks many features for one customer.

02 PART

When do you actually need an FDE

VIEW / Scroll down for my take

Kevin offered a 2x2 decision matrix with two axes: what you're selling, and who's buying from you.

If you sell a highly technical product, like GitHub or Datadog, the buyer is the CTO (Chief Technology Officer) and the users are engineers—they can absorb the complexity themselves. If you sell something not very complex, like Jira or Slack, it doesn't matter if the buyer isn't technical—the tool is configurable.

There's only one situation that calls for an FDE: selling something highly technical to a non-technical buyer.

Kevin specifically emphasized: before setting up an FDE function, ask yourself—is it a "need" or a "want"? It's too easy to want whatever is trendy, too easy to want AI. If your business doesn't have that scenario of "having to sell complex things to non-technical buyers," don't force it.

03 PART

What is an AI FDE: the brand-new 2026 version

VIEW / Scroll down for my take

Palantir was doing this back in 2004 and 2005—so why did this term suddenly blow up in 2026?

Kevin's personal hypothesis: it's not because the world suddenly discovered Palantir's model works well, but because the way the software industry does business has changed.

AI has made writing code and building complex, customizable software extremely easy. Now almost every platform is agentic (meaning AI can do work on its own, not just answer questions) and customizable. The direct consequence: almost everyone building products will run into customers who have no idea what you actually do.

What's happening outside confirms this judgment.

Anthropic's FDE team has embedded directly into FIS, one of the world's largest fintech companies, co-building a financial crime AI agent (an AI program that can autonomously execute tasks) with them, aiming to compress anti-money-laundering investigations from days to minutes, with BMO (Bank of Montreal) and Amalgamated Bank as the first adopters. OpenAI has gone even further: it set up a separate company for deployment, and its acquisition of Tomoro brought in roughly 150 FDEs at once, with an initial investment of over $4 billion.

A company that builds models spending huge sums to maintain a team of engineers stationed on-site at customers—that fact alone is the answer.

Just how lucrative is this business? Kevin cited a set of numbers in his talk.

Palantir's ACV (average contract value, the average amount a single customer pays per year) is $4 million. Second place is ServiceNow at $1.2 million; third is Workday at $600,000. Meanwhile, in the entire US stock market, no publicly listed SaaS (subscription-based software business) company collects even $500,000 a year from a single customer.

This isn't a lead anymore—it's a gap of orders of magnitude.

What's even more striking: achieving these numbers, Palantir's entire company is only a few thousand people.

04 PART

What an FDE does: the workflow

VIEW / Scroll down for my take

Based on Kevin's talk and public sharing from practitioners, I've put together an FDE work chain:

Step one, "drill" into the customer's business. Former Palantir executive Vinoo Ganesh put it vividly: an FDE's job is to collect the customer's nouns and verbs—what words they use for warehouse, what words they use for approval. These nouns and verbs are the ontology of the business.

Step two, assemble on the platform's shared primitives (primitives are pre-built generic building blocks on the platform—data connectors, permission models, workflow templates and the like—which engineers can use directly for assembly instead of building from scratch every time). This is the most critical part of the whole model: an FDE never writes software from scratch. If every FDE builds from scratch, in Kevin's words, then what you have isn't an FDE function—it's an outsourced dev shop—maintenance costs will eat the P&L alive, assuming the engineers don't resign en masse first.

Step three, deliver the outcome, then watch changes in layers. Things customized for a specific customer stay with that customer; anything that can be generalized should, over time, be generalized back into the platform, becoming tomorrow's shared primitives.

Step four, feed intelligence back. Vinoo Ganesh has a line worth copying down on its own: an FDE function that only solves on-site problems and never sends signals back to the product team is just "a consulting team with a fancier title." FDEs are on the front line, and their real value is discovering what should be added to the platform next.

He once gave a very detailed example. A customer's CSV (the most common tabular data file) to Parquet (an efficient storage format used in big data scenarios) migration had been stuck for a long time by a data quality engineer. After observing on-site, the FDE found she had been double-clicking CSV files and eyeballing the data, so he built her a Parquet viewer overnight. Two days later the migration was approved, and the entire pipeline went from 17 hours down to 2 hours.

This is what an FDE's daily work looks like: sitting next to the customer, seeing clearly where the problem is actually stuck.

05 PART

Real-World Cases

VIEW / Scroll down for my take

Is there really a market for FDEs? Or is it just another illusory bubble? I've put together a few cases for you to look at.

Anthropic × FIS (finance): mentioned earlier. FDEs embedded at FIS to jointly build a financial crime AI agent, with BMO and Amalgamated Bank as the first deployments, opening up in the second half of 2026. What's interesting is the business model—the two banks don't pay Anthropic's FDE fees directly at consulting rates; instead, FIS absorbs the investment and spreads it across its entire banking customer base.

OpenAI × Deployment Company: OpenAI has set up an independent deployment company. Tomoro merged in, bringing about 150 FDEs, with TPG (a major US private equity firm) leading 19 investment institutions and consulting firms into the venture, with an initial investment of over $4 billion. The client list includes companies like Tesco and Virgin Atlantic with previously delivered business.

Cognition × Nubank (Latin American fintech): Cognition is the company behind the AI programmer Devin, and it maintains an FDE team embedded at customer sites. Their public case is Nubank—Latin America's largest digital bank with 110 million users—migrating an old ETL system that had been running for 8 years with millions of lines of code. Work originally estimated to take a year and a half with over a thousand engineers sharing the load was compressed by Devin plus the on-site team to a scale of 2 months. The official page reports an 8x improvement in engineering efficiency and 20x cost savings.

What about the situation in China? Let's look at what the big companies are doing.

ByteDance's Volcano Engine is recruiting a "Doubao AI Large Model FDE," with responsibilities explicitly including being on-site for industry-leading customers and identifying core problems in customer workflows, offering a monthly salary of 35,000–70,000 RMB with 15 months' pay. Tencent Cloud is also hiring "Forward Deployed Engineers" through social recruitment. Ant Digital Technologies has written FDEs into its ecosystem partnership news, saying these roles should "go to the production frontline." In other words, China is currently in the stage of recruiting people and building the platform—whoever gets results first will be the first to establish themselves.

06 PART

My Verdict: Future Trends and Whether It's Worth Pursuing

VIEW / Scroll down for my take

First, let's look at how industry insiders are discussing this role.

Joe Schmidt, a partner at a16z (a well-known US venture capital firm), said FDE is "the hottest role at startups right now." Enterprises buying AI is like a grandma buying an iPhone—she wants to use it, but needs your help setting it up. The data backs this up: Indeed's data shows FDE job postings grew 729% year-over-year in April 2026, with an average base salary of $172,000; on Levels.fyi (a tech industry salary lookup site), the median total compensation for US FDEs is $206,000; Anthropic's own JD (job description) offers a base salary of $280,000–320,000 with 25% travel. Amazon is also pouring $1 billion into this role.

Now we come to the salary question everyone cares about most.

Abroad: on Levels.fyi, the median total compensation for US FDEs is about $206,000 (roughly 1.45 million RMB); Indeed's figures show an average base salary of $172,000; top players like Anthropic offer $280,000–320,000.

In China: ByteDance's "Doubao AI Large Model FDE" is listed at a monthly salary of 35,000–70,000 RMB with 15 months' pay, which works out to an annual salary of roughly 520,000–1,050,000 RMB. According to a report by Touzhongwang, entry-level FDEs in China generally start at 20,000 RMB per month, senior roles are mostly above 40,000 × 15 months' pay, and by Liepin's figures, senior FDEs at top-tier companies earn 600,000–1,200,000 RMB annually.

Taking an average: annual salaries abroad are roughly in the range of 1.4 million RMB, while at major Chinese companies it's roughly between 600,000 and 1,000,000 RMB—about a two-fold difference—but the direction and growth are on the same playing field.

Alex Coqueiro, an analyst at Gartner (a leading global IT research firm), has a sobering prediction: by 2028, 70% of enterprises will be forced to abandon agentic AI solutions purchased through FDE projects because vendor costs are too high and they can't sustain them on their own.

He also said something I really think hits the mark:

"If FDE investment can't come down across several consecutive deployments, it means the project is producing dependency, not capability." Someone put it even more bluntly: "CIOs think they're buying software, but what they're actually buying is a professional services contract."

So what's my verdict?

First, the hype around this direction is real, not a manufactured concept.

Once every software platform became customizable, the assumption that "customers can use it themselves" collapsed, and someone has to deliver the results for the customer. The big companies have voted with real money: OpenAI with $4 billion, Amazon with $1 billion, and Anthropic writing FDEs into its official strategy. Job posting data is just a lagging indicator of this fact.

Second, for individuals, whether it's worth it depends on whether you're cut out for it.

This role requires you to both write code and be willing to sit in a customer's conference room listening to them talk about their business—Kevin's exact words were whether you'd dare to put him in front of a customer, and "dare" is the key word. People who possess both capabilities are extremely rare, and scarcity means a premium; offers at the $300,000 annual salary level already show how the market prices this. But it also means a large portion of this role isn't spent in the codebase—it's spent at the customer's site, with travel starting at 25%, and some analyses note that even OpenAI's JD mentions up to 50%—burnout is an occupational hazard of this role. In other words, you'll be constantly on the move, going to the frontline of customers' businesses.

Third, and this is what I most want to say: the long-term value of an FDE isn't in "on-site service," it's in feedback loops. Behind Gartner's 70% prediction lies a real question—is the customer buying capability or dependency?

The real moat of this role is turning what you learn in the field into part of the platform, so the next project doesn't have to start from scratch.

The same logic applies to individuals.

If your job is just running errands for customers, you can be replaced at any time; if every project you complete deepens your understanding of a particular industry by one layer, you're accumulating something no one can take away from you. Before choosing this direction, ask yourself whether you can achieve the latter.

To sum up everything above in one sentence: the AI FDE is a real and growing direction, well suited for people who are "technically competent and also willing to deal with people" to invest in seriously; but it's not a safe harbor—it's the frontline.
