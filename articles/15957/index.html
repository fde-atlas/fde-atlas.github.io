The Client Is Not an Account

Michael Couch | couch.cx · 2026-11-09 · 7 min read

Summary

Michael Couch argues that agencies and consultancies invert their purpose: governance, reporting, and internal network politics turn clients into accounts and work into margin, leaving partners far from operational reality. He calls for partners to become part of the client's fabric through sustained context, trench presence, and intelligent challenge, backed by a dedicated core team plus on-demand specialists. AI tools have collapsed the old scarcity of execution capacity, so the deep integration of Forward Deployed Engineering should become standard. Client focus means independence and honest challenge, and success should be judged by impact on end users, not deliverables.

Key points

  • Commercial machinery inverts the client relationship into account management.
  • Partners need sustained context, trench presence, and intelligent challenge.
  • AI-native tools let small pods ship working systems in days.
  • FDE-style integration should be standard, not optional, in client work.
  • Client satisfaction must not be confused with human impact on end users.

Why the next generation of agencies must become part of the work, not another organisation surrounding it.

There is a peculiar thing that happens inside agencies, consultancies, and professional services businesses. The client becomes an account. The people doing the work become resources. The relationship becomes a revenue line. The work becomes a scope, a forecast, a delivery plan, and a margin.

None of these constructs is inherently wrong. Organisations need to operate. People need careers. Businesses need to make money. Expertise needs to move between teams, markets, and opportunities. But somewhere inside that machinery, a subtle inversion takes place: the organisation built to serve the client begins asking the client to accommodate the organisation.

SCQA (at a glance)

Situation: Enterprises engage agency networks and consultancies to unlock specialized capability, scale, and outside perspective. Complication: Commercial structures, staffing queues, and internal network politics gradually invert the relationship—the partner spends more energy managing its own machinery than understanding the client's operational reality. Question: What does exceptional partnership look like when execution capacity is no longer scarce? Answer: The partner stops operating around the client and becomes part of their capability: outside perspective, inside understanding, shared accountability, and an uncompromising focus on the end customer.

The quiet cost of distance

Most client relationships begin with genuine curiosity. Who are these people? What makes their organisation different? What are they trying to achieve? What stands in their way? What does excellence actually look like in their world?

Then the relationship matures. Governance arrives. Reporting expands. Commercial structures solidify. The people who originally understood the problem move into other roles. New people inherit a scope but not the context that made the work important. Eventually, the relationship becomes extraordinarily sophisticated at describing its own activity while becoming less connected to the reality of the business.

More meetings. More status reports. More handoffs. More people explaining what other people are doing. Not necessarily more value.

I have written before about the Corporate Impotence Cycle: how organisations become increasingly detached from the work they are supposed to understand, until leadership consumes summaries of reality instead of experiencing reality itself. The same phenomenon happens between a client and its partners.

A partner can be present in every governance meeting and still be remarkably far from the work. They can know every executive stakeholder and barely understand the operator trying to make a broken process function on a Thursday afternoon. They can present a comprehensive customer experience strategy without having spent meaningful time with the people actually experiencing it.

Access is not understanding. Proximity is not integration. And activity is not impact.

Become the fabric

The most valuable partners need to stop thinking of themselves as an organisation positioned beside the client. They need to become part of the fabric of how the client operates.

Not through an indiscriminate transfer of people onto client premises. Not through endless secondments. Not by surrendering the independent perspective that makes external expertise valuable. Through genuine, deliberate operational integration:

  • Sustained context: People who understand the client's ambitions, constraints, customers, systems, and culture—without needing to reacquire it at the start of each engagement.
  • Trench presence: People who can sit with an operator, understand why something is failing, and help change it rather than produce another slide deck describing the failure.
  • Intelligent challenge: People who know enough about the organisation to challenge its assumptions intelligently, not merely validate them.

That requires a different commitment to continuity: a dedicated core of people who own the relationship and its outcomes, supported by specialist expertise that can be drawn from elsewhere when genuinely useful. The client should experience one coherent capability, not a collection of internal agency departments with competing incentives.

Outside perspective. Inside understanding. Shared accountability. That is the partnership worth building.

The network still matters. It just cannot become the customer.

There is a legitimate argument for large agency networks and diversified groups. They have access to extraordinary talent. They operate across categories, geographies, technologies, and cultural contexts. They see problems that an individual client may encounter only once. They can recognise patterns, introduce unconventional ideas, and provide concentrated expertise that would be economically unreasonable for any single company to employ full-time.

Some of the most valuable thinking in one industry originates in another. A financial services organisation can learn from retail. A luxury brand can learn from gaming. A manufacturer can learn from software. That exchange is real, and as I argued in In-House Excellence vs Agency Fuel, it is one of the few things an internal team cannot easily manufacture for itself.

But there is an enormous difference between sharing expertise and making a client navigate the internal economics of a holding company. Cross-category intelligence is valuable when it improves a decision. Shared talent is valuable when it solves a problem exceptionally well. Global scale is valuable when it unlocks something a client could not otherwise achieve. None of those things is valuable simply because it exists on an agency rate card.

Share expertise widely. Preserve accountability locally. The network should make the dedicated client team more capable, not give the client a larger bureaucracy to manage.

AI removes the old excuse

For most of modern business history, specialist capability was difficult and expensive to assemble. A strategist could see the opportunity but could not build it. An operator understood the workflow but could not build the tool. A designer understood the experience but depended on multiple engineering teams to bring it to life.

That distance produced entire operating structures: queues, departments, handoffs, and elaborate mechanisms for rationing scarce execution capacity.

Those economics have collapsed. As explored in The End of Software Built by Strangers, AI-native tools now allow multidisciplinary pods to research, prototype, test, and ship working systems in days. A capable operator, an engineer, and a domain expert can now work in the same loop rather than spending months translating intentions across organisational borders.

This is the foundation behind Forward Deployed Engineering (FDE): build alongside the people who understand the problem, work within the reality of their systems, learn from actual use, and transfer what works into durable operating capability.

Why should this level of integration be considered standard in frontier AI deployment, but treated as optional in the broader relationship between agencies and their clients? Creative operations, customer intelligence, experience design, and data activation all decay when expertise is separated from the work. We now have the tools to close that distance. The question is whether we have the courage to redesign the commercial relationship around them.

Client focus is not client obedience

Being relentlessly focused on a client does not mean agreeing with every client request. It does not mean abandoning professional independence, suppressing uncomfortable truths, or becoming staff-augmentation on discount.

Quite the opposite. A truly invested partner must be prepared to challenge: to explain why a proposed initiative will fail, to surface the trade-off nobody wants to discuss, to bring evidence from outside the category, and to protect customer trust when quarterly commercial pressure threatens it. Sometimes the most valuable contribution a partner makes is telling an executive that the thing they want to build should not exist.

That requires proximity and independence together. You cannot challenge effectively without understanding the context. And you cannot serve a client effectively if preserving the contract becomes more important than telling the truth.

The client is not always the customer

The client pays the invoice. But the client is not necessarily the person who experiences the consequences of the work.

Somewhere beyond the brief, the steering committee, the marketing roadmap, and the delivery milestone is a human being:

  • A customer trying to resolve an urgent problem on a mobile device.
  • A patient navigating a confusing healthcare system.
  • An employee using an internal tool that makes their working day either fluid or miserable.
  • A citizen trusting an institution with their data.

Their experience is the only honest test of whether what was built was useful. A campaign can hit its delivery targets and remain completely forgettable. A transformation programme can tick every milestone and still leave employees working around the same broken systems. An AI workflow can reduce unit operating costs while quietly degrading customer trust.

Client satisfaction matters. But it should never be confused with human impact. Serving the client's long-term interest often means advocating for the people who will actually live with the outcome.

What needs to change

This does not require another industry rebrand or a conference keynote about "the agency of the future." It requires different everyday choices:

  • Put the people who understand the problem beside the people who can solve it.
  • Give dedicated teams end-to-end ownership, backed by a specialist network on demand.
  • Make client context durable instead of letting it evaporate every time a team turns over.
  • Connect strategy directly to working systems, and systems to the people who use them.
  • Measure whether the work changed an outcome, not whether a deliverable cleared the queue.
  • Build the client's internal capability so they are more capable after the engagement than they were before it.

The most compelling commercial proposition an agency can make is not headcount, tool licenses, or a slide depicting global scale. It is that the people who matter to the problem are present, informed, empowered, and accountable.

The best partnerships are not defined by how much space they occupy around a client. They are defined by how much better the client becomes because they were there. The client is not an account. The customer is not a metric. And the work is not about us.
