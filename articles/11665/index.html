SmolLM2 (2025) Explained for FDEs

FDE Interviews · 2026-10-18 · 4 min read

Summary

This piece explains the SmolLM2 paper from Hugging Face: a 1.7B Llama-style model trained on roughly 11 trillion tokens, deliberately past compute-optimal, with the real contribution being data curation. The authors had Llama-3-class models rate documents for educational value, trained cheap classifiers on those ratings, and filtered at scale to build FineMath, Stack-Edu, and SmolTalk. Since one run cost about $250,000, the data mix was adjusted by hand across four training stages. For FDEs, the transferable lesson is the filter recipe: apply the same LLM-scored, classifier-filtered pipeline to messy customer data like tickets, code, and documents.

Key points

  • SmolLM2 is a 1.7B model trained on ~11T tokens, past compute-optimal.
  • Core method: LLM-rated educational value, then cheap classifier filtering at scale.
  • New datasets FineMath, Stack-Edu, and SmolTalk are all released openly.
  • Training ran in four stages with the data mix hand-adjusted after each.
  • For FDEs: the filter recipe transfers to curating customer data like tickets and code.

The paper in one minute

SmolLM2 is a 1.7B-parameter Llama-style model (24 layers, width 2,048, tied embeddings) trained by Hugging Face on about 11 trillion tokens, far past compute-optimal, to make a small model as strong as possible. The paper is mostly about data.

The authors compared existing datasets with small ablation runs and, where those were weak, built new ones with the same recipe: have Llama-3-70B or Llama-3.1-70B rate a sample of documents for educational value, train a small classifier on those ratings, score the whole corpus and keep documents above a threshold. FineMath (up to 54B tokens) is math web text that shows step-by-step reasoning; Stack-Edu (about 125B tokens) is well-documented code from 15 languages; SmolTalk is an instruction-tuning mix built around a new synthetic conversation set, MagPie-Ultra, generated with Llama-3.1-405B.

Training ran in four stages because one run cost about $250,000. Stage 1 was 90% English web (FineWeb-Edu and DCLM) and 10% code. After each stage the authors looked at scores and changed the mix: more code, then small math datasets, then Stack-Edu in place of raw code, and finally, during the learning-rate decay, the best math (FineMath4+) and code. Math went from 7.27 to 22.07 in that last 1T tokens.

The base model leads Qwen2.5-1.5B and Llama3.2-1B on several knowledge and reasoning benchmarks and trails Qwen2.5 on math and code. After instruction tuning and DPO it leads on IFEval and trails Qwen2.5 on MMLU-Pro and GSM8K. All datasets and models are released.

Research notes: the problem, approach and results

average math score before and after the final 1T-token decay stage, when the best math data was added

MultiPL-E gain on C++ from keeping only the 23% of code an LLM-trained classifier rated educational

approximate GPU cost of the 1.7B training run (about 1e23 FLOPs), the reason the mix was tuned during one run

Small models have little capacity, so what they are trained on matters more than for large ones. Public math, code and instruction datasets were too small or too noisy, and a full training run costs too much to try several data mixes.

Build new datasets by having an LLM rate samples for educational value, training cheap classifiers on those ratings and filtering at scale (FineMath, Stack-Edu), plus a synthetic instruction set (SmolTalk). Train for 11T tokens in four stages, changing the mix by hand after each stage based on its scores, and add the best math and code during the final learning-rate decay.

SmolLM2-1.7B's base model beats Qwen2.5-1.5B on MMLU-Pro (19.4 vs 13.7) and TriviaQA but trails it on GSM8K (31.1 vs 61.7), MATH and HumanEval. Filtering code by educational quality raised MultiPL-E by 4 to 8 points per language while keeping as little as 23% of the tokens. Math scores tripled in the last 1T tokens.

Why it matters to an FDE

Few customers will pre-train a model, but many will build training or fine-tuning sets from their own messy data: support tickets, internal code, documents. This paper is the clearest public account of how to curate such data and check that it worked.

The filter recipe transfers. Have a strong LLM score a few hundred thousand examples against a written rubric, train a cheap classifier on those scores, run it over everything, and pick a threshold by small experiments. On code, keeping the 23% to 43% rated most educational in C++, JavaScript and Python raised MultiPL-E by 4 to 8 points. The same recipe works for ticket quality, document relevance or example difficulty.

Thresholds trade quality against repetition. A strict filter leaves less data; if training needs more tokens than that, you repeat it. The authors aimed for no more than 4-5 epochs per dataset, and one heavily filtered math set stopped improving around 10 epochs.

When you add specialized data matters. Small, high-quality sets were held back until the end, where they moved scores the most.

Base-model wins may not survive tuning. The base model beat Qwen2.5-1.5B on MMLU-Pro by 5.7 points; after instruction tuning it trailed by 4.9. Evaluate the model the customer will actually use.
