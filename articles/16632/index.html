The FDE Team's ROI Framework: How to Do the Math

Original title: FDE 团队的 ROI 框架：怎么算这笔账

FDE中国 · 2026-09-04 · 7 min read

Summary

This article offers an ROI framework for deciding whether an FDE team is worth funding. It breaks costs into three layers (fully loaded compensation, on-site and compliance expenses, opportunity costs) and benefits into four items (delivery cycle, acceptance pass rate, renewals and expansion, and a data-and-feedback flywheel). The core argument: FDE investment is a multi-period bet—target breakeven in year one and flywheel compounding in year two, not per-project gross margin, which guarantees a loss. Citing a Perspective AI survey of ~1,500 FDEs and Chinese salary reports from 36kr.com, it warns against booking flywheel gains before knowledge-return mechanisms exist, and walks through a hypothetical calculation for a 4-person team at a 2-billion-yuan software firm.

Key points

  • Costs: compensation, on-site/compliance, opportunity cost.
  • Benefits: cycle time, acceptance rate, renewals, flywheel.
  • Per-project margin accounting is a directional mistake.
  • Flywheel gains require real knowledge-return mechanisms.
  • Fill in the sample worksheet and find breakeven before approving.

Most companies get the FDE team's math wrong the first time—not the numbers are wrong, the framework is. The most common mistake is treating FDEs as "slightly more expensive implementation headcount" and evaluating them by per-project gross margin, which inevitably yields a loss conclusion, and then rejecting the entire model for the wrong reason. This article provides an ROI framework for enterprise decision-makers: costs broken into three layers, benefits into four items, and a full calculation walkthrough with a set of assumed numbers. The thesis up front: the ROI of an FDE team is a multi-period ledger and an options ledger; the correct way to compute it is "year one: look at the break-even path; year two: look at flywheel compounding," not per-project hourly gross margin. All numbers in the calculation at the end are hypothetical examples for methodological reference only and do not represent any real company's data.

Thesis and evidence: why it must be a multi-period ledger

First, why it must be a multi-period ledger. An FDE's work focus is not writing code: according to Perspective AI's 2026 annual survey of roughly 1,500 FDEs (getperspective.ai), respondents' median weekly time allocation was about 47% customer-facing, 31% writing code, and 22% internal coordination. Nearly half of the effort does not happen in code but in requirements definition, trust building, and solution convergence; the value of this effort is naturally deferred—it is realized as shorter delivery cycles, higher acceptance pass rates, and subsequent renewals, and does not appear on the current project's P&L.

Next, supply-side pricing. According to a 36Kr report (36kr.com), monthly salary bands for FDE-related roles at leading domestic companies are generally higher than same-level R&D: ByteDance-affiliated FDE roles have been posted at 35,000–70,000 RMB per month with 15 months of pay per year, and Alibaba Cloud Intelligence FDEs at 20,000–50,000 RMB per month with 16 months of pay. Using headcount at this price point for single-project delivery makes the books almost impossible to look good; the premise for it being worthwhile is that the output can be reused. To judge which situation your team is in takes just one action: write down three scenarios you have already delivered and check whether there is anything in them that can be reused on the fourth or fifth customer. This judgment complements the site's FDE delivery quality measurement: the measurement system answers "are we doing it well?", while this article answers "is it worth it?"

Cost layer one: compensation and fully loaded team cost

Beyond salary, calculate the fully loaded cost. A workable formula is "fully loaded cost per person = cash compensation × 1.4 to 1.6"; the multiplier covers social insurance and housing fund, bonuses, and amortized tools and training—this is a common rough industry convention; defer to your company's finance department for specifics. Domestic reference points: besides the big-company figures reported by 36Kr (36kr.com) above, according to a report by Xinhua Daily Media (xhby.net), Zhipu's FDE lead role once reached 60,000–80,000 RMB per month; an analysis by the Tencent Cloud Developer Community (cloud.tencent.com) points out that domestic FDE salaries are generally higher than same-level R&D, and the salary inversion phenomenon is real. Team formation costs should also include recruiting itself: qualified candidates are scarce, and both headhunter fees and hiring cycles are significantly higher than for ordinary R&D roles. According to platform data cited by Xinhua Daily Media (xhby.net), the number of FDE postings rose roughly 42-fold in two years—supply is far from keeping up with demand.

Cost layer two: on-site overhead and compliance costs

On-site work does not end with sending people over. Cost items include at least: travel and accommodation (per the Perspective AI survey, 71% of FDEs travel at least monthly); workstations, equipment, and dedicated networks at the customer site; security approvals and dedicated terminals for intranet development environments; and data masking and audit tools invested to meet customer data compliance requirements. On-site compliance costs in industries such as finance and government are especially not to be underestimated—security training, NDAs, and background checks all lengthen the onboarding cycle; these should be converted into startup costs, not ignored. Every month the onboarding cycle slips, year one loses another month. To estimate this layer of complexity, you can go through the site's data compliance checklist item by item.

Cost layer three: opportunity cost

This is the layer most often missed. Engineers capable of serving as FDEs are almost certainly the backbone you rely on most on the platform side: fast business understanding, complete full-stack skills, reliable communication. Moving them to the front line slows platform-side delivery—that is a real cost. There are also two hidden opportunity costs: first, senior engineers' growth path on the front line differs from the platform side; if there is no reflow mechanism, you are effectively burning down the career lifespan of your organization's scarcest talent; second, once the front-line team is fully occupied by customer projects, you lose the time to use them to polish your own product. Opportunity cost cannot be precisely accounted for, but it must appear in the decision document—without it, the cost side is naturally underestimated by more than 30%.

Benefit one: delivery cycle and acceptance pass rate

The first benefit is speed. The Palantir-style bootcamp approach—building a runnable prototype from the customer's real data within days—has been analyzed by Everest Group as the key mechanism replacing lengthy POCs (everestgrp.com); common industry observation is that on-site co-creation significantly compresses the "requirements clarification—prototype—feedback" loop. The second benefit is stability: acceptance pass rates rise and rework decreases. The logic is straightforward—requirements are aligned with business departments on-site, acceptance criteria are confirmed by all three parties before development begins, and the room for deliverables to deviate from expectations is greatly compressed. Both are metrics directly observable on the project ledger; we recommend measuring via "comparison of similar projects before and after adopting the FDE model," rather than scoring by impression.

Benefit two: renewals, repeat purchases, and trust premium

The FDE's commercial value only truly appears after the second deal. The first deal earns trust; from the second deal onward, you earn a premium: the customer has already validated your on-site capability, and renewal negotiations shift from "whether to switch vendors" to "how much to expand scope." According to a The New Stack report (thenewstack.io) citing OpenAI's official figures, about 75% of its enterprise customers reported positive ROI—only vendors who can actually deliver results get renewals and repeat purchases. For an FDE team built in-house by the customer side, this translates to: business departments are willing to invest larger budgets in scenarios you have validated, and digitalization projects move from "checking the box" to "ongoing operations."

Benefit three: the data and feedback flywheel

The hardest to quantify, and the most valuable. Three types of assets FDEs accumulate in customer environments compound over time: first, scenario templates and solution assets—the delivery cycle for the second similar customer should theoretically be shorter than the first; second, evals and regression assets (for methodology see the site's evals baseline handbook and evals regression patterns), which raise the quality floor with every delivery; third, the voice of the customer, feeding directly back into the product roadmap. According to industry analysis cited by joinplank.com, AI projects co-built with partners are about twice as likely to reach production as purely in-house builds, and deployment accounts for only about 20% of the total lifecycle cost of AI systems—taken together, these two facts show that the flywheel's compounding happens in the last 80% of the lifecycle cost, while most ROI calculations only stare at the first 20%.

Calculation example: walking through the framework completely (hypothetical example, for methodological reference)

All figures below are hypothetical examples for methodology reference only and do not represent any real company. Suppose a B2B software company with annual revenue of RMB 2 billion forms a 4-person FDE team for financial industry clients (3 senior FDEs plus 1 lead).

Cost side (assumed values): fully loaded cost per person of RMB 900,000, totaling RMB 3.6 million for 4 people; on-site travel and stationing expenses at RMB 100,000 per person per year, totaling RMB 400,000; tools, eval assets, and security/compliance investment of RMB 500,000. Total annual cost is approximately RMB 4.5 million.

Benefit side (assumed values): delivery cycle shortened from 16 weeks to 10 weeks, saving RMB 1.2 million in labor occupancy and in-progress costs for similar projects; first-pass acceptance rate improved from 70% to 90%, saving RMB 1 million in rework and after-sales costs; incremental gross profit from renewals and upsells of RMB 2.3 million. Total annual benefit is approximately RMB 4.5 million—roughly breaking even in the first year.

Year two (assumed values): scenario templates and eval assets begin to be reused, the delivery cycle is further shortened to 8 weeks, marginal delivery costs for similar scenarios decline, and the renewal base expands, growing benefits to RMB 6-7 million; costs grow only naturally with workload to approximately RMB 4.8 million—the flywheel starts turning. The key step in the calculation is assigning an observable ledger metric to each benefit item; for reporting structure, refer to the FDE Reporting Mechanism Handbook, and for goal agreements at project kickoff, refer to the FDE Project Kickoff Document Template.

Three common calculation errors

  • Calculating gross margin per project while missing reuse. After allocating FDE costs per project, they look expensive, but its asset outputs serve all subsequent projects. The correct approach is to list "accumulated assets" as an independent benefit item in the books and track how many times they are reused.
  • Counting only explicit costs while missing opportunity costs and compliance startup costs. These two items together often account for 20-30% of total costs; missing them means you won't discover the true picture until the second year.
  • Booking flywheel benefits as certain benefits in advance. The flywheel's premise is that a knowledge feedback mechanism actually exists. An FDE team without a feedback mechanism won't get faster in year two—it will only get more expensive.

Conclusion: three recommendations for decision-makers

First, when approving the FDE team, approve the measurement framework at the same time: delivery cycle, acceptance pass rate, renewal contribution, and number of accumulated assets—all four are indispensable; see FDE Delivery Quality Measurement for measurement design. Second, position the first year as "breaking even and building assets" and the second year as "compounding," and review progress at this pace in management meetings; see Managing FDE Teams: Goals, Metrics, and Knowledge Feedback Mechanisms for day-to-day management mechanisms. Third, after running the numbers, don't rush to expand headcount to full-time: first validate assumptions with a project-based or hybrid model; see Does Your Company Need FDEs: A Judgment Framework for judging whether the demand holds, and Build an In-House FDE Team or Buy Delivery Services for choosing between building and buying.
