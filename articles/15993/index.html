What Does an FDE Interview Cover? Four Assessment Areas and Key Preparation Points

Original title: FDE 面试会考什么？四类考察点与备考重点

FDE中国 · 2026-09-07 · 2 min read

Summary

This guide breaks the FDE interview into four assessment areas: coding fundamentals, ambiguous scenario decomposition, customer-facing role-play, and deep dives into past delivery work. It gives concrete practice methods for each and flags common failure modes, such as jumping to solutions without clarifying requirements or describing projects as feature lists without trade-offs and metrics. The author suggests allocating prep time roughly 30% coding, 30% scenario practice, 20% communication, 20% portfolio, and notes post-interview reflection on follow-up questions is high-leverage.

Key points

  • Four areas: coding basics, scenario decomposition, role-play, delivery deep dive.
  • Coding tests reliability and test habits, not hard algorithms; don't over-invest in grinding problems.
  • Scenario questions reward clarifying first; jumping straight to solutions loses points.
  • Role-play tests absorbing emotion before solutions; project stories need metrics and trade-offs.
  • Portfolio deep dives are fully controllable, so polish them first; suggested split is 30/30/20/20.

Full text

This article provides an actionable preparation framework for candidates preparing for FDE interviews. The most valuable takeaways are the corresponding practice methods for the four assessment areas: for scenario decomposition questions, practice the "clarify first, then decompose" structure; for role-playing, practice "acknowledge the emotions first, then offer a solution"; for deep dives into your work, practice "presenting results with metrics." The three most common ways to lose points: jumping straight to a solution without clarifying, describing projects as a feature list without trade-offs or outcomes, and being unable to explain the root cause and improvements for a production incident. Compared with the long-form interview articles on the site, this piece offers actionable advice at a Q&A level of granularity, and serves as an entry point to the two articles on career transition and portfolio building. There's also incremental value in preparation pacing: for the time allocation across the four assessment areas, we recommend 30% engineering, 30% scenario decomposition, 20% communication, and 20% portfolio. The deep dive into your work is the only part entirely within your control, so it should be polished first and foremost. Additionally, post-interview review matters just as much: the details you were pressed on in each round are precisely the weakest evidence points in your work and experience. Reinforcing them yields more compounding returns than applying to ten more companies. After reading, do one thing: self-assess your strengths and weaknesses against the four assessment areas, and schedule one mock practice session for each weak area. — FDEChina Editorial Team · Practitioner's Edition

What does the FDE interview test?

The direct answer: FDE interviews typically revolve around four assessment areas—engineering fundamentals, scenario decomposition questions, customer communication role-playing, and deep dives into past deliveries. The core logic is simulating the real work of the role: you need to prove that you can write code hands-on, make judgment calls on ambiguous requirements, and hold your ground in front of clients. Among the four areas, there are usually no high-intensity data structure questions like those in traditional algorithm interviews, so don't bet all your prep time on grinding LeetCode.

First area: engineering fundamentals. The common format is live hand-written coding or a small take-home assignment: implementing a small feature that calls an LLM API, processing a messy dataset, or writing a simple retrieval script. What's being assessed is not algorithmic difficulty, but whether your code is reliable, whether you have testing awareness, and whether you can explain your thinking as you write. The preparation focus is getting fluent with the common building blocks of the LLM application stack: API calls, RAG pipelines, tool calling, and eval scripts—see the tiered requirements in Does an FDE need to write code?.

Second area: scenario decomposition questions, usually the most differentiating round. The interviewer gives you a vague client requirement, such as "help our customer service department use LLMs," and watches how you handle it. The most common mistake is jumping straight into a solution—"we could set up a RAG system." The correct approach is to clarify first: what is the current process, where are the pain points, what are the acceptance criteria, and who owns the data. For a structured decomposition method, see The FDE Requirements Discovery Handbook. Third area: customer communication role-playing, where the interviewer plays an angry customer or a stonewalling business stakeholder to see whether you can first acknowledge the emotions, then define the problem, and finally commit to next steps. What's being assessed is your posture, not your scripts.

Fourth area: deep dives into past deliveries. Preparation for this round depends entirely on what you've accumulated before. The interviewer will press for project details: how the scope was locked, what incidents occurred, what the root cause was, and how you fixed it. The most common mistake is describing a project as a feature list, with no trade-offs, no outcomes, and no metrics. Prepare each case following the "context—constraints—solution—results—retrospective" structure; see The FDE Portfolio Guide for how to organize it. For the complete interview process and more real questions, see The FDE Interview Guide.
