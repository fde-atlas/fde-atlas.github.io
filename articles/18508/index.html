The Truth About FDE Hiring Growth: How to Identify a Genuine FDE Role

Original title: FDE 招聘增长真相：如何判断真正的 FDE 岗位

FDE Times · 2026-10-09 · 5 min read

Summary

This article argues that headline growth figures for FDE job postings—over 800% (Financial Times via Fast Company), 1,165% (Bloomberry), and 5,230% (Indeed via KDnuggets)—all measure posting counts or index values, not actual hires or real FDE work. A curated index counted only 224 open positions across 39 companies as of May 30, 2026. Bloomberry's manual review of 1,000 postings found roughly 60% are build-focused FDE roles, 30% resemble pre-sales engineering, and 10% are internal tooling. The title itself originated in 2011 when Palantir renamed its solutions and integration engineers, which a16z's Tom Hollands calls title arbitrage. The author offers two tests: check whether the verbs describe building versus configuring, and whether on-site work feeds back into the product.

Three sources, three growth rates: over 800%, 1,165%, and 5,230%. Yet as of May 30, 2026, a curated index counted only 224 open Forward Deployed Engineer (FDE) positions across 39 companies.

This gap does not mean anyone is wrong. It means these percentages measure something far removed from what you actually care about. They count how often the words "Forward Deployed Engineer" appear in job postings, not the work you would actually be doing.

For engineers who are already FDEs or want to become one, the question worth asking is not how big the market is, but what the posting on your screen actually describes. To answer that, you first need to understand what each number counts.

Three numbers, measuring three different things

Bloomberry compared the number of job postings with the FDE title from January to October 2025 against the same period in 2024, arriving at a 1,165% increase. This method counts titles. A company that renames its Solutions Engineer roles to FDE gets counted in the total, even if not a single additional line of code is written at customer sites.

The over-800% figure comes from the Financial Times, cited by Fast Company, covering January to September 2025. It likewise counts postings, not hires. Fast Company's article itself notes that FDE responsibilities vary from company to company.

The most striking figure, 5,230%, comes from Indeed via KDnuggets, comparing April 2026 with January 2025. KDnuggets itself cautions: this is an index calculated from a small January 2025 base, the data comes from a single job site, and it does not reflect actual headcount.

A simple hypothetical makes the point: a market growing from 10 postings to 100 is a 900% increase, but 100 postings is still a very small market.

Taken together, the three percentages describe the popularity of a label. Only the absolute number in the last row reveals scale, and that scale is far smaller than the headlines lead readers to imagine.

One title, three kinds of work

Counting alone cannot reveal what is inside. That is why Bloomberry's most valuable finding is not the 1,165% figure, but the conclusion from a close reading of 1,000 postings: companies use the same FDE title for three different kinds of work.

About 60% are build-focused FDE roles, which Bloomberry calls the mainstream. About 30% are close to sales engineering, and about 10% are internal tooling roles. This is one person's manual classification of a sample, not an official statistic, but it is enough to change how you read job postings.

Suppose you have saved 10 FDE postings you plan to apply to. If Bloomberry's proportions apply to your list, only about 6 of them involve building software at customer sites.

The 10 postings share the same name, but a year later they would leave you with three very different skill sets.

Renaming titles is nothing new

According to a16z's Tom Hollands, the FDE title itself was born from a rename. In 2011, Palantir gave its Solutions Engineers and Integration Engineers a new name. Hollands calls this title arbitrage: creating a new title to signal that new authority is emerging within the organization.

This definition is worth reading closely: it speaks to signaling authority, and says nothing about whether the work has changed. Hollands also sees the downside of this mechanism: titles can fall into a "Red Queen" race, with prestige inflating while substance fails to keep up.

When a title becomes a hot market signal, renaming a role costs almost nothing yet makes the posting more attractive.

Tandem, a company with a stake in this topic, argues that after the FDE title became popular, many companies began slapping the FDE label on adjacent roles.

According to Tandem, a substantial share of the posting growth comes from renames. You need not accept this claim wholesale, only note that it aligns with the roughly 40% of postings Bloomberry classified as something other than build-focused.

Two tests for reading job postings

Since the aggregate numbers are of no help, you have to evaluate each posting individually. Tandem proposes an output-based test: if a posting describes configuration work but carries the FDE title, the role is an implementation engineer.

KDnuggets offers a second, sharper test: does the work at customer sites feed back into the product? According to KDnuggets, if not, it is consulting with a nicer title.

Apply both tests to two hypothetical postings. Posting A reads: "Configure workflows on our platform for enterprise customers, train users, and support product demos."

Posting B reads: "Write production code to integrate with customer systems, identify patterns that recur across multiple deployments, and work with the product team to fold them back into the platform."

Posting A fails both tests. Its core verbs are configure and demo, and there is no path for results to flow back into the product. Posting B passes both: you write software, and what you learn at customers has somewhere to go in the product.

Posting A is not a bad job. Implementation engineering and sales engineering are legitimate careers. The problem is that if you take it believing you will be doing FDE work, two years later your resume will tell a different story than the one you intended.

How to navigate the hype

When screening postings, look at the verbs before the title. Words like "build," "ship," "write production code," and "work with the product team" usually indicate build-focused roles; "configure," "demo," and "support the sales process" point to the other two categories. The job description is more honest than the title.

In an interview, turn KDnuggets' test into a question: When was the last time code or a finding from a deployment made it into the core product, and through what path? A real FDE team can usually answer right away and give concrete examples. If the interviewer stumbles, you have your answer.

When writing your resume, apply the same test to yourself. Instead of just writing "completed deployments for 5 clients," show the feedback loop—for example, an integration pattern you wrote for one client that later became a general feature.

A sentence like this shows you've done FDE work, not just held the FDE title.

The 1,165% figure will cool down, just like every growth curve measured on a new baseline. What will still matter then is whether you truly brought findings from customers into the product.
