FDE Learning Roadmap | A 3-Stage Path and Priority Skills for Beginners

Original title: FDE学習ロードマップ｜未経験から3段階で進める順序と優先スキル

FDE総研 · 2026-08-14 · 13 min read

Summary

This Japanese guide from FDE Soken lays out a learning roadmap for aspiring Forward Deployed Engineers with no prior experience. It argues FDEs should start by verbalizing business processes rather than learning programming languages first, reversing the usual engineer's sequence into: business understanding, data, implementation, then operational adoption. Learning is split into three stages—foundation, applied, and practical—each gated by concrete deliverables instead of time spent. Skill priorities place problem definition and communication above technical skills; among technical skills, SQL and data cleaning rank highest while machine learning ranks low. The article also advises structuring portfolios as decision records and using two-week deadlines plus audiences to sustain motivation.

Key points

  • Start with verbalizing business processes, not coding; sequence is business, data, implementation, adoption.
  • Three stages (foundation, applied, practical) each end with defined deliverables, not timeframes.
  • Top skills are problem definition and communication; SQL/data cleaning lead technical priorities, ML ranks low.
  • Portfolios should document decisions, rejected options, and limitations, not just successes.
  • Beat dropout with two-week deadlines, pre-booked presentations, and monthly pruning of study topics.

Where should I start with an FDE learning roadmap?

The shortcut for learning FDE is to start with training to articulate customer business processes before learning a programming language.

Forward Deployed Engineer (FDE) is a role popularized by Palantir Technologies along with its deployment model. FDEs embed themselves at the customer's site and take personal responsibility for everything from defining business problems to building and embedding software.

A typical software engineer's learning roadmap builds up in the order of "language → framework → architecture." An FDE learning roadmap, however, needs to be restructured as "business understanding → data → implementation → adoption." Getting this order wrong tends to leave you in a state where, despite having technical skills, you cannot decide what to build in front of the customer.

Comparison axisTypical software engineerFDE
Learning orderLanguage → Framework → ArchitectureBusiness understanding → Data → Implementation → Adoption
Evaluation criterion for deliverablesDoes it work according to spec?Does the spec truly change the business?
Skill to build earlyImplementation speedThe ability to translate problems into an observable form

The learning order differs because the evaluation criteria for deliverables differ. What you should build early in FDE learning is not implementation speed, but the ability to translate problems into an observable form.

Should the first step be "articulating the business" or "code"?

The first step is articulating the business. Capture who looks at what, when, and how they make decisions, in a single diagram.

It is effective to pick a familiar business process and practice putting the people involved, input data, decision points, and outputs (decisions or reports) into a single diagram. The subject can be an approval flow at your own workplace or shift scheduling at a part-time job. What matters is not leaving points where you feel "this is inefficient" as mere impressions. Describe them in terms of observable metrics such as frequency, time required, and number of people involved.

You can self-check whether you have articulated the business by verifying you can write the following four points.

Check itemStandard for how to write it
1. Whose work is it?Write at the granularity of the people actually doing the work, not job titles
2. What data do they look at to make decisions?Go down to the system name and field names
3. What gets delayed if that decision stalls?Write the downstream impact in one sentence
4. How is the current state measured?Express it in counts, minutes, or number of people

In FDE interviews and on the job, you are often asked "why build it" rather than "what can you build." This is a common observation among practitioners close to the field. I cannot say for certain, but I feel that even with the same speed of technical learning, people who can articulate business processes tend to get up to speed faster.

What can beginners discard in the early stages of learning?

In the early stages, you can discard comprehensiveness. Broadly and shallowly collecting framework comparisons and certifications can wait.

What beginners aiming for FDE tend to stumble on is the breadth of the learning scope. The elements in FDE job descriptions span a wide range: cloud, data platforms, frontend, machine learning, project management. But what you need early on is a single thread: "even if small, draw conclusions from data and explain them to people."

Learning you can discardWhy it can wait
Cross-comparison of multiple frameworksBeing able to use one deeply is enough
Comprehensive acquisition of infrastructure certificationsIt pays off after gaining practical experience
High-difficulty levels of algorithm competitionsRarely central to FDE selection
Grand plans for building your own large-scale productLikely to stall learning without completion

Redirect the time saved to practicing picking one public dataset, aggregating it, and explaining the conclusion verbally in 3 minutes. Even without someone to explain to, just recording yourself and listening back is enough to spot missing points.

How do the three stages of the FDE learning roadmap progress?

The key is to progress through FDE learning in three stages—fundamentals, applied, and practical—and define graduation criteria for each stage in terms of deliverables.

Dividing stages by deliverables rather than time lets you measure progress objectively. The goal is not to say "I studied for six months" but to say "I built three dashboards I can explain to a stand-in customer." If you divide by time, even as your study volume grows, your endpoint stays vague and you lose the ability to judge what to add next.

StageGoalCore skillsDeliverable marking graduation
Stage 1: FundamentalsAble to extract facts from dataSQL, spreadsheets, Python basics, visualization2-3 analysis reports using public data
Stage 2: AppliedAble to test hypotheses and shape themAPI integration, data pipelines, lightweight web apps, requirements gatheringA prototype modeled on a real business problem
Stage 3: PracticalAble to deliver and hand over operationsPermission design, operational procedures, documentation, customer liaisonA deliverable with operational procedures others can take over

What should you be able to do to graduate from Stage 1 fundamentals?

The graduation criterion for Stage 1 is being able to summarize facts from raw data in a short time. Prioritize speed over precision.

Limit your tools. Being able to aggregate with SQL, shape with Python, and explain with simple charts is enough. Suitable subjects are public data with clear sources, such as government statistics or municipal open data. The habit of always recording sources connects directly to audits and accountability in FDE practice.

A common failure at this stage is getting stuck trying to make the analysis comprehensive. It is better to move forward by first producing just three things—counts, trends, and skew—and then raising additional questions from there. Keep analysis reports short; the following structure is enough.

ElementGuideline for length
Conclusion1 sentence
Rationale3 points
Limitations1 point

What cycle do you run in the second stage of application?

In the second stage, you turn business problems into hypotheses and validate them with working prototypes. Prioritize speed of validation over polish.

In the second stage, you introduce a "customer role" for the first time. Have colleagues or study partners play the business users, and run the following short cycle.

StepEstimated timeWhat to do
Interview30 minutesAsk the customer role about bottlenecks in their work
Prototype building3 daysTurn the hypothesis into a working form
Review30 minutesShow it to the customer role and check whether their decisions would change

In actual FDE work, the core approach is showing something working before requirements are fixed, in order to draw out requirements. So the number of times you have run this cycle directly becomes your capability.

In the review, don't ask whether the features are good or bad; ask "Looking at this screen, would you change your decisions starting tomorrow?" If they say no, the cause is almost always in the problem framing, not the implementation quality. Use that insight as the starting point for the next cycle.

What should be included as deliverables in the third stage of practical work?

The graduation condition for the third stage is creating a state where things run even without you. Include operational procedures and permission design in the deliverables.

The value of an FDE lies not in building something and stopping there, but in making it take root in the field. Who updates the data? Who do you contact when something breaks? Do the permissions align with job responsibilities? Making a habit of including such operational design in deliverables from the start will also help you get evaluated favorably when transferring internally into an FDE role.

Write the operations manual assuming the person taking over will read it with no prior context. If it covers the following three points, it meets a practical standard.

Required items in an operations manualWhat to write
Prerequisite terminologyDefinitions that prevent the successor from getting confused on first read
Anticipated failuresPossible outages and misoperations
Recovery proceduresWho does what when a failure occurs

What is the priority order of skills required for an FDE?

The highest-priority FDE skills are problem definition and interpersonal skills; technical skills are the means to achieve them.

An FDE is neither a "consultant who understands technology" nor an "engineer who can handle customers." It is a role that moves between both, handled by one person. However, there is a clear priority in the order in which you invest learning time.

CategoryPositioningElements to prioritize most
Non-technical skillsThe foundation that determines pass/failStructuring meeting notes, managing expectations, specifying requirements in the customer's language
Technical skillsMeans to realize problem definitionSQL/data shaping, APIs and external integrations

What is the priority order within technical skills?

On the technical side, data handling comes first. Data shaping with SQL and Python is the foundation for all other technologies.

PrioritySkill areaReason
HighSQL/data shapingCustomer data is always messy and becomes the first barrier
HighAPIs and external integrationsSituations requiring connection to existing systems almost always arise
MediumWeb application implementationAgreement can only be reached once customers can actually touch it
MediumCloud and permission designDirectly tied to operational handover and security requirements
LowMachine learning modelingSome projects need it, but aggregation and visualization are usually enough

Machine learning is ranked low not because it is unimportant, but because in most early FDE projects, the majority of the work is the stage of "first making the current state accurately visible." If you skip visualization and build a model, you cannot explain its effect because there are no baseline figures to compare against.

How much non-technical skill is needed?

Non-technical skills are needed to the level that determines pass/fail. In particular, structuring meeting notes and the ability to manage expectations are what get tested.

What works in the field is not flashy presentation skills, but the following three unglamorous fundamentals.

Fundamental practiceWhat it looks like in the field
Structuring meeting notesBeing able to write down conclusions and action items on the spot during a meeting
Managing expectationsBeing able to clearly communicate early on what cannot be done
Specifying in the customer's languageBeing able to write specifications using the customer's own wording as-is

Even at a company that handles field data like VACAN Technologies, if collaboration with external partners stalls on these three points, rework occurs before technology even comes into play. I cannot say for certain, but the more an environment functions with FDE-style working, the more thoroughly these fundamentals seem to be practiced.

Since non-technical skills are hard to develop from books alone, deliberately create opportunities to practice. Even securing a few chances per month to state conclusions first in front of others—such as moderating study sessions, drafting minutes for internal meetings, or explaining reviews to the customer role—will produce change.

How should learning outcomes be captured in a portfolio?

Keep an FDE portfolio not as a collection of works but as a record of decision-making, structured around five points from problem to limitations.

What is evaluated in FDE selection is the process of judgment, not the polish of the finished product. Therefore, deliverables created at each stage of the learning roadmap must be kept together with the reasoning behind your decisions.

What should be kept as deliverables?

Keep the working product and the reasoning behind its decisions. Writing the background and rejected alternatives in the README conveys the reproducibility of your judgment.

The recommended structure is as follows.

ItemWhat to write
1. ProblemWhose work was stuck, and how
2. HypothesisWhat you thought changing would improve things
3. ImplementationTechnologies used, and technologies not chosen and why
4. ValidationWhat you measured and what the results were
5. LimitationsThe scope this deliverable cannot handle

The fifth item, "Limitations," tends to be overlooked. However, in FDE selection it is read as material demonstrating honesty and understanding of operations. Even validations that failed are, as a record of learning, actually valuable. Conversely, a portfolio showing only success stories hides the decision-making process and is hard to evaluate.

How much can be written while respecting confidentiality obligations?

If you are under confidentiality obligations, you can still write it up by abstracting only the structure and decisions, without exposing customer names, actual figures, or screens as-is.

When using work from your current job as material, abstract the industry one level up (e.g., "multi-store retail operations" rather than "retail") and replace figures with ratios or orders of magnitude. Since the reasoning behind decisions often does not constitute confidential information, centering your portfolio on "why that design was chosen" allows you to create a substantial, content-rich portfolio. Checking your employer's policies before publishing, and making that a personal rule, is also a safe practice.

Management categoryContent to includePurpose
Public versionAbstracted structure and decision-making rationaleWeb publication, application documents
Private versionSpecific details and backgroundPreparation for verbal explanations during selection

Even deliverables that are difficult to publish will not go to waste if you organize them on hand so you can explain them verbally during the selection process. It is realistic to manage them separately as public and private versions.

What can I do when I can't keep up my studies?

Giving up on FDE learning is a matter of design, not willpower. Building deadlines and the eyes of others into your study plan makes it sustainable.

The FDE learning roadmap covers a broad range, and its structure makes it easy to stall midway when studying alone. It is more reliable to identify the cause of losing momentum first and compensate with systems.

Where does the cause of giving up lie?

The main cause of giving up is the level of abstraction of your goal. A goal like "become an FDE" cannot be converted into action, so getting started keeps getting postponed.

There are three common ways people get stuck.

Sticking pointWhat is happening
Scope is too broadYou rethink where to start every time
Deliverables never get finishedYou gain no sense of achievement, so motivation doesn't last
Studying alone, you can't tell what's correctYou lack confidence in whether your direction is valid

Any of these sticking points can be treated as a problem of learning design, not personal aptitude.

How do you build a system for staying consistent?

There are two key points to systematization: setting small deadlines on a two-week cycle, and always having an occasion to show your work to someone.

There are four concrete measures.

Concrete measureAim
1. Produce one "showable thing" every two weeks (unfinished is fine)Prevents the "deliverables never get finished" stall
2. Book your audience in advance (study groups, internal sharing, or even social media)Adds the eyes of others to your deadlines
3. Keep a study log in a public placeMakes gaps visible
4. At the end of each month, decide one study item to "drop"Keeps narrowing the scope

The work of an FDE itself follows a process of showing customers results in short cycles and iterating. Aligning your learning with this format means practice connects directly to real work. When you feel you can't keep going, the realistic adjustment is not to increase effort but to shorten the intervals between deadlines.

The methods described are not the official curriculum of any particular company, and the appropriate order varies depending on each individual's career situation.
