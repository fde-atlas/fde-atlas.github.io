How to Drive Change Management | 8 Steps and Practical Tips for FDEs to Embed Change in the Field

Original title: チェンジマネジメントの進め方｜FDEが現場定着させる8ステップと実務

FDE総研 · 2026-10-01 · 12 min read

Summary

This FDE Soken article argues that an FDE's work only pays off when new processes stick on-site, making change management a core skill alongside engineering. It maps John Kotter's 8-step model onto concrete FDE practices, flagging team-building (step 2) and cultural embedding (step 8) as the most common failure points. It contrasts Kotter's organization-level roadmap with Prosci's individual-level ADKAR model for diagnosing stalled teams, classifies resistance into rational concerns, emotional fears, and conflicting interests, and proposes measuring adoption across speed, breadth, depth, and sustainability—verifying usage persists after FDE involvement winds down to avoid dependency.

Key points

  • FDE success is judged by sustained on-site use, not a working system.
  • Kotter's 8 steps are translated into concrete FDE field practices.
  • Team-building and cultural embedding are the hardest steps for FDEs.
  • Kotter guides rollout planning; ADKAR diagnoses individual adoption stalls.
  • Measure adoption by speed, breadth, depth, and sustainability after FDE exit.

What is change management, and why do FDEs need it?

Change management is the effort to make a new system stick as part of everyday work in the field. The success of an FDE's work is also determined not by a "working system," but by whether it continues to be used on-site.

A Forward Deployed Engineer (FDE) is a role that embeds itself in the customer's field to implement data platforms and AI applications. Palantir is well known for establishing this role early on. However, even after implementation is complete, if the field's actual work doesn't change, the project delivers no value. That is why change management is needed as a second pillar alongside technical skill.

How does change management differ from "project management"?

Project management deals with deadlines and deliverables, while change management deals with changes in people's behavior. Because their subjects differ, both need to run in parallel.

AspectProject managementChange management
Main subjectTasks, schedules, budgetUsers' awareness, motivation, behavior
Definition of successReleased on scheduleThe field continues working in the new way
Representative methodsWBS, Gantt charts, ScrumKotter's 8 steps, ADKAR, Lewin's 3 stages
When it endsRelease and acceptanceWhen adoption is confirmed and there is no return to the old process

In an FDE's field, the release date tends to become the goal of project management. But from the perspective of the people on-site, that is when "working with an unfamiliar interface every day" begins. Change management's role is to bridge this time gap.

Why should FDEs take on change management as their own job?

FDEs are stationed in the field and positioned to understand both technology and business operations. That makes them the role best suited to naturally take on the role of change agent.

External consultants can draft strategies, but they cannot fix interface usability or data quality issues on the spot. Internal IT departments can implement systems, but they may not fully grasp the fine details of field operations. FDEs stand between them, turning complaints like "it's hard to use" into next-day fixes. This speed of response builds trust in the field and supports adoption.

Note that the figure "about 70% of organizational change efforts fail" is often cited. However, Mark Hughes's paper (Journal of Change Management, 2011) points out that this figure has little empirical support. When FDEs advocate for the need for change within a company, they preserve their credibility by avoiding reliance on figures with unclear sources. It is best to base arguments on the company's specific operational challenges.

What is the procedure for carrying out change management?

When FDEs drive change management, it works well to use John Kotter's 8 steps as the framework and map on-site implementation work to each step.

Kotter presented the 8-step process in his 1995 Harvard Business Review article "Leading Change: Why Transformation Efforts Fail" and his 1996 book Leading Change. The following is that 8-step process translated into FDE practice.

What are the 8 steps FDEs use to drive on-site adoption?

The 8 steps run from "sharing a sense of urgency" to "anchoring in culture." The first four build the foundation, and the last four change behavior.

#Kotter's stepConcrete FDE actions in practice
1Raise urgencyVisualize field data and show in numbers "what is being lost under the current way of working"
2Build a guiding coalitionIn addition to decision-makers, bring in respected field members early
3Develop a vision and strategyMake it explainable on one page: "whose work becomes easier, and how"
4Communicate the visionRather than a big all-hands briefing, visit each team's regular meetings and explain briefly
5Remove obstaclesFDEs directly eliminate lack of authority, data gaps, and duplicate entry with legacy systems
6Generate short-term winsStart with use cases that show visible results in roughly 4 to 8 weeks
7Build on wins to drive further changeUse the first successful team's case as material for rollout to neighboring departments
8Anchor new methods in cultureIncorporate the new ways into work procedures, evaluation metrics, and new-hire training

The "4 to 8 weeks" in step 6 is not a strict standard. It is a guideline for showing results before field interest fades. Palantir's "AIP Bootcamp," launched in 2023, is a program that shapes use cases with customers in just a few days, and it well reflects this emphasis on short-term wins.

Which of the 8 steps do FDEs most often stumble on?

The steps FDEs most often stumble on are building the guiding team in step 2 and anchoring in culture in step 8. That is because both are areas that technology alone cannot solve.

FDEs with an engineering background tend to excel at Step 5 (removing obstacles) and Step 6 (generating short-term wins). On the other hand, identifying key people in the field and winning them over as allies often gets postponed. In practical experience, what determines the speed of rollout is whether you can quickly find influential people—the kind whose adoption makes others follow—rather than people with formal titles.

Step 8 is also easily overlooked. Even if the system is used while the FDE is on site, it is not uncommon for teams to revert to the old process after the contract ends or the person in charge changes. To prevent this, you need to create a state where things run even without the FDE. Means to that end include documentation, an internal help desk, and embedding the system into KPIs.

How should Kotter and ADKAR be used differently?

Kotter is a model that organizes how to drive organization-wide change, while ADKAR organizes the stages an individual goes through when changing. For FDEs, using both together is the practical approach.

ADKAR is a model proposed by Jeff Hiatt, founder of Prosci, and was systematized in his 2006 book "ADKAR: A Model for Change in Business, Government and our Community." It takes its name from the initials of Awareness, Desire, Knowledge, Ability, and Reinforcement.

What is the difference between Kotter and ADKAR?

Kotter shows the order in which leaders move an organization, while ADKAR diagnoses where each individual is stuck. Their perspectives are at completely different levels.

Comparison axisKotter's 8 stepsADKAR
Proposer / first publishedJohn Kotter (1995 HBR article / 1996 book)Jeff Hiatt / Prosci (systematized in a 2006 book)
PerspectiveOrganization / leadersIndividuals / users
Number of stages85
Best suited forDesigning the overall roadmap for changeDiagnosing the causes of struggling departments or individuals
Where FDEs use itImplementation planning, explaining to managementInterviewing teams whose adoption is not growing
CaveatsCan become rigid in environments that do not proceed in orderMakes it hard to see organizational structure and authority issues

In which situations does an FDE use which?

Use Kotter when drawing up the implementation plan, and once rollout has begun, use ADKAR to isolate causes when departments with low adoption appear. This division of use is the basic rule.

For example, suppose only one department has low usage of a new system. Interviewing them along ADKAR's five stages lets you isolate the cause as follows.

  • Awareness: Has the reason for the change been communicated in the first place?
  • Desire: Do they understand the reason but feel "there is nothing in it for me"?
  • Knowledge: Did they have an opportunity to be taught how to use it?
  • Ability: Even with the knowledge, can they actually use it within their real workload?
  • Reinforcement: Is using it not rewarded, making the temptation to return to the old procedures strong?

Additional training is warranted only when Knowledge is lacking. If the cause lies in Desire, more training will likely have little effect. In that case, you need to show concrete benefits for that department or review the evaluation system. When an FDE can make this diagnosis in the language of the field, it becomes easier to choose the right intervention.

How does an FDE deal with resistance in the field?

The basic principle is to treat field resistance not as "someone to persuade" but as "information that reveals design flaws." FDEs are in a position to connect that feedback directly to product improvement.

Resistance to change is a natural reaction. Even in Kurt Lewin's three-stage model of "unfreeze, change, refreeze," the difficulty of first "unfreezing" existing practices is taken as a given.

What kinds of field resistance are there?

Resistance falls broadly into three types: rational concerns, emotional anxiety, and conflicts of interest. The effective response differs completely depending on the type.

Type of resistanceTypical voices heard in the fieldEffective response
Rational concerns"This screen can't handle exception cases"Accept it as a specification issue and propose fixes or workarounds
Emotional anxiety"Will I lose my job?"Explain concretely how roles will change and define new roles together
Conflicts of interest"This department will lose its discretion"Don't handle it alone in the field; coordinate with the promotion team and decision-makers

Engineers tend to treat all resistance as "rational concerns" and try to solve it with feature additions. But responding to emotional anxiety with feature additions can actually make the problem worse. When you hear resistance, first judge which type it is; this makes it easier to avoid wasted rework.

What is the FDE-specific way of dealing with resistance?

The FDE's strength is being able to look at the same screen as the field and deliver small improvements on the spot. This "you ask, we fix" experience turns resistance into cooperation.

The following actions tend to be effective in practice.

  • Sit next to them and observe: Don't just interview; watch the actual operations from beside them. You will find inconveniences that are never put into words.
  • Return small fixes quickly: Prioritize requests that can be fixed in a few hours, such as label wording or the order of fields, so people experience that "if we say it, it changes."
  • Invite opponents early: Inviting strongly opposed staff as test users can pay off—they often give high-quality feedback and become powerful champions after adoption.
  • Be honest about what you can't do: Leaving unaddressed requests vague damages trust.

On the other hand, if an FDE implements every field request individually, the product becomes a custom build for each customer. In a model like Palantir's that combines a product with FDEs, judging "can this request be generalized as a product improvement?" and feeding it back to the headquarters product team is also considered an important part of the FDE's job.

How do you measure whether change has taken hold?

Measure adoption not just by usage rate, but by combining depth of use with "whether people have reverted to the old process." Judging by numbers immediately after release is too early.

Along with ADKAR, Prosci identifies three perspectives for measuring change outcomes: "Speed of Adoption," "Ultimate Utilization," and "Proficiency." In FDE practice, these become easier to work with when translated into concrete metrics.

What metrics should be used to measure adoption?

In practice, adoption is best measured along four axes: speed, breadth, depth, and sustainability. If any one of these is missing, you risk overlooking a situation where the system only "appears to be in use."

Measurement axisExample metricsHow to collect data
SpeedNumber of days until a target user first uses the systemAccess logs
BreadthPercentage of target users who use the system at least once a weekAccess logs, account management
DepthUsage rate of key features, percentage of workflows completed entirely within the new systemFeature-specific logs, reconciliation with business records
SustainabilityExtent to which parallel work in the old system or Excel persistsOld system logs, on-site interviews
Business outcomesFigures directly tied to the purpose of the implementation, such as processing time and error countsBusiness KPIs

Sustainability is especially easy to overlook. Even if logins to the new system are increasing, traditional Excel-based management may continue behind the scenes, resulting in duplicate data entry. By checking not only the new system's logs but also how much of the old process remains, FDEs can gauge adoption in a way that reflects reality.

When and at what points should measurement take place?

Ideally, measurement should be conducted at three points: immediately after release, a few months later, and after FDE involvement has been reduced. The last point indicates true adoption.

  • Immediately after release (a few weeks): Check the speed of first use and any operational stumbling blocks
  • A few months later: See whether weekly usage rates and key feature usage rates have stabilized
  • After FDE involvement is reduced: Confirm that usage is maintained even after on-site presence and support are scaled back

The third measurement serves as evidence for determining whether Kotter's Step 8, "Anchoring New Approaches in the Culture," has been achieved. A system that only works while the FDE is present is, strictly speaking, not adoption but "dependence on the FDE." For an FDE's career as well, the ability to build a self-sustaining operation and then move on to the next site is the kind of track record most likely to be valued.

Decision-makers at companies considering FDE engagements are advised to incorporate both "a plan to gradually reduce FDE involvement" and "subsequent measurement of adoption" from the contract and staffing design stage. Doing so makes it easier to ensure that the investment in the FDE does not end as a temporary success but is retained as an organizational capability.
