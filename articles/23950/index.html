How to Acquire Domain Knowledge as an FDE | 5 Steps and How to Verify Your Proficiency

Original title: FDEのドメイン知識の身につけ方｜5つの手順と習得度の確かめ方

FDE総研 · 2026-10-06 · 11 min read

Summary

This FDE総研 article explains how forward deployed engineers (FDEs) can quickly acquire domain knowledge. It argues FDEs need deep understanding of a specific customer's business rather than broad industry expertise, focusing on how processes, data, and decisions connect. Knowledge is organized into four layers—regulation, business processes, data/systems, and organizational decision-making—with processes and data being the best starting points. The author proposes a five-step method: building a glossary, shadowing on-site work, cross-checking data against processes, validating hypotheses with small prototypes, and documenting findings. It also offers a five-level self-assessment scale, signals of customer trust, and three common pitfalls for newcomers.

Key points

  • FDEs should master one customer's business deeply, not industry generalities.
  • Domain knowledge has four layers; start with processes and data/systems.
  • Five steps: glossary, shadowing, data cross-checking, prototype validation, documentation.
  • Progress is measured via a five-level self-check and changing customer questions.
  • Newcomer pitfalls: bluffing, relying on industry clichés, single-source interviews.

What is the domain knowledge an FDE needs?

The domain knowledge an FDE needs is an understanding that allows you to explain the structure of the customer's business processes, data, and decision-making in the field's own words.

It differs in substance from textbook industry knowledge. An FDE does not need to be an "industry expert." What is required is becoming an "expert in this customer's specific business." While consultants often talk about industry-wide trends, an FDE's results are measured by whether the product is actually used in the field. For this reason, even if the scope of knowledge is narrow, depth and accuracy are required.

Why domain knowledge is indispensable for FDEs

Since FDEs are responsible for everything from problem discovery to implementation and adoption at the customer's site, domain knowledge affects results as much as technical skill does.

Forward Deployed Engineer (FDE) is a role said to have been popularized by Palantir Technologies. Within Palantir, a role close to the FDE was called "Delta," distinguished from "Dev" on the product development side. This has been introduced on the company's official blog and elsewhere. An FDE who delivers value alongside the customer cannot decide what to build or what to prioritize without understanding the customer's business. This article organizes, from a practical perspective, the mindset and 5 steps for deepening industry understanding in a short period.

Rather than industry knowledge, the "correspondence between business and data" is the core

What an FDE should understand first is not industry generalities. It is the correspondence of which business processes generate which data, and who makes what decisions based on it.

For example, even if you know the term "yield rate" in manufacturing, you cannot build a data pipeline without knowing which process, which system, and in what units it is recorded at the customer's factory. Conversely, even without knowing the industry's history or market size, you can build the first prototype if you grasp the correspondence between business and data. From a practical standpoint, the faster an FDE can draw this "map of correspondences," the faster and more concrete their conversations with the customer tend to become.

Domain knowledge can be organized into 4 layers

By dividing domain knowledge into 4 layers—regulations, business processes, data, and organization—the order of learning and information sources become clear.

LayerMain contentsExamplesMain sources
Regulations and business customsLaws, industry rules, trading practicesHandling of personal information, audit requirements, contract typesIndustry association materials, laws, customer's legal department
Business processesWorkflow, exception handling, busy seasonsFrom order receipt to shipment, month-end closing processesBusiness manuals, field observation, interviews
Data and systemsTable structures, update frequency, qualityCore system master data, Excel-based operationsData definition documents, checking actual data, IT department
Decision-making and organizationWho decides what, evaluation metrics (KPIs)Scope of a site manager's discretion, metrics used in management meetingsOrg charts, meeting materials, dialogue with key people

Of the 4 layers, the ones an FDE can tackle right after joining are "business processes" and "data and systems." "Regulations and business customs" and "decision-making and organization" are areas that often become visible over time as you build relationships in the field. By consciously starting with the 2 easier layers and continuously deepening understanding of the remaining 2, it becomes easier to plan your learning.

How do you acquire domain knowledge?

You acquire domain knowledge through 5 steps—building a glossary, observing operations, reconciling data, validating hypotheses, and articulating—while going back and forth with the field.

Knowledge gained solely through study rarely becomes usable in conversations with the customer. The distinctive feature of how FDEs learn is that they do not separate learning from hands-on work, deepening understanding while building the product. The 5 steps are summarized below.

StepWhat to doDeliverableCompletion criteria

2. Observe operationsSit in with the field and watch the workBusiness flow diagram and exception listYou can explain the workflow in your own words
3. Reconcile dataCompare actual data with business processesData correspondence tableYou can explain the meaning and origin of key fields

Step 1: Build a glossary in the first week

What you should do in the first week is collect the customer's unique terminology and create a glossary listing definitions and how each term is used.

Even with the same word "inventory," departments often differ on whether it refers to "book inventory" or "physical inventory in the warehouse." The concept of the "ubiquitous language" proposed by Eric Evans in Domain-Driven Design is directly useful in FDE work as well. It is important not to leave the glossary as a personal memo, but to share it with the customer and have them point out errors. The very exchange of correcting mistakes becomes a great learning opportunity.

Step 2: Observe operations in the field

Rather than reading business manuals first, doing "shadowing"—watching the actual work next to the person in charge—leads to faster and deeper understanding.

Manuals often only describe the "ideal flow," and the exception handling and manual workarounds that actually consume time in the field are invisible without observation. During observation, asking "why is this the order?" and "what problems would arise if this task didn't exist?" makes the purpose of the work easier to understand.

Step 3: Cross-check data against business operations

Verify one by one, using actual data, which table and which fields record the business processes you observed.

This is where FDEs with engineering backgrounds can shine the most. Missing fields, inconsistent values, and misaligned update timing often reflect business-side circumstances, such as differences among the people entering the data or the timing of closing processes. In practice, it is effective to read data "dirtiness" as a clue for understanding the business.

Step 4: Validate small hypotheses with prototypes

Even with incomplete understanding, building a small dashboard or tool and showing it to the field helps you quickly find gaps in your knowledge.

When you show it and ask, "Could you make your daily morning decisions with this screen?", the field will often respond with concrete feedback like "This number is too late if it's from the previous day" or "This categorization doesn't match reality." The advantage of prototype validation is that it can draw out tacit knowledge that interviews alone cannot surface.

Step 5: Articulate and share what you learned

Finally, organize the domain knowledge you learned into documentation so it can be shared with your team and with people who join later.

In FDE work, knowledge tends to accumulate in individual heads. Articulating it helps not only the team but also helps you find holes in your own understanding. The places where you get stuck finding words when trying to explain are usually the places where your understanding is shallow.

Can you catch up quickly even in an unfamiliar industry?

Even in an unfamiliar industry, if you narrow your learning to the target business processes, you can acquire the understanding needed to hold an initial discussion with the customer in a relatively short time.

However, what you can reach in a short time is only the level where you can start conversations with the customer. It is not the level where you can make the same judgments as veteran field practitioners. Being aware of this difference is important for maintaining the customer's trust.

Compete on "depth in the target business," not on "knowing everything"

The shortcut for newcomers to catch up is not to broadly learn the entire industry. It is to dig deeply into a single point of the business you are responsible for.

For the industry's overall picture, it is often enough to get a rough grasp from industry associations' public materials and introductory books. Spend the remaining time focusing on observing the business you handle and checking the data. From a practical standpoint, if you can quickly build one area where the customer's staff think "ask this person about that process and you'll get answers," subsequent interviews and consensus-building become easier.

Identify areas where you can leverage your previous experience

Even if your previous industry was different, if there are areas with similar business structures, much of the knowledge you gained can be used as is.

Experience from previous jobEasily transferable areasTips for transfer
EC and web service developmentRetail, store operations, demand forecastingHandling customer behavior data and inventory data
Business system (ERP, etc.) developmentManufacturing, logistics, back officeMaster data management, closing processes, permission design
Data analysis and BIFinance, healthcare, public sectorMetric definitions, data quality management
IoT and embedded developmentManufacturing sites, infrastructure, facility managementSensor data, real-time processing

For example, in a business like VACAN that handles data generated from stores and facilities, experience with IoT and real-time data can be useful even in a different industry. If you separate what is transferable and what is not at the outset, it becomes easier to prioritize your learning.

Three common pitfalls for newcomers

Common stumbling blocks for newcomers are three: pretending to know, relying on generalities, and over-relying on a single speaker.

PitfallWhat happensHow to avoid it
Pretending to knowYou gloss over unfamiliar terms with guesswork, and design assumptions later collapseConfirm on the spot. That actually earns more trust
Relying on generalities"This is normal in the industry" knowledge doesn't apply to the customer in front of youPrioritize the customer's actual data and business observation over industry conventions
Over-relying on a single speakerYou only hear from the point of contact and miss gaps between the field, management, and executive perspectivesTalk to multiple people in different positions

How do you measure your domain knowledge proficiency?

Measure proficiency with action-based criteria, such as whether your explanations make sense to the customer's staff and whether you can predict edge cases.

Domain knowledge proficiency cannot be measured with scores like a certification exam. Therefore, it is realistic to verify it using both self-judged criteria and the reactions you get back from the customer.

Grasp your current position with a 5-level self-check

You can grasp your current position by judging it across 5 levels, from "understanding the terminology" to "making proposals to the customer."

LevelStateHow to verify
1. Terminology understandingCan understand most meeting terminologyCan you write meeting minutes accurately yourself?
2. Business explanationCan explain business flows to othersCan you explain the business flow to team members?
3. Data explanationCan explain the meaning and origin of key dataCan you explain the reasons for missing fields or anomalous values?
4. Edge case predictionCan predict edge cases and problemsCan you point out risks before the field does?
5. ProposalCan propose business improvements in the customer's languageAre your proposals used directly in the customer's discussions?

Levels 1 to 3 are standards to reach in the early stages of a project, while levels 4 to 5 are reached through continued involvement. Having the mindset of raising your level one step at a time helps you avoid excessive impatience.

Regularly check the customer's reactions

Rather than relying solely on self-assessment, regularly reviewing the customer's reactions helps you notice gaps in your understanding early.

An easy-to-spot sign is a change in the content of the questions the customer asks you. If the questions shift from requests for explanation like "What is this?" to consultations like "How should this case be handled?", it is evidence that your domain understanding is starting to be trusted. It is also effective to make a habit of explicitly asking at weekly meetings, "Is there anything where our understanding differs?"

Keep updating domain knowledge for each customer

For an FDE, domain knowledge is not something you acquire once and are done with. It is an asset you keep updating every time the customer changes.

The five steps—glossary building, business observation, data cross-checking, hypothesis validation, and articulation—can be repeated in the same form even when the customer or industry you handle changes. As you repeat the steps, you acquire not just knowledge of a specific industry, but "the way of learning itself" that works in any industry.
