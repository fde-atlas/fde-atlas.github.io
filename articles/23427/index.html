What does a manufacturing Forward Deployed Engineer (FDE) do?

Original title: 制造业前线部署工程师（FDE）做什么

FDE Times · 2026-11-17 · 5 min read

Summary

This article contrasts two manufacturing FDE job postings—Instrumental requires real factory-floor experience, while Re:Build Manufacturing asks for 3–5 years of software experience. It argues that industrial AI projects rarely stall on models; the bottleneck is the integration layer with operational technology, where data lives in fragmented sources like OSIsoft PI and ODBC exports, and sensor drift must be caught in the data pipeline. FDEs can own data engineering, but production-line domain knowledge stays with factory engineers, making the FDE a translator between OT and IT. It closes with practical advice for software engineers seeking such roles.

Key points

  • Instrumental demands genuine shop-floor experience; Re:Build hires software engineers instead.
  • The real bottleneck is the OT integration layer, not the model itself.
  • Sensor drift must be detected in the data pipeline before retraining models.
  • Domain knowledge stays with factory engineers; FDEs translate between OT and IT.
  • Aspiring FDEs should build multi-source connectors and drift-detection projects for their portfolios.

In manufacturing, FDEs often have to do the data engineering themselves, while the truly critical knowledge on the production line remains in the hands of the factory's own engineers.

Key Takeaways

  • Instrumental requires FDEs to have actually worked on a factory floor, while Re:Build hires people with 3–5 years of software experience and has FDEs work alongside the factory's production, engineering, and quality teams.
  • The bottleneck for industrial AI lies in the integration layer with OT: historians store data in wildly varying formats, and sensor drift must be handled in the data pipeline.
  • FDEs can take on the data engineering, but domain knowledge still depends on factory engineers; some sources suggest creating a dedicated role that "translates" between OT and IT.

In Instrumental's job posting for a Forward Deployed Manufacturing Engineer, one line stands out for its unusual bluntness: candidates must have actually worked on a factory or contract manufacturer floor, "not next to it, but in it."

This year, Re:Build Manufacturing also hired an on-site Forward Deployed Engineer (FDE), but with very different requirements: 3–5 years of software development, technical support, or deployment experience, plus AWS.

Both companies are sending FDEs into factories, but they give different answers to a question every engineer moving into industry will face. That answer determines what you should practice and where you should send your resume.

The answer has two halves. Data engineering, the FDE can carry—and in practice has no choice. But production line knowledge always stays with the factory's people, and the FDE's real job is to stand between the two sides as a "translator."

The First 90 Days Start with Questions

The classic FDE model comes from Palantir. According to a16z, engineers at Palantir—internally called "Deltas" and "Echoes"—sit inside the customer's organization and own what they build end to end, rather than delivering and leaving.

One Palantir engineer described the start of an energy sector project. The first thing the team did on site was decide which problem to solve within 90 days. To do that, they interviewed the customer and read as much as they could about the energy market.

This detail shows that engineers don't arrive with domain knowledge; they learn on the job. But learning enough to pick the right problem is one thing; understanding deeply enough to replace someone who has spent years on the production line is another.

Re:Build's job description draws this boundary quite clearly. Its FDE works directly with the factory's production, engineering, and quality teams to solve problems quickly on site. Domain knowledge stays with those teams; the FDE's job is to connect.

An analysis of manufacturing AI by fde.academy adds that factory engineers tend to be cautious. Integration with OT systems requires extra care and usually must be done together with them. In other words, there are things an FDE, no matter how capable, cannot do alone.

The Heaviest Work Is in the Integration Layer

Domain knowledge can be borrowed from the factory; data cannot. Industrial Monitor Direct's analysis of industrial AI deployments concludes that the bottleneck repeatedly appears in the integration layer between AI systems and a factory's operational technology—the model is rarely where things get stuck.

The reason lies in the data itself. A single factory's data may be scattered across OSIsoft PI (now part of AVEVA), Wonderware InSQL, and ODBC exports in all sorts of formats. Consolidating them into one usable place is a data engineer's worth of work.

Then there are the sensors. fde.academy stresses that drifting or faulty sensors must be handled in the data pipeline; handling them only at the model layer is not enough. That is data engineering, and according to fde.academy, it is precisely the FDE who takes on this work.

A Drifting Sensor Draws the Line of Division

Take a hypothetical example. You build a model that predicts product defects on a production line, pulling furnace temperatures from PI and reading quality inspection results from ODBC files exported each shift. A few weeks later, the model starts reporting defects in batches, but the actual scrap rate hasn't changed.

If you only look at the model layer, the common reaction is to retrain. That way, the model treats the wrong values as normal, and real defects slip through.

The right approach is to add a step in the data pipeline: compare this sensor against a reference sensor, flag it when the deviation exceeds a threshold, and hold the data back rather than feeding it straight into the model.

Writing that check is the FDE's job. But knowing that it's the sensor drifting and the furnace isn't actually overheating, or that this sensor was just calibrated last week, relies on the factory engineers' knowledge. Without either side, that check either can't be written or gets the wrong threshold.

The Person Standing Between OT and IT

a16z argues that the Palantir model depends on an extremely scarce kind of talent: people with both business and technical abilities, who can understand the customer's problem and build the system with their own hands.

In a factory, the bar is even higher, because domain knowledge there means understanding every sensor and every shift. The combination of business and engineering is already scarce, and expecting a newly arrived FDE to replace someone who has spent years next to the machines is a gamble that's hard to build a hiring plan around.

Industrial Monitor Direct's article offers a more pragmatic path: create a dedicated role that "translates" between OT and IT, rather than splitting that work across multiple teams.

The person in this role doesn't need to know the production line as well as a factory engineer, but must know how to ask the right questions and speak both sides' language. That is exactly how Re:Build positions its FDE.

Palantir's deployment teams work from a small set of reusable components, spending their time selecting and validating the right components for each customer.

They don't rewrite everything from scratch each time. The time saved goes into the integration layer, which is where projects most often get stuck.

So the real difference between the two job postings is where the domain knowledge lives. Re:Build is willing to leave it with the factory teams; Instrumental wants part of it already installed in the head of an FDE who has stood on a shop floor.

Which Path to Take

If you're a software engineer with 2–8 years of experience who has never set foot in a factory, roles like Re:Build's are a realistic entry point. They require software development, technical support, or deployment experience—things you already have. What you lack is industrial data, and that you can practice on your own.

Practice on the place where projects most often get stuck. Write a connector that reads time-series data from multiple sources in different formats, then add a step that detects sensor drift before the data enters the model. A small project like that on GitHub carries far more weight than a carefully polished model-training notebook.

The second skill is asking questions. The Palantir engineer mentioned above started by interviewing customers and reading industry material. You can practice this in your current job: the next time the operations team gives you a requirement, don't write code right away—ask them how they spot bad data.

When reading job postings, look for the sentence describing who you'll work with. Phrasing like "collaborate with production, engineering, and quality teams" means the domain knowledge stays with the factory.

If it says real factory floor experience is required, the company expects you to be the domain expert yourself, and no online course can close that gap.

On your resume, avoid vague lines like "built a defect prediction model." Spell out which data sources you integrated, which data errors you caught in the data pipeline, and who on the operations side you worked with. The people hiring FDEs for factories will read these sentences to judge whether you can handle the part of the project where things most often get stuck.

A 90-day factory project doesn't need someone who knows everything. It needs someone the factory engineers trust enough to tell them which sensor is lying.
