Table Talk: FDEs, Bosses, and AI Tools — Three Hurdles

Original title: 饭桌旁听：FDE、老板与 AI 工具的三道坎

53AI · 2026-08-04 · 13 min read

Summary

Based on an overheard lunch conversation between sales and technical people, this article examines three questions: what an FDE (Forward Deployed Engineer) actually does, why SMEs treat AI as an efficiency tool rather than a strategic need, and why AI adoption must be initiated by the boss. The author argues FDEs are not senior implementers but people who embed AI into client systems, reduce friction, and turn field experience into reusable delivery methods. They suit ToB AI vendors, complex enterprises, and compliance-heavy industries. For SMEs, AI pays off only when savings convert into revenue. Employee resistance stems from uncertainty, so bosses must set stable expectations, clarify accountability, and start with AI as an assistant before increasing automation.

Key points

  • FDEs bring AI to the client's front line and make it reliably usable.
  • Their value lies in reducing friction and building reusable delivery methods.
  • SMEs pay for AI mainly when it directly helps make money.
  • Bosses must champion AI adoption and stabilize employee expectations.
  • AI needs to be usable by staff, manageable by tech, and explainable to bosses.

Full text

Contents: The Six Guys at the Next Table; What Is an FDE; Who Really Needs an FDE; SMEs' "Non-Strategic Needs"; Why It Has to Be Initiated by the Boss; Turning AI from a Tool into a Method; Written After Leaving the Restaurant

I didn't go there to "do research." I just went out for lunch; the restaurant was full, and the waiter seated me at a small table by the aisle. Six men at the next table weren't speaking loudly, but the density was high: a single sentence could contain "overseas," "CTO," "front line," "delivery," "channels," "boss," and "replacement anxiety." It was hard not to get drawn in.

They ordered the kind of "all-in-one" meal: rice with a few side dishes—quick to eat, even quicker to talk over. They roughly split into two groups: one that sounded like sales, one that sounded like technical. In the middle sat two people who seemed "more like leaders," speaking at a slower pace, but every sentence set a direction.

The technical side opened first, with a touch of "character introduction": the guy across from them had studied overseas, had been exposed to AI for over a decade; he had previously been a CTO at some big company (he didn't name it); then he left to start his own business, now working on a tool related to "image design." The sales side didn't rush to argue back, just asked one question: "So what are you most afraid of right now?"

The technical guy smiled and named three fears: fearing clients think too big but land too slowly; fearing employees resist from day one; and fearing that in the end everyone talks about models, and no one talks about "who will actually deliver the models to the front line."

Then the conversation naturally landed on three points: what the buzzword FDE (Forward Deployed Engineer) actually is; what it means that AI tools are "a need but not a strategic need" for SMEs; and why AI tools usually have to be initiated by the boss to gain traction.

The term "FDE, Forward Deployed Engineer" has indeed been buzzing lately. The technical guy at the next table put it bluntly: it's not the person writing proposals in the office, but the person who takes the solution to the customer's site, to the very front line of the business, and makes it actually run.

You can think of an FDE as a kind of "engineering translator." He has to understand what technical colleagues say—"models, plugins, RAG, agents, permissions, call chains"—as well as what frontline colleagues say—"can this button require fewer clicks, can this process be faster, can this result be more stable." More importantly, he has to translate both sides' language into executable actions: which systems to integrate, which processes to change, what permissions to add, how to roll out gradually, and who takes the blame when things go wrong.

A one-sentence definition: If pre-sales is responsible for articulating the "possibilities," and R&D for building the "capabilities," then the FDE is responsible for turning "possibilities" into "habits": getting frontline people to use it every day, smoothly and with confidence.

The technical side mentioned a phenomenon: in the past, companies bought software and were buying a "system"; now companies buying AI are buying "results." A system can be pushed through with training and policies; if results don't materialize, they're immediately questioned: Why do we still have to copy and paste manually after integrating a model? Why do answers to the same type of question vary in quality day to day? Why does it stop working the moment we switch departments?

AI makes "delivery" more like an engineering discipline: you have to integrate data, set up permissions, control risk, write prompts, iterate on evals, handle exceptions, and polish things together with the business. If any link breaks down, what the business perceives isn't "slightly off" but "completely useless." As customers care more and more about "genuinely usable," a role that sits further forward naturally becomes necessary.

In the discussion at the next table, the FDE's task list wasn't written out as items, but I could group it into three parts:

Part one: Deployment and integration. Embedding AI capabilities into the customer's existing systems: how the knowledge base retrieves data, how permissions are inherited, how logs are kept, how call chains are traced, how sensitive information is masked. A good FDE turns "it runs" into "it runs in a controlled way."

Part two: Scenario and process redesign. Many companies don't lack a model; they lack a design for "replacing the original process with the model": which steps let AI produce the first draft, which steps require human review, which steps can be fully automated. FDEs often have to sit next to the business, watching how they click, how they complain, how they work around the system, and then turn these "real behaviors" into redesign plans.

Part three: Evals and iteration. AI's problems aren't always "errors"; more often they're "wrong answers." You need to build eval sets, define metrics, run A/B tests, locate failure cases, and then go back to prompts, knowledge bases, tool calls, and permission policies to fix things. This work is neither building big features like pure R&D nor watching resources like pure ops—it sits in between, continuously pulling quality upward.

If you've done ToB projects, these roles all sound familiar: all go to the customer's site, all handle problems, all "get things done." The technical guy at the next table gave a vivid distinction: pre-sales is like "the person who explains the road clearly," delivery is like "the person who paves the road flat," ops is like "the person who makes sure the road doesn't break," while the FDE is like "the person who walks a stretch of the road with the customer."

The hard part of walking that road with them is that problems keep appearing that were "never written into the contract or the requirements": the business changes a process on short notice, a data source suddenly becomes unavailable, the permission system is more complex than expected, a department suddenly refuses to share knowledge, or a leader wants to see a demo that "works today." The FDE doesn't have to solve every problem with his own hands, but he must be able to break problems apart so that R&D, product, and pre-sales each catch their own piece.

The FDE's value often shows up as "reduced friction." What drains people most in AI projects isn't writing code once, but countless small frictions: every "wrong permission," every "unstable API," every time "the frontline would rather do it by hand than click a button." When an FDE does his job well, you can hardly see him directly in the reports, but you'll notice: fewer complaints, less rework, shorter launch cycles, smoother renewals. "What we fear most is a customer thinking they bought a smart colleague, only to find after delivery that all they got was a chat window."

After the technical guy at the next table said this, the sales side nodded quickly.

If you understand the FDE as "advanced implementation," it sounds like every company needs one. But in fact, it's more like a role that appears at a specific stage: when a company is already willing to put AI into its core processes, yet unwilling to hand the risk over to a "black box," it needs someone standing on the front line.

The first type: companies building ToB AI products. Especially those selling "capabilities" rather than "fixed systems." Capabilities need to be embedded, embedding requires being on-site, and on-site requires someone who can withstand "results are expected on day one." Many teams get pulled back and forth between pre-sales, R&D, and delivery, only to discover that what they lack most is someone who can string everything together.

The second type: clients with strong AI transformation needs and complex systems. For example, organizations with multiple business systems, scattered data sources, and strict permission systems. Without someone internally who "understands both the business and engineering," AI projects easily become "great demo, hard launch." Some clients call this role "AI Application Engineer" or "Head of Intelligent Implementation"—essentially very similar.

The third type: industries that emphasize compliance and accountability. When you must explain "why this answer was given," "where this content came from," and "who triggered what action and when," every step needs to be engineered: data, permissions, logs, rollback, gradual rollout. An FDE must understand both the technology and the boundaries.

The profile given at the next table was interesting: not "the strongest algorithms person," nor "the pre-sales person best at presenting slides," but someone who has experienced frontline setbacks.

If we must summarize, an FDE typically combines three abilities: being able to write code for integrations (at least able to independently diagnose problems), being able to understand business processes and willing to stay on-site, and being able to build a reusable delivery methodology instead of firefighting by hand.

Many teams treat the FDE as a "stopgap": whatever R&D can't finish gets pushed to him, whatever the customer is pressing hardest on gets dumped on him. But this usage quickly kills the role, because the FDE's value isn't in being universal glue—it's in distilling on-site experience, time after time, into reusable components, templates, and eval systems, so that the next delivery is lighter and more stable.

The second point of contention at the next table was sharper: SMEs certainly need AI tools, but often it's not a strategic need, because what SMEs face is a survival problem. Tools can only improve efficiency, and don't necessarily bring immediate growth.

This sounds like "pouring cold water," but it's actually very real. For large companies, efficiency is part of strategy; for small companies, strategy is often squeezed thin by cash flow, orders, and team stability. When you talk to them about "organizational upgrades over the next two years," they care more about "whether we can sign two more deals this month."

AI tools can save time in many scenarios: writing copy, making summaries, researching, generating first drafts, auto-archiving. But the time saved doesn't necessarily become new revenue, unless the company can reinvest it into activities that generate orders.

The most common situation for SMEs is: after saving a person-hour, the boss doesn't suddenly have a "growth project to reinvest in," so the efficiency dividend stays at "feeling a bit lighter" and rarely shows up in the financial statements.

The salesperson at the next table made a very pragmatic observation: in SMEs, strategy isn't a "vision," it's more like a "bet." Betting means opportunity cost: invest in this, and you can't invest in that; pursue this, and you might delay current orders. So when you say "this is a strategic investment," the boss instinctively asks: if I bet on this, how long until I see returns? If I don't see returns, can I cut my losses in time?

This is why many AI tools get positioned in SMEs as "efficiency plugins": first control costs and speed up processes, then see whether efficiency can be converted into growth. If you try to elevate it to a "strategic level" right away, it often makes the boss more cautious.

Three levels for articulating value clearly Saving money: Can it reduce outsourcing, rework, and low-value manual labor? Saving time: Can it make key processes faster (e.g., bidding, pre-sales, delivery, customer service) and shorten the cycle from lead to payment? Making money: Can it directly reach more customers, improve conversion, raise average order value, or free up sales to spend more time on customers who will actually close? Many AI products speak fluently about the first two levels, but what actually makes SMEs willing to keep paying is usually the third.

If you want an SME to treat AI as strategy, you can't just show that "the model is powerful"—you have to make the returns visible: get a result in a shorter cycle, then solidify that result into a process.

The salesperson at the next table put it bluntly: "Don't treat customers as people who love new technology. Most bosses are more like captains running a ship, and what they care about most is whether it can hold up when the storm comes."

So in SMEs, the order of AI adoption is often reversed: instead of planning a grand blueprint first, grab the easiest-to-validate process and turn it into a small system that "gets used every day." Only after it's truly embedded in daily work will the boss start redefining it: from "tool" to "method," from "saving time" to "changing organizational habits."

Content and distribution: Not "generating articles," but turning content production into an assembly line: topic selection, material research, structure, first draft, review, distribution—each step embedded in the tool, so one person can sustain the output that used to take two or three.

Pre-sales and bidding: In many industries, bid documents and proposals are highly repetitive; what really takes time is research, finding templates, aligning formats, and fixing typos. If AI can raise "first-draft quality" and "format consistency," the value is very tangible.

Customer service and after-sales: Customer service teams aren't afraid of AI "writing beautifully"—they're afraid of wrong, slow, or inconsistent answers. Connecting the knowledge base, permissions, and tickets, letting AI first provide "reviewable suggestions" and then gradually increasing automation, is a safer path.

Internal collaboration and knowledge retention: In many small teams, knowledge is scattered in individual heads, and departures or role changes create gaps. AI won't automatically solve this, but it can become a new entry point: making the team willing to put materials in one unified place and write experience down as searchable snippets. Don't underestimate this—much of SMEs' "low efficiency" is essentially information not flowing.

The third issue has almost become a consensus: AI tools in the sales process are almost always initiated by the boss, because they naturally touch on employees' sense of security. Employees worry about being replaced, or that their experience will be "extracted" and turned into a replicable process.

This isn't "employees overthinking"—it's a very plain judgment: the smarter the tool, the more the role seems to be redefined. Even if the reality is "you won't be replaced by AI, only by people who know how to use it," the fear remains.

Many AI projects die from "everyone thinks it's great, but no one wants to use it first." If the boss just says "we need to embrace AI" in a meeting, it quickly becomes a slogan; the boss must turn it into organizational-level certainty: who is responsible for the pilot, whether failure counts against performance, how good usage is rewarded, and how accountability works when things go wrong.

In other words, the boss isn't paying for a tool, but for an organizational change. Without the boss's backing, any rollout will be slowly dragged to death by "we're busy," "it's unnecessary," and "afraid of making mistakes."

When I heard them discuss "employee resistance," one detail stung: what many employees resist isn't the tool itself, but the uncertainty in the process. Today you're asked to use AI for summaries, tomorrow for replying to customers, and the day after, "why haven't you learned it yet?" When the rules keep changing, the safest approach is to use it less, wait and see, and procrastinate.

So the key to the boss's backing isn't shouting slogans, but stabilizing expectations: which processes must use it and which can skip it; what counts as an error and who's responsible when it happens; for employees, is it a bonus or a performance metric. Only when expectations are stable does fear decrease.

The real concerns of the three "payers" The boss: Can it deliver results faster, and can I contain the risk? Frontline employees: Will it make me take the blame? Will it make me less important? Tech/management: Can it be integrated into permissions, auditing, and operations systems? Can problems be traced when they occur? If your product and solution only win over one of these people, the project will struggle to complete a full cycle.

The tech person at the next table didn't preach much, just offered a pragmatic approach: first let AI play an "assist" role, write the boundaries of responsibility clearly, then gradually increase automation. For example, in customer service, AI suggests and humans confirm; in pre-sales, AI writes the first draft and sales adds key insights; in engineering, AI generates unit tests or documentation rather than directly modifying mainline code.

Rather than arguing with employees about "whether you'll be replaced," show them something more concrete: which repetitive tasks this tool will free them from, where their professional judgment actually matters more, and whether their growth path becomes clearer as a result.

Someone on the sales side said something I remember clearly: "We're not selling AI; we're selling 'change the boss can control.'" It sounds like marketing, but it actually captures the underlying logic of B2B adoption: change itself isn't scary—losing control is. The boss: Can it deliver results faster, and can I contain the risk?

After the whole meal, my biggest takeaway was: what people were really discussing wasn't a particular model or set of features, but how AI earns its "reason for long-term existence" within an organization. This requires satisfying three conditions at once: usable by the frontline, manageable by tech, and explainable to the boss.

Many teams have experienced this frustration: dazzling in the demo, unused after launch. The cause usually isn't the model but the path: users have to switch back and forth between three or four systems, permissions are always wrong, answers occasionally spout nonsense, and no one knows who to turn to when something breaks. Any single friction point is enough to send the frontline back to their old methods.

So "landing" isn't about building the button—it's about smoothing out the friction bit by bit. You'll realize this is exactly where a role like the FDE is needed: making AI feel like a deliverable system rather than a one-off demo.

The first cost is the "cost of explanation." AI's output must be explainable—at least enough to explain "why I said this." Otherwise the frontline won't trust it, compliance won't approve it, and the boss can't back it. Many teams treat the cost of explanation as part of the "product experience," but it's more like the infrastructure of "organizational trust."

The second cost is the "maintenance cost." Knowledge goes stale, processes change, permissions get adjusted, and APIs get upgraded. Once an AI application enters the core of a business, it can't be treated like a one-off project that ends at delivery. There must be a mechanism among FDEs, product, engineering, and operations to support long-term maintenance; otherwise, the initial results will gradually decay, and the project will be labeled "AI isn't reliable."

When the table next to me talked about "evals," I strongly agreed: AI's success shouldn't rest on subjective impressions alone. Even if you don't build particularly sophisticated metrics, you should at least answer three questions: What is the usability rate of the answers? How much time does human review take? What percentage of frontline staff are willing to keep using it?

Small and medium-sized businesses especially need this kind of "visible success," because they have to judge in a very short time: Is this money worth it? Should we keep investing in this direction?

They mentioned that when that entrepreneur was building an image design tool, a salesperson asked a very practical question: "So are you selling to designers, or to people who don't know design?" The person thought for a moment and said that the ones truly willing to pay are often people who "don't want to learn design but have to do design": operations, marketing, sales, even the boss themselves.

That remark suddenly made me realize something: AI products easily get obsessed with "capability showcases," but the real commercial landing point is often "making something a person doesn't want to do, can't do well, yet has to do, easier." Whether it's image design, customer service replies, or first drafts of bid documents, the essence is the same.

At that table, the salespeople's language sounded more like "transactions": how do you get the boss willing to pay, how do you articulate the value more clearly. The engineers' language sounded more like "engineering": how do you make it run stably, how do you keep risks under control. On the surface the two sides often clash, but I actually think that's a good thing.

For products like AI, if you only have sales, it's easy to raise customer expectations to the sky; if you only have engineering, it's easy to turn the project into a long-term experiment. The teams that truly survive are usually the ones that can switch back and forth between these two languages: bold enough to make commitments, yet aware of boundaries; talking about results, but also about costs.

They finished eating and left quickly, leaving only a few empty bowls on the table. As I got up with my tray, it suddenly felt like watching a miniature version of an industry drama: an entrepreneur back from overseas, carrying the story of "an AI image design tool"; a group of salespeople trying to find the boss's pain points; a group of engineers trying to turn a hot concept into a system that actually runs.

If discussions about AI in past years were more about "capability," then nowadays more and more discussions inevitably revolve around "organization": who deploys it, who vouches for it, who bears the anxiety that change brings. That's also why FDEs are getting hot, why small and medium-sized businesses are more sensitive to "growth," and why the boss has to step onto the stage.

Perhaps for many years to come, we'll repeatedly run into the same problem: technology is moving faster and faster, but organizations aren't speeding up their own pace of change in step. What truly decides the outcome isn't how smart the models are, but whether we can turn that intelligence into something stable, trustworthy, and sustainable in everyday operations.
