For enterprise AI to make money, who should be the first hire? OpenAI's former Chief Research Officer has the answer.

Summary

This article distills Bob McGrew, former OpenAI chief research officer, on why companies adopting AI should first hire a Forward Deployed Engineer (FDE). His argument: models are ready, but enterprises lack someone who embeds on-site, understands messy real workflows, and ships working results within months. Drawing on his Palantir experience and startups like Castle and Happy Robot, he explains how FDEs turn pilots into large contracts, why AI agents cannot scale like standardized SaaS, and how product teams later abstract field-proven solutions into general capabilities, such as Palantir's ontology. He also outlines two FDE profiles: Echo (domain insiders who break old processes) and Delta (fast, pragmatic engineers).

Key points

  • McGrew says the first AI hire should be an FDE, not an architect or product manager.
  • FDEs iterate on-site, turning rough demos into deliverable products in about three months.
  • AI agents face bespoke workflows, so they cannot replicate like standardized SaaS.
  • Business model shifts from selling licenses to selling results; early deployments may lose money before margins improve.
  • Echo and Delta teams at Palantir show FDEs paving paths that product teams later scale.

Full text

What do enterprises fear most when adopting AI?

It's not immature technology, and it's not market rejection. It's this: the tool launches, and nobody uses it.

Bob McGrew, a key developer of ChatGPT and former Chief Research Officer at OpenAI, has deep insight into this. He led the development of key models like GPT-4 and O1, and today his advice is:

Don't build the platform first, don't hire architects first—first find people who can deliver results fast.

He's not talking about product managers, nor consultants,

but an emerging role that hundreds of YC companies are scrambling to hire:

FDE—Forward Deployed Engineer

On September 8, 2025, McGrew explained this role in full on YC's Lightcone podcast. He also published a post on X (formerly Twitter) emphasizing his core methodology:

"Turn the unscalable into a scalable pattern first (Doing things that don't scale — at scale)."

The message is simple: build people and processes first, not technology.

Success stories like Claude, Castle, and Palantir all have such "implementation experts" behind them.

At this juncture, we have to ask again:

For enterprise AI to make money, what exactly should the first step be?

Why not the CTO, not the product director, but this new role that has never appeared in org charts?

This role is called FDE. It sounds like a technical position, but it's not an engineer.

Its full name is "Forward Deployed Engineer." Bob McGrew put it in one sentence:

"An FDE isn't there to give you demos or sit through requirement discussions. They go straight to the field and solve the problem together with the customer."

✅ FDEs aren't engineers?

If you think of an FDE as a consultant, you're wrong.

A consultant stands on the sidelines, produces a plan, and lets others execute. An FDE is the one who gets hands-on, who makes the software actually work.

McGrew stresses that FDEs don't do formalities. He says:

"At Palantir, we discovered early on that going to traditional government salespeople was useless. They'd say: 'I can take you to dinner and introduce you to clients, but I won't touch the product.'"

An FDE isn't an ordinary engineer either.

Engineers draw architecture diagrams in the office; FDEs embed at the customer's site and solve unprecedented problems on the spot.

Bob McGrew used a scenario to illustrate:

"Customer problems are usually brand-new, never-seen-before challenges. The value of an FDE lies in this: where others see the impossible, they make it possible."

Moreover, an FDE must turn a demo into a deliverable product in a short time. A rough prototype is fine, but the result has to be usable.

Not code that goes ten years without a bug, but code that can ship in three months and genuinely solve the customer's problem.

✅ What does an FDE actually do?

McGrew gave an example:

When he was building intelligence systems at Palantir, customers were initially unwilling to share their real needs. They were intelligence agencies—the information was classified.

So they brought a basic demo, and the customer said it had nothing to do with their work. But the very next sentence changed:

"The customer said: 'If you could change this part and switch to a different view, we might be able to use it.'"

So the FDE noted the feedback on the spot, made the changes overnight, and brought it back.

This is the complete on-site iteration process: the traditional product approach is that sales relays customer feedback and engineers write code at headquarters; with an FDE, the engineer sits right next to the customer, listens, and makes changes immediately.

So what's the difference between an FDE and a product manager? McGrew put it bluntly:

A product manager stands beside the user to understand the workflow; an FDE directly changes the workflow.

And a good FDE keeps going deeper. After solving the first problem, they look for new entry points—sometimes delivering even more value than originally expected.

This is a process of fully penetrating the customer's business.

This section isn't about "defining the FDE," but answering a more concrete question:

"When building an AI product, who is the key role that can actually win customers?"

The core problem the FDE model solves is the "last mile" between technology and business in complex enterprise scenarios.

McGrew summed up this model with a vivid metaphor:

The FDE lays a gravel path first, and the product team later paves it into a highway.

This division of labor allowed Palantir to resolve the tension between personalized needs and scalable replication.

✅ It's not that the company is special—it's that the scenario has changed

Palantir took this path because its customers were extremely complex:

  • the National Security Agency, counterterrorism command, law enforcement systems...
  • Every organization is different, every process is different—there's simply no standard product that can be used out of the box.

Bob McGrew believes AI agents today are in exactly the same position Palantir was in back then.

"You think you're building a general-purpose agent tool, but every company's workflows, tools, and data permissions are all different. No one can buy it and have it work right away."

This is completely different from traditional SaaS. SaaS products rely on "process standardization":

  • Every company runs payroll in roughly the same way, and the steps for preparing financial statements are similar too.
  • So SaaS companies built a unified template—set up your account, and everyone can use it.

But AI agents aren't.

They're an entirely new species, without SaaS's mature processes to replicate. Nobody knows what an AI agent should look like.

In this situation, simply waiting for customers to use it on their own rarely produces real results.

✅ It's not a service transformation—it's that the product's path to market has changed

When many people hear "FDE," they assume it's a service role—"companion-style operations" or "custom development."

McGrew directly rejected this notion:

"An FDE doesn't make the product fit the customer; rather, through the customer, they distill what's truly universal."

For example:

An AI company builds risk control systems for banks. The first bank wants credit card fraud detection, the second wants money laundering identification, the third wants to prevent internal violations... every customer's needs are different.

If you custom-develop every time, the work will never end.

But the FDE realized these needs are all essentially "identifying anomalous behavior patterns," so the product team abstracted a general anomaly detection framework—one system solving all the problems.

This is the key value of the FDE model: it completely changes the product's path to market.

✅ Why do today's AI agent companies need FDEs even more?

Over 100 YC startups are now hiring FDEs—three years ago, hardly anyone had heard of this role.

Why the sudden explosion?

Because AI companies have discovered that the key to agent deployment is integrating into real business workflows. But where are those workflows?

Not everyone can articulate their workflows clearly—many customers can't even explain why they do things the way they do. You have to send someone on-site to observe and follow along to find the real entry point.

That's why the teams behind OpenAI, Anthropic, and Claude increasingly emphasize: an AI agent isn't just about capability—it's about the outcome of action.

The technology is ready, but the world's pace of adoption hasn't caught up.

What enterprises need isn't a stronger model or higher accuracy, but someone who can plug into their workflows and deliver results.

That person is the FDE.

If the FDE's first role is "making AI land in the real world," then the second role is "helping enterprises land big deals."

You can think of the FDE as the "owner of outcomes"—what they sell isn't features, but results.

In the SaaS model, companies sell "licenses," "modules," and "seats." But in the world of AI agents, customers will only pay for results.

✅ The logic of making money has changed: from "selling software" to "delivering results"

How does traditional SaaS make money? Through scale.

Polish one feature, then replicate it across a hundred companies. The contracts aren't big, but they're replicable.

AI agents are different:

  • Every customer's problem is unique
  • The process is complex, workflows vary, and the data is messy
  • If you try to replicate quickly, chances are you can't

McGrew's answer is:

"Don't chase rapid replication—go deep on solving the problem. What you're selling isn't the product itself, but a proven solution."

When an FDE is on-site with a customer, it's not to install a product, but to take on a specific problem and deliver a result.

This explains why the FDE model has such a unique business logic: the initial deployment may lose money, but over time, as the results delivered become more valuable, costs actually go down.

This process unfolds in three stages:

  • At first, the FDE goes on-site and spends a lot of time solving the first problem—costs are high;
  • Then, the customer starts relying on the AI tools, feedback becomes more precise, and needs become more focused;
  • Finally, the FDE no longer needs to change much—the product can now adapt to more customer scenarios, and profits start coming in.

✅ A real case: how to go from "pilot" to "big deal"?

McGrew mentioned several real examples of AI agent startups, such as Castle and Happy Robot:

Castle is a voice AI company that helps several major US banks handle mortgage calls

  • At first, they could only handle a few scenarios, but the results they delivered were highly accurate and customers were satisfied
  • Later, customers proactively increased their contract amounts and handed over their entire workflows

Another is Happy Robot, which also builds voice agents, mainly serving logistics companies like DHL:

  • Their contracts with customers aren't priced by features, but by "number of successful calls"
  • The more customers use it, the more they see the labor costs saved, and the more willing they are to increase their budgets

Both examples illustrate one point:

The FDE isn't a cost center—it's the starting point for driving revenue.

✅ Only with customer trust can you pull in the contracts

Many people mistakenly believe the biggest barrier to AI sales is insufficient features. The real barrier is: customer trust.

McGrew says:

"Enterprise customers have often been through many failed projects—they may not even believe you can succeed. So you have to deliver the first result first. Once they trust you, everything after that becomes easier to discuss."

That's why an FDE must be someone who can deliver on-site, someone who can get the entire workflow running end-to-end within two or three months and produce tangible results. Only with that result will customers truly trust you—and only then is the next big contract possible.

As long as the results you deliver keep growing in value, customers' budgets will follow you willingly.

At the end of the day, the FDE is the first person to make AI land in the real world.

But not everyone can do this.

Bob McGrew made it very clear in the conversation: when hiring FDEs, you're not looking for "smart people"—you're looking for "people who can carry the load."

✅ What kind of person can be an FDE?

Within Palantir, McGrew divided FDEs into two types of roles:

  • The Echo team: embedded on-site with customers, understanding needs and finding use cases for deployment
  • The Delta team: quickly building working product prototypes, deploying them on-site, and delivering results

The Echo team ideally has industry backgrounds—retired military officers, medical experts—but more importantly, they need to be "rebels": people who know the industry's workflows well but realize the old ways aren't good enough, and who come to tear down old processes and build new paths.

The Delta team has engineering backgrounds, but they're different from ordinary engineers. Don't look for people who pursue perfect architecture—look for people who can write code quickly, accept rewrites, and cope with changing workflows. The priority is delivering first; the code may be ugly, but it has to work.

Both types of people are essentially "project-style founders": able to blaze trails into the unknown, hold up under pressure, and talk to customers with a clear sense of direction.

✅ How do product teams and FDEs work together?

McGrew was very candid:

"We interviewed many product managers from big traditional tech companies. They were all excellent, but they weren't used to abstracting general solutions from multiple customers' needs."

One customer says they want to look up "people," the next says they want to look up "ships," and another says they want to look up "accounts."

So how should the product be designed?

McGrew's answer: don't customize for each customer—abstract instead:

"People, ships, and accounts are different, but they share common ground: they all have basic information, and they all form connections with other things. Design that shared underlying logic well, and one system can handle all types of data."

This is the origin of Palantir's famous "ontology" design. It wasn't the FDEs who proposed the abstraction—it was the product team who distilled it from the FDEs' hard-won lessons.

But in practice, there's also friction between product teams and FDEs. Product teams often feel the tools they've built are "great to use," only to find the FDEs on-site don't want to use them.

What's the solution?

"Send the product team on-site to use the tools alongside the FDEs and improve them together."

This is the core of the FDE model: deliver results first, then abstract them into general capabilities.

The product team's role isn't to work behind closed doors, but to scale the solutions validated in the field.

The FDE is a product of reality forcing the issue. If you want results, someone has to take the first step.

As McGrew put it:

Model capabilities have advanced incredibly fast, but enterprise deployment is far from keeping up.

It's not that the tools aren't strong enough, or that the models fall a bit short—it's that enterprises have no one who can put them into practice. What you're missing is the person who can integrate AI into real-world workflows and deliver the first result.

What the FDE does is turn "uncertain opportunities" into "repeatable processes." It's the starting point of results and the front line of growth.

If an enterprise wants to use AI well, don't start by asking whether the model is good enough—start by asking:

"Do we have someone like that?"

Someone who can pave the way on-site at the customer. Someone always has to walk the path first.
