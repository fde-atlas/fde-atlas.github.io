Interview with Volcano Engine's Tan Dai: Short dramas are just one small scenario—Seedance can serve the real economy

Original title: 对话火山引擎谭待：短剧只是一个小场景，Seedance能服务实体产业

界面新闻 · 2026-06-23 · 16 min read

Summary

In an interview with Jiemian after Volcano Engine's 2026 FORCE conference, president Tan Dai discussed the new Doubao 2.1 Pro model, which the company says matches or beats Claude Opus 4.6 on coding, agent, and vision benchmarks while costing nearly 80% less. He argued short dramas are only a small use case for Seedance, positioning video generation as a foundation for world models, with adoption in embodied AI, manufacturing, and autonomous driving data synthesis. Because video models use Diffusion architectures with lower HBM demands, he said compute allocation is not a constraint. Volcano Engine has also built an FDE team to co-create solutions with clients in automotive, healthcare, finance, and semiconductors.

Key points

  • Doubao 2.1 Pro upgrades coding, agent, and VLM capabilities, benchmarked against Claude Opus 4.6
  • Priced at 6 yuan per million input tokens, claimed ~80% cheaper than Claude Opus 4.6
  • Tan Dai says short drama is a small scene; Seedance targets embodied AI, manufacturing, and autonomous driving
  • Diffusion-based video models need less HBM, so compute allocation poses no conflict with coding models
  • Volcano Engine's FDE team co-creates AI solutions with clients across key industries

Full text

Jiemian News reporter | Jiemian News editor | Wen Shuqi

"Three years ago, when we decided to make MaaS our most important business, many in the industry still thought selling tokens was a money-losing business." Speaking about how to build a market moat, Tan Dai, President of Volcano Engine, said that strategic foresight is also a key factor in staying competitive.

According to IDC data, Volcano Engine ranks first in China's public cloud MaaS service market with a 49.5% market share. According to Volcano Engine, as of June this year, the Doubao large model's daily average token usage had exceeded 180 trillion, growing more than 10-fold over the past year.

At the launch point of a new round of flagship models, Volcano Engine is trying to further consolidate its share of the domestic enterprise-level AI application market through stronger execution capabilities in complex task environments and cost advantages.

On June 23, Volcano Engine held its 2026 FORCE conference, releasing multiple video, image, and audio models, including Doubao 2.1 Pro.

At the conference, Tan Dai said that large model capabilities must cross the "productivity qualitative leap point" to truly meet the needs of production scenarios, and that Claude Opus 4.6 was the first model to cross that point. As the core of this release, Doubao 2.1 Pro was upgraded in three major dimensions: coding, agents, and vision-language models (VLM).

According to industry benchmark results published by Volcano Engine (such as SWE-Pro, OSWorld, etc.), the model performs at a level comparable to or better than leading overseas models (such as Claude Opus 4.6) in coding and multimodal capabilities.

In the application demonstration segment, Volcano Engine showed a case of Doubao 2.1 Pro running continuously for nearly 18 hours in an RTL chip design test and completing the full engineering workflow, to verify its stability in long-cycle, complex tasks; in another demonstration, the model supported over 500 agents collaborating simultaneously to generate a 3D virtual city.

Doubao 2.1 Pro is priced at 6 RMB per million input tokens and 30 RMB per million output tokens, with cache-hit pricing at just 1.2 RMB. Volcano Engine said its overall cost of use is nearly 80% lower than Claude Opus 4.6. In addition, the Turbo version for high-frequency scenarios is priced at half that.

Currently, the Doubao 2.1 model is available via Volcano Engine's open API service and is being integrated into products such as Doubao, TRAE, and Coze.

In addition, the company previewed the video generation model Seedance 2.5, which will launch in July. The model supports generating 30-second native videos and has begun seeking deployment scenarios in physical industries such as embodied intelligence, industrial manufacturing, and autonomous driving.

The SOTA performance of the Seedance series models and the growing demand for AI short dramas are becoming two mutually reinforcing forces, making Seedance the productivity leader in this market. But Volcano Engine does not seem satisfied with this. Tan Dai told Jiemian News that the team still hopes Seedance can go deep into production environments across all industries.

"Especially for high-end productivity manufacturing and the construction of world models — that's what we value most," Tan Dai said.

Regarding the Seedance supply issue that the industry widely cares about, Tan Dai also emphasized that video generation models and Coding/Agent models have different structures, being mainly based on the Diffusion architecture, which has relatively lower requirements for underlying chips (especially high-bandwidth HBM). As a result, the team has done extensive optimization on model inference in Volcano Ark, enabling Seedance to utilize all kinds of compute, including low-end chips.

"So it has no conflict with Coding or Agent models in terms of compute; compute allocation is not a problem at all," he said.

In the media interview after the launch event, Tan Dai also discussed API price trends, how the Seedance video model can move from content generation to world models, whether selling tokens is really a good business, the current competitive landscape of AI coding, and the next-stage direction of large-scale industry deployment of agents, among other topics.

Regarding the recent phenomenon of listed AI large model companies' market capitalizations repeatedly hitting new highs, Tan Dai also responded to whether Volcano Engine has plans for an independent spin-off listing. "As far as I know, there are currently no listing plans," Tan Dai said.

The following is the transcript of the interview with Tan Dai, lightly edited:

Media: Recently, the API prices of domestic large models have shown mixed rises and falls. From both Volcano Engine's own operations and the industry's overall cost trends, how should we understand the pricing strategy of your new models today?

Tan Dai: First of all, when we look at model prices, we can't just look at the absolute price; we need to consider it together with its value. The more a model can do, the greater the value it creates. Considering price and value together, whether it's Volcano Engine or other mainstream models in the industry, although the list price per token is rising, the value created per token is rising even faster. So for models, the cost-effectiveness is actually improving. This also echoes a point: today's models, whether coding, agents, or video generation, have truly crossed the "productivity qualitative leap point" and can create more real value.

Media: Video models like Seedance 2.0 have brought Volcano Engine very strong single-day revenue generation. To what extent can this growth continue? Also, the short drama industry has basically been taken over by Seedance — has the demand-side pie for video models basically reached saturation?

Tan Dai: That's a really good question. I'd like to make three points:

First, all the revenue figures about Seedance circulating outside are wrong, and they're too high. I also call on everyone to stop spreading them — this puts a lot of pressure on me, with finance coming to ask me every day whether I'm hiding revenue (laughs).

Second, film and short dramas are actually just one part of Seedance's deployment; in the long run, it may only be a small scenario. We have already seen Seedance being widely used in many industries. For example, in manufacturing and retail, people use it to create product explainers — videos tailored to different countries, languages, and audiences — which helps companies better communicate with consumers. We've also seen many in the knowledge and education industries use it to turn knowledge into videos; in high-end manufacturing, such as "embodied intelligence," many companies are using Seedance for data synthesis, breaking through the bottleneck of obtaining data from the real world; autonomous driving companies also use it to synthesize extreme weather or edge-case road conditions, making their algorithms run more robustly.

Third, from this perspective, we believe Seedance is actually the foundation for building "world models." Video generation enables large-scale unsupervised training with minimal assumptions about the data, making it a highly effective method for synthesizing world models. Building a good video model requires strong underlying capabilities; essentially, it relies on the capabilities of the Doubao model. The Doubao 2.1 Pro we released today has surpassed Claude Opus 4.6 in coding and agent capabilities, crossing the production-grade threshold. So, broad application across thousands of industries, and serving as the foundation for world models — that is where Seedance's greater future potential lies.

Media: We understand that Seedance is becoming a very important API procurement source for more and more video production service providers and agencies. How does Volcano Engine view this industry trend? What kind of ecosystem positioning do you hope to establish? Also, from the perspective of compute assurance, what is the current resource allocation mechanism?

Tan Dai: Let me answer the compute question first. Video generation models (Seedance) and Coding/Agent models have different structures; video models are mainly based on the Diffusion architecture. They have relatively lower requirements for underlying chips, especially high-bandwidth HBM. We have done extensive optimization on model inference in Volcano Ark, making it very easy for Seedance to utilize all kinds of compute (including low-end chips). So it has no conflict with Coding or Agent models in terms of compute; compute allocation is not a problem at all. This is precisely an important reason why Seedance has been able to achieve large-scale, widespread adoption — we have made tremendous optimizations and innovations in model architecture and engineering capabilities.

As for the industry ecosystem, we hope Seedance can deeply penetrate the production environments of all kinds of industries, especially high-end productivity manufacturing and the construction of world models—this is what we value most.

Media: Regarding "world models," there are many schools of thought right now. What is ByteDance's approach in this field? Does the training data rely mainly on video data or on real interaction data like embodied intelligence?

Tan Dai: We are also trying multiple routes in parallel internally. But so far, "video generation" appears to be a very effective path for synthesizing world models, because it makes the fewest assumptions about existing data and can directly leverage massive amounts of video for unsupervised learning. Many embodied intelligence companies are now using Seedance to synthesize data to feed back into their model training, so we are very optimistic about this path—it is also the scenario where Seedance will be more valuable in the future.

Media: We've noticed that when Volcano Engine discusses multimodal generation, it still talks about the boundary between "can generate" and "commercially viable." How does the team define the standard for this boundary? What are the remaining bottlenecks for large-scale commercialization of multimodal models?

Tan Dai: The concept of the "productivity inflection point" we mentioned today is very important. Defining this boundary is actually quite simple: look at what the existing business processes in each industry are like, and what requirements each process places on model capabilities. Once those requirements are met, you've crossed the boundary.

Data doesn't lie. Before Seedance 2.0 came out, many people said video generation was a toy—and the data indeed showed that: call volumes on weekends far exceeded weekdays, meaning people were playing with it in their leisure time. But after Seedance launched, the data flipped—weekday call volumes far exceeded weekends. This fully shows that people are genuinely using it in work, production, and data synthesis. That is a leap in productivity.

Media: Let me ask a question on behalf of our leadership—they are all loyal Doubao users and have recently felt that Doubao's quality seems to have declined. Is this preparation for launching a paid version?

Tan Dai: I use Doubao intensively every day myself, and I haven't noticed any decline in quality. Also, to clarify: the Doubao App is not actually within the scope of Volcano Engine's business, but as far as I know: first, the Doubao App will remain free and continue to serve users at a high quality—that commitment has never changed; second, it will soon launch a professional task mode for productivity scenarios, powered by our newly released Doubao 2.1 Pro model. Volcano Engine's API has been paid from day one, so there is no such thing as degrading quality to charge fees.

Media: What is Volcano Engine's current priority—focusing more on the development of foundational model capabilities, or more on the Harness (execution environment/toolchain) model?

Tan Dai: Both are very important. Volcano Engine's ultimate mission is to help enterprises and developers solve real business problems. What customers want is not just a good model or a good API—they need a complete solution for how AI and Harness land in the enterprise environment.

This involves how models integrate with internal enterprise systems, how they combine with enterprise data, and how to handle security, agent identity authentication, and compliance with regulatory requirements. That's why we emphasize the "AI cloud-native architecture" today: from the foundational models at the bottom, to the middle layer of MaaS (including some Harness), to the upper layer of Agent Kit (with more Harness tools), and finally to the top layer of various AI workbenches. We offer zero-code, low-code, and high-code options, all aimed at meeting the diverse needs of different roles within enterprises.

The priorities advance in an interleaved fashion: when models haven't reached the inflection point, improving models matters most; after crossing the inflection point, Harness and implementation become equally important.

Media: Zhipu's valuation in Hong Kong is very high, and overseas, Anthropic (Claude) and Google have made major breakthroughs in AI Coding. What plans does Volcano Engine have for closing the gap with the most advanced models? How do you view the high expectations the market has placed on you?

Tan Dai: Agent-oriented capabilities are something we take very seriously. Coding is just one form of demonstrating model capability, but it is extremely important, because it represents a model's strong generalization ability—the ability to automatically call tools and even write software itself to fill in missing tools.

Claude Opus 4.6 was the world's first model to cross the "productivity inflection point." This year we've also seen more models cross this threshold. Our newly launched flagship Doubao 2.1 Pro has likewise crossed the productivity inflection point. Judging by various eval data, it consistently surpasses Claude Opus 4.6, and in some scenarios even matches the higher versions. This means it has truly entered production environments capable of handling complex, long-horizon tasks.

Media: Some believe large models easily fall into the gross margin trap of "the more active the users, the higher the inference costs," and some competitors say that simply selling tokens is not a healthy business. What's your view? What are the key metrics for judging whether an AI product is a healthy business?

Tan Dai: I think selling tokens is a very healthy business—I don't know who said it isn't.

Media: On commercialization and safety: what key steps has Volcano Engine taken in building the commercialization chain for AI technology? Also, the use of facial material has recently sparked copyright disputes, and Seedance 2.0 implemented verification restrictions on faces. What safety adjustments will future versions make?

Tan Dai: Safety has always been our top priority. As you can see, before officially opening up the API, we spent several months refining Seedance's entire safety strategy. This includes not only IP copyright protection on the business side, but also user-side facial verification and more.

The commercial preview version adopts an Opt-in (voluntary authorization) model, using electronic contracts to authorize revenue sharing and form a healthy commercial loop. In the future, if we do B2B facial avatars, we will also use proper authorization verification mechanisms (similar to the avatar feature in Jianying).

Media: On compute, you mentioned the self-developed DPU route. What are Volcano Engine's thinking and next steps regarding self-developed DPUs and underlying compute? Roughly what proportion of Volcano Engine's underlying compute is currently "domestic compute"?

Tan Dai: Volcano Engine launched its self-developed DPU shortly after its founding. In today's large-scale AI computing, DPUs and switches play a crucial role in better offloading various workloads—networking, storage, virtualization, and compute—to improve overall efficiency, and we have always invested deeply in in-house development. Volcano Engine's reputation for being a leader in AI over the past few years is inseparable from our deep work on underlying infrastructure.

As for domestic compute, we use a great deal of it. For example, Volcano Ark has done extensive compute adaptation and optimization, enabling models like Seedance to make good use of all kinds of compute, both domestic and international. I can't quite recall the exact percentage, but the share is quite substantial.

Media: In the first half of the year, competitors made a lot of noise about AI Coding, while Volcano Engine seemed louder on the Seedance video model. How do you view this competitive difference? What growth expectations do you have for AI Coding in the second half?

Tan Dai: Actually, we have always taken Coding extremely seriously. Last year, at this very venue, Ding Kun spoke at length about Coding, back when many competitors hadn't yet started making moves. The reason people felt Seedance was louder in the first half is mainly that it was genuinely the global SOTA (state of the art) at the time, which drew a lot of attention.

But internally, we have always believed Coding is the more core and more important capability. We will do much more in this area in the second half. We have already established deep partnerships with a large number of high-end semiconductor companies, internet companies, and SaaS companies, applying Doubao's code model and Trae (our AI IDE) deeply into their R&D workflows.

Media: Everyone thinks Volcano Engine has an advantage in building video models—its ability to leverage the internal "Hongguo Short Drama" platform. As Seedance enters other industries in the second half, what is the commercialization playbook?

Tan Dai: I don't think the integration with Hongguo is our advantage. Hongguo's strategy is completely independent, and videos generated by Seedance often even fail Hongguo's review (laughs). So that's not an advantage. Our real advantage is "strong model capability." To enter more industries, the core approach is still to keep making our models stronger.

Media: Many big tech companies are pursuing full-stack layouts including AI chips. How do you view the urgency of such a layout? Does ByteDance have plans to fill in the chip gap?

Tan Dai: From the perspective of a cloud provider, I don't think whether you have your own in-house chips is particularly important. What customers buy is your model capability—whether you can solve their problems—not whose chips you use underneath. Take Anthropic, for example: they don't have their own chips either, but that doesn't stop them from building powerful models.

Media: In the first half of the year, when people talked about tokens, they started discussing metrics of "token value," such as core system integration rates and automation efficiency. How does Volcano Engine drive its teams to increase token value?

Tan Dai: The stronger a model is, the greater the value it can certainly generate. But to truly land that value in industries, you must deeply understand the industry and co-create deeply with customers. For example, we understand coding and internet applications, but we don't necessarily understand pharmaceuticals or education.

So this year we specifically set up an FDE (Forward Deployed Engineer) team that goes deep into each industry and co-creates deeply with benchmark customers. This way, we can better understand what AI can do for that industry, and customers can understand AI's potential, so we can deliver more complete solutions and let tokens truly enter actual production and create value.

Media: What is the size and background of the FDE team? Which industries does it currently cover?

Tan Dai: FDEs are not sales, nor pre-sales; they must have strong technical implementation capabilities, especially in landing AI code. Second, we place great importance on members' diverse industry backgrounds. For example, someone with a bioengineering background handles the biopharmaceutical industry, and they possess irreplaceable know-how when doing implementation. We currently cover quite a few industries—key sectors like automotive, healthcare, education, finance, and semiconductors all have dedicated teams.

Media: You mentioned the upgrade of Agent Kit today, including multiple tool products for zero-code, low-code, and high-code (such as Trae, ArkClaw, Coze, etc.). How do you think about this layout, and how do you cover different groups?

Tan Dai: An enterprise organization has people in different roles—professional developers, product managers, and functional staff like HR and finance. Their needs for an AI workbench are completely different: some need zero-code out-of-the-box solutions, some need low-code drag-and-drop, and professionals need a high-code environment.

The workloads of application scenarios also differ—some are pure code development, others are general office tasks like handling Office/PPT. It's hard for a single product to do everything, so we offer a diverse toolbox matrix covering different dimensions from zero-code to high-code. In the future, this matrix may evolve and converge, and we will iterate gradually as AI develops.

Media: Regarding visual models, do you favor the pixel-centric video generation route, or a route combining multiple types?

Tan Dai: We will definitely try multiple directions. Currently, the pixel generation route (Diffusion) is running faster and performing better, so we invest more effort in it; but we are also working on other approaches like 3D generation (for example, Doubao's 3D model). Whichever route, the ultimate core is to better integrate "generation" and "understanding."

Media: Can you share Volcano Engine's thinking on the overseas expansion of its models?

Tan Dai: We attach great importance to overseas markets. Of course, Volcano Engine mainly focuses on the Chinese market; overseas, we have another entity in charge.

Models are inherently global. If the capability is good enough, they will naturally attract customers worldwide. For example, Seedance now has nearly half of its usage coming from overseas, and many large multinational companies and creator platforms (like Canva) are using it. What overseas users value most is model capability and cost-effectiveness. We have also established MaaS access points around the world (such as Southeast Asia, the Middle East, and Europe) to make it easier for global developers to call our models.

Media: When judging internally whether a new scenario deserves a standalone Agent product or should just be a skill module of an existing Agent, are there any metrics to measure this?

Tan Dai: First, look at the business prospects. If the market size of this scenario can't even reach 1 billion, it's best not to make it a standalone Agent product—just write it as a skill and add it in. As model capabilities get stronger, tasks that previously required complex Agents may now be solved by writing a skill or configuring a dynamic workflow.

Media: Given current industry demand, what stage do you think China's large model market is in?

Tan Dai: It's still at a very early stage. If last year we ran about 500 meters, this year we've run a little over a kilometer. But this "kilometer" is crucial, because it marks that model capability has crossed the "productivity qualitative-change point." Now, as long as domestic models reach or exceed this standard, it means they can truly be used in production and create commercial value.

Media: The conference mentioned that Doubao's large models now reach a daily token call volume of 180 trillion. What is the proportion between internal business and external customers? What does the compute ledger look like?

Tan Dai: The 180 trillion is the total for all Doubao large models (including internal business, external calls, and the Doubao App). In terms of pure token volume, the Doubao App accounts for a large share; but in terms of economic value or the consumption generated per token, external customers (ToB scenarios) account for a higher proportion. That's because external calls are basically complex production-grade usage, and each processing generates higher value. We haven't separately reconciled and evaluated the overall compute ledger internally.

Media: Volcano Engine currently holds a 49.5% share of the domestic large model market. With such fierce competition, what moat does Volcano Engine rely on to hold this position?

Tan Dai: The core comes down to two things:

First is model capability, especially whether we can be the first to cross the "production-grade qualitative-change point."

Second is how to bring models into enterprises. This includes implementing the FDE model, deep understanding of industries, deep cooperation with ecosystem partners, and our own team's professionalism in AI solutions.

There may also be one more thing: strategic foresight. Three years ago, when we decided to push MaaS as our most important business, many in the industry still thought selling tokens was a money-losing business. Confidence in the future is also key to our competitiveness.

Media: You mentioned that digital employees have token assessments. Will real employees in reality also have token usage included in their assessments? Will there be token welfare support policies?

Tan Dai: Internally, employees can voluntarily enable a system to track their daily token usage, but this is purely for personal observation and efficiency improvement—it will never be used as a KPI assessment. In practice, we've found that blindly applying AI is sometimes useless—if the goal setting or first principles of a task are wrong in the first place, no amount of AI can solve it. So before using AI, you still need to return to the essence of the business and think clearly about the goal.

Media: How do you view the recent phenomenon of AI companies' market values constantly hitting new highs? Does Volcano Engine have plans for a separate spin-off IPO in the future?

Tan Dai: As far as I know, there are currently no IPO plans.
