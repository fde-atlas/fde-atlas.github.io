The Complete Overview of Skills Required for FDE | Explained in 8 Areas

Original title: FDEに必要なスキルの全体像｜8領域で解説

日本Fde協会 · 2026-08-18 · 6 min read

Summary

This article by the Japan FDE Association maps the full skill set of a Forward Deployed Engineer across four stages—understanding, design/implementation, governance, and adoption—covering eight areas. It argues that coding alone is insufficient: FDEs must elicit business requirements, prototype quickly instead of waiting for perfect specs, and handle often-overlooked governance work such as access control, logging, audit trails, and documentation to prevent knowledge silos. Author Maekawa draws on experience as a system PM and trader. The association also runs a private FDE certification as a learning benchmark.

Key points

  • Skills span four stages: understanding, design/implementation, governance, adoption.
  • Field listening and problem framing are the foundation, not coding alone.
  • Show working prototypes early rather than perfect requirement documents.
  • Governance—permissions, logs, audit trails—is critical for enterprise deployment.
  • Documentation and handover prevent over-dependence on the individual FDE.

"How far do the skills required for FDE actually extend?" — This article is written for those who feel this way. The FDE skills required of an FDE (Forward Deployed Engineer — an engineer who embeds in the customer's workplace and solves problems from both technical and business perspectives; not to be confused with Full Disk Encryption) go beyond just the ability to write code. As the Japan FDE Association, we are not in a position to sell any particular service, so from our standpoint of observing real-world practice across the industry, we can objectively organize the complete picture of these skills. By reading this article, you will gain an overview of the scope of FDE skills and understand which areas you should explore next. Let's take a look together.

FDE Skills Start with "Technology × Business Understanding"

The starting point for an FDE is correctly understanding the customer's on-site business operations. No matter how strong your technical skills are, you won't achieve results if you misidentify the problems to solve. This is the foundation.

The Ability to Interview On-Site Staff and Organize Problems

When our Representative Director, Maekawa, worked as a systems PM at a major engineering firm, he stood between an 8-person development team and more than 20 stakeholders, handling everything from interdepartmental coordination to reporting to management. Through work spanning industries such as shipping/receiving management for petrochemical complexes and core systems for entertainment companies, he keenly realized that neither technology alone nor business knowledge alone is enough — the value lies in having someone on-site who understands both. We organize this foundational interviewing technique in detail in FDE Interviewing Techniques.

The Ability to Use AI Practically and Improve Business Operations

Next, you need the ability to translate the organized problems into a form that can be solved with AI agents or LLMs (large language models). The key point is pragmatism: "use AI where it works." Even from a story we heard from the CEO of a SaaS company, cases where introducing AI itself becomes the goal and fails are not uncommon. For details on the approach, see How to Approach AI-Driven Business Improvement.

Design and Requirements Definition — The Ability to Show Something That Works

Once the problems are clear, you decide how to design business applications, workflows, and data integrations. What makes this distinctly FDE-like is the attitude of "show something that works first."

Getting the Fastest Feedback with Prototypes

When Maekawa moved from finance to the e-commerce business, despite having technical skills he lacked marketing knowledge, and sales were nearly zero for the first six months. Once he stopped aiming for perfection, conducted thorough market research and interviews, and switched to an approach of showing working products to get reactions, the numbers began to grow. From this experience, he values an approach of validating with prototypes rather than waiting for a perfect requirements document. For details, see FDE Prototyping Techniques.

Questioning "Is This Really Necessary?" in Requirements Definition

We sometimes receive feedback like: "I was shocked when they first asked me, 'Is that really necessary?' They stopped us from unnecessary development." Not taking requirements at face value and making decisions to cut them back by working backward from the purpose is also part of FDE skills. Prompt design, RAG (a mechanism combining retrieval and generation), and agent evaluation design are also included at this stage. The approach to requirements definition is covered in 5 Things FDEs Do in Requirements Definition and Practical Techniques to Avoid Failure.

Often Overlooked Areas — Governance, Auditing, and Preventing Dependency on Individuals

This is where we differentiate from typical FDE explanations in the market. Most explanations stop at "an engineer who embeds in the field" and barely touch on what comes beyond. However, this is exactly what is actually scrutinized in enterprise deployments.

Security, Access Control, and Logs

As long as you handle information, access control over who can access what, and log management that records operations, are unavoidable. At manufacturing client sites, we have repeatedly seen situations where, even with a convenient system built, deployment to production stalls if access control is ambiguous. You need to design not just for functionality, but all the way to a form that can be operated safely.

Deployment Based on Internal Controls and Audit Trails

From his experience as a trader at a financial institution, Maekawa understands firsthand the importance of segregation of duties (the concept of separating responsibilities to prevent fraud) and records that can withstand audits. Keeping things in a state where you can later trace who approved what and on what basis a transaction was processed — whether you can provide deployment support based on these audit trails makes a big difference in enterprise projects.

Documentation and Handover to Prevent Dependency on Individuals

We sometimes hear comments like "You know our business better than our own employees," but this also poses a risk of dependency on individuals. A system that only its creator can operate will eventually break down. Organizing documentation and handing over so the customer can operate independently is part of an FDE's job.

Viewing the Complete Picture at a Glance

Let's organize the FDE skills covered so far as a broad flow. Based on our experience building organizations at a startup, the core of FDE is being able to carry this entire flow through "on your own."

StageKey Skills
UnderstandingBusiness interviews and problem organization
Design and ImplementationAI utilization, application/integration design, prototyping
GovernanceAccess control, logs, audits, and internal control compliance
EstablishmentDocumentation, handover, training, and continuous improvement

Technical skill is merely the entry point. Only when governance and handover are included does the complete picture of FDE skills that deliver results in real-world practice come together. First, decide which stage you want to explore in depth, and proceed to each area. We also organize career paths in How to Become an FDE?.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q. Are technical skills all that's required for an FDE?

A. No. In addition to technical skills such as business interviews and design, it also includes access control, logs, deployments that can withstand audits, and documentation and handover to prevent dependency on individuals. In enterprise deployments, these latter areas are especially scrutinized.

Q. Which area should I start learning?

A. First, interviewing that correctly draws out on-site problems is the foundation. From there, moving on to prototyping — showing something that works as fast as possible — and requirements definition will help you grasp the flow. Please choose the sub-articles in each area that suit you.

Q. Is there a benchmark for measuring skill proficiency?

A. As a guideline for learning and evaluation, there is the FDE Certification, a private certification operated by the Japan FDE Association. While it is not a national qualification, it can be used as an indicator of practical ability. For details, please see the Certification Page.
