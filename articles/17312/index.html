What roles should a complete enterprise FDE team include?

Original title: 一支完整的企业FDE团队，应该配置哪些角色？

FDE中国 · 2026-09-07 · 10 min read

Summary

This article argues that enterprise FDE teams fail when all responsibilities fall on a few hero engineers, and proposes a structure organized around three outcome responsibilities: business value, production quality, and asset reuse. A 6–8 person frontline squad covers roles like Echo, FDPM, tech lead, Delta engineers, data integration, and evals. A back-office platform team handles SRE, security, customer success, and knowledge engineering. Customers must also provide a mirror team with real time and decision authority. The editor adds a practical caveat: a full squad costs over a million RMB per quarter, which fixed-price contracts in China cannot sustain—so budget first, then staff.

Key points

  • FDE teams must own three outcomes: business value, production quality, and reusable assets.
  • Frontline squad of 6–8: Echo, FDPM, tech lead, Delta engineers, data integration, and evals roles.
  • Back-office platform roles (SRE, security, customer success, knowledge engineering) enable scaling beyond projects.
  • Customer mirror team needs six named interfaces with time and decision authority, not just names.
  • Editor's caveat: full squads cost over RMB 1M per quarter; fixed-price contracts can't fund them—budget before hiring.

This staffing model looks complete on paper, but put it next to a real quotation and the first failure point is money. A six-to-eight-person squad running for a quarter easily costs over a million in fully loaded cost, while most enterprise AI projects in China are still signed at a fixed price under traditional software logic, and the contract value simply cannot sustain a full roster. So roles quietly become part-time, and the evals engineer is usually the first to be sacrificed—"we'll patch up quality after launch" has practically become an industry default, and the pitfall where model upgrades cause performance regressions usually starts from exactly this gap. In other words, missing headcount often means missing gross margin; running the numbers before forming the team gets closer to the truth than recruiting strictly by the org chart. The second failure point is on the customer side. The original text requires the mirror team to "have time and decision-making authority," which is precisely the scarcest resource in manufacturing and government/enterprise clients: the backbone staff who truly understand the processes won't free up their hands for an external project, and what you end up with is often just "people who have time for meetings." The frontline signal for judgment is simple—if the client's IT can't even set up test accounts within two weeks, or the business Sponsor sends a subordinate to meetings three times in a row, the project will most likely stall at "technically feasible." Cross-border and supply chain projects also carry a hidden cost hotspot: customs, logistics, and finance each define the same "exception item" differently, and if the data owner is only a figurehead, your golden test set is skewed from day one, and all subsequent eval accuracy is an illusion. So my recommendation is concrete: at the kickoff of your next project, write the six interfaces of the mirror team into the project charter, specifying each person's weekly committed hours and key decision deadlines (e.g., data permissions opened within two weeks, UAT locked to the contract cycle), with any miss triggering a renegotiation of scope and schedule. That is far cheaper than discovering after launch that nobody signed off on acceptance. — FDE China Editorial · Practitioner's Perspective

# What Roles Should a Complete Enterprise FDE Team Include?

Source: FDE Frontline · September 2, 2026, 12:19

When many enterprises build an FDE team, their first instinct is to find a few full-stack engineers who "understand models, can write code, and can communicate with clients."

This configuration often produces a Demo quickly, but tends to stall when entering production: no one continuously confirms business value, client requirements keep expanding, data interfaces get stuck in the IT department, model upgrades cause performance regressions, there is no monitoring after launch, and business staff never truly change how they work.

The problem is not that the engineers aren't strong enough, but that the team piles responsibilities that should be shared across multiple roles onto a few "hero FDEs."

In May 2026, when OpenAI introduced the Deployment Company, it summarized the FDE's work as designing, building, testing, and deploying production systems inside enterprises, connecting models with clients' data, tools, controls, and business processes. The latest document from the Ministry of Industry and Information Technology likewise uses the term "Forward Deployed Engineer team," requiring them to be rooted at user sites and ensure scenario implementation.

Both formulations point to the same fact: enterprise AI delivery has never been a one-person job.

A complete FDE team must be accountable simultaneously for business value, production quality, and capability reuse. Self-made diagram.

There is no single standard staffing for an FDE team. Finance, manufacturing, retail, and government projects differ in headcount, and during the PoC phase a single person may hold multiple roles. What truly cannot be missing is accountability for three types of outcomes.

First, the ability to turn vague problems into business scenarios worth investing in, and to clearly define scope, metrics, and acceptance criteria.

Second, the ability to connect Agents, RAG, or workflows to real data and production systems, running stably under permissions, evals, monitoring, rollback, and human takeover mechanisms.

Third, the ability to drive continuous adoption by business staff, and to accumulate connectors, Skills, test sets, industry templates, and lessons from failures, so that the next delivery is faster and cheaper.

If the team can only deliver the first outcome, it is more like a consulting team; if only the second, it is more like a project development team; and if it leaves right after launch, the business will likely revert to manual processes. A complete FDE system must link all three outcomes into a cycle.

The frontline squad owns outcomes, the back-office platform owns reuse, and the client mirror team owns organizational implementation. Self-made diagram.

II. The Frontline Squad: 6–8 People Accountable for Delivery Outcomes

A standard production project can be staffed with a 6–8 person frontline squad. Headcount is not a hard target; the key is that each of the following six responsibilities has a clear owner.

1. Echo / Industry Solutions Lead

Echo is responsible for entering the business site, understanding how the client actually works, redefining problems, and identifying high-value scenarios. The core deliverable is not a generic research report, but business process diagrams, scenario priorities, value hypotheses, and an industry object model. Evaluation should focus on scenario value, client trust, and whether the solution can be reused across clients.

The name Echo can be seen in Palantir's current recruiting system, where its Deployment Strategist role is grouped under Echo, with responsibilities including going deep into client workflows, identifying the most important problems, and delivering jointly with engineers. Domestic teams don't necessarily need to adopt the name, but someone must be accountable for "what to do and why it's worth doing."

2. FDPM / Forward Deployed Product Manager

The FDPM translates business goals into deliverable scope, managing milestones, client expectations, test cases, acceptance criteria, and risk registers. Traditional product managers usually work around generic roadmaps, whereas the FDPM directly faces one client's real environment, is accountable for deployment outcomes, and feeds frontline feedback back into the core product.

This title is still taking shape, and variants such as Deployed Product Manager and Field Product Manager also appear in the market. Whatever it's called, the team needs someone who continuously controls scope, so that every piece of client feedback doesn't become a queue-jumping requirement.

3. FDE Technical Lead

The technical lead connects the client's architecture with internal products, making end-to-end decisions on model selection, overall architecture, data boundaries, integration approaches, and launch strategy. They should not be the sole author of all code, but must ensure consistency in key technical decisions and know which capabilities should be customized and which must be consolidated into the platform.

4. Delta / Applied AI Engineer

Delta is the primary building force, usually staffed with 2–3 people, responsible for Agents, RAG, workflows, prompts, front-end and back-end, and business applications. Palantir currently groups its Forward Deployed Software Engineer role under Delta, with responsibilities ranging from high-level system design and prototype development to application building and data integration.

Delta's evaluation should not be based solely on lines of code; it should focus on task success rates, iteration speed, code quality, and whether frontline feedback can be quickly converted into product improvements.

5. Data and Systems Integration Engineer

A large share of enterprise AI time is consumed on databases, APIs, ERPs, CRMs, identity and permissions, and legacy systems. This role is responsible for data pipelines, connectors, permission mapping, and interface documentation, and is accountable for data quality, interface stability, and connector reuse rates. If a project relies only on manually copying data, no matter how strong the Agent, it cannot enter a real business closed loop.

6. AI Evals and Quality Engineer

This person is responsible for golden test sets, automated evals, regression testing, long-tail errors, and A/B testing. Anthropic's 2026 published Agent evals practices emphasize that evals should span the entire lifecycle: automated testing for rapid iteration before release, production monitoring to detect real distribution shifts, and human review to calibrate subjective quality.

In early projects, eval responsibilities can be held concurrently by a senior Delta, but "who defines success and who blocks quality regressions" must be written into the team's division of labor.

Standard projects center on 6–8 full-time members, with platform, security, and customer success providing shared support. Self-made diagram.

III. The Back-Office Team: Determining Whether FDE Can Move from Project-Based to Scalable

Beyond the frontline squad, a back-office platform and productization team serving multiple projects is also needed. They don't necessarily stay on-site for the full duration of every project, but their responsibilities cannot be absent.

Platform engineering/SRE handles deployment, observability, scaling, cost, rollback, and incident response. Google SRE's Production Readiness Review checks architecture dependencies, metrics monitoring, emergency response, capacity, and change management. For enterprise agents, these requirements must also cover model versions, token costs, tool-call traces, and human takeover rates.

Security and compliance leads are responsible for data access, privacy, identity and permissions, auditing, third-party model risks, and fallback boundaries. The NIST AI RMF requires organizations to clarify AI risk accountability, pre-launch testing, production monitoring, as well as mechanisms for appeals, overrides, incident response, and decommissioning. Security should not enter at the last week before launch; it should decide from the scenario design stage which data can be seen and which actions can be taken.

Customer success/organizational change leads are responsible for training, adoption plans, operational data, and expansion roadmaps. A system going live is only the starting point; project value turns from "usable" into "used" only when business staff incorporate it into their daily workflows. Early on, this responsibility can be held by the FDPM concurrently; strategic customers should have a dedicated person assigned.

Product/knowledge engineering leads are responsible for abstracting field experience into Skills, connectors, templates, industry ontologies, eval sets, Playbooks, and failure case libraries. OpenAI's current requirements for FDE managers explicitly include codifying effective practices into tools, methods, and inputs to the product roadmap. This role determines whether FDE keeps repeating custom work or builds compounding returns where delivery gets faster over time.

The field discovers problems, projects validate capabilities, the platform accumulates assets, and assets accelerate the next delivery. Self-made diagram.

IV. The customer side must build a "mirror team"

No matter how complete the vendor's staffing is, enterprise AI delivery cannot be completed unilaterally. The customer needs at least six clear interfaces: a business Sponsor to confirm priorities and drive cross-department collaboration; business process owners to provide real rules and exceptions; an IT or architecture lead to handle system integration and launch approval; a data lead to confirm definitions, quality, and access permissions; a security and compliance lead to approve logging and risk boundaries; and frontline seed users to participate in trials, UAT, and subsequent rollout.

The key here is not filling names into a contact list, but ensuring these people have time, decision-making authority, and willingness to share accountability for acceptance results.

If only the customer's IT department participates and business leads are long absent, the team can usually only prove "the technology works"; if only business departments participate while IT and security do not get involved early, the project will get stuck after the demo on accounts, interfaces, and launch approval.

External FDEs and the customer's business, IT, data, security, and seed users form a joint delivery mechanism. Self-made diagram.

V. How to allocate headcount for different project sizes

Lightweight PoC: 3-4 people. An Echo or FDPM owns value and scope, one technical lead controls the architecture, one or two Deltas build the prototype, and data, security, and platform experts review as needed. The goal is to validate value and the technical path; PoC code must not be treated directly as a production solution.

Standard production project: 7-10 people. A complete frontline squad is staffed, with SRE, security, customer success, and productization roles shared from the back office. This is a relatively stable configuration for a single department or single core process.

Strategic customers or complex industries: 10-15+ people. Echo and FDPM should each be dedicated full-time, Deltas are divided by direction such as agents, applications, and integrations, evals, security, SRE, and customer success are staffed independently, and a program director and executive Sponsor are appointed. Finance, manufacturing, healthcare, government, and multi-vendor projects especially need internal technical anchors to ensure someone always holds the core architecture, assets, and responsibilities.

Roles can be merged, but responsibilities cannot disappear; the closer a project is to production, the more quality and governance roles need to be brought forward. Self-made diagram.

VI. Don't just measure "launched on time"

FDE team metrics should cover at least four groups of outcomes.

Customer value includes task completion rate, processing time, cost changes, user adoption, and satisfaction; engineering quality includes eval coverage, system availability, time to recovery, access control, and human takeover capability; asset accumulation includes Skill and template output, connector reuse counts, cross-project hours saved, and feedback adoption rate; business results include the Land-to-Expand cycle, renewal rate, scenario expansion rate, per-customer delivery cost, and project gross margin.

Early-stage teams can devote most of engineers' energy to delivery, but must reserve stable time for asset accumulation. As the team matures, the weight of reuse metrics should gradually increase. Otherwise, the more projects there are, the busier the experts get, and delivery costs actually rise.

Only by measuring value, quality, reuse, and business results together can FDE be prevented from degrading into free pre-sales or custom outsourcing. Self-made diagram.

Finally: what is the minimal complete configuration?

If this team is compressed into one sentence: 1 Echo to define the problem, 1 FDPM to drive scope and organization, 1 technical lead to control the architecture, 2-3 Deltas to complete the build, data and eval roles to ensure inputs and quality; SRE, security, customer success, product, and knowledge engineering join at key milestones, and the customer side provides a mirror team with decision-making authority.

In the PoC phase, the minimal core can be compressed into four types of responsibility-Echo, FDPM, senior Delta, and product/knowledge engineering-held concurrently by a few people. But once entering production, data integration, evals, SRE, security, and customer success must be filled in.

To judge whether an FDE team is truly complete, ask three questions in a row: Can it push a vague business problem through to production launch? Can it get business staff to actually use it and produce quantifiable value? Can it make the next similar delivery noticeably faster and cheaper?

Achieving only the first makes it a project delivery team. Only achieving all three makes it a true enterprise FDE team.
