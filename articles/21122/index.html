Summary

This article from FDE board (Westeria Inc.) examines doing FDE-type work as a freelance contractor in Japan. It argues that although job postings titled "FDE" are rare for contract work, substantively equivalent engagements—generative AI adoption support, PoC-to-production implementation, and internal capability building—are growing, so searching by job content is more practical than by title. Engagements fall into three types (advisory, implementation, internalization support) with rates rising alongside responsibility. Since no reliable public rate statistics exist, the author suggests deriving a floor from a salaried FDE salary divided by twelve, plus premiums for social insurance, gaps between projects, and factors like on-site presence. On contracts, it distinguishes ukeoi (fixed-price, completion obligation) from juninrei (effort-based), recommending the latter for AI projects with vague requirements. It also covers the November 2024 freelance law (written terms, payment within ~60 days) and disguised-employment risks when embedded at client sites.

Key points

  • Contract FDE jobs exist under other names; search by content, not title.
  • Three engagement types: advisory, implementation, internalization support.
  • No reliable rate data; derive pricing from salaried salary plus premiums.
  • Juninrei contracts usually fit AI projects better than fixed-price ukeoi.
  • Japan's 2024 freelance law mandates written terms and ~60-day payment windows.

Job postings with the FDE title are mostly for full-time positions, but the actual substance of the work—embedding AI deeply into a customer's business and getting it to the point of real-world use—can also be done on a contracting basis. In fact, in Japan, projects of this type are, in some respects, growing in number first. This article organizes the typical project formats, how to think about rates, and contract considerations when doing FDE-equivalent work as an independent contractor.

The Bottom Line Up Front

  • Postings with the FDE title on a contracting basis are not common, but projects with essentially the same content (AI adoption support, PoC through implementation, in-house capability building) exist in a certain volume as of the time of writing (2026-08). Searching by work content rather than title is the practical approach.
  • There are no public statistics on rates, and survey figures vary widely by platform. Don't take listed amounts at face value; instead, build your own framework based on working days, deliverable definitions, and whether on-site work at the client is required.
  • Contracts change responsibility depending on whether they are quasi-delegation (jun'inin) or contract-for-work (ukeoi). If you end up taking direct orders at the client site, disguised contracting issues can arise, so it's important to align the contract form with reality. Under the Freelance Act, effective 2024-11-01, there are also rules on written disclosure of terms and payment deadlines.

Do FDE Projects on a Contracting Basis Exist?

The reason it's hard to find postings searching "FDE contracting" is a matter of the title. The name FDE is mostly used for employees of vendors with their own products, and external contract postings use different wording. Terms like "Generative AI adoption support," "AI PoC support," "LLM implementation support," and "AI in-house capability support" are examples.

Looking at the substance of the work, the overlap with FDE is substantial: interviewing the client about their business, verifying available data, building something that works, and making it stick in the field. The differences are that the foundation you build on is not the vendor's own product but the cloud or model the customer chose, and that your involvement ends when the contract period ends.

Therefore, when looking for this work on a contracting basis, it's practical not to filter by the word FDE but to search by work content. Reading full-time FDE job postings first makes it easier to judge the substance when you look at contract project descriptions. Full-time FDE positions currently open in Japan are compiled in the FDE board job listings.

Three Main Project Types

AI adoption projects that come through on a contracting basis fall into the following three types by scope of responsibility. Even when they're all called "AI adoption support," the content differs, so confirm which type it is before signing.

Compensation levels also tend to rise in this order. The broader the scope of responsibility and the clearer the measure of results, the higher the rate—but the accountability demanded also becomes heavier.

  • Advisory/Companion Type: The client does the hands-on work; you review designs and advise. Often 1–2 days per week, with longer durations. No implementation responsibility.
  • Implementation Type: You build from PoC to production yourself. 3–5 days per week. Deliverables and deadlines are clear, and this is closest to FDE work.
  • In-House Capability Support Type: You take responsibility until the client's engineers can build it themselves. In addition to implementation, articulating designs and teaching become part of the work. Medium to long duration.

How to Think About Rates—Handling Published Figures

To be frank, as of the time of writing, there are no reliable public statistics on FDE-equivalent contracting projects. Various freelance platforms publish survey figures, but the populations and definitions differ by platform, and the range shown for AI-related monthly rates is quite wide. Citing a specific figure as the market rate would not be honest, so I'll first present primary information from actual job postings and then show how to build up a rate. As a rare example, among jobs listed on FDE board, some companies publish contracting monthly rates alongside full-time hiring, and as of 2026-09 an offer of "1–2 million yen per month for contracting" can be confirmed (an FDE job in the AI × PMI domain). There are also multiple FDE postings that explicitly state "contracting accepted." You can check current listings on the FDE board job list by selecting the tag "contracting accepted."

A useful benchmark when thinking about rates is full-time annual salary. Estimate the annual salary you could be offered as a full-time employee doing the same work, divide by 12, and add a premium for being a contractor (self-paid social insurance, coverage for gaps between projects, no paid leave). The figure from this calculation is a rough lower bound assuming 100% utilization. Annual salary ranges for full-time FDE jobs are covered in the salary article.

Then adjust for the following factors: whether on-site presence at the client is required (add compensation for travel time), whether deliverables are clearly defined (the vaguer they are, the more risk premium to add), whether integration with existing systems is involved (investigation effort is hard to estimate), and whether English is required. Conversely, remote-centric projects with a clear scope can be worthwhile even at a lower rate.

Quasi-Delegation or Contract for Work?

Contracting agreements, in legal classification, fall mainly into contract-for-work (ukeoi) and quasi-delegation (jun'inin). This difference determines who is at a disadvantage when disputes arise, so don't dismiss it as a formality.

A contract for work pays compensation for completion of a deliverable. You cannot claim payment if it isn't completed, and you bear responsibility for repair if the deliverable has defects. It suits projects where requirements are settled and what to build is clear.

Quasi-delegation pays compensation for the performance of the work itself. There is no obligation of completion, but you bear a duty to perform the work with the care expected of a professional. AI adoption projects often don't have a defined deliverable at the start, so in substance quasi-delegation fits most cases. If you sign a contract for work with vague requirements, you risk the claim that "the expected results weren't achieved = not completed."

In the contract, clearly state the scope of work and definition of deliverables, compensation and payment terms, confidentiality, ownership of intellectual property, and whether subcontracting is allowed. For IP in particular, in AI-related projects it's wise to confirm how created prompts and evaluation data are handled as well.

The Freelance Act and Payment Practices

On 2024-11-01, the so-called Freelance Act (Act on the Rationalization of Transactions with Specific Subcontracted Business Operators) came into effect. When commissioning work to individuals without employees, the ordering company assumes certain obligations. Knowing the content also makes negotiation easier for the party receiving the work.

There are two main points. One is the obligation to clearly state the terms of the transaction: the scope of work, compensation amount, payment due date, and other conditions must be presented in writing or by email or similar means. Projects that start with only verbal agreements can be challenged for revision at this point. The other is the payment due date, which must be set within as short a period as possible, and no later than 60 days from the date of receipt of the deliverables.

In practice, it is reasonable to avoid projects where the terms are not presented in writing or where payment terms are extremely long, or to request correction before starting work. For the details of the law and how it applies to your specific project, consult a professional as needed.

Risks of on-site work at client locations and disguised contracting

Since the FDE way of working assumes being embedded at client sites, when working as an independent contractor you need to be careful about issues of direction and control. Even if the contract is a service agreement, if in reality the client manages your attendance and gives detailed instructions on daily tasks, the working arrangement could be judged as disguised contracting.

As the contractor, the points to watch are: maintain the ability to decide how the work is done yourself, avoid being incorporated into morning meetings or attendance management, and accept requests in units of deliverables or issues. Working at a client site itself is not the problem; what matters is whether you are performing the work as an independent business operator.

In practice, it is safest for both parties to agree in writing before starting work on "who you receive requests from and in what units" and "how often and how you report progress." Treat this confirmation not as a courtesy to the client, but as a procedure to protect yourself.

Full-time FDE vs. independent contracting: which to choose

Which is better depends on what you prioritize. Independent contracting gives you great discretion over your workload and allows you to serve multiple clients in parallel, so your breadth across industries grows quickly. On the other hand, a full-time FDE gains deep experience working intensively with their own company's product, and can stay involved with a client's outcomes over a period of years. Internal career paths also open up, such as moving to the product side or into management.

In terms of income, independent contracting can come out ahead if your schedule is fully booked, but you need to compare including periods between projects, the burden of social insurance and taxes, and whether bonuses or stock compensation are provided. In particular, foreign FDE positions often have compensation structures that include stock, so a simple monthly salary comparison does not reveal the reality.

As a practical approach, it is safest to keep an eye on both options. The FDE board job listings show full-time FDE positions you can apply for in Japan, and if you register as a candidate, you will be notified when a matching position is posted.

Frequently asked questions

  • Are there independent contractor positions with the FDE title?
  • Postings using that title are still not common. However, as of the time of writing (2026-08), there are a certain number of projects with essentially the same content under names such as generative AI implementation support, PoC implementation, and AI in-house development support. Search by the work content, not the title.
  • What rate is reasonable?
  • There are no reliable public statistics, so I cannot state a specific figure. I recommend taking the annual salary of a full-time employee doing the same work, dividing it monthly, and adding a premium for your share of social insurance and periods between projects. Then adjust based on whether on-site work is required, how clearly deliverables are defined, and English requirements.
  • Should the contract be a contract for work (ukeoi) or a quasi-mandate (jun'inin)?
  • For projects where the deliverable is not fixed at the start, a quasi-mandate better matches the reality. Under a contract for work you bear an obligation to complete the deliverable, so if you accept a contract for work with vague requirements, you risk the client claiming that a lack of results constitutes non-completion. Choose based on the nature of the project.
  • Should I avoid projects that involve being stationed at the client site?
  • Being stationed on-site is not itself the problem; the reality of direction and control is what matters. If you confirm in writing before starting that you can decide how the work is done and that requests come in units of deliverables or issues, an on-site arrangement can still be legitimate.
  • Can I move to a full-time FDE role after gaining experience through independent contracting?
  • Yes. In fact, experience implementing solutions at client sites is directly valued in FDE selection processes. For each project, record what was ambiguous, what decisions you made, and whether the work was actually used; this will be useful both in your resume and in interviews. You can check currently open positions in the FDE board job listings.
