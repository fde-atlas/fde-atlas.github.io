Written by Shohei Tokumitsu, CEO of DeFactory, this long-form guide maps out a survival strategy for freelance engineers in Japan heading into 2026. It argues that income depends less on raw skill than on structural choices: which contract layer you sit in, which project phase you own, and how you retain clients. Using En Japan data (average monthly rate of ¥783,000 in December 2025, over 470,000 listings) and government labor-shortage projections, it shows that deep subcontracting chains siphon 20–40% in margins, making end-client direct contracts the key lever. The article covers rate-raising tactics, agent selection criteria, transitioning to direct contracts, tax and retirement planning, and highlights FDE (Forward Deployed Engineer) roles paying ¥1.3–1.8 million monthly.

Key points

  • Average freelance monthly rate hit ¥783,000 in Dec 2025, but realistic annual income is ¥7–9 million.
  • Each subcontracting layer takes 15–20%; moving to end-direct deals can add ¥200,000–300,000 monthly.
  • Four independent rate levers: move upstream, sharpen skills, shorten the contract chain, negotiate with evidence.
  • FDE roles command ¥1.3–1.8 million monthly, among the highest-paying positions.
  • Freelancers need roughly 1.3–1.5x a salaried income to match the same living standard.

To earn a sustainable income as a freelance engineer over the long term, skills alone are not enough. Your rate is determined not only by technical ability, but also by structural decisions: which supply chain you position yourself in, which phases you take on, and how you keep projects going.

From the current state of the market to how annual and take-home income work, tactics for raising your rate, strategies for winning projects, and designing your finances and working style — we will organize the key points for building a long-lasting career, one by one.

According to a survey by the Ministry of Economy, Trade and Industry, the supply-demand gap for IT professionals could reach up to 790,000 people by 2030. What is lacking is not simply "manpower," but talent in specialized fields directly tied to business competitiveness, such as AI, cloud, and security.

This situation is changing how companies hire. Companies that have concluded that full-time hiring alone cannot keep up are actively leveraging external, ready-to-contribute talent. Behind the sustained high demand for freelance engineers lies this structural shift.

According to En Japan's periodic survey, the average monthly rate for freelance projects in December 2025 was 7.83 million yen... wait, 783,000 yen, and the number of listed projects exceeded 470,000. On the surface, these numbers suggest a boom, but one caveat is needed here.

Demand is for "people who can immediately address a company's challenges," not for implementation resources. Those who handle only simple implementation are easily caught up in price competition, while those who can handle design, project leadership, and decision-making sit at the center of high rates.

Source: Average Monthly Rate for Freelance Engineers, December 2025 (En Japan Co., Ltd.)

According to Relance's "Freelance Engineer White Paper 2024," 59.5% of engineers reported increased income after going independent. The average annual revenue is approximately 6.32 million yen, so the notion that "income always drops when you go independent" does not match reality.

However, the remaining 40% have seen their income stay flat or decline. Going independent does not automatically make you money, and there is a clear gap between those who succeed and those who struggle.

Three factors mainly determine that gap. First, whether you understand market rates. If you keep accepting projects without knowing what your skills are currently worth, you are likely to get locked into below-market rates.

Next, whether you can articulate your skills as market value. Even with the same experience, those who can articulate design work, leadership experience, and client-facing skills are evaluated more highly. And finally, whether you know how to choose projects. If you only pick projects deep in the supply chain, your share will be cut no matter how good your skills are. Success or failure as an independent is largely decided here.

Freelance engineers' working styles fall broadly into four categories: on-site, remote, hybrid, and juggling multiple projects. Data from the second half of 2025 shows that the average rate for remote projects exceeds that of on-site projects, so the perception that "remote work means worse conditions" does not reflect reality.

However, "flexibility in working style" and "shallow supply chain position" are separate issues. Even working fully remotely, if the project is buried under multiple layers of subcontracting, your take-home pay will not grow. Conversely, even a hybrid arrangement with only two office days per week can command a high rate if it is a direct contract with the end client.

In terms of contract routes, projects are a mix of those deep in SES-style supply chains, those via freelance agents, direct end-client contracts, and quasi-direct contracts via referrals. The discussion of "supply chains," "agents," and "direct contracts" covered in detail from Chapter 2 onward assumes this distinction, so keep it in mind here.

To answer the question "How much can I actually earn?", you need to understand three separate figures: monthly rate, annual income, and take-home pay. Looking only at averages can lead to significant discrepancies from reality.

As you can see, simply becoming a freelancer doesn't necessarily increase take-home pay. If you keep taking deeply layered subcontracted projects, your take-home pay may stay the same as—or fall below—your salaried days.

Beyond differences in taxes and social insurance premiums, when you include "hidden costs" such as no retirement benefits, no paid leave, sales costs, and out-of-pocket expenses, you need sales of roughly 1.3 to 1.5 times your salaried annual income to maintain the same standard of living. Getting into a business flow with access to direct end-client projects is the prerequisite for profiting as a freelancer.

According to En Japan data, in September 2025 the average monthly rate for remote projects was ¥790,000 versus ¥738,000 for onsite work. In December 2025, remote was ¥802,000 and onsite ¥766,000—remote exceeded onsite in both cases.

Behind the higher rates for remote work is a structure where a premium is placed on "people who can be trusted to work remotely." They are more likely to be evaluated on results, and hiring competition occurs nationwide, so immediate effectiveness is strongly demanded.

On the other hand, fully remote positions tend to attract many applicants and become highly competitive. Hybrid arrangements that allow one or two days a week in the office expand the pool of available projects and can sometimes be advantageous in rate negotiations.

Rather than focusing on "remote or not," optimizing across three points—rate, ease of sales, and continuity—is the rational approach for long-term success.

Ways to raise your rate aren't limited to "polishing your skills." The four axes of process, skills, business flow, and negotiation each have independent impact. Where you start can greatly change the results you get from the same amount of time.

Among the four, the most immediately effective is shortening the business flow. Even without changing your skills, simply changing how you take on projects can make a difference of ¥200,000–300,000 per month. Alongside skill improvement, it's worth starting by reviewing your business flow first.

Upstream processes command high rates not because they're "prestigious work." Processes such as requirements definition, design, technology selection, and client negotiation have a large impact on a project's overall success, and the cost of failure is high. Companies pay high compensation to those who can take on that risk.

In the freelance market too, monthly rates for PM/PMO roles generally range from ¥8 million to ¥12 million. Moreover, PMs who understand the realities of engineering are rare and tend to be highly valued in the market.

Shifting upstream doesn't mean suddenly becoming a manager. Propose design reviews on your current project, organize technical issues and explain them to the client, lead alignment meetings with customers. Building up this kind of value outside of implementation, one piece at a time, is the starting point.

By taking these steps, your position shifts from "implementer" to "person who moves projects forward," and your rate range naturally transitions as well.

Attracting attention in recent years is the FDE (Forward Deployed Engineer).

An FDE is an engineer role that handles everything end-to-end—from requirements definition and implementation to business integration—while stationed at the client site. The role has shifted significantly from "builder" to "person who solves the client's business challenges." Monthly rates center around ¥1.3–1.8 million, making it one of the roles with the highest-rate projects among engineers who can handle upstream processes.

Even if an end company tries to procure external talent at 1 million yen per month, margins are taken out each time a prime contractor, subcontractor, or agency is involved. The rate that ultimately reaches the individual varies greatly depending on the number of layers in the transaction chain.

End company (1 million yen) ↓ Prime contractor (margin 15-20%) Primary contractor (800,000-850,000 yen) ↓ Secondary contractor (margin 15-20%) Subcontractor (650,000-700,000 yen) ↓ Agency/agent (margin 10-20%) The freelancer themselves (500,000-600,000 yen)

Even with identical skills and experience, a deep transaction chain alone creates a difference of 100,000-200,000 yen per month. This is a gap that skill improvement alone can hardly recover.

Rate negotiations are more likely to succeed when conducted with a three-part set: changes in your role, market rates, and quantifiable results—not emotional arguments.

The suitable timing for negotiation comes at four points: contract renewal, when your scope of responsibility expands, when market rates clearly rise, and when you take on a leadership role. Evidence you can use includes market rate data from sites like Freelance Start, changes in your assigned processes, quantifiable results, and how difficult you would be to replace.

Approaching the negotiation with the tone of "I would like to request a readjustment of terms relative to my current role and market standards" rather than "please raise my rate" turns the negotiation into a matter of adjustment rather than confrontation.

Sample email (when your role has expanded):

Over the past few months, my scope of responsibility has expanded from implementation-focused work to design and stakeholder coordination. Taking current market rates into consideration, could we discuss terms at the time of the next contract renewal?

Preparing drafts for the three patterns—role expansion, rising market rates, and contract renewal—means you can use them immediately when the time comes. Keeping market rate data on hand allows you to present it as "objective evidence" during negotiations.

Thinking "the higher the pass-back rate, the better" when choosing an agent is only half right. Even with a high pass-back rate, if the original order rate is low, your take-home pay will not grow.

For example, if you take a project at a rate of 600,000 yen through Agent A with a 10% margin, your take-home is 540,000 yen. If you take a project at a rate of 800,000 yen through Agent B with a 25% margin, your take-home is 600,000 yen. Even with a lower pass-back rate, a higher original rate results in higher take-home pay.

The metric you should correctly evaluate is the multiplication of "pass-back rate × original order rate × transparency of the transaction chain." A pass-back rate of 80% or more is considered a fair standard, but at the same time, "what kind of projects they have" is what matters.

When choosing an agent or project, there are five points to check.

  • Direct end contract ratio: The fewer layers of subcontracting, the higher your take-home pay
  • Average rate range: The difference between the 700,000-yen range and the 900,000-yen range amounts to over 2 million yen per year
  • Margin transparency: Agents who disclose their margins are a sign of trustworthiness
  • Payment terms: The difference between a minimum of 35 days and 60 days changes the burden on your cash flow
  • Remote/hybrid flexibility: Directly tied to the number of available projects and ease of working

The biggest advantage of direct contracts is the elimination of margins. By contracting directly with the end company, you gain more freedom in negotiating terms, and continuing and expanding the work becomes easier.

However, since you must handle everything yourself—sales, drafting contracts, invoicing, and dealing with issues—relying solely on direct contracts right at the start of your independence carries high risk.

A realistic step is to first build a track record and connections through an agent, and then move to direct projects once a relationship of trust has been established.

  • Enter a project through an agent and build a track record on-site
  • Deepen your relationships with the point of contact and the client
  • Propose a transition to a direct contract at the time of contract renewal
  • Gradually increase quasi-direct projects obtained through referrals in parallel

Note that the Freelance Act, enacted in November 2024, mandates written disclosure of transaction terms and setting payment deadlines for compensation (in principle, within 60 days). Keep in mind that the more direct contracts you have, the more managing contract terms becomes your own responsibility.

Engineers who land long-running projects aren't chosen for code quality alone. The factors that generate renewals, referrals, and repeat requests come down to four points: response speed, accuracy of progress reporting, expectation management, and cooperativeness.

What clients ultimately look for is the reassurance that "this person is safe to rely on." Progress is visible, problems are reported early, and there's no friction with others. People who provide this reassurance are less likely to have their contracts terminated, even at a somewhat higher rate.

Conversely, people who can write code but are slow to reply, sloppy in reporting, or passive won't be retained regardless of their rate. The essence of freelancers who thrive long-term lies in the twin pillars of skill and trust.

Engineers with 80-point technical skills and 120-point communication skills ultimately earn steadily for the longest time.

Beyond building a system to earn, you also need a design to protect, grow, and sustain the money you earn. To avoid "this isn't what I expected" after going independent, organize your workload, taxes, and retirement planning all at once.

A combination of a main project at 4 days per week (¥700,000/month) plus a side project at 1 day per week (¥150,000/month) yields a monthly income of ¥850,000 and an annual income of about ¥10.2 million. Holding multiple projects diversifies the risk of depending on a single company.

The purpose of juggling projects is not to pack your schedule, but to diversify risk and maintain negotiating power. If you depend on a single main client, you tend to end up on their terms in rate negotiations. With multiple income streams, you don't have to accept unreasonable conditions.

Two things to watch out for: the risk of quality degradation and damaged relationships from overwork, and the fact that some projects include clauses prohibiting side work. Check the contract terms before joining.

There are three major high-priority preparations in your first year of independence.

① Separate business bank account and card If personal and business expenses are mixed together, bookkeeping and tax filing become complicated. Separating them from the start greatly reduces the burden later.

② Introduce accounting software and monthly bookkeeping Visualizing monthly income and expenses becomes material for business decisions. Without knowing how much you're spending on what, you can't see the overall picture of your business, let alone tax savings.

③ Set up a blue tax return system The blue tax return special deduction can be up to ¥550,000 (¥650,000 if certain requirements such as e-filing are met). Along with business-related expenses such as PC, communication costs, books, seminars, and development server fees, set up this system early. The difference from white tax returns can amount to hundreds of thousands of yen per year.

Incorporation is not a topic where you can definitively say "you'll definitely benefit once your income exceeds X yen." The balance of pros and cons changes depending on revenue, profit margin, family structure, social insurance status, and consumption tax treatment.

Incorporating offers benefits such as being able to design income as executive compensation, setting aside retirement benefits as an expense, and a wider range of deductible expenses. On the other hand, costs also arise, such as incorporation fees, accounting and tax costs for corporate settlements, and the corporate share of social insurance premiums.

As a guideline, it's safest to ask an accountant for an estimate once profits exceed a certain scale and continuity into subsequent years becomes visible. Considering not just tax rates but also external credibility, hiring, use of subcontractors, and retirement benefit design makes your decision-making criteria clearer.

Freelancers don't have the retirement benefits, additional employee pension, or welfare benefits that company employees have. Retirement preparation must be designed by yourself from the moment you go independent.

There are four main systems you can utilize.

  • Small Business Mutual Aid (Kojo): A national mutual aid system operated by the SME Support Agency. It's a mechanism for saving funds for business closure or retirement, and premiums are fully income-deductible. It functions as a de facto retirement system for sole proprietors.
  • iDeCo (Individual Defined Contribution Pension): Premiums are fully income-deductible and investment gains are tax-free. Sole proprietors can contribute up to ¥68,000 per month in total combined with the National Pension Fund.
  • NISA (tsumitate-NISA): Investment gains are tax-free. Many people use it alongside iDeCo.
  • Income compensation insurance: Covers income risk when you can't work due to illness or injury. For freelancers, who lack the sickness and injury benefits that company employees have, it's worth considering.

For health insurance, after leaving your job you need to compare whether National Health Insurance or voluntary continued coverage is more advantageous. If you have many dependents, voluntary continued coverage may be advantageous; if your income from the previous year drops significantly, National Health Insurance may be lighter on your wallet.

Beyond building a system to earn, what's needed next is designing your own skills and stance. Even after fully setting up external arrangements such as deal flow, taxes, and agent selection, it ultimately comes back to "what kind of person you are when engaging with projects."

Freelance rates are determined by market evaluation, not internal evaluation. Rather than versatility like "I can do anything," scarcity like "leave this domain to me" tends to be valued more.

Being able to handle design with TypeScript/React, writing high-load APIs in Go, or connecting data pipelines with LLM applications in Python. Being strong in such specific contexts directly translates into higher rates.

It's easiest to organize skill development into three directions: taking on upstream work in your current project (stepping into design, requirements gathering, and client liaison), expanding into adjacent areas (backend + infrastructure, frontend + design skills, etc.), and connecting new technologies to practical work (using AI, generative AI, cloud-native, etc. in the field early).

What DeFactory values is talent that can connect technology to business value. There are many projects here with people who don't just "write code" but think about what to build and drive it through to realization.

The first 30 days after joining a project greatly influence your subsequent rate negotiations, renewal rate, and referral rate.

Passive people just complete the tasks they're given. People who grow first grasp the big picture, confirm stakeholders' expectations, articulate issues proactively, and deliver anticipatory contributions, however small.

On your first day, you should confirm the project background, current challenges, expectations for you, and communication style. Spend your first week on greetings to stakeholders, reviewing documentation, and listing current issues. From weeks 2 to 4, the ideal approach is to deliver small wins while building the impression that "this person can be trusted with work."

Your behavior here becomes the starting point for contract renewals, referrals, and rate negotiations. It helps to think of your first 30 days as an extension of sales.

Freelancers who thrive long-term see their channels for winning projects gradually change. Even if agents dominate early on, over time repeat requests from past clients, referrals from acquaintances, and invitations via communities increase.

This is not about "becoming famous"; it means creating a state where your work quality is visible to third parties. Referrals arise from three conditions: technical skill at a level others can trust with work, trust that makes people want to work with you, and visibility that lets outsiders understand what you do.

Means of visibility include GitHub, technical blogs, speaking at study groups and events, and social media. The goal is not to grow follower counts, but to be recognized as "this person is strong in this area." Reputation is not something that happens by chance; it can be deliberately designed.
