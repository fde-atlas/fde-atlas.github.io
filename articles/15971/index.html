A customer need your product team refused

FDE Interview Prep · 2026 · 2 min read

Summary

This is an interview-prep piece on answering the prompt about a customer need your product team refused. Citing Ramp's Software Engineer, Forward Deployed posting (September 2026), it argues a strong answer must show both halves: the customer still got what they needed, and you carried evidence back to the product team. It lays out five moves—state the underlying need, fairly restate the product team's reason for refusing, describe a workaround with clear ownership and costs, bring data on other affected customers back to the team, and report the outcome. It flags traps like end runs around the roadmap and stopping at the workaround, lists likely follow-up questions, and includes a fictional hospital-network example answer.

How to answer

This question tests two directions an works in at once. Ramp’s Software Engineer, Forward Deployed posting, as of September 2026, asks its engineers to make prioritization calls on when to build a custom solution and when to accelerate a broader initiative. Source 1Software Engineer, Forward Deployed @ RampPublisherRamp (Ashby job board)Source typecompany job posting So the strong answer has both halves: the customer still got what they needed, and you took evidence back.

Five moves:

  • The need, not the request. What the customer was trying to do and what it cost them without it.
  • The refusal, stated fairly. The product team’s reason in their terms: a roadmap conflict, a model it would break, one customer’s edge case. If you can’t state their reason, you weren’t ready to push back.
  • The workaround. What you built outside the product, who owns it, what it costs to run, and how it avoids a fork. Tell the customer plainly that it is a workaround, not a feature.
  • The case back. Evidence, not advocacy: other customers with the same need, what their workarounds cost, and the narrowest version of the ask. Say who you took it to and in what form.
  • The outcome. Shipped, shipped differently, or still no. If it stayed no, say what you did and why: raised it through your own manager with the product manager in the room, or accepted it and made the workaround something the customer could run without you. Then what happened to the workaround.

Say the product team’s reason out loud before your own: “Their no was fair; it would have meant a second permission model to migrate.” It shows you can work with the people who own the roadmap.

The first trap is the end run, where the customer’s executive forces the feature and the product team is the villain. The second is stopping at the workaround: the customer is happy, and the next one hits the same wall.

Follow-ups

What the interviewer may ask next, once your first answer is on the table.

  • What would you have done if the product team had said no a second time?
  • Who maintained the workaround after you left, and what did it cost them?
  • How did you find the other customers with the same need, and how sure were you of the count?

Where answers go wrong

  • Tells it as the product team being wrong and the customer’s executive forcing the change, which reads as going around the people who own the roadmap.
  • Stops at the workaround and never takes evidence back to the product team, so the next customer hits the same wall.
  • Builds a workaround that only they can run, or one that forks the product, so the customer is stranded when the engineer rotates off the account.

Answer this in two minutes

Write the answer you would say out loud. The clock starts with your first word.
