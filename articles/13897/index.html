What is a Forward Deployed Engineer (FDE)?

Original title: 什么是前线部署工程师（FDE）？

Product Builder · 2026-10-15 · 13 min read

Summary

This guide explains the forward deployed engineer (FDE) role: engineers embedded with customers who write production code on real data until a generic platform actually solves the customer's problem, then feed recurring patterns back into the product. The role traces to Palantir, whose internal names are Delta for FDEs and Echo for deployment strategists. AI labs like OpenAI and Anthropic now hire FDEs because the bottleneck is moving from impressive demos to reliable workflows on customer data. The article distinguishes FDEs from solutions engineers and consultants, offers a first-week discovery checklist, career-switch paths, advice on reading job postings, and interview expectations, while warning that some FDE titles are rebranded hourly-billed services work.

Key points

  • FDEs sit with customers, building production code on real data.
  • The role originated at Palantir (Delta, paired with Echo strategists).
  • AI companies hire FDEs because deployment, not model capability, is the bottleneck.
  • Key distinction from solutions engineers and consultants: who writes code and where it ends up.
  • Watch for FDE titles that are actually hourly-billed services roles.

Full text

The engineer who sits on the customer's side—writing real code on real data until the product actually solves the customer's problem. Where this role came from, what it involves, and why AI companies are hiring for it in droves.

A Forward Deployed Engineer (FDE) is a software engineer who works directly with customers to make the product succeed in the customer's environment. Instead of building features at headquarters for everyone, they sit with one customer's team, learn their data and workflows, and then write code—integrations, data pipelines, custom tooling—that makes a general-purpose platform actually work for that customer.

You can think of an FDE as a product engineer whose "user" is an organization and whose office is often at the customer's site. The best FDEs also bring what they learn back to the company: the patterns they rebuild for customer after customer become the product's next features.

Three customer-facing technical roles that are often confused. The difference comes down to who writes the code and where that code ends up.

Writes production code with customers on the company's own platform. Success is measured by whether the customer gets real value—often including what the product team learns along the way.

Helps customers understand and adopt the product, usually before or around the sale: demos, technical Q&A, proofs of concept. Builds less, explains more.

Delivers a project for a client, usually with whatever tools fit. They leave when the engagement ends; what they build rarely feeds back into a product.

The role is usually traced back to Palantir. Palantir's business was built on sending engineers to government and enterprise customers to make its data platform work on messy, real-world problems. Palantir's own engineering blog distinguished between "Devs" and "Deltas": the former build the core product, while the latter are the Forward Deployed Engineers who take it into the field.

AI brought this idea into the mainstream. Frontier models are powerful, but most companies struggle to get from an impressive demo to a reliable workflow built on their own data. FDEs bridge exactly that gap—wiring models into real systems with retrieval, tools, and evals. AI labs and AI startups are standing up forward deployed teams for the same reason Palantir did: the bottleneck is often deployment—and part of the job is recognizing when a model simply isn't good enough for a use case yet.

Four pieces of work nearly every FDE encounters week after week.

The customer asks for a dashboard; the real problem is that three teams can't agree on the same number. The FDE looks past the request and digs all the way down to the broken workflow.

Get data access first—security reviews, compliance sign-offs, network approvals—then untangle messy systems, clean the data, and write the integrations nobody wants to write. Most of the value hides in this unglamorous "plumbing" work.

Put something useful in the customer team's hands within days, then iterate with them. A tool that runs on their own data is more convincing than any slide deck.

Notice what gets rebuilt over and over across customers, push it back to the product team, and argue for it—because most field work never becomes product.

One enterprise deal, two ways of delivering it.

Customer: "We want to use AI to speed up how our claims team reviews documents."

The Solutions Engineer runs a pilot with clean sample files and hands it off to the customer's IT team. Three months later, it fails on scanned PDFs—and most real claims documents are scans. The renewal is suddenly in doubt.

Spends the first weeks embedded with the claims team while pushing security reviews and data access forward, then builds a data pipeline on a few hundred real documents: it handles scans, flags low-confidence cases for human review, and logs every decision. Within about two months, reviewers rely on it daily—and scan handling eventually ships in the product.

Three kinds of builders, three different "users."

At AI companies, many FDEs are effectively AI engineers specializing in one customer's problem. What they share with product engineers is ownership: neither stops at "the code runs"—they stop at "the problem is solved."

FDEs often have a product-side counterpart. AI companies have started hiring Forward Deployed Product Managers—sometimes called deployed PMs—who work inside key customer accounts, define what to build, unblock deployment, and advocate for which customer needs should become product features. Palantir's long-standing equivalent is the Deployment Strategist, internally called "Echo," paired with the engineers' "Delta." In Palantir's own words, the two roles blend product, engineering, and strategy, and the boundaries are often blurry. If you're a technical product manager who would rather sit next to customers than a roadmap, this path is worth a look—though such roles are still rare today.

An honest self-assessment.

  • Gets energy from customers and real problems, not just from a tidy codebase
  • Can win the trust of executives as well as skeptical engineers and IT teams on the customer's side
  • Can debug a data pipeline and brief an executive in the same afternoon
  • Enjoys variety—juggling multiple customers, industries, and data stacks at once
  • Wants to go deep in one codebase for years—this role is inherently broad
  • Prefers to avoid travel, meetings, and the ambiguity of not knowing who the "user" is
  • Needs a predictable rhythm—customer emergencies, travel, and juggling multiple accounts are the norm in this job
  • Wants to set product direction—FDEs influence the roadmap, but product managers own it

This role favors engineers who build fast and can talk to anyone.

  • 1Build end-to-end delivery skills. FDEs are often responsible for the whole solution: data coming in, the logic, the interface, results going out—usually as part of a small team with minimal supervision. Practice turning a vague problem into a working tool with little support.
  • 2Practice on messy, real data. Clean datasets won't teach you this job. Build something on real spreadsheets, real APIs, real piles of files—and handle every gnarly edge case.
  • 3
  • Prove you can work with customers. Actively join customer calls, support escalations, or serve internal "customers" like other teams. Interviewers want evidence that you can understand problems from someone else's perspective.

What to figure out in your first week with a new customer, before writing lots of code. Ask what's actually happening today, not what people think they want.

# [Customer Name] — Week One Discovery ## Business Problem [Where it hurts today, in their own words. When it last happened and how much it cost.] Success metric: [one number, its value today, the target] ## Users and Workflow [Who does this work, step by step, and where it breaks down] ## Data and Access Data sources: [systems, files, APIs—who owns each one] Access path: [security review, data processing agreement (DPA), PII, data residency rules] ## Systems to Integrate [Where the output must land, and who approves changes there] ## Constraints [Deadlines, budget, compliance, network, rules about models or vendors] ## Who Decides Sponsor: [name] · Day-to-day owner: [name] · Veto holders: [IT, security, legal] ## What "Done" Looks Like [In production, with real users, how it's measured, by when] ## Risks [Things models can't yet do reliably—and how we plan to test them with evals] ## Feedback to Product [Things we're rebuilding that product should provide for all customers]

The risks row is exactly where evals come in: test the tricky cases on the customer's own data before anyone commits to a launch date.

Common paths into forward deployed work, and the gaps each path usually needs to fill.

  • Software engineers The most direct path—Palantir hires FDSEs from engineering backgrounds, including new graduates. Your gap is usually customers, not code. Join customer calls or support escalations proactively, and practice turning vague requirements into working tools on messy data.
  • Solutions engineers or sales engineers You already know how to win customer trust. The gap is production code: building and owning systems after the deal closes, not demos before it. Ship something real end to end, and learn enough about retrieval and evals to own a deployment on your own.
  • Technical consultants Discovery and stakeholder experience transfer directly—OpenAI's new deployment company even acquired a consulting firm to help staff up. The shift is going deep on one platform and feeding what you learn back to its product team, rather than picking the right tool for each project.
  • Data scientists or analysts Much of an FDE's work is data work: finding the right datasets, cleaning data, building pipelines. Fill the software engineering gap—maintainable code, deployment, debugging in production—and practice explaining results to non-technical stakeholders.

The title is used loosely; some roles are really sales engineering or solutions roles with a new name. Look at the responsibilities, not the title.

  • Palantir (FDSE)
  • The original version: a software engineer embedded with a single customer, configuring and extending Palantir's platform to solve their problems, usually on a small team and often paired with a Deployment Strategist. Job postings mention travel to customer sites, and government projects may require security clearance.
  • AI lab FDEs
  • Building production-grade AI systems inside large customers—agents, integrations, MCP servers—and passing frontline feedback back to research and product teams. Usually a senior role, often with heavy travel. Both OpenAI and Anthropic introduced outside investors in 2026 and created separate deployment or services companies, so confirm which entity actually employs you.
  • "FDE" at AI startups
  • Usually builders who get customers up and running on the product, own integrations, and work closely with founders. Sometimes it's really sales engineering with a new title—the responsibilities tell you which.
  • Services or implementation firms
  • Consultancies and staffing firms now sell FDEs as a service. It can be real engineering work, but you're closer to a consultant: billed to the customer by the hour, and what you build rarely influences the product.
  • Your time is billed by the hour and utilization is the core metric—whatever the title says, it's a services job
  • No feedback loop back to product: ask for a recent feature that came out of frontline work
  • Compensation tied to sales metrics or commission—that signals a sales engineering role
  • Lots of solo travel to customer sites—ask how much travel, and who else owns each customer

FDE interviews split roughly half engineering, half customer skills:

  • Practical coding and data Build something useful from realistic, messy data. Tests whether you can deliver under real conditions.
  • Problem decomposition Turn a broad, vague customer problem into a plan you could start tomorrow. Tests structured thinking under ambiguity.
  • Customer scenarios Work through a technical problem with a (simulated) customer, or push back on an unrealistic request. Tests communication and judgment.
  • Behavioral interviews Talk about how you've owned outcomes, handled pressure, and worked with non-technical people. Tests whether you fit a demanding, fast-changing role.

Interview formats vary a lot by company and level—use this to know what to practice, not as a script.

"Forward deployed" is borrowed from military language for troops stationed near the front line. Palantir applied it to engineers embedded with customers and popularized it—the internal nickname "Delta" dates back to the company's early days, when the business development team named itself after letters of the NATO alphabet. Palantir's official title is "Forward Deployed Software Engineer" (FDSE)—worth knowing when job hunting. The model later spread through enterprise software, and then to AI companies, which built teams to help large organizations get models into production.

FDE experience is highly transferable. Common next steps include product management (you've seen more customer problems than almost anyone), engineering management, solutions architecture, and founding a company—many founders started by building the same thing for several customers.

Every FDE team faces the same tension: one-off custom work wins customers, but too much of it turns a product company into a services company. Healthy teams maintain a steady loop between frontline engineers and product teams, so today's custom work becomes tomorrow's standard feature.

Common FDE mistakes: building custom solutions nobody can maintain after they leave; saying yes to every customer request; and losing touch with the product team back at the company. The job is making customers successful while making the product better—doing only one of the two is only half the job.

Forward Deployed Engineers work directly with customers to make the product succeed in the customer's environment. They learn the customer's workflows and data, write integrations and custom tools, ship working software fast, and feed lessons back to the product team.

Solutions Engineers mainly help customers evaluate and adopt a product through demos and technical guidance. Forward Deployed Engineers write lots of production code with customers on the company's platform to solve the customer's real problems.

AI models are powerful, but most organizations struggle to turn them into reliable workflows on their own data. Forward Deployed Engineers bridge that gap—connecting models to real systems, building evals, and handling the edge cases demos skip over.

The role is most associated with Palantir, which embedded engineers to work alongside government and enterprise customers. The model has since spread to enterprise software and AI companies.

Solid full-stack and data engineering skills, comfort working with messy real-world data, a knack for spotting problems, the ability to communicate clearly with non-technical people, and—increasingly important—experience reliably deploying AI models.

For engineers who enjoy working with customers and value variety, this is a great path. You get exposed to a large number of real problems in a short time, which lays an excellent foundation for later moving into product management, engineering management, or entrepreneurship. The trade-off: frontline work may be less visible when it comes to promotion, and returning to core product engineering sometimes requires deliberate effort.
