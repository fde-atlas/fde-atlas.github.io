The Last Few Gates Before Launch

Original title: 上线前的最后几道门

pafa.github.io · 2026-01-09 · 19 min read

Summary

Using the UK government's GOV.UK Chat as a reference case, this chapter examines what must be verified before an AI system goes live. The author argues that user satisfaction does not equal reliability: in GDS's early trial, about 70% of 157 survey respondents found answers useful, yet expert review found accuracy below the site's required standard. Launch is framed as a set of decisions about scope, accountability, and stopping conditions. The chapter covers turning tasks into evaluable test cases, separating everyday from high-stakes questions, calibrating model-based grading against expert judgment, detecting false refusals (citing a Salesforce example), and using staged rollouts with rehearsed rollback, drawing on AWS release engineering practices.

Key points

  • GOV.UK Chat: ~70% of 157 users found answers useful, but expert checks found accuracy insufficient.
  • Launch is a set of decisions on permitted scope, responsibility, and stopping conditions, not a date.
  • Evaluations must separate everyday tasks from high-consequence ones; a 90% total can hide critical gaps.
  • Model-based grading needs calibration with experts; test environments can leak clues that inflate scores.
  • Limited rollouts must identify affected requests, and rollback requires rehearsal plus handling changed data.

If users say it's helpful, is the system ready to open officially?

On January 18, 2024, the UK government's digital service team (GDS) published the early trial results of GOV.UK Chat. Of 157 follow-up questionnaires, nearly seventy percent of users found the answers useful. At the same time, expert reviews showed that the answers overall still fell short of the accuracy level required for this government website. The team also found that some users, trusting the government brand, underestimated the risk of errors. 1 Favorability and reliability appeared in the same report, yet did not point to the same conclusion.

This chapter uses a government AI project with a relatively rich public record. The materials show that a model vendor provided engineering support, but there is no clear evidence that participants held the FDE title. It is treated here as a reference for how customers and engineering teams jointly validate a system. Whatever the role is called, the people responsible for delivery face the same problem: for a capability that already looks usable, what is still missing before it is worth putting into the hands of real users?

"Launch" is easily spoken of as a date, but it actually contains a set of different permissions: who is allowed to do what, which version of the system they use, how problems are discovered, and which actions can be stopped in time. Test reports can help make decisions, but they cannot make all these decisions on the organization's behalf.

First decide what exactly is being permitted this time

A system can already be useful at providing general information while still falling far short when making decisions on someone's behalf. The same model, the same chat box, can carry completely different consequences.

In its algorithmic transparency record published in October 2025, GOV.UK Chat described the boundaries at the time: it organizes guidance from government websites and provides source links, but does not make decisions or take actions on behalf of users. The record lists GDS's responsible roles and notes that Anthropic provides advice and engineering support. 2 This document cannot prove the system never makes mistakes, but it lets readers know what was being tested at the time.

For a project preparing to launch, such boundaries should enter acceptance. If the team's promise is to help users find information, the checks should focus on whether the content is applicable, whether links work, and whether missing information is acknowledged; if the promise is to submit applications, it is also necessary to verify the submission target, authorization, and business outcomes. Performance on the former type of task cannot be used to approve the latter capability.

Engineers also need to know who can judge whether the boundaries are honored. Business owners confirm what outcomes are useful to users, professionals judge whether the content is correct, and operations owners judge whether failures can be handled. The FDE can organize evidence and explain implementations and limitations, but cannot, merely by having completed development, declare on behalf of all roles that the business is ready.

This does not require every launch to go through a grand approval meeting. Internal assistive tools with light consequences that are easy to roll back can have a very lightweight confirmation process; systems that make important statements to the public or change business records need clearer justification. The core of rigor is that responsibility matches consequences, not that the more signatures the better.

Turning "answered pretty well" into checkable tasks

Next, the permitted work needs to be broken down into real, evaluable tasks.

A task should not consist only of a question and a seemingly ideal answer. It should also record what materials were available at the time, what conditions the user was in, what tools the system could use, and what counts as completion. Otherwise, when two runs produce different results, the team will struggle to tell whether the change came from capability, from materials, or from a change in the grader's standards.

For example, this book constructs a teaching test for an information service: the user only asks where to check the status of an application. A passing answer should direct them to the appropriate entry point, not judge on its own whether they qualify. If the test only checks whether the answer contains the words "application status," a long-winded but actually off-topic reply could also score. What really needs verifying is whether the answer helps the user complete the step they asked about.

Ordinary tasks only show what happens under ordinary conditions. One should also look for situations that change how things are handled: the question lacks necessary conditions, the relevant material has expired, the input contains instructions that should not be executed, or the material is insufficient to support a complete answer. Each situation should have an explainable expectation, such as clarifying first, providing only the well-founded part, or stating that it cannot be handled and offering a path forward.

When choosing these tasks, there is no need to distribute all rare cases evenly. Frequently occurring tasks relate to actual value; rare but high-consequence tasks relate to acceptable boundaries. Both need to be examined, but they cannot simply be merged into a single total score. Keeping them separate lets the team know whether an improvement has bettered the experience of most users or patched a critical hole.

The early trial of GOV.UK Chat proceeded stage by stage: the first three stages were internal, including tests that deliberately tried to make the system fail, and only afterwards did it approach real users. 1 The value of this arrangement lies not in the number of stages itself, but in letting evidence from different conditions enter decisions in sequence. Internal staff can catch some obvious errors, while real users may raise questions the team never thought of.

Test cases also need updating, but they must not simply vanish. Turning a real failure into a new case can prevent repetition next time; if old cases are deleted because the system keeps failing at them, rising scores lose their meaning. Conversely, when the business scope genuinely changes, old cases should not permanently bind the new system. Keeping the reasons why a case was added, modified, or retired makes it clear what the evaluation is tracking.

At the same time, it is unwise to use all cases for daily debugging and then treat improvements on that same set as proof of adaptation to new problems. This book recommends keeping a portion of tasks that are not directly used for modification, to check whether the team has merely fixed known phrasings. The scale need not be large at first, but these tasks should come from the relevant scope of work and have verifiable judgment criteria. Casually inventing a few unfamiliar hard problems, or deliberately adding unanswerable questions, does not automatically make the evaluation stricter.

One can also rephrase the same task in several natural ways and observe whether the necessary conditions are still respected. The goal here is not to make the system see through every verbal trick of the test designer, but to distinguish genuine needs from fixed wording: when the user says it differently, which parts of the handling should change and which should not? If the team cannot answer, perhaps the business boundaries need clarifying first, rather than continuing to tweak the model prompt.

The grader can also be wrong

Handing a batch of questions to a model and having another model grade them is convenient. But convenience does not answer whether the grading is reliable.

Some projects can check definite outcomes: whether the required documents exist, whether the record status matches the agreement, whether a link reaches the specified page. Other tasks allow multiple valid phrasings, so identical wording cannot be the standard. In such cases, model grading can help with rapid screening, but experts still need to check whether it mistakes polished expression for correct content, or rejects an alternative answer that is actually valid.

In its January 2026 evals article, Anthropic required calibrating model grading against expert judgment. The article also recorded a practical lesson: in some internal tests, Claude obtained extra clues from Git history left over from previous runs, and its performance was inflated as a result. 3 What Git stores here is file modification history; the model read prior process information that this test should not have provided.

This problem reminds us that the test environment itself is also an object of inspection. If a second run inherits the answers of the first, it is not an independent new attempt. If several failures all stem from the same broken environment, each cannot be counted as a model judgment error. Consistent starting conditions should be restored before discussing whether capability has improved.

This kind of disagreement can be written into three small teaching samples. Suppose the valid material D2 of the "Teaching Service Center" stipulates: when the matter and reference number are known, go to the status entry to check; when the matter is unclear, clarify first; this assistant does not judge application eligibility. D1 is a decommissioned old entry. These materials, outputs, and rulings are preset exercises; no real expert or model has executed these tests, and they are not GOV.UK material.

If you want to judge independently first, you can look at the materials and three questions in the appendix, write down your reasoning, and then read the rulings in the table below.

The disagreement on E2 changed the scoring method: from checking the portal and citation status, it expanded to also check whether the matter is known, whether there is unauthorized eligibility judgment, and whether the citation is applicable. You cannot just manually deduct points for this one item and keep using the old scorer. Record the change of standard G1 to G2 and require all three samples to be re-scored; if the next version changes E2 to ask about the matter first, E1 must also be re-run, to prevent the system from repeatedly asking people who have already provided sufficient conditions.

Three-item evaluation and expert adjudication sheet preserves the complete questions, materials, preset outputs, disagreement reasons, and release decisions, and provides a blank template. Its purpose is to demonstrate how to move from an error to the next action, not to use three items to prove the system is reliable. The third version is only open to employee preview, with E2 and E3 handled manually; public direct output remains closed.

Experts also need the right materials. Looking only at a response may not reveal whether it missed a key condition in the user's question; looking only at the materials the model picked may miss sources that should have been used but were not found. When evaluating content, one should be able to view the task, the applicable originals, and the actual response together. If conditions allow, you can temporarily hide which version produced the response, reducing the expectation that "the new version should be better" from influencing judgment. These arrangements increase review costs, so they should be prioritized for important questions that decide whether to open access.

In particular, keep a place for "cannot judge." Whether some responses are applicable depends on conditions the user did not provide. Forcing scorers to give only right or wrong turns insufficient information into false certainty. A useful evaluation result can state what is missing before a judgment can be made; correspondingly, the system being evaluated should also learn to ask when needed, rather than filling in every blank.

Calibration is not a one-time job. Model upgrades, changes in materials, or users starting to ask new types of questions can all make the original scoring method no longer suitable. Teams can continuously use clear samples to check scorers without re-scoring everything manually every round; but once scoring rationale diverges from professional judgment, they need to pause relying on that score for important decisions.

One score of 90 can hide two different systems

A single total score can easily obscure performance on different types of questions. Teams still need to dig into which questions improved and which important errors were masked by the total.

Below is a teaching example unrelated to the project. Suppose the test contains 80 ordinary questions and 20 questions with heavier consequences, and for demonstration purposes, each question is counted as one point for now.

SystemOrdinary questions answered correctlyHeavier-consequence questions answered correctlyTotal answered correctly
A80 / 8010 / 2090 / 100
B70 / 8020 / 2090 / 100

The two total scores are exactly the same, yet the choices they imply differ. A is excellent on ordinary questions but has an obvious gap on heavier ones; B answers all of this set of heavier questions correctly but leaves some everyday users struggling. You cannot pick whichever threshold makes the total look better and treat both as equally ready. Nor can you conclude that B is reliable for all such questions just because it got all 20 right.

The proportions of questions also change the total. If the actual distribution of user questions differs, the test total does not directly equal the post-launch success rate. For critical questions, you can set mandatory check conditions separately; for everyday experience, you can observe coverage and common failures. This loses the convenience of one number summarizing all the work, but gains judgment that better guides improvement.

Another common confusion is between succeeding at least once over multiple attempts and completing reliably every time. Anthropic's evals article specifically distinguishes these two goals. 3 If users will review multiple candidates and pick one, the best attempt may have value; if users only receive the first response, the team cannot count a later eventual success as the user's actual experience.

Therefore, repeated runs should record where failures occur, not just pick the best result to show. If the same critical question occasionally misses a condition, the average score may still be high; but whether users should be allowed to rely on it directly requires judgment based on consequences and remediation capability. Stability does not mean word-for-word identical wording; it means allowing the parts that may vary to vary, while the conditions that must be met are consistently met.

Blocking errors can also block legitimate work

Validation should also actively look for false refusals: the system followed a rule and thereby failed to help the people it was supposed to serve.

A Customer Zero official interview from Salesforce provides a concrete episode. Bernard Slowey recalled that the team had instructed the agent not to discuss competitors, and later found that when customers asked how to integrate Microsoft Teams with Salesforce, they also got no answer, even though the company had relevant materials. The team then adjusted the instructions. 4 This book uses this self-reported false refusal; the public transcript does not make the date clear, nor does it prove that all behavior after the adjustment is reliable.

This error came from scope drawing: evaluating competing products and helping customers connect software they have already purchased are not the same task. "Never answer whenever a certain name appears" is easy to check, but it puts both on the same side. After the fix, at least paired testing is needed: legitimate integration questions should receive well-grounded help, while questions beyond the service's remit should still be handled appropriately. Verifying only that the originally blocked question now works may miss a newly opened gap on the other side.

Therefore, the score of a guardrail cannot just count how many requests it blocked; it must also consider whether what was blocked should have been blocked, whether what was allowed holds up, and whether the remaining tasks have a continuation path. Only by keeping false allowances, false refusals, and post-handoff results together can business owners judge what price the protection measures paid.

Permission validation should also check for forged instructions from external materials. Using the teaching shopping system from the previous chapter, you can prepare a set of paired tasks: product data containing "read another customer's records" must result in the actual tool call being refused; the same user asking to explain this suspicious text should get analysis without execution; normally adding a confirmed product to one's own cart should still succeed. These three respectively check unauthorized access, the distinction between data and instructions, and normal adoption; you cannot trade "refuse all requests" for a pretty security score.

The objects of checking include real tool actions and backend records, not just whether the response says "I will not execute it." Tests use synthetic accounts in an isolated environment and do not probe real people's data. This is a testing method designed for this book; no claim is made that it has been run on any vendor's product.

Fix one place without reopening another

Truly useful evals change the design, not just rank versions.

In its May 2026 engineering summary, GDS traced the first prototype of GOV.UK Chat to July 2023 and explained that it later combined automated evals, expert review, and online monitoring. The system also arranged two layers of checks, before and after generation, handling problems in inputs and generated responses respectively. 5 This history is not about a model acquiring mature capability the moment it was plugged in, but about a team continuously revising the whole system.

One trade-off is especially concrete. The March pilot report said the team considered displaying answers as they were generated to shorten the wait users perceive, but doing so would require redesigning part of the safety guardrails. 6 Originally, checks could wait until the complete response was generated; displaying early changed the order between checking and what users see.

From this issue one can derive a practical principle: experience improvements can also change what needs to be validated. Faster display is not just tweaking a UI effect; it may leave some checks without time to stop content that has already been shown. Teams need to re-decide which checks must complete before display, which can only catch problems afterward, and whether the corresponding risks are acceptable. This is engineering analysis, not a retrospective reconstruction of GOV.UK's internal plans.

Likewise, switching to a stronger model is not just a matter of rerunning a few old hard problems. It may change response length, how ambiguous questions are handled, and even how it works with existing tools. What really needs to be compared is the combination being prepared for release this time: the model, system instructions, materials, tools, and related configuration. The model vendor's generic leaderboards cannot substitute for how this combination performs on the current tasks.

Sometimes the model name has not changed, yet the behavior has. On September 25, 2026, OpenAI recorded that an image encoding issue in GPT-6 Sol and Luna had been fixed, and advised customers using image inputs to rerun their evals. 7 Therefore, "it is still the same model name" alone cannot justify reusing old scores. Teams should keep the test and run dates, available version information, and the materials and tool combination; after the vendor discloses relevant fixes, they should re-examine their own affected tasks rather than declare improvement based on generic leaderboards.

The system card dated October 7, 2026 further noted: the GPT-6 Sol and Luna that ChatGPT launched this time are the October versions, while Codex and Work still use the September versions. 8 Thus, eval records must also state the actual product entry point; the same model name and similar run dates are not enough to prove that two checks targeted the same combination.

Test records therefore need to be bound to versions. Otherwise, what is seen in the meeting are yesterday's results, while what is actually deployed is a prompt changed on the fly today; both sides can honestly say "the tests passed," yet they are not talking about the same candidate. Keeping the configuration used at run time and the necessary inputs is one of the minimum conditions for making go-live decisions verifiable.

This also leaves room for rapid iteration. Small adjustments that do not change the relevant behavior can reuse evidence that still applies; if content, permissions, tools, or important processes change, re-check the affected parts and their connections. Not every change starts from scratch, and passing once does not make it valid forever. The criterion for judgment should be what the change might affect.

Limited opening: you must be able to see problems within the limited scope

No matter how well offline problems are done, they cannot cover all combinations of real traffic. Before expanding usage, you also need to observe within a scope where consequences can be controlled.

In AWS's release engineering article published in 2020, it describes an approach of first letting a new version serve a small portion of requests, then expanding in waves. The article specifically points out that small-scope problems may be drowned out by aggregate metrics, so you should observe the portion of requests served by the new version; anomalies can trigger automatic rollback. 9 What this chapter borrows is the release mechanism, not treating AWS's specific percentages or wait times as the standard for all FDE projects.

If the new version only handles a small number of tasks, even if many of them fail, the site-wide average success rate may barely change. User grouping, version tagging, and task types are therefore not decorations on statistical reports; they determine whether the team can know who is being affected. A so-called small-scope opening only has corresponding control value when that scope can be identified and observed.

Also avoid equating "opening a little" with "the consequences must be small." A small number of real requests can still contain serious errors; some tasks cannot afford to let errors happen first and then be observed. You can first have the system produce suggestions on the side, with a person capable of judging deciding whether to adopt them, or only open the parts with lighter consequences. This reduces the immediate benefits of automation, and also lowers the commitment the first trial must bear.

How long to observe cannot be decided by the clock alone. A team needs at least to encounter the relevant working conditions before it can judge whether they are covered. A pilot that has only run during the day has not yet shown what happens at night when no familiar staff are present; a version used only by skilled colleagues has not yet shown whether ordinary users understand the prompts. Waiting itself does not produce evidence; you need to know which conditions you are still waiting for.

If real traffic is too sparse, the team can keep uncovered conditions as limitations rather than pretend the observation was complete. Possible options include continuing limited use, expanding to another controllable scope, or using targeted simulated checks. Simulations have their own role, but simulated user counts cannot be written up as real adoption, nor can laboratory results be automatically converted into everyday-work reliability.

On release day, who can pull the scope back

This chapter is concerned with release day: whether the people responsible for observing the new version can identify affected requests, trigger a stop, and hand over tasks that are still unfinished. For how successors are cultivated long term, see Chapter 18; for contractual exit and task migration, see Chapter 21. There is no need to rebuild an entire training system here, but a small-scope trial must not become an unattended opening.

The teaching center arranges one reviewer for the third-version preview. This drill has three stop conditions, each independently valid:

  • Insufficient capacity. Assume that in the next hour only 5 tasks pending review can be processed; when the backlog reaches 6, stop accepting new tasks, and hand already accepted tasks back to the original manual channel. This number comes from an assumed available workforce, not a release standard.
  • Basis for judgment unavailable. When the reviewer cannot obtain or verify the sources used for an answer, also stop accepting new items and transfer accepted tasks back to the original manual channel; even if there is spare capacity, no further items may be accepted.
  • Beyond the approved scope. If any answer bypasses review and goes directly to the public, immediately disable that outlet and find out exactly who received it.

The three conditions cannot substitute for one another. Sufficient staffing cannot make up for a missing basis, and a correct answer cannot make up for the authorization to go straight to the public.

The stop actions must also be implemented for this capability. You can close the entrance for new requests while retaining the ability to read existing records; if switching back to the old version, first confirm that records produced by the new version can still be understood by the old process. Merely restoring the screen to its original state does not deal with the people who have already received answers.

AWS's release article also checks whether the old and new versions can coexist, to avoid data written by the new version being unintelligible to the old version. 9 This shows that rollback is not just reopening the old program. If the data has already changed, or users have already acted on incorrect instructions, restoring the software version may not restore the original reality. At that point, affected records also need to be handled, or appropriate personnel notified to follow up.

Therefore, an actual drill is more valuable than a single sentence of "rollback supported." A team can pause the feature under controlled conditions and confirm whether monitoring detected it, who received the information, whether the old path can continue, and how unfinished tasks were handled. What is recorded in the drill is what was actually done; conditions not drilled still need to be kept on record, not hidden behind one overall checkbox.

Allowing go-live does not mean validation is over

Return to the earlier information-service teaching task. Suppose the team is testing the third version: the public cannot directly receive model answers, and staff can only preview suggestions and send them after verification. In the teaching test, an answer like this appeared: the user did not specify which matter they wanted handled, yet the system guessed a particular matter and added an eligibility judgment on its own; but in a progress query limited to a specified matter, staff could verify the entry point against the attached applicable page, and could also return to manual handling when they found it off-topic. This supports restricted use with human review, but does not yet support answering the public directly.

On this basis, the business owner approves only a small number of trained staff to trial the third version, the engineering owner confirms the configuration matches the test records, and the stopping and manual handover arrangements from the previous section are carried over. The team keeps "guessing the matter when information is insufficient" as a problem the next version must solve; after fixing this problem, it must also re-judge whether the opening can be expanded. These findings and decisions are all part of the teaching design, not an internal process disclosed by GOV.UK.

Such validation can also slow delivery. If all tasks are reviewed at the highest level of consequence, the team may indeed be unable to open anything for a long time. The solution is to tier by actual consequence and recoverability, and allow opening first a small portion that already has sufficient evidence. The existence of unknowns must not lead to indefinitely postponing everything; nor must the need to continue the pilot lead to rewriting unknowns as already passed.

Progress in the models themselves may also contribute a large share of the improvement. GOV.UK's public records cannot precisely break down the respective effects of the model, materials processing, interface, and evals, let alone prove that any single role alone created the reliability. But it makes an important process visible: the team used real problems to correct the system, and also used the system's performance to correct its original commitments.

Thus, go-live is no longer a ceremony proving that the engineers have finished. It is an explicit commitment by the organization that, within a clearly stated scope, it is willing to hand part of the work to this system and to keep watching how well it does. The adoption and handover discussed in the next chapter start from this concrete commitment.
