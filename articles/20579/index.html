What is an FDE (Forward Deployed Engineer)? A Clear Explanation of the Job Description and Role

Summary

This Japanese article from BizCodeX-FDE, a school that trains FDEs, explains what a Forward Deployed Engineer is and does. It traces the role's origin to Palantir, whose blog contrasts product-focused "Dev" engineers with customer-side "Delta" engineers. The core definition: an engineer who enters the customer's operations, structures undefined problems, builds small prototypes to validate them, and stays accountable until the solution is used in production and delivers measurable results. The authors walk through a fictional chatbot project stage by stage, list the documents produced at each phase, compare FDE with solutions engineers, IT consultants, and PdM, dispel three common misconceptions, and outline required skills and a learning path. The piece also promotes their six-month, 400-hour school.

Key points

  • FDE originated at Palantir; OpenAI and other AI companies now use the title.
  • The role's essence is accountability for customer outcomes, not just delivery.
  • A case study shows the workflow: reframe requests, observe the field, prototype, deploy, hand off.
  • Misconceptions addressed: FDE is not on-site residency, not inherently AI work, not a solo superhero.
  • The article doubles as marketing for BizCodeX-FDE's six-month training program.

The term FDE (Forward Deployed Engineer) has been appearing more and more in job postings and articles. In Japanese it translates to "engineer deployed to the front lines." From the name alone, you can tell it's an engineer who goes out into the field, but it's hard to see what they actually do every day and what responsibilities they are entrusted with.

In this article, we explain the job content and role of an FDE as concretely as possible. After covering the definition, we follow the flow of a single project to trace "what an FDE actually does" and "what they leave behind in the field," and also touch on how the role differs from similar occupations, common misconceptions, and who is well suited to it.

This is written by us at BizCodeX-FDE (operated by Lime Design Lab, Inc.), a school that trains FDEs. From our position of breaking down the FDE's work daily through creating teaching materials and giving lectures, we have compiled this so as to convey as much of the feel of the field as possible. If you want to first learn about the origins of the FDE role and the background of its spread overseas and in Japan, please also see our page on What is an FDE, which includes sources.

What is an FDE (Forward Deployed Engineer)? In a nutshell

An FDE is an engineer who enters the field of the customer's business operations, decides together with the customer what problems to solve, builds and verifies systems including AI on a small scale, and takes responsibility all the way through to the point where they are used in production and deliver results.

In our teaching materials, we define it a bit more strictly as follows:

An engineer who enters the customer's business operations, structures undefined problems, verifies them technically on a small scale, takes responsibility for customer outcomes and adoption, and feeds the insights gained back into their own company's products, standards, and knowledge.

The most important part of this is "taking responsibility for customer outcomes and adoption." Getting something to work and delivering it are merely intermediate steps. It's only when the work and decisions of people in the field actually change that a milestone is reached.

The origin of the name "Forward Deployed"

The FDE is a role that originated at Palantir, a US company that builds data analytics platforms. On Palantir's official blog, software engineers who build the product itself are called "Dev," while Forward Deployed Software Engineers who deploy the product at customer sites are called "Delta" internally; the former is contrasted as "one capability, many customers" and the latter as "one customer, many capabilities." The same article explains that Delta's mission is to deliver technical outcomes for the customer, and that success is measured by impact on the customer's goals (Palantir Blog "Dev versus Delta," April 2019).

Later, generative AI companies began establishing roles with the same name, and it became widely known. For example, in OpenAI's job posting, the scope an FDE covers is described as spanning problem discovery, technical scoping, system design, building, and deployment to production, with success measured by usage in production and measurable impact on the business (OpenAI Careers "Forward Deployed Engineer (FDE) - SF," accessed November 2026).

Note that the scope of an FDE's responsibilities varies considerably from company to company. Some positions focus on interaction with customers, while others focus on development. Rather than judging by the job title alone, we recommend looking at "what the role is responsible for."

The FDE's role is to "connect" | Standing between the customer, the field, and development

If we were to express the FDE's role in one phrase, we would say it is standing between the stakeholders and connecting them.

Most of the reasons system implementations fail lie not in the technology itself, but between people. Those who know the business don't understand systems, and those who build systems don't go deep into the business. If you try to bridge that gap only through requirements documents and meetings, at some point it becomes a game of telephone, and you end up with something that "was built but never used." The same structure is why generative AI prototypes (PoCs) tend to stall without reaching production.

The FDE stands in this gap. They verify with their own eyes the troubles of the people who will use the system in the field, align success criteria with the decision-makers, coordinate conditions with related departments such as IT and accounting, and work hands-on together with the builders. Being in a position where they can speak the same language as everyone is the source of the FDE's value.

Taking responsibility for outcomes does not mean shouldering everything yourself

Hearing "take responsibility for the outcome" might make you feel like you have to do everything yourself. In reality, it's the opposite. If the problem blocking the outcome lies outside your own scope of responsibility, find the person who can decide on that problem (the problem owner) and create a state where that person can act. This is also an important part of an FDE's job.

For example, suppose an FDE asked to automate sales daily reports learns along the way that "the report format is set company-wide by headquarters' sales planning department and will change next fiscal year" (this is an example for illustration). If they build to the current format, it will have to be rebuilt next fiscal year. The format is decided by the sales planning department, not the FDE. Here, instead of closing with a report that "the requested scope is complete," the FDE asks the requester to arrange an opportunity to confirm the content and timing of the revision with the sales planning department, and redefines the scope of what to build. The accumulation of such actions is what responsibility for outcomes actually means.

What an FDE does | What happens over the course of a single project

From here, we'll look at the FDE's work through the flow of a single project. The subject is an example based on a fictional case used in our teaching materials.

Suppose you are consulted by a manager in the IT department of a certain company: "Could we use a generative AI chatbot to automatically answer internal inquiries?" The department is small, and it has already been decided that one staff member will leave soon. The manager believes "a chatbot should be able to answer most inquiries."

1. Receiving the request and reframing it before responding

Requests that reach an FDE usually take the form of a means, such as "we want to build a chatbot." What makes an FDE distinctive is not jumping straight into product selection or screen design here.

First, they ask about the background and deadline, and formulate a one-sentence hypothesis of "whose what needs to change for success." In this example, the starting point would be something like "create a state where internal inquiries don't pile up even with fewer staff." The chatbot is positioned as one promising means to that end.

After the initial consultation, we summarize what we know, what we still don't know, what we will decide going forward, and how to proceed into a few pages of documents, and send them back saying, "This is our understanding—is it correct?" We call this a discussion paper.

2. Go into the field and gather facts

Next, we verify what is actually happening in the field. We read the actual inquiries, observe how the people handling them work, and actually count the volume and effort involved. We also map out who is involved and who decides what.

At this point, it is important to record reported claims and observations separately. A statement like "this takes dozens of hours a month" and the time actually measured are treated as different facts. Since workarounds in the field usually have a reason, when we find a method that seems odd, we don't immediately reject it—we first ask why it's done that way.

3. Agree on the problem definition and how to measure success, compare options, and build small

Based on the gathered facts, we agree with the customer on the problem to solve and how success will be measured. This is the first major fork in the road.

Then we lay out two or three options and compare them. The chatbot the manager has in mind is included as an option, alongside non-AI options that simply organize the inquiry channels and procedures. For the chosen option, instead of building everything at once, we create a thin prototype that works through only a small part of the workflow, and validate it with an evaluation set (a combination of expected questions and expected answers). We also check at this stage how it behaves with malicious inputs and questions outside its permitted scope.

4. Prepare for production deployment

Even if the prototype works well, that alone isn't enough to go to production. Who can see which data, whether personal information can be sent to external services, and how to stop it when it returns a wrong answer. If internal reviews and procedures take time, we work backward from that timeline and include it in the plan.

We deploy to production in stages, keeping the ability to roll back if problems arise. We also write down known constraints and remaining risks without hiding them, and prepare the materials and approval paths so the customer can decide "whether to go live" at the decision meeting.

5. Make it actually used, hand it over, and bring the learnings back

Going to production is not the end. We measure whether it is actually being used and whether inquiry backlogs have decreased. If it isn't being used, we verify the reasons in the field and return to the next decision point.

Then, so the customer can keep running it even after the FDE leaves, we leave behind operational procedure documents (Runbooks) and handover documents. Finally, we feed the learnings from this project that could be useful for future customers back into our own products, standards, and implementation templates. This connects to Palantir's philosophy of "incorporating what is common across many customers into the product."

Deliverables the FDE leaves behind in the field

An FDE's work remains not only as the systems they build, but also as many small documents. In the early stages before building begins, it can look from the outside like nothing is being done. That is precisely why we document what we verified and what we decided, align understanding with the customer, create a basis for decisions, and hand things over to the next person in charge and to our own company. They don't need to be large documents—many fit on a single page.

The deliverables covered in our training materials, organized by phase, are as follows. The names and numbers are just examples and change depending on the project.

PhaseMain deliverablesWhat they are used for
Consultation and issue framingDiscussion paper, success criteria confirmation sheetCommunicate how we understood the request and align with the customer on what change would count as success
Understanding the fieldCurrent business flow, stakeholder map, pain points written in the field's own wordsServe as material for agreeing with the customer on the problem definition and how to measure success
Designing outcomesKPI tree, baseline and measurement design, decision criteriaLet the customer and us look at the same numbers to decide whether to continue or stop
Prototyping and evaluationAI usage specification, evaluation set and evaluation results, permission and approval designDecide the scope delegated to AI and the conditions for stopping it, and provide the rationale

Handover and feedback loopObservability items, Runbook, handover documents, learnings memo and feedback proposalEnsure things keep running without the FDE, and feed learnings back into products and standards

Looking at this table, you can see that "writing code" is only a part of the FDE's work. That said, being able to build things yourself is a prerequisite for an FDE. Precisely because they can build, FDEs can quickly produce a small prototype in the field that says "this will let us verify it," and they can take responsibility for the production design as well.

Even for the same request, the FDE's first move is different

The FDE way of working becomes clearer when compared alongside traditional contracted development or vendor approaches. Here is an example we use in our study sessions (a fictional example for illustration).

The request: "The inventory search screen is slow—please make it faster."

The typical approach is to measure the search response time, review indexes and architecture to speed it up, and report back, "We've made the improvements." This does properly fulfill the request in its own way.

The FDE, however, first asks, "Could you show me where that screen is actually being used?" Observing next to the person managing inventory, we discovered that the inventory numbers shown on the search screen sometimes didn't match reality, and each time that happened, they had to walk to the warehouse shelves to check. What was really consuming their time was not the screen's speed, but the back-and-forth verification caused by data that couldn't be trusted.

Once you see this, what should be built changes completely. Making the search faster doesn't reduce the walking to the shelves. Instead of building exactly what the request says, you verify the facts in the field first, then decide what to change. This difference in the first move is the clearest point that distinguishes the FDE from other ways of working.

Similarly, using a request to automate daily sales reports as an example, a comparison of the traditional approach and the FDE approach is summarized in a diagram on the What is an FDE page.

There are many roles similar to FDE. All of these are general tendencies, and the scope can differ by company even with the same job title.

When you want to tell from a job posting or role definition whether "this is a job close to FDE," ask yourself the following three questions.

FDE is not in opposition to these roles. You work together with them as the counterpart in handoffs: receiving from the solution engineer the customer circumstances seen in sales talks, and handing off post-adoption operations to the TAM or customer success.

Common misconceptions about FDE

As the term FDE has spread, slightly off-target images have spread along with it. Here are three that often come up in study sessions.

Misconception 1: FDE means being stationed at the client's site

Because of the name "Forward Deployed," it is sometimes taken as a premium version of on-site staffing. It is true that FDEs often go into the field. But the essence is not whether you are stationed there. As long as you fulfill the role of understanding the customer's business and taking responsibility through to results, you can do FDE work even if it is mostly online. Conversely, even if you are at the client's site every day, if you are just completing assigned tasks, that cannot be called the FDE way of working.

Misconception 2: FDE means someone who develops using AI

Today's FDEs use AI in many situations, and the power of AI-driven development is indispensable. Even so, AI is only a means. As in the inventory search example earlier, if the real problem lies in data reliability, there are things to fix before AI. Being able to compare alternatives that do not use AI and decide "this time we won't use AI" is also an important part of FDE work.

Misconception 3: FDEs are supermen who do everything alone

Hearing "from upstream to production operations," you may feel it is impossible for one person. But what is required of an FDE is not doing every step alone; it is connecting the people involved and delivering through to results.

With the evolution of AI, the burden of writing code, researching, and creating materials has dropped considerably. Just as each time programming languages moved to a higher layer, development became possible without understanding all of the lower layers, combining people and AI has expanded the scope one person can cover. An FDE does not need to be a superhero. What is needed, we believe, is not special talent but the accumulation of systematic practice.

Skills required of an FDE, and who is a good fit

The four foundational competencies required of an FDE

In our teaching materials, we organize the competencies required of an FDE into the following four. Rather than one of them standing out, it is important that all four are in place.

CompetencyContent
Domain knowledgeUnderstanding the industry's terminology and business flows, and who makes which decisions, to get closer to the real reason behind the customer's pain
Technical skillCombining data, APIs, and generative AI to quickly build, with your own hands, prototypes that can verify what needs verifying
Communication skillHearing the circumstances behind the words, explaining technical constraints in the other person's language, and presenting what needs deciding in a form that allows a decision
Problem-solving skillStepping away from the requested means to redefine the problem, forming hypotheses, and testing them on a small scale

In addition, today's FDEs are also expected to have "the ability to make things you can be trusted with in production," such as designing the scope delegated to AI and the conditions for stopping, evaluation (Evals), designing permissions and security, and designing production operations.

Who is a good fit for FDE

  • You want to see through to "how what you built is actually being used"
  • You want to be involved not just in receiving specs, but from "what should we build in the first place"
  • You do not mind listening to people in the field, and you can honestly ask about what you do not understand
  • Even when circumstances change, you can enjoy forming hypotheses, testing them on a small scale, and iterating
  • You try to explain technical matters in language that non-engineers can understand

Conversely, for those who want to focus on deeply building out a fixed spec with a fixed procedure, a path as a specialist engineer may suit better than FDE. This is not a matter of which is superior; the goals of the work are simply different.

How to become an FDE | A path for engineers and DX professionals to build experience

There is no fixed qualification for becoming an FDE. In most cases, you build on top of experience creating software as an engineer, or experience working on business improvement in an IT or DX department, adding what is missing.

We organize the capabilities an FDE needs into the following three pillars.

  • Upstream processes and consulting: Enter the field, turn requests into problems that can be verified, and agree with the customer on how success will be measured and how to proceed
  • AI-driven development: Design the scope delegated to AI and the conditions for stopping, and build small prototypes while verifying through evaluation
  • Production operations design and security: Design permissions, data, and integrations, roll out to production in stages, and hand off to operations

The hard part is accumulating these three through actual work alone. We often hear from people who say they spend long stretches assigned to implementation and testing, and rarely get a turn at bat in upstream or production design. We feel this is often not a matter of personal effort, but a problem in how the work is divided up.

If you are self-studying, you can start in your own job by writing, in one sentence, "whose what needs to change for this to be a success" whenever you receive a request. If you have a chance to go into the field, before building anything, once observe the work beside the person who will use it. Even that alone will considerably change how you see the work.

If you want to learn systematically, a school is also an option. For how to choose, we have summarized the key points in How to Choose an FDE School | 7 Checkpoints to Avoid Failure.

Frequently Asked Questions about FDE Job Duties and Roles

QWhat is an FDE (Forward Deployed Engineer) in a nutshell?

AAn FDE is an engineer who works on-site at the client, decides together with the client what problems to solve, builds and verifies small systems including AI, and takes responsibility until the solution is used in production and delivers results. "Forward Deployed" means being positioned at the front line. The role originated at Palantir in the US, and recently generative AI companies have begun adopting it as well.

QPlease tell me specifically about an FDE's job duties.

AThe basic flow is: receiving consultations and rephrasing requests, going on-site to gather facts, agreeing with the client on problem definition and success metrics, comparing options and building small prototypes for evaluation, preparing for production release, handing over so the solution is actually used, and bringing learnings back to your own company. There is time for writing code, as well as time for meetings with clients and aligning conditions with related departments.

QIs FDE an on-site (resident) job at client locations?

AWhether you are stationed on-site is not the essence of being an FDE. While visiting the field is common, the role can also be fulfilled online. What matters is understanding the client's business and taking responsibility until results are achieved.

QWhat is the difference between an FDE and a Solutions Engineer?

AAs a general tendency, a Solutions Engineer demonstrates from a technical standpoint that a product fits the client's challenges during sales meetings, supporting the decision to adopt it. An FDE goes further, taking responsibility until the client's business actually changes after implementation and results can be measured. However, the scope of job titles differs by company, so when reading job postings, we recommend looking at the goal of the work and whether you build things yourself.

QIs an FDE the same as an AI engineer?

ANo, they are not the same. While FDEs often use AI, AI is just one of the tools. Depending on the problem, an approach without AI may be better. The core of an FDE's work is deciding with the client what to build, delivering it to production, and taking responsibility for the results.

QWhat experience is needed to become an FDE?

AThere are no specific qualifications required. In addition to experience building software, experience with upstream processes (organizing issues and defining requirements), AI-driven development, and production operations and security design makes it easier to approach the role. BizCodeX-FDE is a school for engineers or professionals with at least one year of practical experience in IT, DX, or AIX departments, where you learn these three areas along the flow of client projects.

QDo you have to be a superman who does everything alone to work as an FDE?

ANot necessarily. What is required of an FDE is not doing every process alone, but connecting stakeholders and delivering results. Now that AI is reducing the burden of work, with systematic practice, the range that can be covered by people and AI together, from upstream to production operations, is expanding.

Summary | An FDE is an engineer who connects work through to results

Let us once again summarize the job duties and role of an FDE (Forward Deployed Engineer).

  • An FDE is an engineer who works on-site at the client, decides with the client what problems to solve, builds and verifies small solutions, and takes responsibility until the solution is used in production and delivers results
  • The core of the role is connecting the people who use the solution in the field, the decision-makers, related departments, and the builders
  • The work progresses through a flow of rephrasing requests, gathering facts on-site, agreeing on the problem and how to measure success, prototyping and evaluating, preparing for production, handing over, and feeding learnings back
  • Not only the systems you build, but also small documents that serve as the basis for decisions are left behind at each stage
  • It is not about being stationed at client sites, nor about being someone who uses AI, nor a superman who does everything alone

FDE is still a new profession in Japan. That is precisely why we believe that being able to explain in your own words "what this work takes responsibility for" will be a strength going forward, more than the job title itself.
