What do FDE interviews ask? Breaking down the five-round process and how to prepare for each round

Original title: FDE 面试会问什么？五轮流程拆开，每轮怎么准备

FDE 指南 · 2026-11-21 · 6 min read

Summary

Drawing on publicly shared interview experiences from Palantir and AI startups, this article breaks down the typical five-round FDE interview: technical screen, take-home project, system design, customer simulation, and hiring manager. It stresses the process is not a domestic standard and should be used as a capability map. For each round it contrasts strong answers with common failure patterns, noting that later rounds test communication, expectation management, and post-launch thinking—skills that cannot be built by cramming.

Key points

  • The five-round process comes from overseas companies like Palantir, not a universal standard.
  • Only the first two rounds are purely technical; the rest test non-technical skills.
  • Technical answers should start from customer constraints, not vendor APIs.
  • Customer simulation is the key differentiator, testing how you translate vague feedback.
  • Common rejection reasons: unclear code ownership, no end-to-end delivery, poor debugging.

First, let's clarify where this process comes from

The "five-round FDE interview process" circulating online mainly comes from publicly shared interview experiences at overseas companies (such as Palantir and a number of AI startups), and has also been compiled and reposted in Chinese-language communities. It is not a universal standard for positions in China—only a handful of companies in China hire FDEs, and their processes vary widely: some merge everything into one or two technical rounds, and some have no customer simulation stage at all.

So the right way to use this process is to treat it as a capability map: look at what ability each round is testing, then check your own gaps against it. Don't treat it as a standard answer for some specific company to memorize. To see what the role itself is hiring for, first read What is an FDE engineer position?.

The five rounds: what each one actually tests

The most complete process in public accounts is 4–5 rounds, with each round focusing on something entirely different:

RoundTypical durationMain focus
Technical screen1–2 hoursCoding + system design fundamentals
Take-home project3–5 hoursDelivering a small application end to end
System design1–2 hoursHow to architect a solution into a customer's environment
Customer simulation1–2 hoursRole-play, testing on-the-spot communication and judgment
Hiring managerAbout 1 hourMotivation, career goals, fit

Key observation: only the first two rounds are purely technical; the last three all test "things beyond technology". This matches the FDE role's positioning of "being accountable for production outcomes and facing customers"—the interview structure itself is designed to filter out "people who can only write code".

Technical rounds: the questions aren't hard—the answers are

Several types of technical questions appear repeatedly in public accounts, and the way you answer matters more than the answer itself.

Design question: "How would you design an integration of an LLM into a customer's existing data pipeline?"

A good answer starts by asking about the customer's constraints—data volume, latency requirements, compliance restrictions—and only then discusses RAG architecture (embeddings, vector stores, retrieval), data isolation and encryption, batch vs. real-time processing, and monitoring. The pitfall answer is skipping the context and jumping straight to "use some vendor's API"—the interviewer wants to hear whether you think in terms of the customer, not which API you know how to call. For the specifics of this capability, see the capability layers broken down in the engineer competency model.

Troubleshooting question: "A customer's system is returning empty results—what do you check first?"

This tests methodology, not memorization. A systematic answer: check logs and errors first → verify along the data flow (input → processing → output) → check recent changes (deployments, configuration) → try to reproduce. Pitfalls are "I'd restart the service" or "I'd look at the code"—too vague, showing no diagnostic path.

Optimization question: "This LLM call has high latency—how do you optimize it?"

A good answer offers multiple levers and explains the trade-offs: caching frequent queries, using a smaller model for simple requests, streaming responses, batching, shortening context, edge deployment. The pitfall is saying only "switch to a faster model"—ignoring the impact on cost and accuracy.

The customer simulation round: this is the real watershed

There is no standard technical answer in this round, but the failure patterns are highly consistent.

"The customer says your solution is too slow. What do you do?"

A good answer first clarifies what "slow" means—latency or throughput? Then reproduces and confirms it, then presents options and sets expectations ("this change would take about two days to implement and test"). Two typical pitfalls: defensively rebutting "our solution isn't slow", or shifting blame with "that's a problem with your infrastructure".

"The customer's CEO asks why implementation is taking so long. How do you respond?"

This tests transparent communication. A good answer gives concrete progress ("70% done, blocked on a data format issue"), a revised timeline, and options ("we could launch the core features first"). Pitfalls are vagueness ("we're making progress") or over-promising ("it will definitely be ready next week" when you know it won't be).

In one sentence: the customer simulation round doesn't test whether you can "placate customers"—it tests whether you can translate vague emotional feedback into concrete, actionable problems, and then manage expectations honestly. This is precisely the core difference between an FDE and a regular engineering role.

The behavioral round: telling one good STAR story

"Tell me about a time you owned something end to end" is almost guaranteed to come up. The STAR structure (Situation–Task–Action–Result) helps you tell the story clearly, but two details make or break it:

  • The subject must be "I", not "we". The interviewer wants to hear what you specifically did; crediting everything to the team is the same as not answering.
  • The result must be measurable. For example, "recovered within 4 hours, customer renewed"—not "it got resolved eventually".

Similar questions include "how do you quickly learn a new technology" and "what do you do when you're stuck". For the former, give a concrete method plus a recent example—don't just say "I'd Google it"; for the latter, show the right balance of "investigating independently for a while first, exhausting available resources, then asking for help with what you've already tried"—neither running to your manager immediately nor grinding away forever.

The architecture round: making "what happens after launch" clear

"You need to deploy for 50 customers. How do you scale? What breaks first?"

A good answer proactively discusses multi-tenant vs. single-tenant, where the bottlenecks are (database connections, API rate limits, memory), how to front-load monitoring and alerting, and cost—50 customers means 50 times the call volume. The pitfall is discussing only feature implementation while completely ignoring operational reality.

"What if the database goes down?"

Answer from several angles: redundancy (read replicas), failover, graceful degradation (serving cached data), monitoring and alerting, and backup and recovery. Two pitfalls: "the database won't go down" (too naive) and having no disaster recovery plan at all.

The end of the interview: your questions are being scored too

When asked "do you have any questions for us", good questions earn points in reverse—for example, "what do the top performers on this team have in common?", "how do you balance customer customization with product standardization?", and "what does the feedback loop between FDEs and the product team look like?"

Questions that clearly cost you points: "what does this role actually do?" (shows you didn't do your homework), asking about salary right away (save it for later), or "how quickly can I transfer to a product role?" (implies you don't actually want to be an FDE).

Universal elimination lines: unrelated to the questions, but the most fatal

Compiling the elimination reasons repeatedly mentioned in public accounts, the commonalities are strong: being unable to explain code you wrote, blaming the customer or saying "that's not my job", having no end-to-end delivery experience, panicking when you don't know something, never having used APIs or integrations, and being unable to debug systematically.

By contrast, those who get offers also share common traits: they have delivered end-to-end projects, can explain technical concepts clearly, stay calm under pressure, ask questions before making assumptions, and have real customer-facing experience.

None of these can be patched quickly by grinding problems—they correspond to the second and third layers of the FDE three-layer competency model, namely "AI implementation" and "field and organization". Technical questions can show results in a week or two of prep, but these two layers can only grow out of real projects.

Frequently asked questions (FAQ)

What is the biggest difference between an FDE interview and a regular software engineer interview?

Regular engineering interviews focus mainly on coding and system design; FDE interviews add two rounds on top of that—customer simulation and architecture implementation—specifically to verify on-the-spot communication, expectation management, and awareness of "what happens after launch". The technical questions themselves are usually no harder than a big-tech backend interview, but the answers are expected to carry a customer perspective.

For career changers without a CS background, where is an FDE interview most likely to trip them up?

The production engineering layer. The elimination reasons in public accounts—being unable to explain code, no end-to-end delivery, inability to debug systematically—almost all fall in this layer. The way to fill the gap is to first build a real project that is deployed, has evals, and includes a retrospective, and use it to substantiate "I can own the outcome"—rather than memorizing answers.

Do FDE interviews in China also follow these five rounds?

Not necessarily. This process mainly comes from experience compiled from overseas companies and Chinese-language communities. Domestic positions vary widely—many companies consolidate into two or three rounds of technical and business interviews, and client simulation sessions are not common. Just use it as a capability map to identify gaps; the actual process depends on the recruiter's description.

What's most worth preparing before the interview?

Ranked by cost-effectiveness: First, be able to present your real projects in a "problem—action—result—retrospective" structure that can withstand follow-up questions all the way down. Second, practice client simulations, especially expressing how to break emotional feedback down into specific problems. Third, go over the approaches for high-frequency technical scenarios like RAG, evals, and troubleshooting. Salary figures are not the focus of interview prep; handle compensation negotiation separately.
