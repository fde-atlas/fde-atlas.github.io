What Is the US Department of Defense Impact Level IL5 — FDE Deployment Guide

Original title: 美国国防部影响等级 IL5 是什么 FDE 部署指南

FDE Times · 2026-11-04 · 5 min read

Summary

A practical guide for FDEs working with US Department of Defense customers, explaining how impact levels (IL) shape deployment decisions. FedRAMP is only the floor: IL4/IL5 add Cloud Computing SRG requirements, and IL5 further requires FedRAMP+ controls and physical isolation from non-government tenants. Data classification is decided in writing by an authorizing official, not the engineering team. Only US persons may access IL4/IL5 data, a rule based on legal status rather than location, affecting staffing and log access. The article closes with five questions to answer before writing code and notes Palantir's IL5, IL6, and FedRAMP High authorizations.

Key points

  • FedRAMP is the baseline; impact levels add controls on top.
  • IL5 requires FedRAMP High plus FedRAMP+ and physical isolation.
  • Authorizing officials classify data; FDEs must get written decisions.
  • Only US persons can access IL4/IL5 data, regardless of location.
  • Palantir holds IL5, IL6, and FedRAMP High authorizations.

Key Takeaways

  • FedRAMP is the baseline for all DoD cloud services; impact levels describe what must be added on top of it
  • IL5 requires FedRAMP High, plus the DoD's FedRAMP+ controls and SRG requirements, and physical isolation from non-government tenants
  • Only U.S. citizens, U.S. nationals, or U.S. persons may access IL4/IL5 data, and this restriction applies to the FDE team as well

In your first week with a U.S. Department of Defense (DoD) customer, the most important questions often have nothing to do with APIs or data schemas. They are much shorter: which impact level does this data belong to? The answer determines where you can deploy, how isolated the environment needs to be, and who on the team is authorized to open a log file.

Engineers used to commercial customers often stumble here. On a project for a retailer, you pick a region, turn on encryption, and start writing code. With a U.S. government customer, many decisions have already been made before you arrive, sometimes by people you have never met, and your job is to understand those decisions precisely.

If you want to work as a Forward Deployed Engineer (FDE) at a company selling software to the U.S. government, it is worth mastering these fundamentals before you send in your resume. That way, from the very first meeting with the customer, you can ask the right questions.

FedRAMP is the foundation; impact levels tell you how many floors to build on top

A memo issued by the DoD Chief Information Officer in December 2014 established FedRAMP as the minimum security baseline for all DoD cloud services. On top of that, the Cloud Computing Security Requirements Guide (Cloud Computing SRG) adopts the FedRAMP Moderate baseline at every impact level, and considers the High baseline at some levels.

Think of it this way: FedRAMP is the foundation, and impact levels tell you how many more floors you need to build on top. The table below summarizes the most commonly heard levels and how each one changes day-to-day work.

LevelDataControl baselineWhat it means for FDEs
IL2Lowest level in the tableFedRAMP ModerateClosest to a commercial project
IL4CUI and non-mission-critical dataAccepts FedRAMP High, but non-control requirements in the SRG still applyOnly U.S. persons may access the data
IL5CUI requiring more protection than IL4, and National Security Systems (NSS)FedRAMP High + FedRAMP+ + SRG requirementsAdds physical isolation from non-government tenants
IL6SECRET-level classified data and NSSAdditional controls for classified dataOutside the scope of this article

Controlled Unclassified Information (CUI) refers to data that is not classified but still subject to controls. This article focuses on IL4 and IL5: these two levels involve CUI and impose personnel restrictions that directly affect FDE teams.

Why "we have FedRAMP High" doesn't answer the question

At IL4, the SRG accepts a FedRAMP High Provisional Authorization (PA) and no longer requires further assessment of controls or control enhancements. Even so, requirements in the SRG that are not controls must still be assessed. So "having High" is only a starting point, not a pass.

At IL5, the gap is even bigger. To obtain an IL5 PA, a service must have FedRAMP High, plus the DoD's FedRAMP+ controls and the specific requirements of the Cloud Computing SRG. If a vendor in your supply chain only shows a High authorization, the next question should be: where is the IL5 PA?

One more thing that is easy to overlook: your team does not decide which level the data belongs to. Designating CUI or mission data as IL5 is the authority of the Authorizing Official, the person responsible for classifying that information. The FDE's job is to find that person and get the decision in writing.

Example: retrieving maintenance documents in an IL5 environment

Suppose you are deploying a retrieval-augmented generation (RAG)-style search tool for a logistics unit's maintenance documents. The documents carry CUI markings, and some of them contain export-controlled technical specifications. You have already built a working demo on your company's commercial cloud account.

The first step is to meet with the Authorizing Official. Suppose they determine the data is IL5; then the demo can only serve as a rough sketch of the approach, and the real deployment architecture must be rebuilt in a different environment.

The second step is the environment. IL5 requires physical isolation from tenants outside the DoD or federal government, such as public tenants or state and local government tenants. Logical isolation is permitted between DoD and federal tenants.

The result is that every component that touches the data, from the model and vector store to the logging system, must reside in an environment that meets IL5 standards, including the model endpoints you are used to calling on shared infrastructure.

The third step is personnel. Service provider personnel who can access IL4 and IL5 data must be U.S. citizens, U.S. nationals, or U.S. persons; foreign persons may not hold such access. If your team includes a highly capable colleague who is a foreign person, they cannot view logs containing customer data, even while on call.

Note that this restriction is based on each person's legal status, not their work location. When drafting the team roster, verify each person's status individually rather than just looking at office locations.

The fourth step is export controls. Data subject to rules such as EAR falls into the CUI category, adding another layer of restrictions on who can access the data.

By design, filter data out of logs from the very beginning, and separate permissions on operational infrastructure from permissions to view content, rather than waiting for an audit to fix things.

Five questions to answer before writing code

First, who is the Authorizing Official? What level have they assigned to the data, and is there written documentation? Second, does every service in the architecture have a PA at that level? For IL5, does it have full FedRAMP+, or does it stop at High?

Third, list every component that touches the data, including caches, logs, and debugging tools, and document where each one runs. Fourth, who will see the data, including support and on-call staff, and are they U.S. persons? Finally, is the data subject to export controls?

Put these five answers into the scoping document, alongside the timeline. If the fourth question forces a team change, the customer needs to know in the first week, not the week of launch.

Mistakes to avoid from day one

Break one habit: pulling data samples onto your laptop or into a commercial environment "just for debugging." For IL5 data, a single copy like that can violate both the isolation requirements and the personnel restrictions at the same time.

Also, don't equate FedRAMP High with IL5, or assume that having High settles IL4 while forgetting the non-control requirements in the SRG. When building the team, vet access eligibility alongside skills to avoid reorganizing midway.

What doors can this skill open?

Palantir, the company most closely associated with the FDE model, obtained FedRAMP High in December 2024, having previously received FedRAMP Moderate and DoD IL5 and IL6 authorizations. Its job postings for Forward Deployed Software Engineers on U.S. government projects require holding a Top Secret security clearance or being willing to obtain one, and working on-site with customers.

If you are not a US person, let's be blunt: roles involving IL4/IL5 data will not be open to you. Even so, the habit of doing deployments under data classification constraints applies equally to banking, healthcare, or government clients in any country.

When describing projects on your resume, clearly state the constraints you have handled, such as environment isolation, controlling who can see data, and log filtering, rather than just listing your tech stack.

Exercise for this week: take one of your recent projects, assume its data is classified as IL5, and write down everything that would have to change. If the list runs longer than a page, you'll understand why government clients ask about the impact level first.
