FDE Handoff Checklist: How to Hand Systems Over to the Support Team

Original title: FDE 交接清单：如何把系统移交给支持团队

FDE Times · 2026-11-15 · 5 min read

Summary

This article argues that a green-light go-live means little if the customer's behavior hasn't changed—Rocketlane's test for failed implementations. It frames handoff to support teams as an acceptance gate, not a farewell demo, drawing on PostHog's FDE handbook, Educative's course, and Google's Production Readiness Review. Key practices: document everything the customer must do without the FDE, agree readiness criteria (including cost) at kickoff, assign a drift owner with weekly sampling checks, run a support-led review with pass criteria, and set explicit hypercare end dates and sign-off milestones.

Key points

  • Handoff is an acceptance gate requiring support sign-off, not a final demo.
  • Anything customers must do without the FDE belongs in durable docs, not Slack.
  • Borrow Google SRE's PRR: support teams accept systems only after review.
  • Drift monitoring needs an owner—e.g., weekly sampling with a 6% error threshold.
  • Common failures: open-ended hypercare and tracking uptime while output quality degrades.

Handing the system to the support team should be an acceptance gate, with standards agreed in advance and a named recipient, not a farewell meeting.

Key Takeaways

  • A green dashboard at launch proves nothing. If the customer's way of working hasn't changed, the project has failed.
  • Everything the customer must do when the FDE is not present should be written into long-lived documentation, not left in Slack.
  • The support team should take over only after a readiness review, with a named drift owner, a hypercare period, and a sign-off date.

Picture this scenario. On a Friday afternoon, you're wrapping up a project for a logistics company: an agent that reads order emails. The demo went smoothly, and the dashboard is all green. Three weeks later, the customer success lead messages you: the customer says the agent "keeps making errors lately," but nobody on the team knows when the errors started, where they're coming from, or who should fix them.

Rocketlane, a company that makes customer implementation software, describes exactly this pattern: successfully launched projects quietly turning into failed deployments. Its test is uncomfortable: if user behavior hasn't changed, the implementation has failed, even if the project closed out with a green light.

For Forward Deployed Engineers (FDEs), the internal handoff to the support and customer success teams is the moment outcomes are most easily abandoned, because that's when the person who understands the system best walks away.

The handoff is an acceptance gate, not a farewell

PostHog's FDE handbook draws the lines clearly: once the technical work is done, the FDE hands the customer relationship back to the account owner, along with a record of what was built. From then on, CS and the Technical Account Manager (TAM) maintain the long-term relationship, tracking whether the customer is getting value from the product.

This division of labor only works if the receiving side is capable of taking over. Educative's FDE course calls the handoff the FDE's last formal act, and warns: handing over a system with no documentation, no monitoring, and no trained owner creates dependency, not a deliverable.

The course puts it bluntly: a receiving team cannot operate what it doesn't understand.

Google solved a similar problem for its SRE teams early on. In the book *SRE*, the Production Readiness Review (PRR) is a prerequisite for an SRE team to take on operational responsibility for a service.

The same logic applies to handing off to a support team: the system isn't "pushed onto" the support team; it is "accepted" by them after inspection.

The handoff documentation must answer the 2 a.m. questions

PostHog has an easily copied rule: anything the customer team must do when the FDE is not present goes into long-lived documentation. Slack threads, call recordings, or your memory don't count.

Back to the order-email agent. Here is a minimal handoff package, where every item must answer a question the support team will actually face:

ItemQuestion it answersOrder-email agent example
What the system does and doesn't doCustomer asks, "Why wasn't this email processed?"Only reads English emails from three mailboxes; ignores image attachments
Data flow and dependenciesWhere do I look when something breaks?Mailbox → queue → LLM → customer's ticket-creation API
Signs of degradationHow do I know things are getting worse?Share of emails falling into "unclassifiable," number of manually corrected tickets
RunbookHow do I handle common failures?Ticket API returns 401: token expired, who reissues it and where
Running costWill peak season blow the budget?Cost per email, cap agreed with the customer
OwnersWho makes the call when something goes wrong?Names of the customer-side owner, CSM, and on-call support engineer

Cost is often treated as a problem for later. Educative includes it among the readiness criteria to be settled before handoff, on the grounds that cost sustainability is itself a readiness condition, not a post-launch headache. If the support team doesn't know the cap, the first unusually high bill the customer receives becomes a customer-relations incident.

Who can spot drift before the customer does?

Listing signs of degradation in the documentation isn't enough; the next question is: who actually looks? An article on the Master of Project blog, "The FDE Leaves: Who Owns Your AI After Go-Live?", has a table on drift monitoring. In the "who detects it by default" column, the common answer is: nobody, until a user complains.

The article also argues that AI systems need an owner covering the entire lifecycle, not just the build phase. For the order-email agent, this advice can become a weekly routine.

Suppose every Monday, a designated support engineer randomly samples 50 tickets created by the agent and compares each one against the original email. The alert threshold is 3 bad tickets, i.e., 6%.

With that number, "keeps making errors lately" becomes verifiable data. Week one: 1 bad out of 50, no worries. Week four: 4 bad out of 50, and the support team immediately opens an internal ticket and fixes it before the customer notices.

How to run the acceptance review

Let the support team lead the review, with you in the seat answering questions. Here's a sample script, with a pass criterion for each question:

Support lead: "If the unclassifiable rate doubles, where do I look?" Pointing to a dashboard and a line in the runbook is a pass; "just come find me" is a fail.

CSM: "If the customer asks why an image invoice wasn't processed, what do I say?" A pass requires the "doesn't do" scope to be documented and signed off by the customer.

Support engineer: "What if the API token expires in the middle of the night?" A pass requires naming the customer-side person authorized to reissue the token and their contact info.

Every failed question goes onto a to-do list with a deadline. Only when the list is cleared, or both sides agree the remaining items are acceptable, does the support team sign off.

Start on day one, not the final week

For this process to work, the first two steps must start early. Readiness criteria, including cost, should be agreed with the support team and CS at project kickoff. The handoff documentation should be written alongside the build, while you still remember the reasoning behind every design decision.

Closing out requires concrete milestones. Following Master of Project's advice, set a hypercare period (the time you keep supporting the project after you leave) and a date for formal sign-off by the customer-side owner. Attach a record of what was built, just as PostHog does when handing the customer relationship back to the account owner.

Common mistakes that doom a handoff

The most common mistake is treating the final demo as the handoff. A demo proves the system runs; a handoff proves someone else can run it. The second mistake is a hypercare period with no end date. You'll keep getting pulled back in, and the support team will never truly take over the system.

The third mistake is subtler: measuring only uptime. An agent can be online 100% of the time while getting more wrong by the day. So the signals you track must measure output quality, and whether the customer has actually changed how they work—exactly Rocketlane's test.

This skill belongs on your resume

When reading FDE job postings, watch for phrases like "handoff," "enablement," and "transition to support." If they appear, ask in the interview: how long after go-live does the FDE stay tied to the customer, and who takes over the system afterward. When it's your turn to talk about your experience, one concrete handoff carries as much weight as one concrete build.

Don't just write "deployed an agent" on your resume. Spell out that you handed the system over to the support team with an operations manual, readiness criteria, and a weekly drift-check process, and then explain what happened after you left.

The measure of a good handoff is: three months later, the customer doesn't need to remember your name, and the system still runs correctly.
