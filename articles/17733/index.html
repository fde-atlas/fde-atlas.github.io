What is the travel ratio for FDEs? On-site time for Forward Deployed Engineers

Original title: FDE 出差比例多少？前线部署工程师驻场时间

FDE Times · 2026-10-08 · 5 min read

Summary

This article examines how much time forward deployed engineers (FDEs) actually spend at customer sites. It cites a typical range of 25%–50%: Palantir around 25%, OpenAI and Istari Digital up to 50%, and India-based FDE roles at 4–10 days per month. On-site work is hard to replace remotely because of how companies define the role and because of security constraints like air-gapped systems. The article warns that intense travel, especially during early deployments, can contribute to burnout, per Paraform. For Vietnamese engineers used to account-manager intermediaries, the main gap is lack of direct customer-facing experience; the author advises reading travel terms carefully, seeking direct client exposure now, and choosing complex environments like banks or hospitals for practice.

Key points

  • Most FDE roles require 25%–50% of time on-site with customers.
  • Palantir is about 25%; OpenAI and Istari Digital up to 50%.
  • Air-gapped systems and security rules make remote work impractical.
  • Paraform flags intense travel, especially early deployments, as a burnout risk.
  • Vietnamese engineers often lack direct client experience due to account-manager buffers.

Key takeaways

  • The common range is 25%–50% of time on-site at the customer: Palantir is about 25%, OpenAI and Istari Digital up to 50%.
  • The role itself is designed around working side by side with customers, and some physically isolated systems simply cannot be accessed remotely. Remote positions exist, but they are the minority.
  • Vietnamese engineers who are used to dealing with customers through an account manager should build up direct customer-facing experience before applying.

Istari Digital's job posting for a Forward Deployed Engineer (FDE) is blunt: candidates may need to travel, up to 50% of the time, including to overseas customers in Australia. The next sentence is even more notable: the FDE will work on-site, embedded in the customer's team, to understand the problems the customer faces.

Many Vietnamese engineers reading an FDE job description focus only on the technical requirements and skim past the "travel" line at the end. But for most of these roles, being on-site at the customer is part of the job itself. So if you want to enter this field, the first question to ask yourself is: have you actually worked sitting next to a customer? Whether you are willing to travel is secondary.

Travel amounts vary, but almost everyone travels

According to The Pragmatic Engineer, Palantir requires FDEs to spend about 25% of their time on-site at customers. Commure's estimate is higher, up to half of the time. The Pragmatic Engineer also notes that visiting customers repeatedly and working side by side with them is quite common in this profession.

FDE Academy, a website dedicated to this role, gives an overall range of 25% to 50%, with OpenAI reaching up to 50%. For India-based FDE roles, it estimates 4 to 10 days per month on-site at customers, with occasional travel in the Asia-Pacific region.

For Vietnamese engineers, this is the closest reference available.

Travel amounts differ from company to company, but the working style is the same: you must be inside the customer's environment, not handling tickets from afar.

Why can't it all be done over Zoom?

The first reason lies in how companies define the role. Palantir writes that "Deltas" (its internal name for Forward Deployed Software Engineers) are responsible for deploying the platform to customers.

Deployment here means getting the system running within the real business processes of a real organization, not shipping a build and waiting for feedback.

The second reason is technical, and harder to get around. FDE Academy believes that security requirements and air-gapped systems are the main reasons on-site work cannot be replaced by remote work. Its example: a defense contractor running an air-gapped network will not let you log in from home.

Imagine you need to debug a data pipeline running on a hospital's or factory's internal network. There is no VPN, logs cannot leave the building, and the only person who clearly understands how the data flows is an operations engineer on the early shift. To get the job done, you have to go there in person.

FDE Academy acknowledges that some FDE roles allow remote work, but most do not. If you see an FDE job description that says "fully remote," it is worth asking: who are this company's customers, and where is the data stored?

It sounds appealing, but frequent travel comes at a cost

Visiting customers across several cities, or even several countries, is quite attractive to an engineer with three years of experience who is used to working at a fixed desk. But the recruiting platform Paraform warns that travel schedules can be extremely intense, especially during the initial deployment phase, and they do not always ease up quickly.

Paraform lists this as one of the causes of burnout.

So in interviews, do not just ask about the travel percentage. Also ask how long a typical project lasts, and how predecessors got through the first few months.

"On-site" also has two meanings worth distinguishing. Going to a customer for a few days of workshops is very different from what Istari means by embedding in the customer's team. The latter requires you to remain an employee of your own company while working day to day as a member of the customer's team.

Account managers, and the gap for Vietnamese engineers

This is exactly where Vietnamese engineers tend to get stuck. Second Talent, a staffing company (with a commercial interest in recruiting), observes that Vietnam's outsourcing model usually places an account manager between the engineer and the customer. This is the claim of an interested party, so verify it against the projects you have worked on.

If this model matches your work situation, you have likely never personally led a requirements clarification meeting or responded directly to customer complaints. By the time requirements reach you, they are already filtered tickets.

So the real obstacle for Vietnamese engineers may not be visas or plane tickets, but the lack of experience handling ambiguous requirements inside a customer's office. Your technical skills can be strong, but if you have never sat next to users, your first on-site engagement can easily leave you at a loss.

Want to enter the field? Start preparing now

First, read the travel terms in every job description carefully. When companies recruit FDEs in Southeast Asia, the India-based benchmark of 4 to 10 days per month is a realistic baseline. If family or health circumstances do not allow that level of travel, you should clarify it before applying, not when you receive an offer.

Second, find ways to bypass the account manager layer in your current job. Volunteer to join demos, requirements meetings, or launch support shifts at the customer's office.

A single line on your resume saying "worked directly with the customer's operations team during a two-week launch, handling change requests on-site" carries far more weight than a list of frameworks.

Third, choose customers with complex environments whenever possible. None of the job descriptions above list this as a requirement, but experience gained where data cannot leave the premises (such as banks, hospitals, or factories) is likely close to what FDEs face in air-gapped environments.

The line "up to 50% travel" in a job description conveys more than just how much travel is involved. It signals that the company is looking for someone who can work directly with customers, with no one in between.
