Buy a dedicated AI tool or build on a general-purpose platform? | The three-layer structure to review before the approval process

This article organizes the market's three-layer structure—general-purpose platforms, dedicated AI products, and on-the-ground implementation—that you should understand before writing an approval request for a dedicated AI tool. It shows, with cited sources, what is happening to dedicated tools as platforms expand their features, and summarizes it all into a five-question selection checklist you can use in your approval request.

What You Will Learn from This Article

  • The three-layer structure of the AI tool market (general-purpose platforms, dedicated products, on-the-ground implementation) and how money flows in each layer
  • The structure by which platform feature expansion becomes a headwind for dedicated tools, along with public examples
  • A five-question checklist you can use as-is for approval requests and tool selection

A department requests the introduction of an AI tool, and you create a comparison table—the first thing to check at that point is neither the number of features nor the price. It is: "Will this tool's core functionality still lie outside the general-purpose platform a year from now?"

This article divides the AI tool market into three layers, organizes it structurally, and distills it into a selection checklist you can use directly in your approval request.

The intended readers are information systems departments and corporate planning staff who write or review approval requests for AI adoption.

In the Approval Request Comparison Table, Dedicated Tools Always Win

Dedicated AI tools are products that excel in comparison tables. Their feature lists are long, their demos show actual business screens, and their pricing is clear. General-purpose platforms (ChatGPT, Claude, Copilot, Notion AI, etc.), on the other hand, "can do anything, but it is hard to write down what they actually do for you." If you score by feature count, dedicated tools win.

The problem is that such a comparison table is a still image of "today's features." In the world of AI, platform-side features grow on a scale of months.

A feature that today exists only in a dedicated tool may be standard in the platform your company has already contracted a year later—more and more cases come to light at the renewal approval stage for tools already in use.

The Market's Three-Layer Structure

The AI tool market becomes clear when viewed as three layers.

Layer 1: General-purpose platforms. Platforms such as ChatGPT, Claude, Copilot, and Notion AI that are not tied to any particular business function. Core capabilities—text generation, search and reference (RAG), and agent execution—continue to concentrate in this layer. Many companies already have some form of contract with them.

Layer 2: Dedicated AI products. SaaS for specific purposes, such as "meeting minutes AI," "writing AI," or "sales email AI." They offer fast deployment and interfaces tailored to the work, but their core engines are almost always the same models as Layer 1.

Layer 3: On-the-ground implementation. This is the option of implementing "your company's own way of doing the work" on top of a platform you already have, rather than buying a tool from either layer. It is delivered as a service or in-house development, and the resulting assets remain with your company.

What Is Happening in Layer 2—Platform Evolution Becomes a Headwind

Layer 2's structural weakness is that its core functionality overlaps with Layer 1. Every time the platform evolves, the "reason for being dedicated" of a dedicated tool is replaced, one by one, by standard features.

This is not an imaginary risk.

Jasper, considered a leading AI writing SaaS in the early days of the generative AI boom, raised a large round in 2022 at a $1.5 billion valuation, but in 2023 it was reported to have revised its annual recurring revenue (ARR) forecast downward by at least 30%, cut its internal employee share valuation by 20%, and carried out layoffs. Sources: Jasper, an Early Generative AI Winner, Cuts Internal Valuation as Growth Slows (The Information, September 29, 2023) (internal valuation cut), The AI Wrapper is Dead (NFX, July 2026) (summary of funding, downward revision, and layoffs)

That NFX essay summarizes companies of this type as having "bet that foundation models would stay at their current level." It points to the structure in which products that thinly layer a single function on top of a platform (wrappers) get swallowed by the platform's own evolution.

To be fair, not everything in Layer 2 is at risk. Dedicated products that possess what platforms structurally cannot—industry-specific data, regulatory compliance, ownership of entire business workflows—remain strong even as platforms evolve. What needs to be judged is "whether the tool's strength is of a kind that cannot be built into the platform."

Layer 3—The Side Where Platform Evolution Is a Tailwind

Layer 3's on-the-ground implementation works with the opposite dynamics to Layer 2. Because you build your business processes on a platform you have already contracted, the smarter the platform becomes, the faster and cheaper the implementation gets.

Another difference is how assets are retained. With dedicated tools, business data and operational know-how accumulate inside the tool and are lost if you cancel.

With implementation on top of a platform, both the data and the process designs (prompts, procedures, judgment criteria, integrations) remain on your company's platform side. Even if you change vendors, the assets do not disappear.

The reason Gomumari stands in this layer with FDE (field-driven implementation) is precisely these dynamics. Implementation happens on top of a platform the customer already has—the more the platform evolves, the faster and cheaper the implementation becomes.

That said, the question "will it still lie outside the platform a year from now?" should also be directed at Layer 3 itself.

If platforms become able to automatically assemble business processes, the price of the implementation work itself will keep falling. What remains even then is the business-side resolution—choosing which processes to target, what to treat as exceptions, and which metrics to judge effectiveness by—along with your company's own data and process designs accumulated on the platform.

When implementation gets cheaper, it is the sellers of implementation who suffer, not the buyers who have kept their assets on their own side.

A Checklist for Approval Requests—Five Questions

In selection and approval discussions, apply these five questions to each candidate tool.

  • Will its core functionality still lie outside the platform a year from now? Text generation, summarization, RAG, and agent execution have consistently been functions that concentrate on the platform side.
  • On which side do business data and operational know-how accumulate? Judge by what remains the moment you cancel.
  • Could the same business process be implemented on the platform your company has already contracted? If so, the additional SaaS cost should be compared against "the outsourcing cost of implementation labor" (Typical Costs of Introducing AI Agents).
  • Can the effect be measured before and after introduction? An introduction that cannot be measured in units like "how many hours this process saves" will make it impossible to write the renewal approval request.
  • What is the tool's real strength? Features get swallowed by the platform. Industry data, regulatory compliance, and ownership of the entire workflow do not.

Next Steps

Before asking "should we buy a tool," start with "which tasks do we want to reduce, and by how much"—that is how to write your proposal based on the three-layer structure. For how to select target tasks and how to proceed with implementation, see AI Implementation FDE (Service) and AI Agent Deployment (Service).

FAQ

Frequently Asked Questions

We've already deployed a dedicated AI tool. Was it a waste?

No. If it has "strength that won't be swallowed by the platform" per the five questions above, it's a reasonable investment. What's risky is having multiple overlapping single-function tools under parallel contracts with the platform. At renewal time, it's worth considering whether to consolidate into implementation on the platform side.

We've already contracted a general-purpose AI platform, but our teams aren't using it effectively. What should we do?

That's where Layer 3 (on-the-ground implementation) comes in. Rather than "distributing the platform and training people," decide on target tasks and build templates tailored to that task's constraints, data, and exceptions on top of the platform. The process is described in AI Implementation FDE (Service).

Can implementation on top of a general-purpose AI platform be done in-house?

For routine tasks completed entirely with the platform's own features, in-house implementation by the IT department is entirely feasible. For tasks spanning multiple systems or with many exceptions, a realistic approach is to build the first task with a partner experienced in implementation and effect measurement, then leave the template in-house.
