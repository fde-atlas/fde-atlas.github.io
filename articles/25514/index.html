Forward Deployed Engineer vs Consultant

Ryz Labs · 2026-10-03 · 6 min read

Summary

This Ryz Labs article compares forward deployed engineers (FDEs) with consultants, solutions engineers, product software engineers, and staff augmentation. Its core argument: judge a role by what it ships and what it is accountable for, not by title. FDEs write production code in a customer's environment and stay accountable after launch; consultants deliver recommendations and roadmaps; solutions engineers support pre-sales and hand off after the deal closes. The piece offers five vetting questions for buyers (code ownership, on-call duty, accounts per engineer, handover, shipped systems) and cites typical FDE pricing of $7,000–$15,000 per engineer per month. It also promotes Ryz Labs' own ~7-person AI pod teams, with fraud detection and compliance review case results.

Key points

  • FDEs ship production code and own it post-launch; consultants only advise.
  • Judge roles by deliverables and accountability, not job titles.
  • Solutions engineers support sales and hand off once deals close.
  • Vet FDE claims: code location, on-call duty, accounts covered, handover.
  • Typical FDE pricing runs $7,000–$15,000 per engineer monthly.

Full text

A forward deployed engineer (FDE) writes production code in one customer's environment and stays accountable for it running. A consultant assesses the problem and hands over a recommendation. A solutions engineer supports the sale with demos and proofs of concept, then moves to the next deal. A software engineer builds one product for many customers from inside the vendor. If you need a working system on your data, in your cloud, used by your people, you need FDEs. If you need a decision about what to build, a consultant is the better buy.

Titles drift. Some companies call pre-sales staff "forward deployed" because it sounds better, and some consultancies rebrand delivery staff the same way. Judge the role by what it ships and what it is accountable for, not by the title on the profile. A useful test: if the person covers fifteen accounts and nobody pages them when the system breaks, they are doing solutions engineering.

This is the comparison buyers ask about most, because both roles show up on your premises (or your video calls), interview your people and talk about your workflow. The difference is what happens after the interviews.

A consulting engagement typically runs discovery, current-state analysis, a target-state design and a roadmap. The deliverable is a document and a recommendation. Good consultants are very valuable here: they compare vendors, size the opportunity, and help leadership agree on a direction. Some firms then staff a build phase, often with a different team from the one that did the assessment.

An FDE engagement starts with the same discovery, but it is short and it ends in a pull request. The FDE sits with the analysts or operators who will use the system, gets approved access to the data, and starts building in your repos and cloud. The design changes as the team learns what the data actually looks like. Nobody hands a roadmap to someone else to execute, because the people who learned the workflow are the people writing the code.

  • You haven't decided whether to build, buy or wait, and need an independent view.
  • The question is organizational: operating model, governance, budget, change management across many business units.
  • You need a board-level business case or a transformation program across dozens of initiatives.
  • The decision is made. You know the workflow you want to change and need it working.
  • A pilot impressed people in a demo but never got connected to real systems.
  • The hard part is your data, your security review and your legacy integrations, not the model.
  • You want to own the result: the code, the prompts, the evaluation sets and the know-how.

Solutions engineers (also called sales engineers or pre-sales engineers) work for a vendor and help a prospect decide to buy. They run demos, answer security questionnaires, build a proof of concept on sample data and map the product to the buyer's requirements. Their success is measured by technical wins and closed revenue, and once the deal closes they hand the account to someone else.

FDEs pick up where that handoff happens. Their job is to make the software do real work for one customer: integrate it with the customer's platforms, adapt it to edge cases the demo never showed, and stay with it through launch. At AI vendors such as OpenAI and Anthropic, both roles exist side by side. The solutions engineer helps you buy; the FDE helps you deploy what you bought.

From the buyer's side, the practical point is this: a solutions engineer's proof of concept tells you whether a product can work. It does not tell you what it will take to make it work in production on your data. Ask for FDE time, or an FDE team, for that second question.

Palantir, which made the FDE title well known, describes the split simply in its engineering blog: a software engineer (internally a "Dev") focuses on one capability for many customers, while a forward deployed software engineer (a "Delta") focuses on one customer and many capabilities. Both write production code. What differs is the shape of the work.

  • Scope. A product engineer goes deep on one area, such as the query engine or the permissions model. An FDE goes broad: data pipelines on Monday, an API integration on Tuesday, an evaluation harness on Wednesday, a security review on Thursday.
  • Feedback. A product engineer hears from users through product managers and analytics. An FDE hears it directly, often from the person sitting next to them.
  • Constraints. A product engineer controls their environment. An FDE works inside someone else's: their cloud account, their identity provider, their change-management process.
  • Skills beyond code. FDEs need to run a working session with business users, write an architecture note a security team will accept, and say no to scope that won't pay off.

That last point is why FDEs are hard to hire. The role needs a strong production engineer who is also comfortable in a room with operators and compliance reviewers. See our guide to what forward deployed engineers cost for what that combination pays.

Buyers who already use staff augmentation sometimes ask whether FDEs are the same thing with a new name. They overlap: in both, engineers work on your team, in your repos and standups, reporting to your leads. The difference is who owns the outcome.

  • With staff augmentation, your engineering managers set priorities and the added engineers take on a share of the roadmap.
  • With a forward-deployed team, a tech lead on the team owns delivery of a specific system, working with your leaders, and the team is accountable for that system reaching production.

Many companies use both: an FDE team, run as an AI pod team, builds the first production AI system, and augmented engineers such as AI engineers join the internal team that will run and extend it. Our comparison of dedicated AI teams and staff augmentation covers that decision in more depth.

When a vendor or firm proposes "forward deployed" help, these questions separate the real thing from a relabeled role:

  • Where will the code live? FDE work goes into your repos and runs in your cloud, through your CI. If the answer is a vendor environment or a slide deck, it isn't FDE work.
  • Who is on call when it breaks? FDEs own production. Consultants and solutions engineers usually don't.
  • How many accounts does each engineer cover? An FDE goes deep on one customer, or a handful. A portfolio of a dozen means pre-sales.
  • What gets handed over at the end? You should get a running system, the source, the evaluation sets and documentation your team can work from.
  • What have they shipped? Ask for systems in production with results, not demos.
