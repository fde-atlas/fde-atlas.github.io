Impact Mapping Reading Notes: How FDEs Control Scope

Original title: 影响地图 Impact Mapping 读书笔记：FDE 如何控制范围

FDE Times · 2026-11-16 · 5 min read

Summary

This article is a reading note on Gojko Adzic's 2012 book Impact Mapping, aimed at FDEs struggling with scope creep. Impact mapping is a collaborative strategic planning method: before estimating any feature, you identify which measurable business goal it serves, which actors must change behavior, and how. Using a hypothetical insurance claims system with 12 requested features, the author shows how only features attached to a map branch get built, curbing both client-driven scope creep and engineer-driven over-engineering. The method must be drawn with clients and stakeholders, not alone, and doubles as a prioritization tool. The article ends with advice on featuring this skill in resumes and interviews.

Key points

  • Ask which business goal a feature serves before estimating anything.
  • Every requirement must attach to an existing map branch or wait.
  • The same test curbs scope creep and over-engineering.
  • Maps must be drawn collaboratively with clients and stakeholders.
  • Read it before a new project and use it in the first requirements meeting.

Key Takeaways

  • Gojko Adzic's 2012 book is a practical guide to impact mapping, a collaborative strategic planning method.
  • Its greatest value for an FDE is connecting every feature to a customer goal, thereby curbing scope creep and over-engineering.
  • Read it before your first project starts and apply it directly in requirements gathering; this is not a book to read as theory.

A customer sends over 12 features with a one-line note: "All of these this quarter." The scenario is hypothetical, but every Forward Deployed Engineer (FDE) has run into something like it. The engineer's instinctive reaction is to estimate each item one by one and slot them into the plan.

Gojko Adzic's *Impact Mapping: Making a big impact with software products and projects* argues for the opposite: before estimating anything, ask which business goal each feature serves.

The book was published on October 1, 2012, ISBN 978-0955683640. Adzic calls it a practical guide to a simple, collaborative strategic planning method. For a software book, 14 years is a long time, but the problem it targets has not gone away: plans and delivery roadmaps drifting away from business goals. On a customer site, this happens all too easily.

For an FDE, this is the most valuable question in the entire project. The method's official website puts the purpose bluntly: to help you build products that make an impact and deliver projects that make an impact, rather than just shipping software.

Who is this book for? Why should a junior FDE read it?

Adzic says the primary audience is senior people involved in building software products. He also writes for business people assigned to software projects, promising to help them communicate their ideas better to delivery teams.

An FDE with two to eight years of experience sits right between these two groups. When you sit down with the person responsible for the customer's business goals, you may be the one translating goals into engineering work, with no one beside you to do the interpreting. Using this book does not require you to already be senior; it is precisely because the job demands that you act like a senior person that you need it.

The reading order is: read it before starting a new project, then bring the method straight into your first requirements meeting. This is not a book to finish and shelve. It only has value when you draw a real map with a real customer.

First idea: the impact is the product

According to impactmapping.org, an impact map shows how a delivery plan connects to the outside world, including customers and users, and that connection is not static.

This changes your first question. "What does the system need to do?" is not yet the right question. The right question is: whose behavior needs to change, and how does that change help the customer achieve their goal.

Drawing a small map for 12 features

Suppose the customer is an insurance company, and all 12 features revolve around its claims processing system.

The top layer is the goal the customer's management cares about: for example, cutting claims processing time from five days to two. The second layer answers who needs to change how they work to achieve that goal: the company's internal claims reviewers, and the policyholders who submit claims.

The third layer records the behavior changes. Reviewers no longer manually re-enter data from documents; claimants submit all required materials the first time, instead of having their claims returned for missing items.

Features only appear at the bottom layer. Automatic data extraction from documents hangs under the reviewers' change; the required-materials checklist and incomplete-submission reminders in the claims form hang under the claimants' change.

Three features now have a place on the map. The other nine, such as custom dashboards or PDF report exports, may still be reasonable, but for now they attach to no branch, so they wait. Now you have a clear reason to say "not for now," and that reason has nothing to do with being short-staffed.

When practicing, start from the top layer. If the customer cannot yet articulate a quantifiable goal, requirements gathering is not finished, and no feature should be estimated.

Second idea: the map is a tool for cutting waste

The official website points out one specific benefit: reducing waste by preventing scope creep and over-engineered solutions. For an FDE, these are exactly the two most costly traps.

Scope creep comes from the customer, with one more requirement added at every meeting. Over-engineering comes from you: a requirement that only needs to work well in one department, and you build a general-purpose data pipeline.

The map handles both cases with the same test. Every new requirement must attach to an existing branch on the map; if it cannot, a new branch must be opened, with a clear statement of which goal it serves.

Every architectural decision must also answer the same question. The method is positioned as a way to keep organizations from losing direction when building products and delivering projects. For an FDE, "losing direction" usually means spending three months developing strictly to the requirements document, only to solve the wrong problem.

Third idea: draw the map with the customer, not for the customer

Adzic calls this a collaborative method, and that is the most notable word in the whole definition. A map you draw alone in a hotel room and then present is just another plan.

A map drawn on a whiteboard together with the budget owner and end users is different: in that moment, they see with their own eyes that the feature they want connects to nothing.

The book's page quotes a practitioner: in a very short time, the method helped their team make better prioritization decisions.

And prioritization is exactly where an FDE is most easily squeezed between the customer and the headquarters product team. With a map the customer helped draw, the argument shifts from "whoever shouts loudest" to "which branch is closer to the goal."

How to put this skill on your resume and bring it into interviews

The beauty of this skill is that it leaves concrete traces in every project you have done. When reading FDE job postings, watch for phrases like "understand customer business goals," "determine scope priorities," and "work directly with stakeholders"; if they appear in the job description, this skill deserves top billing.

When writing your resume, do not start with what you did. Write it in the order of the map: what the customer's goal was, whose behavior changed, and only then the system you delivered. When asked about a project in an interview, talk about the features you persuaded the customer not to build, and what your reasoning was.

A good FDE does not measure themselves by how many features they delivered, but by how many features the customer no longer needs because the real problem has been solved.
