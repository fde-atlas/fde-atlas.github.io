Why do generative AI use case selections fail?

Generative AI use case selection tends to fail for two reasons: choosing based on technology, and starting with vague evaluation criteria.

In generative AI implementation projects, we often hear stories like "we ran a PoC (proof of concept) as a trial, but couldn't move to production." In a July 2024 announcement, Gartner predicted that "at least 30% of generative AI projects will be abandoned after PoC by the end of 2025." The main reasons cited include poor data quality, inadequate risk management, rising costs, and unclear business value (source: Gartner press release, July 29, 2024). Among these reasons, "unclear value" is, in most cases, determined at the use case selection stage.

Choosing based on technology leads to "AI that no one uses"

If you start with "what can we do with the latest LLM," you are more likely to select use cases that are not connected to business problems.

A common case is making chatbots or internal document search (RAG) the first theme simply because "it seems like generative AI." Although the demo gets good reviews, it is not integrated into frontline business workflows, so usage stops within a few months. From an FDE's (Forward Deployed Engineer's) field perspective, any use case that cannot be stated in one sentence as "whose work, which task, and how many minutes will be reduced" is safer to put on hold at the selection stage.

Without evaluation criteria, the loudest department's proposals win

If evaluation criteria are not decided in advance, priorities are determined by political power, and budgets concentrate on themes that are unlikely to produce results.

In use case identification workshops, it is not uncommon for dozens of candidates to emerge. Without a common yardstick for comparison, themes that interest executives or proposals from departments that made strong requests tend to be prioritized. The failure patterns can be summarized as follows.

Failure patternTypical symptomsPreventive measures at the selection stage
Technology-drivenDemo is well received but not used in businessIdentify business problems and target tasks for reduction first
Lack of evaluation criteriaPriorities are decided by internal politicsAgree on evaluation criteria and weights before generating candidates
Unverified dataData turns out to be unusable after the PoC startsVerify data location, permissions, and quality at the selection stage
Lack of success criteriaNo one can judge whether the PoC succeededDefine numerical criteria for production migration in advance
Missing operational designNo one to maintain the system after the PoCAppoint business owners and operational structures at the selection stage

What are the evaluation criteria and prioritization methods for generative AI use cases?

The basic approach is to evaluate on two axes, "business impact" and "feasibility," screen out by risk, and then set priorities.

If you add too many evaluation criteria, scoring becomes a mere formality. For this reason, the frameworks FDEs use in the field are often kept simple. Here, we organize the evaluation criteria commonly used in practice and how to set priorities.

Evaluate on 3 axes: business impact, feasibility, and risk

Candidates are evaluated on the 3 axes of "value, feasibility, and risk," with risk treated as a screening condition rather than an added score.

Evaluation criterionMain perspectivesQuestions to verify
Business impactReduced work hours, sales contribution, quality improvement, number of people affectedIf successful, whose work changes, what changes, and by how much?
FeasibilityData availability and quality, system integration, compatibility with generative AICan it run within 3 months with existing data and systems?
RiskImpact of incorrect answers, personal/confidential information, regulatory complianceWhat impact would incorrect output have on business or customers?
Sustainability (auxiliary axis)Presence of business owners, ease of integration into current workflowsAfter going to production, who will keep using it and who will improve it?

Business impact and feasibility are evaluated relatively, for example on a 5-point scale. Risk, on the other hand, is judged as "acceptable/not acceptable," and candidates judged unacceptable are deferred even if their scores are high. In practice, this distinction prevents confusion in discussions.

Decide on "the first one" with a 2×2 matrix

The standard practice is to start with "quick wins" that are high in both impact and feasibility, creating success stories within the company.

CategoryBusiness impactFeasibilityRecommended action
Quick winHighHighStart PoC with top priority
Strategic themeHighLowProceed in parallel with data preparation and integration infrastructure
Small improvementLowHighHandle through voluntary frontline use or SaaS adoption
DeferLowLowDo not start at this time

From an FDE's standpoint, for the first project, prioritizing "reaching production reliably" over "maximum impact" tends to speed up overall progress. The first success story builds trust within the company, and that trust makes data provision and interdepartmental coordination easier.

What steps do FDEs follow for use case selection?

FDEs select use cases by embedding themselves in the field, following 5 steps: "business observation → candidate identification → evaluation → verification design → agreement."

The defining characteristic of the FDE role, popularized by Palantir, is that it embeds closely with customers on-site and takes end-to-end ownership from problem discovery through implementation. In use case selection as well, the emphasis is not just on hearing requests in a meeting room, but on observing actual work to identify structural bottlenecks.

Five steps and the deliverables of each step

Clarifying the deliverables for each step keeps the selection process from becoming dependent on individuals and makes it easier to explain decisions to stakeholders.

  • Work observation and interviews: Sit alongside frontline staff to identify tasks that take a long time, tasks where judgment depends on specific individuals, and recurring tasks. Deliverables: a workflow diagram and a list of issues.
  • Identifying use case candidates: Translate the issue list into "forms that generative AI can solve." Map them to processing patterns generative AI excels at, such as summarization, classification, extraction, draft generation, search, and dialogue. Deliverable: a list of candidates.
  • Evaluation and prioritization: Score candidates using the three axes from the previous chapter and place them on a 2x2 matrix. Deliverable: a prioritized list of use cases.
  • Verification design: For the top candidates, define the data to be used, test cases for evaluation, success criteria, and the verification period. Deliverable: a PoC plan document.
  • Stakeholder agreement: Agree on judgment criteria and risk response policies with the business owner, the IT department, and legal/security staff. Deliverable: an agreed-upon execution plan.

Why steps 1 and 4 deserve the most time

FDEs spend the most time on work observation and verification design. Skipping these makes it impossible to judge whether the PoC succeeded or failed.

Interviews alone will not surface inefficiencies that the people doing the work consider "just the way things are." For example, tasks like transcribing information across multiple systems or searching past response histories often only reveal their true scale when you actually observe them firsthand.

In verification design, what is especially important is building the evaluation dataset (pairs of expected inputs and expected outputs) together with the business staff. The output quality of generative AI tends to be judged on a vague sense of "seems good." If you prepare concrete examples in advance that business staff would judge as passing, the post-PoC assessment becomes objective. This is a step where, from firsthand experience, I often feel it is the point that determines whether selection succeeds or fails.

How to take a selected generative AI use case from PoC to production?

Moving to production requires deciding on three things before the PoC begins: "criteria for the go/no-go decision," "the operational structure," and "the cost outlook."

Most PoCs end as "verification for the sake of verification" because the production state was never envisioned at the PoC design stage. FDEs design the PoC as the first stage toward production.

Set production judgment criteria from the PoC stage

Judgment criteria should combine not only "accuracy" but also "reduction in work hours," "usage rate," and "operating costs."

Judgment itemEvaluation perspectivePoints to note when setting it
Output qualityPass rate on the evaluation datasetThe pass criteria are set by the business staff
Business impactChange in time required for the target tasksMeasure the pre-implementation time required in advance
Usage statusContinued use by target usersDistinguish one-time trials from continued use
CostAPI usage fees, infrastructure costs, maintenance effortEstimate based on expected production usage volume
Risk responseDetection of erroneous outputs and human review proceduresClearly specify where humans perform final confirmation

The level of numerical targets varies greatly depending on the nature of the work and current task times. For this reason, rather than applying a uniform benchmark, it is more realistic to agree with the business owner on targets based on pre-implementation measured values.

Operational design required for production

After going to production, you need to decide who will handle prompt and model updates, output monitoring, and responding to user feedback.

Generative AI applications are not finished once built. Upgrades to foundation models can change output tendencies, and prompts and reference data need to be updated in line with changes to business rules. FDEs aim to put the following structure in place before stepping away at the point of production launch.

  • Business owner: Responsible for final judgment on output quality and prioritizing improvement requests
  • Operations staff: Responsible for log monitoring, regular regression testing with the evaluation dataset, and incident response
  • Governance staff: Responsible for usage policies, data handling, and audit response

Whether you can create a state where things run without the FDE remaining on-site full-time determines the success of production deployment.

How to identify use cases that are not suited to generative AI?

If a use case falls into any of the following categories, it is likely unsuitable: errors in output cannot be tolerated, explanation of the basis for judgment is mandatory, or the task can be solved with rules.

In generative AI use case selection, deciding "what not to do" is as important as deciding "what to do." Removing unsuitable themes early lets you concentrate limited resources on high-value candidates.

Four characteristics of tasks unsuited to generative AI

When the probabilistic nature of generative AI output conflicts with the certainty the business requires, consider alternative means.

CharacteristicConcrete examplesAlternative means to consider
No errors whatsoever can be toleratedFinal calculation of amounts, legally binding decisionsRule-based systems, human judgment
Rigorous explanation of the basis for judgment is mandatoryCredit decisions, final hiring decisionsExplainable statistical models + human judgment
Can be fully described by rulesStandard conditional branching, fixed-format conversionsConventional programs, RPA
Input data does not exist or cannot be obtainedDecisions relying on undocumented tacit knowledgeFirst build mechanisms for data capture and recording

However, even for tasks that are "unsuited as a whole," generative AI may be usable for some of their steps. For example, a human makes the credit decision itself, while generative AI is used to summarize the documents that inform the judgment or point out missing information.

A checklist for judging suitability

Breaking the task down into steps and asking for each step "can a human perform the final check?" makes it easier to judge suitability.

Here is a summary of the questions FDEs check during selection.

  • If there is an error in the output, can a human notice and correct it within the flow of the work?
  • Even with an "80%-quality draft," does it still reduce work time?
  • Is the input data accessible both in terms of permissions and technically?
  • Compared with implementing the same processing as a conventional program, can you explain why generative AI should be used?
  • Is the structure such that erroneous output could reach customers or business partners directly?

Tasks where you can answer "yes" to 1, 2, and 3, "no" to 5, and explain the reason for 4 are strong candidates for generative AI use cases. Conversely, for tasks where 5 is "yes," it is safer to first consider whether the design can incorporate a human review step.

Use case selection is a process in an FDE's work that tests both "technical skills" and "business understanding." For engineers aiming to become FDEs, in addition to knowledge of models and tools, the experience of observing on-site operations and building consensus among stakeholders using evaluation criteria directly becomes a career strength.
