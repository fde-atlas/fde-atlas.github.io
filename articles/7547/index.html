Epilogue · What Veterans Take With Them, and What They Should Let Go

Original title: 尾声 · 老兵带走什么，又该放下什么

FDE 的来路 · 5 min read

Summary

This epilogue to a series on the origins of FDEs asks what veterans of enterprise digitalization should keep and discard as AI reshapes software delivery. The author argues the FDE title matters less than the underlying question: where does responsibility for enterprise digitalization actually end? Veterans bring durable assets—alertness to metric definitions, respect for exceptions, production awareness, and patience with organizational inertia. But they should drop habits like prioritizing technical completeness over business value, building big platforms before demand, treating requirement documents as the business itself, and treating acceptance as the end of responsibility. Newcomers need not master history; they must learn to ask old questions about data sources, exceptions, permissions, and reversibility. AI pushes software from recording to acting, moving delivery boundaries forward without canceling old problems.

Key points

  • FDE signals that enterprise software delivery boundaries are moving forward, not a heroic role
  • Veterans' assets: metric-definition vigilance, respect for exceptions, production awareness, organizational patience
  • Habits to drop: technical completeness first, platform-before-demand, documents-as-business, acceptance-as-endpoint
  • Young engineers don't owe history; they must learn old questions about data, exceptions, and reversibility
  • AI makes software act on incomplete information, raising the stakes of responsibility and risk

As enterprise digitalization veterans enter the FDE era, which experiences remain assets, and which project habits must be let go? The epilogue returns to responsibility, judgment, and long-termism.

By the time I finished writing, the acronym FDE no longer seemed all that important.

It may continue as a job title, or it may be absorbed by Solutions Architects, delivery engineers, AI product managers, or a cross-functional team. Companies will change names, roles will change boundaries, and today's hot tools will quickly become outdated.

What remains is a question that is increasingly hard to avoid: for people doing enterprise digitalization, where exactly does responsibility end?

If it ends at data ingestion, whether anyone uses the reports is not your problem; if it ends at system go-live, whether processes actually change is not your problem; if it ends at model output, whether the subsequent actions are safe is also not your problem.

Each of these boundaries has a practical justification. No one can be responsible for all the outcomes of running a business.

But if the boundaries are drawn too cleanly, what the customer receives will be a string of deliverables that each succeed on their own yet add up to nothing.

What Veterans Can Take With Them

People who have lived through databases, ERP, data warehouses, BI, and data platforms need not feel anxious about not being able to recite the latest model parameters.

A few things in old experience are hard to acquire quickly.

First, vigilance about definitions and metrics.

The same revenue, customer, inventory, or completion status may mean different things in different departments' mouths. Veterans have seen numbers that reconcile but whose meaning is wrong, and they have seen a meeting burn two hours just to confirm how the "customer count" in a report is actually calculated.

Second, respect for exceptions.

Flowcharts are always cleaner than the field. Real enterprises keep running on manual corrections, special approvals, offline confirmations, and the judgment of seasoned staff. Exceptions are not always reasonable, but you cannot pretend they don't exist just because the model can't see them.

Third, production awareness.

Between a successful demo and stable daily operation lie permissions, concurrency, dirty data, upstream and downstream failures, user habits, and accountability. Those who have stepped in these pits know that "it runs" is only the beginning.

Fourth, patience with organizational inertia.

Technology can be upgraded in a week; job divisions, performance metrics, and departmental interests cannot. Many projects don't lack a correct answer—they lack anyone with the authority to turn the answer into action.

There is also a piece of common sense already paid for with tuition: go-live does not equal success.

None of these things depreciate because of AI. On the contrary, once software starts taking action, the costs of definitions, exceptions, production, and organization all grow larger.

Some Things Should No Longer Be Carried

Experience protects people, and it also traps them.

The first thing to let go is placing technical completeness above business value.

A beautiful platform architecture, a unified model, and comprehensive governance do not mean a specific problem has been solved. If a deployment in the smallest scope has not yet proven that anyone wants to use it, you should not use grand future benefits to justify today's costs.

The second is building the big platform first, then waiting for the business to come use it.

Common capabilities certainly matter, but commonality is not designed in meetings. It takes repeated collisions with several real scenarios to know what is truly shared.

The third is treating requirements documents as the business itself.

A document records only what certain people could articulate at a certain moment. Without walking through the actual work, you don't know what judgments hide inside those "it depends."

The fourth is treating acceptance as the end point of responsibility.

Contracts need an end point, and teams need project closure. But for product and professional judgment, whether users adopt it, whether work changes, and whether experience accumulates still need to be seen.

Finally, let go of a sense of entitlement: the idea that only after many years in data are you qualified to talk about being a real FDE.

Old experience provides a map of the pits, not an admission ticket. Seniority cannot substitute for field understanding, let alone for results.

Those Just Starting Out Don't Owe a History

Never having personally done an ERP implementation does not prevent a young engineer from becoming an excellent FDE.

New tools make prototyping fast. Interfaces and workflows that once took weeks to build can now be seen in days or even hours. This is a real advantage: ideas can face user scrutiny earlier.

But speed also creates illusions.

Being able to generate an application from a document does not mean understanding the business; being able to have an AI agent call tools does not mean knowing what permissions it should have; completing a task in a demo does not mean it deserves trust in a production environment.

The homework for young people is not to make up a technical chronicle, but to learn to ask the old questions: where does the data come from, who defines the terms, how are exceptions handled, whom do actions affect, can failures be rolled back, and what proves the value.

Not having stepped in old pits is not a flaw. Not knowing where the pits are is what makes it easy to use speed in the wrong direction.

No Need to Wait for the Next "This Time Is Different"

In the prologue, we started from several familiar promises.

ERP hoped to write business into unified processes, BI hoped to let enterprises see themselves, and data platforms hoped to consolidate and reuse scattered capabilities. Each solved real problems, and each left parts unfinished.

AI has indeed brought a rupture. Software in the past mostly recorded and displayed; today's software begins to generate judgments, call tools, and affect reality with incomplete information. This brings action, risk, and responsibility into the delivery center all at once.

Yet this does not cancel the old questions.

Objects still need to be defined, data will still be dirty, organizations still have boundaries, users may still not use it, and business value still needs to be calculated. The stronger the model's capabilities, the greater the temptation to bypass these questions—and the higher the cost when things go wrong.

Therefore, the FDE does not deserve to be mythologized as a heroic role.

It is more like a signal: the delivery boundary of enterprise software is moving forward. Someone must stand between technology, business, and the field, connecting problem definition, prototyping, production, adoption, and feedback; and must also stop when something is not worth doing or cannot be safely automated.

This work will not be done by one person. Business owners must bear business judgment, security and compliance staff must hold the boundaries, product teams must absorb commonality, and managers must change incentives. What the FDE can do is keep these otherwise scattered responsibilities from falling so easily into the gaps of handoffs.

After thirty years, we have become very good at making enterprises "see." The hard part of the next leg is making change happen after seeing, without pretending that change comes without cost.

The FDE is not the data professional's next job; it is the moment when enterprise digitalization is finally forced to take full responsibility for "from seeing the problem to solving the problem."
