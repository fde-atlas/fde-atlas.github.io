Estimating a 5-Year Total SaaS Cost | How Price Hikes and the Weak Yen Affect the Break-Even Point

Original title: SaaSの5年総額を試算する｜値上げと円安で損益分岐はどう動くか

TIMEWELL · 2026-09-29 · 21 min read

Summary

TIMEWELL's Ryota Hamamoto argues that comparing annual SaaS fees against development costs leads to wrong build-vs-buy decisions, and instead models five-year total costs. He shows overseas SaaS prices rise through both dollar list-price hikes (e.g., a groupware vendor raising prices up to 33% in 2026) and yen-based repricing, compounded by yen depreciation from 106.78 to 158.78 yen per dollar (2020 vs. 2026 average). In a model case (300 users, $60/user/month, 160 yen/dollar), building in-house saves about 40.62 million yen over five years, but only breaks even in year four. User count, unit price, and estimate reliability flip the conclusion; exchange rates and price hikes merely shift the crossover year. He recommends presenting base, optimistic, and pessimistic scenarios with exit conditions, and describes how his FDE service narrows development-cost estimates through prototyping.

Key points

  • Five-year totals, not annual fees versus dev costs, should drive build-vs-buy decisions.
  • SaaS costs rise via dollar price hikes and yen repricing; yen weakened ~49% since 2020.
  • Model case: building saves 40.62 million yen over five years, breaking even in year four.
  • User count and unit price flip the outcome; at 150 users or $40/month, building never pays off.
  • In-house costs must include maintenance, security, parallel running, and dedicated staff.

Hello, I'm Ryuta Hamamoto from TIMEWELL Corporation.

When a SaaS renewal quote arrives and the number is higher than the previous year, many people think, "Wouldn't it be cheaper to build it ourselves?" That feeling only grows when you hear that generative AI has made development faster. Yet if you write your approval request by putting the annual fee and the development cost side by side in that rush, you will usually make the wrong call. The comparison period is too short, and only half of the cost of building is included.

This article is the third in a five-part series on canceling expensive SaaS and in-housing development. In Part 1's decision criteria, I covered "whether it is worth considering at all," and in Part 2, "which operations are okay to build in-house." This time, we verify in numbers "how much it will cost over 5 years" for those candidates. The materials are three: the actual trends of price increases and the weak yen, the 5-year total of a model case built on our assumptions, and a table of the variables that move the break-even point. On the build side, we always include maintenance, security, and personnel costs. An estimate that omits these is nothing more than an estimate made to get approval.

SaaS bills rise through two channels: dollar-denominated price increases and yen price revisions

Overseas SaaS that you pay for in yen goes up in price through two routes. One is a revision of the dollar-denominated list price itself; the other is a revision of the yen-denominated price based on exchange rates. Buyers in Japan receive both.

How large the yen-denominated revisions can be is clearly shown by the example of a major overseas groupware vendor. In April 2023, this company raised the yen-denominated prices of its corporate online services by 15% and its on-premises products by 20%. According to reports, the monthly price of the basic plan stayed at 6 dollars in dollar terms, while only the yen price rose from 650 yen to 750 yen 1. In dollar terms, it was a price increase of not even one cent. In April of the following year, 2024, it again raised both its corporate software and cloud services by 20%, explaining that going forward it may evaluate and adjust yen-denominated prices about twice a year in light of exchange rate fluctuations against the dollar 2. Then, in July 2026, the dollar-denominated list price itself was touched. The range ran from no change to as much as 33% depending on the plan, and the company cited "ongoing investment in AI features and the infrastructure that supports them" as the reason 3.

Dollar-denominated price increases are not limited to this company. In August 2025, a major CRM vendor raised the top editions of its main products by an average of 6%, explaining that this reflected enhanced AI features 4. Also in November 2024, a major domestic no-code platform changed its Light plan from 780 yen per user per month to 1,000 yen and its Standard plan from 1,500 yen to 1,800 yen, while raising the minimum number of contracted users from 5 to 10 5. The stated reasons, at every company, are added features and investment in operations. I am not saying the price increases themselves are unfair. However, those budgeting over 5 years need to set their numbers on the assumption that prices will rise.

Let's also look at exchange rates. When we simply average the monthly averages of the dollar-yen rate in the Tokyo market published by the Bank of Japan by year, 2020 was 106.78 yen, 2024 was 151.50 yen, and January through August 2026 was 158.78 yen 6. Compared with 2020, that is a yen depreciation of about 49%. For a SaaS costing 60 dollars per user per month, even if the dollar list price never changed, the monthly cost per person would have risen from 6,407 yen to 9,527 yen.

This impact is clearly visible in corporate budgets as well. In the Japan Users Association of Information Systems (JUAS) "Corporate IT Trends Survey Report 2026," the second most common reason for IT budget increases was "the impact of the weak yen, rising labor costs, and vendor price increases," cited by 46.6% of companies in their FY2025 plans 7. Price increases and the weak yen are no longer an issue for only some companies.

Putting the annual fee and development cost side by side leads to the wrong conclusion

In the same JUAS survey, the top expected benefit of in-housing system development was "reduced development costs" (41.3%) 7. The motivation to cut costs is natural. The problem lies in how the comparison is made.

"We pay 34.56 million yen a year for a SaaS, but we could build it for 30 million yen. We'd break even in one year." Calculations like this are often seen. They are easy to understand, but they lead to the wrong conclusion in three ways.

First, you keep paying for the SaaS while building. Most SaaS contracts are annual, and they renew automatically unless you give notice by a certain period before the renewal date. If development takes 8 months and migration plus parallel operation takes 4 months, the first year's SaaS fees remain in full. I will go into cancellation notice deadlines and data extraction in detail in Part 4 on cancellation and migration practices, but for estimation purposes, it is realistic to assume "you pay both in year one."

Second, costs continue after you build. In the same JUAS survey, the FY2025 IT budget allocation was 75.9% for "maintaining and operating current business" and 24.1% for "launching new business initiatives" 7. Three quarters of corporate IT budgets are spent keeping what already exists running. From the following year onward, a system you built in-house falls into that three-quarters side.

Third, the SaaS side also has hard-to-see costs: administrators' configuration work, adding and removing users, maintaining integrations with other systems, and so on. To compare fairly, you need to include internal labor on both sides.

National guidelines follow the same logic. The Digital Agency's "DS-310 Basic Policy on the Appropriate Use of Cloud Services in Government Information Systems" strongly recommends the use of SaaS while stating that this is not unconditional. It says that when SaaS usage fees at the operational stage become expensive due to a gradual increase in users or similar factors, whether cost reduction is truly achieved should be carefully evaluated from a lifecycle cost perspective, and if the same functionality can be achieved with another managed service, both approaches should be compared and evaluated. For SaaS billed by account count and expensive SaaS, it also calls for attention to the risk of costs ballooning as the number of accounts grows 8. The estimates in this article put this "comparison of both approaches" into the form of a private company's approval request.

One more thing to check before building is unused licenses. In a survey published in January 2026 by a US SaaS management tool vendor, of the more than 40 million licenses the company manages, an average of 36% were unused relative to recommended usage levels 9. It is US-centric, and it is customer data from a company that sells SaaS management tools, so it cannot be applied directly to Japanese companies. Even so, before deciding whether to build, it is worth taking a look at your own login records once.

Calculating the 5-year total with a model case (300 users, 60 dollars per month, 160 yen per dollar)

From here on, the estimates are based on assumptions we have set. They are not the actual costs of any real company. Every figure is a placeholder intended for readers to replace with their own numbers.

The subject is a business SaaS used by 300 people. The list price in dollars is $60 per user per month, and the exchange rate is the average of 158.78 yen from January to August 2026, rounded to 160 yen per dollar. We assume the dollar list price rises 3% per year. This is set more conservatively than the revision ranges mentioned earlier (average 6%, maximum 33%) because we did not want an estimate skewed in favor of the build side. The build side is assumed to rebuild only the features actually in use and to cancel the SaaS at the renewal date at the end of year one.

Over five years, continuing with SaaS costs about 188.48 million yen, while building in-house costs about 147.86 million yen. The difference is 40.62 million yen, so building comes out cheaper. However, the build side only catches up on a cumulative basis in year four. At the end of year three, it is still 4.24 million yen behind. In year one, both the SaaS fees and the development costs are paid, making the build side 44.7 million yen heavier. This is a situation where you would want to write "payback in three years" in the approval document, but under these assumptions you cannot.

There are also costs we deliberately left out: major rebuilds or platform replacements that could occur midway through the five years. If the same conditions continued beyond year six, the gap would widen by roughly 24 million yen per year. That said, by then the likelihood of a rebuild rises, so we recommend cutting the approval estimate off at five years.

Why maintenance, security, and staffing are included in the build-side costs

There is a reason the assumptions table sets the build side's maintenance and fixes at a relatively heavy 25% of the initial development cost. Multiple studies show that even though generative AI speeds up writing code, the effort of fixing and of handling security may actually increase.

In a randomized controlled trial published in July 2025 by the US research organization METR, 16 experienced open-source developers worked on 246 real tasks, and the condition where AI was allowed took 19% longer. The participants had predicted beforehand that they would be 24% faster, and even after finishing they still felt they had been 20% faster 10. In a February 2026 update, the same METR reported that with AI tools as of late 2025, the change in time required was −18% (confidence interval −38% to +9%). The authors' view is that a speedup is likely, but the true effect is hard to see due to biases such as developers who do not want to work without AI declining to participate 11. It cannot be said that things are not getting faster. But neither is it a solid enough number to justify cutting estimates in half.

DORA, a research program studying software development organizations, reported in its 2025 report, based on roughly 5,000 respondents, that AI use is positively associated with delivery speed but continues to show a negative association with delivery stability. While 90% of respondents use AI and over 80% feel their productivity has increased, 30% trust AI-written code little or not at all 12. In a study by GitClear, a company that makes development analytics tools, examining 211 million lines of changes, the share of lines that were "moved" as part of code refactoring fell from 24.8% in 2021 to 9.5% in 2024. Conversely, "copy-and-paste" lines rose from 8.4% to 12.3%. The share of lines rewritten shortly after being written also rose from 3.3% to 5.7% 13. The natural reading is that the cost of fixing things after they are built has not fallen as much as the cost of building them.

The same goes for security. In code written by over 100 language models for the security product company Veracode, 45% failed security tests. The company reported that even newer, larger models showed no improvement in safety 14. This needs to be read with the caveat that it is vendor-sponsored research. Even so, it is not a reason to drop pre-launch assessments and post-launch monitoring and vulnerability response from the budget.

Finally, the cost of people. In IPA's "DX Trends 2025," when 334 Japanese companies advancing in-house development were asked about their challenges, "securing and developing talent is difficult" stood out at 82.3%. "Cost awareness becomes lower compared to outsourcing" was also cited by 15.0% 15. A system you build in-house requires someone internally to receive requests, prioritize them, and decide whether to fix things. That is why the table includes personnel costs for 0.5 FTE from year one. Removing this line makes the estimate 5 million yen more favorable to the build side every year.

The cost of parallel operation is another easily forgotten line. IPA's "User Guide for Successful System Rebuilding" cautions, regarding parallel operation—performing the same operations in both the current and the new system—that because it increases the burden on user departments, you should confirm how much of it is acceptable 16. The 3 million yen in the table assumes 60 main users doing duplicate data entry for 30 minutes every day for one month. If the number of people or the duration doubles, this line doubles too.

Variables that move the break-even point

The conclusions of the model case can flip with a change in just one assumption. The table below varies one variable at a time while keeping the others at baseline. The 5-year difference is "continuing with SaaS" minus "building in-house"; a positive value means building is cheaper.

What matters most is the number of users and the SaaS unit price—in other words, the SaaS annual cost itself. With 150 users, or at $40/month, building cannot catch up within 5 years under these conditions. In other words, for SaaS with a small annual cost, rebuilding it never pays for itself. It sounds obvious, but this check is often missing at approval meetings.

Exchange rates and price increases also matter. A 10-yen move in the dollar shifted the 5-year difference by about 8.6 million yen. Since the building side also pays cloud costs in dollars, it is affected by a weak yen too, though less than the SaaS side. However, whether the price increase was 0% or 10% per year, the conclusion that building wins within 5 years did not change under baseline conditions. Exchange rates and price increases are variables that shift the flip year by about one year. What flips the conclusion is the number of users, the unit price, and the reliability of the building side's estimates.

That estimate reliability is the second most influential variable. If development costs balloon to 1.5x and maintenance runs at 40% per year, building loses by 16.38 million yen even under baseline conditions. Both are common deviations in system development. Conversely, if development comes in at half, the gap widens to 70.62 million yen. I believe the figures submitted for approval should not be a single number but a range like this table: present three scenarios—baseline, optimistic, and pessimistic—and let management judge whether the pessimistic case is acceptable.

Another row in the table worth noting is the 240-user case with licenses cut by 20%. Building's 5-year advantage shrinks to 10.84 million yen, and the flip moves to Year 5. Simply taking inventory before building can change the decision. That said, some major SaaS base contracts include a clause that if you reduce quantities at renewal, prices are reset regardless of the previous unit price. You may cut seats intending to save, only to see the unit price rise so savings fall short—so review the contract first. This point is also covered in Part 4.

It is also safer to write exit conditions into the approval document. For example, a single line such as: "If development costs exceed 60% of budget at the four-month mark, renew the SaaS and stop building." The SaaS renewal date becomes the decision deadline. Build-or-buy is not a one-time decision; if you treat it as a judgment that can be revisited at each renewal, the weight of the approval process should feel a little lighter.

How this differs from contracted development

If you execute this plan with outside help, the table changes depending on whether you outsource to contracted development or proceed with an FDE (Forward Deployed Engineer—an engineer who works on-site with customers building software in their environment).

In contracted development, billable hours are revenue. Both the initial development row and the maintenance/update rows from Year 2 onward remain as payments to the vendor for the full 5 years. Because they build what the specification says, features that turn out to be unnecessary once built are usually built to completion anyway. From the buyer's perspective, you are entrusting the most uncertain items in the table—"development cost" and "maintenance rate"—to the vendor's effort estimates.

Our FDEs treat effort as an investment, not revenue. By showing a working prototype on day one and building while verifying which features are actually used, we avoid building unused features. Common mechanisms usable by other companies are returned to our product, while procedures unique to your company remain as your configuration. The completion condition is that your own staff can maintain it themselves. Restated in the table's terms: the goal is to shrink the development cost row and bring the maintenance row down to a size you can own in-house. How to structure the contract is described in the article on measuring outcomes and contracts.

What our FDEs can do

With our FDE offering, we start by rebuilding this article's estimate using your actual numbers. We verify contract renewal and notice dates, license counts versus actual login records, and which features are used and unused, replacing each placeholder in the table with real figures one by one.

The most uncertain item—development cost—is not decided by a desk estimate alone. We build a prototype using real data with personal information masked, confirm how much actually needs rebuilding, and only then turn it into a number. Having a prototype surfaces facts early—"we barely use this screen," "we can't do without this report"—which narrows the range of development cost. It is an approach that shrinks the second most influential variable in the sensitivity table first.

What we build is hosted in your cloud environment as a default, with a design that lets you choose the region and other details to match your requirements. Our practice and its limits

There are a few things I want to state honestly.

First, if the estimate says continuing with SaaS is better, we will tell you so. That applies when user counts are small, unit prices are low, or the work is complex and bound by regulations, as with accounting or payroll. Under these conditions, it is more advantageous to keep the SaaS and spend time on license inventory and renewal negotiations instead of building. Which kinds of work are worth rebuilding is covered in Part 2.

Next, the 25% maintenance rate and security costs in this article are not figures derived from our own five-year track record. We do not yet have actual data from operating business applications built as an FDE for five years. That is why we clearly state that the figures in the table are assumptions, and we have disclosed all the premises so readers can recalculate with their own numbers.

Also, we are a builder-side vendor. We are aware that we are in a position inclined toward the "build" conclusion. That is precisely why we set SaaS price increases conservatively and weighted the builder side with heavier maintenance, security, and personnel costs. If any line still feels too generous, make that line harsher and recalculate. If the conclusion changes, that is the answer for your company.

Finally, we are a small company, and the number of projects we can take on simultaneously is limited. The closer a SaaS renewal date is, the earlier you should contact us, so we can secure time for prototyping and estimation.

Summary

  • SaaS payments rise through two routes: dollar-denominated price increases and yen-denominated revisions. The annual average yen-dollar rate moved from 106.78 yen in 2020 to 158.78 yen in January–August 2026 (our calculation from the Bank of Japan's monthly averages)
  • Compare on a five-year total basis, not by lining up annual fees and development costs. For the builder side, include SaaS fees until termination, migration, parallel operation, maintenance and modifications, security, cloud, and in-house staff
  • In our hypothetical model case (300 people, $60 per month, 160 yen per dollar), building was 40.62 million yen cheaper over five years, with the cumulative crossover in year four
  • What flips the conclusion is the number of users and unit price, and the reliability of the builder-side estimate. Exchange rates and price increases only shifted the crossover year by about one year
  • In your internal approval document, write the criteria, the optimistic and pessimistic ranges, and the exit conditions, and set the SaaS renewal date as the decision deadline

Build or continue is not a question of which is correct. It is a question of which assumption, if it breaks, flips the result. Replace each line of the table with your company's own numbers, and you will see where the focus of the discussion lies.
