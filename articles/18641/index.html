What if you don't have FDE experience: build it in your current job

Original title: 没有 FDE 经验怎么办：在现在的工作中积累

FDE Times · 2026-10-14 · 5 min read

Summary

This guide argues that engineers without FDE experience can build it at their current job rather than switching roles first. It advises picking a recurring, poorly-defined problem nobody owns, interviewing the users who hit it to find the root cause, proposing options to non-technical stakeholders in business terms, shipping a fix, and measuring one before/after metric. A worked example uses recurring file-import failures at a warehouse software company. The article shows how to turn the work into a one-page outcome document and rewrite resume bullets to highlight ambiguity and stakeholder communication, and lists pitfalls like choosing projects too large or skipping documentation.

Key points

  • Ambiguous, undefined problems are core FDE work and exist at every company.
  • Pick a small recurring issue from support or ops queues, not a huge redesign.
  • Interview users first; the root cause often differs from the ticket text.
  • Present trade-offs to non-technical stakeholders in their own terms.
  • Document results with a measured number and rewrite resume bullets accordingly.

Key Takeaways

  • Vague problems with unclear requirements are the core of FDE work, and your current company almost certainly has some.
  • Pick a recurring problem, go to where users actually encounter it, and solve it end to end instead of waiting for tickets.
  • A concrete results document, plus a single resume line framed around a vague problem, carries more weight than any amount of storytelling.

You read a Forward Deployed Engineer (FDE) job description: work directly with customers, take on problems with no requirements documents, and decide for yourself what to build. Then you look at your own resume: three years of writing APIs from tickets broken down by a PM. The gap is real, but closing it doesn't require quitting your job first.

According to fde.academy, vague problems with unclear requirements are exactly what FDEs handle every day. That means experience handling vague problems counts no matter where you get it, including at your current company.

The accompanying advice is very concrete: before submitting your resume, proactively take on a project with unclear boundaries at your current job. This article explains how to pick that work, how to do it, and how to turn the results into something an interviewer can see.

Why You Should Actively Seek Out Vague Problems

Most engineers are trained to avoid ambiguity: if requirements are unclear, go back to the PM; if there are no acceptance criteria, don't start work. On a product team, that's a good habit.

FDEs are paid precisely to do the opposite: walk into territory no one has defined, figure out what the real problem is, and keep going until users have something usable in their hands.

The path Scaler describes for moving from backend engineer to FDE starts at your current company: first look internally for FDE or deployment-related work. MindStudio points out where FDE judgment comes from: only by staying close enough to customers and real workflows can you understand why a missing data field matters.

Both point to the same place. FDE experience exists where code meets users, and every company has such a place.

An Example: A Missing Field in an Import File

Suppose you're a backend engineer at a company that sells warehouse management software to enterprises. Every week, the customer support team forwards you a few tickets that say only one sentence: "The customer's file import failed." No team owns this problem, so each time someone patches it manually and moves on to other things.

This is exactly the kind of work MindStudio recommends starting with: a concrete problem that recurs in your current job. The failed-import tickets fit almost perfectly: it's vague enough, because no one knows what "failed" specifically means; it's small enough, because it revolves around a single process; and it touches real users.

The first step isn't opening the code—it's sitting down next to a support colleague. This set of interview questions can be used almost as-is:

You: When a customer last reported an import failure, what was the first thing you did? Support: Open the customer's file, compare it against the template, and find the column with the error. You: Which column fails most often? Support: The warehouse code column. Customers export from their old system, which doesn't have that column, so the whole file gets rejected. You: After a rejection, what does the customer do next? Support: They call us, or enter the rows manually one by one.

These three questions turn "import failed" into a concrete problem: a required field that's missing from customers' real data. This is exactly the understanding of missing data fields mentioned earlier, which you can't get just by reading tickets.

Next comes the technical decision. You could allow the field to be empty and assign a default warehouse, or build an interface that lets customers map columns themselves. Rather than deciding on your own, bring both options to the head of operations and explain them in their language:

"Option one can be fixed in a couple of days, but inventory might be recorded in the wrong warehouse. Option two takes two weeks, lets customers choose their own warehouse, and means they won't have to call support again. Which risk would you rather accept?"

Not once did you say "nullable" or "schema." The listener is choosing between two consequences they can understand, and what you're practicing is exactly one of the skills Scaler says you should develop now: explaining technical decisions to non-technical people.

Do It Yourself: Six Steps from Ticket to Resume

Step one: go find the work proactively instead of waiting to be assigned it. The support queue, the operations team's chat channel, the spreadsheet the sales team maintains itself: anywhere people repeatedly do manual work because the software falls short, there's a problem worth taking on.

Step two: get close to users, just like sitting next to support above. Step three: write the requirements yourself: describe the problem and the expected outcome in one sentence, then take it to your manager and ask to own it for a fixed period. You're writing requirements for something that has no requirements document—that itself is the practice.

Step four: present options to a non-technical decision-maker. Step five: ship the chosen solution, measure one number before and after, such as the number of these tickets per week, then organize everything into a results document. Step six: rewrite your resume around what you just did.

Turning Work into Evidence

A results document proves you can do FDE work, not just talk about it. In the example above, it could be a one-page document: how vague the original problem was, who you asked, what you found, which option you chose and why, and the measured results.

Add screenshots or a short demo, with sensitive data removed. A number you measured yourself becomes the backbone of the whole story in an interview.

As for the resume, Scaler's advice is to highlight every experience of facing real ambiguity and communicating directly with non-engineers. Put the two versions side by side and the difference is obvious at a glance:

Ticket-style resume

  • Developed CSV import functionality
  • Fixed a data validation bug
  • Wrote unit tests for the import module

FDE-style resume

  • Took ownership of a recurring import failure problem no team owned
  • Interviewed support and operations to find the root cause: a missing field in customer data
  • Presented two options to the head of operations, shipped the chosen one, and compared ticket counts before and after

The right-hand column doesn't mean you're better. It describes the same work, just putting the vague problem and non-engineers first—which are exactly the two things the earlier resume advice tells you to highlight.

Common Pitfalls

The most common pitfall is choosing work that's too big. "Redesign the entire customer onboarding process" sounds impressive, but it easily drags on, gets tangled in internal politics, and ends with no result you can clearly explain. A recurring problem confined to a single process is enough.

The second pitfall is solving a vague problem without meeting anyone. If you just read logs, guess the cause, and fix it, you're practicing debugging, not FDE skills. The most valuable part of the example above is the three questions you asked support, not the code.

The third pitfall is finishing without leaving a trace. In an interview six months later, you won't remember that number, or who said what. Write that one-page document in the week you finish, while every detail is still fresh.

The last pitfall is subtler: stepping into another team's territory without a heads-up. Proactively taking on vague work is different from unilaterally changing someone else's system. Send a short message to the team that owns the code and invite them to review it—that brings them to your side, and it's exactly the stakeholder management FDEs need every day.

This Week's Exercise

Pick just one problem you've seen reported at least twice in the past month. Write down your current understanding of the problem in one sentence, then rewrite that sentence after your first conversation with someone who has encountered it.

The bigger the difference between the two sentences, the clearer it becomes what FDEs do every day: turning what people complain about into a problem that can be solved.
