Today's Assessment

Today marked a milestone worth recording for China's FDE industry: Moonshot AI launched the Kimi Enterprise Partner "Moonshot Program," publicly assembling a Forward Deployed Engineer team with five listed IT service providers—the first time a domestic model vendor has explicitly written "FDE + partner network + reusable industry solutions" into its enterprise business mechanism.

Two complementary signals on the same day sketch the full picture: Tencent Cloud disclosed that in Fujian it has partnered with more than 800 partners serving over 50,000 customers (the regional ecosystem foundation is in place); OpenAI released the Agents API, turning long-running execution, tool calling, and execution environments into managed capabilities (the runtime foundation is rapidly standardizing).

Together, the three signals point to one conclusion: the unit of competition in enterprise AI is shifting from model capability to "who can organize partners, get on-site, complete production deployments, and turn results into reusable solutions." The foundation will become increasingly standardized, and on-site responsibility will become increasingly valuable.

Key Signals

Kimi's "Moonshot Program": First batch of five listed IT service providers to jointly build an FDE team

  • Sources: IT之家; Caixin
  • On September 10, Moonshot AI announced the launch of the Kimi Enterprise Partner "Moonshot Program," aimed at jointly building a Forward Deployed Engineer (FDE) team with IT service providers and integrators, with the goal of deploying AI into enterprises' core business. First-batch partners: Teamsun, Kingsoft Cloud, Yakang, AsiaInfo, and ChinaSoft International—covering software services, cloud computing, infrastructure, and telecommunications. Partners gain access to Kimi models, the Hosted Agents foundation, and engineering methodology support, and can consolidate project results into industry solutions.
  • Why it matters: This is a strong signal that FDE is moving from a job-title topic into vendors' channel and delivery systems—model vendors have realized that the hardest part of enterprise AI is not putting capabilities in the cloud, but having people go on-site: turning vague requirements into tasks, connecting agents into systems, containing risks through permissions and evals, and getting business owners to sign off. Five listed companies entering together also means FDE talent demand is about to see a wave of institutionalized scaling. Evaluate such programs with a seven-level evidence chain: partner signing → FDE headcount/certification → customers and tasks → production deployment → dual acceptance → solution reuse → revenue sharing—currently at level one; partner count only proves channel entry.

Tencent Cloud's Fujian ecosystem foundation: 800+ partners, 50,000+ customers

  • Source: Xinhua Net | Original link
  • At the Tencent Cloud Xiamen City Summit, it was disclosed that after eight years of deep cultivation in Fujian, Tencent Cloud has joined forces with more than 800 partners to serve over 50,000 Fujian customers; more than 60% of Fujian's top 100 private enterprises are Tencent Cloud customers. On the product side: WorkBuddy completed over 50 version iterations in its first six months, and the open platform introduced over 100 ecosystem partners in its first batch; a white paper on manufacturing agent practices was released at the same time.
  • Why it matters: The customer and partner foundation for regional markets is in place—but foundation scale and AI adoption depth are two different things. The 50,000 customers figure is a cumulative cloud services metric; active customers, production tasks, and business outcomes for AI products have not been disclosed. This gap is exactly where regional FDE service providers fit: major vendors provide platform capabilities and customer reach, while local service providers handle the on-site work of pressing capabilities into enterprise processes, organizing adoption, and delivering acceptance of results—converging with Kimi's partner logic.

OpenAI releases the Agents API: Agent runtime foundations accelerating standardization

  • Source: OpenAI official | Original link
  • On September 10, OpenAI released the Agents API: developers can create and run cloud agents with a single API call, choosing between OpenAI-managed sandboxes, their own infrastructure, or partner sandboxes. Among cited official customers, the logistics platform Nash said it runs thousands of long-running agents and manages hundreds of millions of deliveries.
  • Why it matters: Agent runtime foundations are rapidly standardizing—future FDE differentiation will no longer be "whether you can launch an agent," but whether you can define tasks, configure customer environments, restrict permissions, design evals, handle exceptions, and hand business results over to customers for sign-off. The contrast between the Chinese and international routes is clear: Kimi chose "models/Hosted Agents + partner FDEs," while OpenAI chose "managed sandboxes + developer ecosystem"—both are splitting delivery into two layers: the foundation and on-site responsibility. Service providers should position themselves at the on-site responsibility and industry asset layers, avoiding redundant construction that duplicates foundation vendors. Note: sandboxes reduce risk, but they do not automatically solve business authorization, data compliance, or accountability for erroneous actions.

Implications for FDE Practitioners

  • Keep the foundation replaceable; own your assets: Treat OpenAI's Agents API, Kimi's Hosted Agents, or domestic cloud platforms as interchangeable execution layers; business objects, task definitions, eval sets, logs, sign-off records, and industry knowledge assets must be held in portable form—when the foundation changes, the assets remain.
  • Evaluate vendor partner programs with an "admission checklist": model/agent foundation, customer channels, on-site permissions, training and certification, solution asset ownership, quality gates, incident liability, project allocation, revenue sharing, exit mechanisms—clarify all ten items one by one before deciding which ecosystem to join.
  • Regional service providers' position is at the "on-site responsibility layer": Major vendors have platforms and customer reach, but lack people who can go on-site. Rather than fighting the ecosystem, define clear complementary boundaries: platform capabilities + local FDE delivery, starting with joint validation on one real workflow.
  • A platform/product prototype is not operations: Any platform must pass a nine-level funnel from prototype to transaction—visit → registration → certification → posting real requirements → valid bids → contract/budget → delivery → acceptance and settlement → repeat purchase. Pages and features are entry-level capabilities; without contracts, sign-offs, and settlements, "matching" cannot be counted as a transaction outcome.
