FDE Leading Without Authority: How to Drive Customer Teams

Original title: FDE 无职权领导：如何推动客户团队

FDE Times · 2026-10-08 · 5 min read

Summary

This guide explains how FDEs drive change in client teams they have no authority over. It argues that an FDE's power is borrowed from a sponsor and must stay aligned with that person, that credibility comes from working deliverables rather than persuasion, and that presenting green/yellow/red graded options lets client engineers own the decision. A worked example covers an ETL job overwriting historical data, followed by common mistakes and advice on demonstrating this skill to employers.

Key points

  • An FDE's authority is borrowed from a sponsor and must stay aligned with them.
  • Credibility comes from working code, not slide decks.
  • Offer green/yellow/red options so the client team owns the decision.
  • Avoid bypassing engineers or letting the sponsor fade from view.
  • On resumes, describe a concrete case of leading change without authority.

Nobody on the customer's data team reports to you. To change their data pipeline, you rely on the sponsor who brought you in and the credibility you've built yourself.

Key Takeaways

  • Your authority at the customer comes from your sponsor, and you must stay aligned with this person at all times.
  • Credibility comes from deliverables that actually run; no amount of eloquence can substitute for it.
  • Split options into green, yellow, and red tiers, and let the customer's team make the final call, because people defend decisions they chose themselves.

In your second week on site, you discover that the customer data team's ETL jobs overwrite old records every night. As a result, the agent you're building has no historical data to work with when users ask questions. Fixing this requires the team to change its own data pipeline, and not a single person on that team reports to you.

Every Forward Deployed Engineer (FDE) runs into this situation sooner or later. Palantir's FDE job posting warns candidates up front: they will go deep inside customers, accept the mess that comes with it, and share in the consequences, in exchange for genuine ownership. But owning a product does not mean you can boss around another company's engineers.

Technical leadership at a customer site is therefore a distinct skill, and one that can be practiced. LeadDev puts it bluntly: leading without authority relies on influence, not power. The remaining questions are where that influence comes from and how to use it.

Your authority is borrowed

Will Larson made an observation about Staff+ engineers that applies just as well to FDEs. In his view, the organizational power you hold is lent to you by someone more senior. To keep it, you must stay tightly aligned with your sponsor, who is usually your manager.

Larson was talking about sponsors inside your own company, but the same logic applies on the customer side. There, the sponsor is usually the person who agreed to bring you in: the operations director, or the head of data.

When you say "this pipeline needs to change," the data team cooperates because they believe it's their sponsor's wish, and it has little to do with your abilities.

This has a practical consequence: if the direction you push isn't endorsed by your sponsor, you're spending authority nobody ever gave you. The first time might work. The second time, the engineers will go straight to their boss, and the borrowed authority will be revoked.

Credibility first, proposals second

Borrowed authority only gets you through the door. Whether engineers genuinely commit and see things through depends on credibility. LeadDev suggests building credibility by letting others see your knowledge and competence; for an FDE, the most compelling evidence is code that runs.

Harper's FDE job posting states the goal plainly: go deep into customers, build, deploy, and earn technical trust. Only once that trust exists can you ask them to change their architecture. In the first week, a small fix for a problem they're actively struggling with beats ten pages of proposal slides.

That said, technical credibility alone isn't enough. An article analyzing the echo/delta pattern points out: a team that only manages relationships, with no engineering team behind it to deliver, ultimately delivers nothing; while an engineering team lacking customer needs and momentum will build dazzling software the customer never actually uses.

An FDE must hold both ends.

Example: Letting the data team choose the pipeline fix

Back to that data-overwriting ETL job. Suppose you've already fixed a small bug in the data team's internal tool, and confirmed with your sponsor that the agent needs historical data. Only then do you bring the problem into the meeting room.

LeadDev recommends presenting options with a three-color rating: green means do it now, yellow means proceed with caution, red means stop and reconsider. Instead of saying "you must change the pipeline," you write three options on the whiteboard:

OptionColorMain risk
Add a daily snapshot table, leaving existing jobs untouchedGreenExtra storage cost
Change the job from overwrite to appendYellowReports reading the old table may break
Have the agent maintain its own historical cacheRedTwo sources of truth emerge, and data drifts out of sync over time

The conversation might go like this. You say: "The agent needs to know what a record's value was last week. Here are three approaches. You know this system better than I do—are these colors right?"

The head of data might reply: "The yellow one is actually green, because only two reports read that table." At that point, the decision is theirs.

This aligns with what Palantir expects of FDEs: making architecture and design decisions together with other engineers, not imposing them. You bring perspective and data; they bring knowledge of their own systems.

Five steps you can try yourself

  • Identify who your sponsor is, and directly ask what their priorities are this quarter.
  • Before asking anyone to change anything, deliver something small that solves a real pain point.
  • Write the problem up as green, yellow, and red options, with a one-sentence risk note for each.
  • Ask the customer's engineers to correct the color of each option.
  • Regularly sync progress with every relevant level.

The last step is the most easily overlooked. Palantir describes the FDE as the person who maintains relationships across levels: from frontline users who work with the system daily, to the executives making decisions; LeadDev likewise treats open, transparent, regular communication as essential.

Mistakes that cost an FDE their influence

The most common mistake is treating your title at your own company as power. The customer's engineers owe you nothing, and a proposal that opens with your company's name sounds more like an imposition than a collaboration.

The second mistake is going over the engineering team's head straight to leadership. You might win a decision, but you'll lose the people who have to operate the system after you leave.

The third mistake is letting your sponsor fade from view. Their priorities have shifted, yet you're still pushing the old direction with authority that no longer has anyone behind it. The final mistake is doing only half the job: maintaining relationships without delivering, or delivering well with nobody who needs it.

How to demonstrate this ability to employers

If you're targeting FDE roles, read carefully the lines in job descriptions that mention stakeholders, ownership, or technical trust. This is exactly where employers assess this ability.

Don't write "strong communication skills" on your resume. Write about a time you got a team that didn't report to you to change its design: which options you proposed, which one they chose, and what happened after deployment. A story like that carries more weight than any adjective.

The hardest part of leading without authority is accepting that someone else's name goes on the decision. The more willing you are to give up the credit, the more likely the systems you leave behind will have someone willing to take ownership of them.
