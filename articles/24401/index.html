What Is AI-Driven Development? How to Proceed and Ground Rules for Team Use

Original title: AI駆動開発とは｜進め方と、チームで使うときの決めごと

Summary

This article explains AI-driven development, where AI produces artifacts from requirements through code and tests while humans convey intent, approve plans, and verify results—distinct from AI-assisted and fully autonomous approaches. It draws on AWS's AI-DLC methodology (announced July 2025, with Inception, Construction, and Operation phases and short 'bolt' cycles replacing sprints), Stack Overflow and DORA survey data showing high adoption but low trust, and a METR experiment where AI slowed experienced developers by 19%. The author proposes five practical steps and six team agreements, plus three questions for clients commissioning AI-driven development work.

AI-driven development is an approach that puts AI at the center of the development process: AI produces the deliverables from requirements organization through design, code, and testing, while humans shift to communicating intent, approving plans, and verifying results. It differs from using AI for code completion in where human work sits. Rather than humans writing and AI assisting, AI creates and humans decide.

One trigger for the term spreading was AI-DLC (AI-Driven Development Life Cycle), announced by AWS on July 31, 2025. The Japanese translation appeared on the AWS blog on August 8, 2025. However, the scope of "AI-driven development" varies by company and person. Here, drawing on AWS's methodology, developer surveys, and the official documentation of AI coding tools, I organize what AI-driven development involves and what teams should decide when adopting it.

One reason FDEs (Forward Deployed Engineers) can build quickly at customer sites is also this approach. The role itself is explained in What is an FDE? The meaning and work of a Forward Deployed Engineer.

What is AI-driven development? How it differs from AI-assisted development

AWS's AI-DLC announcement divides the use of AI in software development into two types. One is AI-assisted, where AI enhances specific tasks such as documentation, code completion, and testing. The other is AI-autonomous, where AI is expected to build entire applications without human intervention based on user requirements. AWS writes that both have produced results that cannot be called optimal in terms of development speed and software quality, and that AI-DLC was created to address these challenges.

At the core of AI-DLC is the idea that AI executes and humans oversee. AI creates detailed work plans, asks questions to align intent, and delegates important decisions to humans. Only after human verification does AI proceed to implementation. AWS describes this flow as running through three phases: Inception, Construction, and Operation.

Phase (AI-DLC)What AI doesWhat humans do
InceptionBreaks business intent into requirements, stories, and units of workThe whole team verifies AI's questions and proposals (mob elaboration)
ConstructionProposes logical architecture, domain models, code, and testsClarifies technical decisions and architecture choices on the spot (mob construction)
OperationUses the context of previous phases and handles Infrastructure as Code and deploymentOversees

AWS states that AI stores plans, requirements, and design deliverables in the project repository, retaining context across phases. Conventional sprints are replaced by short work cycles called "bolts," measured in hours or days rather than weeks.

In my view, the difference between the three approaches can be organized by who advances the work and who decides.

AI-driven development is not about leaving everything to AI. It is an approach where the volume of work is handed to AI, while humans keep hold of judgment and verification.

Does AI make development faster? What the survey numbers say

Many people already use AI in development. In Stack Overflow's 2025 developer survey, 84% answered that they use or plan to use AI tools in development, up from 76% the previous year. Google's DORA "State of AI-assisted Software Development," published on September 23, 2025, reported from a survey of about 5,000 technology professionals worldwide that 90% of respondents use AI at work and over 80% believe their productivity has increased.

On the other hand, trust in the output is low. In the same Stack Overflow survey, developers who do not trust the accuracy of AI tools (46%) outnumbered those who do (33%). The most common complaint, cited by 66%, was "AI solutions that are almost right, but not quite." In DORA's survey as well, 30% answered that they trust little or none of the code AI produces.

On speed itself, there are also contrary results. On July 10, 2025, the research organization METR published the results of a randomized controlled trial in which 16 experienced open-source developers worked on 246 real issues in their own repositories. Issues where AI use was allowed took 19% longer to complete. Developers had predicted beforehand that AI would make them 24% faster, and even after finishing, they felt they had been 20% faster. METR notes this is a snapshot of AI capabilities as of early 2025 in a single setting.

The central idea of DORA's report is that AI does not fix teams; it amplifies what is already there. AI adoption showed a positive relationship with software delivery throughput and product outcomes, but a negative relationship with delivery stability persisted. DORA explains that without mechanisms such as automated testing, mature version control, and fast feedback, the volume of changes increases and things become unstable.

In my view, what follows from this is that whether AI-driven development makes you faster is determined not by the tools but by whether you have mechanisms to verify AI output quickly. The faster production gets, the more verification becomes the bottleneck.

How to do AI-driven development: 5 steps

Translating AWS's AI-DLC and the recommendations of Claude Code's official documentation "Best practices for Claude Code" into team procedures yields the following five steps. The ordering is my own organization.

1. Put intent into words

AI fills in what is not written with guesses. Write down what to build, what not to build, and the conditions for completion first. This is why, in the Inception phase of AI-DLC, business intent is broken down into requirements and units of work. How to define requirements is summarized in Requirements definition for AI development.

2. Have AI write the plan, and humans verify it

Don't let it write code right away. The Claude Code documentation notes that if you let it write code immediately, it may produce code that solves the wrong problem, and recommends proceeding in the order of exploration, planning, and implementation. For this purpose, it provides plan mode (a mode where the AI reads files and answers questions but makes no changes). AI-DLC also centers on a flow where the AI creates a plan, which is verified by a human before implementation. If a human stops things at the planning stage, less time is spent reading code that goes in the wrong direction.

3. Provide verification the AI can check itself

The Claude Code documentation lists giving the AI means to verify its own work as the first priority. If there is something that returns a pass/fail result—tests, build exit codes, linters, screenshots of the screen—the AI can run its own loop of working, verifying, reading the results, and fixing. Without such means, humans must play the role of verification, and every mistake waits to be noticed by a person. The same documentation also says not to ship if you cannot verify.

4. Review with a different set of eyes

Look at the diff with eyes that lack the context of the person who made it. The Claude Code documentation recommends a procedure where, before finishing work, you have a subagent with fresh context review the diff and report what is missing. However, it also cautions that a reviewer told to find missing points tends to report something even when there are no problems, and chasing everything leads to over-engineering. Decide in advance to pick up only feedback related to correctness and requirements.

5. Record decisions in instruction files

To avoid explaining the same things every time, record decisions in files. In Claude Code, CLAUDE.md is read at the start of every conversation. The documentation warns to keep it short and readable; if it is too long, important rules get buried and half of it is ignored.

Rules for team use

If you use it individually, you can use tools according to your own preferences. In a team, AI instructions and the depth of verification differ from person to person, and code of inconsistent quality ends up in the same repository. In my view, you should decide the following six things first.

What to decideExample contentWhere to put it
Common instructions for the AIBuild and test procedures, coding conventions, design rulesCLAUDE.md for Claude Code, .github/copilot-instructions.md for GitHub Copilot
Definition of doneWhich tests must pass to be complete, what humans verify by eyeInstruction files and pull request templates

Instruction files can be put in the repository and shared. The Claude Code documentation positions project CLAUDE.md as something to share with the team through version control, and recommends keeping it focused on project standards without personal preferences. GitHub also allows you to write instructions for Copilot that apply to the whole repository via copilot-instructions.md placed in the repository's .github directory.

DORA's report lists, as places for organizations to start, clarifying and widely sharing AI policy, connecting AI to internal context, and strengthening safety nets. The table above can be seen as those three points scaled down to the team level.

The scope the AI may touch and information that must not be included are also security issues. My thinking on permissions granted to AI agents is written in AI agent security, and how to create internal usage policies in How to create internal usage policies for generative AI.

AI-driven development from the client's perspective

AI-driven development is not irrelevant for companies that outsource development. If a contractor builds with AI, speed may increase, but as DORA's research shows, without a verification system stability suffers. In my view, it is good to ask the following three questions before placing an order.

  • With which tests and reviews is AI-generated code verified?
  • Are instruction files for the AI and design records included in the deliverables?
  • To which tools, and with which settings, will the company's code and materials be passed?

The second is especially important if you are considering bringing development in-house. If instruction files and records remain, your own team can take over the same approach. How to divide work between in-house and outsourcing is summarized in AI development: in-house or outsource to a development company?

AI-driven development is not something that ends with installing tools. Write down intent, verify plans, automate verification, and record decisions. In my view, the more a team builds this unglamorous machinery first, the better it can carry AI's speed all the way to production.
