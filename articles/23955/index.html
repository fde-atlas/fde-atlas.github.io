What's the Difference Between FDE and SIer? A Practical Comparison of Contracts, Skills, Career Moves, and How to Choose

Original title: FDEとSIerの違いとは？契約・スキル・転職と使い分けを実務比較

FDE総研 · 2026-09-08 · 15 min read

Summary

This Japanese article compares Forward Deployed Engineers (FDEs) with SIer (system integrators), arguing the essential difference is what counts as value: SIers deliver systems matching agreed specifications, while FDEs are judged by whether customer business metrics actually improve. FDE, a role defined by Palantir, builds customer-specific work on a reusable in-house product, with revenue tied to subscriptions and renewals; SIers work on fixed-scope, person-month contracts. The author details how these models shape scope management, incentives, skills, and evaluation, offers advice for SIer engineers moving into FDE roles, and proposes that companies use FDEs for exploratory phases and SIers for stable, large-scale operations.

Key points

  • The dividing line is who defines the problem: FDEs discover it on-site; SIers receive defined requirements.
  • FDE contracts center on product usage fees and renewals; SIer contracts on person-month deliverables.
  • FDE skills span four layers: business understanding, data modeling, implementation, and adoption.
  • For career moves, a concrete story of changing one customer workflow beats large-project scale.
  • Companies should pair both: FDEs for pilots and exploration, SIers for production-grade operations.

What is the essential difference between an FDE and an SIer in the first place?

The essential difference between an FDE and an SIer lies in whether the deliverable is a system built to spec, or results achieved in the customer's business.

FDEs (Forward Deployed Engineers) and SIers (system integrators) both look like jobs that "deliver software for customer companies." But where they place value differs. For an SIer, value comes from meeting agreed requirements; for an FDE, value comes from moving the customer's business metrics. The differences in contracts, skills, and evaluation covered later all stem from this single point. If you'd like a video explanation, see FDE seminar video (What is an FDE? How it differs from consulting and system development).

What kind of role is an FDE (Forward Deployed Engineer)?

The FDE is a role defined by Palantir, where one person handles everything from problem definition to implementation and adoption at the customer's site.

When deploying its own products, Gotham and Foundry, Palantir doesn't stop at selling the software. It has built a structure that "forward deploys" engineers to customers' field sites. FDEs observe the customer's operations, design data models, and build workflows on top of the company's product. In Japan too, SaaS and AI companies have recently begun creating similar roles under names like "FDE," "solutions engineer," and "implementation engineer."

One thing to note is that an FDE is not a "resident version of a contract developer." FDEs basically work on top of a reusable asset—their own product—and build only the customer-specific differences. As a result, the scope one FDE covers is broad, and speed in decision-making is required.

How is the role of an SIer (system integrator) defined?

An SIer is a business that undertakes everything from requirements definition to design, development, and operations based on a contract, and delivers a system.

Japanese SIers developed as outsourcing partners for customers' IT departments. Their strengths lie in process management in areas requiring large scale, long duration, and high reliability, and in fulfilling accountability through documentation. In areas like financial core banking and public systems, where "never going down" and "withstanding audits" are top priorities, the SIer structure remains rational today.

On the other hand, the multi-layered prime contractor/subcontractor structure and person-month-based estimation can make it hard to respond to change. Processes built on the assumption that requirements are fixed are a poor fit for projects where the requirements themselves must be discovered. It's more accurate to see this not as a problem of SIer capability, but as a problem of the behavior that contracts and processes demand.

Where is the dividing point between FDEs and SIers?

The dividing line is "who defines the problem." In SIer work, the customer defines it; in FDE work, the engineer defines it in the field.

As a practitioner, what hits hardest is the bottom row of the table. SIer sites surely know the frustration of "we delivered it, but no one uses it." An FDE is easier to understand if you see it as a role designed to eliminate that frustration along with its structure.

How do contracts and revenue models differ between FDEs and SIers?

FDE contracts center on product usage fees, while SIer contracts center on labor hours and deliverables. This is the biggest difference.

This isn't just a difference in sales methods. Because the incentive structure of "what engineers do to generate profit" changes, even day-to-day decision criteria differ between FDEs and SIers.

How do contract types and billing mechanisms differ between FDEs and SIers?

FDEs center on product usage fees and implementation support costs, while SIers center on fixed-price (contract for work) or quasi-mandate contracts.

ItemFDE modelSIer model
Main contractProduct usage fee (annual, etc.) + implementation and accompanying support costsFixed-price contract (deliverable responsibility) / quasi-mandate contract (duty of due professional care)
Estimation unitContract amount and usage scope per customerPerson-months, labor hours
Condition for revenue growthUsage becomes established, leading to renewals and expanded usageDevelopment scale expands / maintenance and operations become long-term
Position of additional developmentCan be returned to all customers as product featuresAdditional estimates for individual projects
Cancellation / lost deal riskEvaluated annually at renewalRelatively stable within the contract period

For an FDE, contract renewal is effectively their report card. You're evaluated not on "whether you built it," but on "whether the customer judged it worth paying for again next year." Piling up working hours doesn't count as results. Conversely, at SIers, working hours translate directly into revenue, so efforts to finish quickly with a small team aren't necessarily rewarded.

How does the philosophy of scope management differ between FDEs and SIers?

FDEs narrow the scope to deliver value quickly; SIers fix the scope to prevent deviation. The thinking runs in opposite directions.

Under a SIer's fixed-price contract, ambiguous scope translates directly into the risk of running at a loss. That is why freezing requirements and controlling changes through a change management process is the correct behavior for a SIer. An FDE, on the other hand, does not build the whole thing from the start. They first narrow the focus to a single business process, a single screen, or a single decision, deliver something that works, and then decide the next scope based on what they learned.

My impression is that accidents tend to happen when only the people are moved without understanding this difference. If you bring the SIer discipline of "we can't start because the specs aren't finalized" into an FDE setting, the work stalls. If you bring the "build it first and show them" approach into a SIer setting, it can constitute a breach of contract. The starting point is understanding that neither is superior; the behavior demanded by the contract is simply different.

How do incentive structures change day-to-day decisions for FDEs versus SIers?

Since user adoption is the FDE's revenue source, they decide to pull out quickly. Since billable hours are the SIer's revenue, completion tends to take priority.

Even when faced with the same fact—"this feature isn't being used"—the decisions diverge. For an FDE, stopping development and moving to a different business process is a justified decision, because time spent on an implementation that won't contribute to contract renewal is a direct loss. For a SIer, abandoning an agreed-upon scope midway would require a contract change, so it is more rational to complete the work first and then move on to the next proposal.

Reaction to the same factFDESIer
Feature is not being usedStop building it and move to another business processComplete the agreed scope, then move on to the next proposal
Basis for the decisionWill it contribute to contract renewal?Does it require a contract change?

This is not a difference in individual engineers' attitudes. It should be seen as the revenue model shaping day-to-day decision-making.

How do the required skills and working styles differ between FDEs and SIers?

FDEs need the breadth to handle business understanding and implementation on their own, while SIers need depth within their phase and management capability.

The difference in skills is not about "which is more advanced," but about "how much of the process one person covers alone." Below, I will organize, in order, the FDE skill composition, how far the strengths of SIer alumni carry over, and the differences in evaluation metrics.

What skill set is required of an FDE?

What an FDE needs is the drive to see everything through: business interviews, data modeling, implementation, and adoption support.

FDE skills are easiest to organize when viewed as the following four layers.

  • Business understanding: Observe the customer's operations on-site and articulate who makes what decisions and when.
  • Data modeling: Structure scattered ledgers, core systems, sensor data, and so on into a form usable for decision-making.
  • Implementation: Independently handle whatever is needed, from configuring and extending the company's own product to API integrations and front-end development.
  • Adoption: Get on-site users to actually use it, embed it into operations, make the impact visible, and turn that into contract renewal.

Of these four layers, the one least developed in a SIer career is usually the fourth: "adoption." The dividing line is not technical skill. It is whether you can consider "persisting until it gets used" to be part of your job.

How far do SIer alumni's skills carry over into FDE work?

Requirements gathering, quality management, and stakeholder coordination cultivated at a SIer become highly valued strengths in FDE work as well.

The upper half of the table represents assets that transfer directly to FDE work. The lower half should not be discarded; rather, the situations in which they are used change. For example, documentation doesn't become unnecessary. It just takes a different form: not thick design documents, but short handover materials that the next person in charge and the on-site team will read.

How do working styles and evaluation metrics differ between FDEs and SIers?

FDEs are evaluated on customer usage, while SIers are evaluated on how well they meet deadlines, quality standards, and budgets. {{34} Perspective | FDE | SIer

PerspectiveFDESIer
Main evaluation metricsCustomer usage and contract renewalAchievement of deadlines, quality, and budget
Rhythm of workShort cycles alternating between on-site observation and implementationProgress divided by project phase
Poorly matched inclinationsWorking styles focused solely on code eleganceStarting to build before agreement is reached

An FDE's day tends to be a series of back-and-forth cycles. Spend the morning at the customer's site, implement what you saw that afternoon, and have users try it the following week. As a result, it is hard to secure blocks of uninterrupted implementation time. For people whose professional identity is built on deep technical expertise, this can be the biggest gap with FDE work.

What is needed to move from a SIer to an FDE role?

What is essential for the career move is being able to concretely describe experiences where you defined a problem yourself and carried it through to implementation.

What is needed is not certifications or a specific tech stack, but evidence that you have practiced the FDE way of working at least once. I will organize this around four points: what interviewers evaluate, the gaps to fill, the order of transition, and how to assess job openings.

What do FDE interviews focus on?

In FDE interviews, being able to concretely describe firsthand experience of changing a customer's business tends to matter more than matching tech stacks.

What FDE hiring managers want to know can be roughly summarized in three points.

  • Experience defining problems: Can you explain a problem you identified yourself on-site, rather than one given to you as a requirement?
  • End-to-end experience: How far did you go with your own hands, from interviews through implementation, release, and checking usage?
  • Decisions to pull out or pivot: When things didn't go well, on what basis did you change direction, and how?

If you have fallen into the habit of talking about "scale" from your SIer days, conversations may not connect. In FDE interviews, the evaluation tends to invert: a story about changing one customer business process with a small team is valued more highly than a story about handling a subsystem on a large project.

What gaps should be filled before moving from a SIer to an FDE role?

There are mainly two gaps to fill: the technical touch that dulled while away from implementation, and the habit of articulating results in numbers.

GapHow to fill itWhy it matters for FDE
Hands-on feelGet to the point where you can independently handle SQL, API integrations, and simple front-end implementationFDEs work with business data, so data-processing fundamentals are especially important
Ability to express results as metricsTake stock of which business numbers you affected, such as processing time, cases handled, and error ratesEvaluation is tied to usage and contract renewals
Product thinkingBuild the habit of asking "which parts would apply to other customers too"FDE implementations lead to feature proposals for the core product

When taking stock of your results, reduce the granularity within the bounds of your confidentiality obligations and prepare it in a form you can talk about. The more your work centered on design and management, the more regaining your hands-on implementation feel first will change your persuasiveness in interviews.

How should the transition steps from SIer to FDE be structured?

The realistic approach is to practice FDE-like ways of working while still at the SIer, then move over with that track record.

Rather than changing jobs abruptly, proceeding in the following order has higher repeatability.

  • At your current job, deliberately increase opportunities to visit customer sites.
  • Propose small improvements that are not formal requirements yourself, and carry them through to implementation.
  • Write up that one project in the form of "problem → hypothesis → implementation → result."

Whether or not you have that one project dramatically changes the persuasiveness of your resume, because it directly provides material for answering the three interview perspectives (problem definition, end-to-end execution, and withdrawal judgment).

How can you discern the reality behind FDE job postings?

FDE job titles are not standardized. Discern the reality by who defines the problems and whether the company has its own product.

FDE-like roles may exist under titles such as "Solution Architect," "Implementation Engineer," or "Customer Engineer." Conversely, some jobs titled FDE are actually close to on-site contracted development. In interviews, always confirm "who defines the problems" and "does the company have its own product." If these two remain vague when you join, the reality will diverge greatly from the working style you expected.

Question to askAnswer close to FDEAnswer close to contracted on-site work

How should companies allocate work between FDEs and SIers?

There is one criterion for the split: whether the problem is already defined, or needs to be discovered from scratch.

FDEs and SIers are not competitors; the right fit changes depending on the project phase. Let's look in turn at suitability by project type, division of labor when using both, and points to confirm before contracting.

Which projects suit the FDE model, and which suit the SIer model?

If requirements are fixed and large scale or high reliability is needed, the SIer model fits; if you are still searching for requirements, the FDE model fits.

Project natureSuitable setupReason
Core system renewal or regulatory complianceSIer modelRequirements are clear, and process management and quality assurance carry heavy weight
Exact reproduction or migration of existing operationsSIer modelSpecifications exist as the existing system, and deviation is not allowed
On-site inefficiency is vagueFDE modelProblem definition itself is the first task
Data utilization benefits are unverifiedFDE modelYou can build small, verify, and feed into investment decisions
Pilot before company-wide rolloutFDE modelLearning can be iterated with a small team and short cycles
Uninterrupted, always-on stable operation is a prerequisiteSIer model (plus an operations setup)Organizational guarantees for incident response and maintenance are required

Reducing the six rows of the table to a single question: if the problem can be written as a specification, use the SIer model; if not, use the FDE model.

When using FDEs and SIers together, how should roles be divided?

If using both, the key division is to entrust the exploration phase to the FDE and production operation of established processes to the SIer.

In actual companies, combined use is more common than a binary choice between FDE and SIer. First, the FDE goes on-site and validates hypotheses about the business; only what proves "effective company-wide" is put into stable operation by the SIer or the in-house IT department. If this division works, you can achieve both speed of exploration and robustness of operations.

A common failure is applying SIer-style contracts and schedules to the exploration phase. This tends to unfold as: six months pass just writing the requirements document, and by the time it is finished the premises have changed. Conversely, running areas that form the company-wide backbone with a small FDE-style team increases the risks of over-dependence on individuals and operational breakdown.

From the perspective of a business company like VACAN that handles real-time on-site data, gaps between what is happening on-site and the business flows the system assumes are constantly arising. The first to spot those gaps is not a document but a person standing on-site. The raison d'être of the FDE role lies in the fact that the person who finds the gap has the authority and technical skill to fix it right away. When designing your setup, I consider this a more important point than contract structure.

What checkpoints should the ordering party confirm before contracting an FDE or SIer?

Before contracting, the ordering party needs to agree on "who defines the problems" and "how results will be measured."

  • Make explicit whether problem definition is done by the ordering party or delegated to the receiving side
  • Consider whether success metrics can be set as business metrics rather than deliverables
  • Narrow the initial scope to a range where effects are visible within a few months
  • Decide in advance the setup and handover method for moving to production operation after validation
  • Secure time for key on-site people to participate in discussions

The last item is often overlooked, but it most determines the success or failure of the FDE model. If the site is too busy for anyone to let you observe, calling in an FDE or an SIer will make no difference to the outcome.
