Can You Become an FDE with No Prior Experience? The Conclusion

In short, while a direct transition from "complete inexperience" (zero implementation and zero customer-facing experience) is difficult, transitioning from an adjacent role is entirely realistic. FDE (Forward Deployed Engineer) is a relatively new role with a small talent pool, and companies are expanding their potential-based hiring. "Inexperienced" here means "never having held the FDE title"—if you have practical experience as a software engineer, SIer, sales engineer, consultant, or customer success professional, that is a solid foundation.

The essence of an FDE is "being able to implement things yourself while structuring the customer's business challenges and driving them to completion." In other words, the prerequisite is already having either "implementation experience" or "customer-facing experience." Having both is ideal, but even starting from one, you can get there by systematically filling in the missing side. The definition of the role itself is covered in detail in What Is an FDE?, and the differences from adjacent roles in FDE vs. Software Engineer and FDE vs. Consultant.

The Prerequisites: Either Implementation Experience or Customer-Facing Experience

An FDE is a role that runs on two wheels: "technical implementation skills" and "customer-facing skills." When starting from scratch, the first thing to do is to accurately identify which wheel you already have and which one you lack. Trying to build both from zero at the same time is a roundabout approach. The standard approach is to make your strength your foundation and fill the gap as quickly as possible. An overview of the required skills is summarized in Skills Required for an FDE.

Implementation Experience: Can You Build Something That Works Yourself?

An FDE doesn't "write design documents and have others build from them"—instead, they write code with their own hands at the customer's site, connect APIs, and deliver something that works. What's required is not elegant architecture but the implementation ability to "just get it working, no matter what." Experience building simple tools in Python, developing business systems, or integrating APIs in personal projects—whatever the form, if you have ever "completed something that works from scratch on your own," that forms the foundation of implementation experience. If you have no implementation experience at all, start here.

Customer-Facing Experience: Can You Structure Problems and Secure Buy-In?

An FDE listens to a customer's vague requests, structures the real problems, prioritizes them, secures stakeholder buy-in, and moves things forward. Proposals, requirements definition, negotiation, expectation management, progress reporting—these skills come naturally from work in sales, consulting, presales, customer success, and contract development. For pure engineers who "understand technology but have never spoken with people outside the company," this is the biggest area for growth.

Ownership of Driving the Business: Can You Take Responsibility for Results?

What decisively sets FDEs apart from other engineering roles is that they are evaluated not on "whether they built it" but on "whether it was used and produced results." The ownership to take responsibility until the implementation is adopted in the field and the customer's business metrics or revenue actually move—this is a matter of mindset rather than job title, and even beginners can start developing it today. This ownership is also heavily probed in interviews (see: Common FDE Interview Questions).

Self-Assessment: Which Wheel Is Stronger for You?

Try writing down two things: "What is the most recent deliverable where I did the most hands-on work?" and "How much experience do I have securing buy-in from people outside my team or department?" If the former is stronger, implementation is your strength (your gap is customer-facing); if the latter is stronger, customer-facing is your strength (your gap is implementation). This assessment will be the starting point for determining which route to take in the roadmaps by background in the next chapter.

Roadmaps by Background

The fastest route is to build on the strengths of your current career and fill in the missing elements. Here we present strengths, gaps, and concrete action plans for six representative backgrounds. Start with the one closest to your own situation. The common goal for every background is to "create a track record of taking something beyond a PoC into production where customers (internal customers count) actually used it."

From Software Engineer: Building Customer-Facing Experience

Your strength is implementation skills. Your gaps are client engagement and requirements definition. The fastest path is to gain experience treating other departments in your company as "internal customers": interview them about their needs, define requirements, build tools, have them use them, and support them until adoption. Raising your hand for projects involving client interaction or shadowing pre-sales is also effective. On the technical side, mastering LLM API usage and RAG construction through personal projects will dramatically boost your immediate value. If you can fill the gap of "can write code but has little experience facing customers," you can become one of the candidates closest to an FDE. For a summary of the differences, see The Difference Between FDE and Software Engineer.

From SIer/SE: Add product thinking and AI skills

You have foundations in both client-facing work and implementation, making you the group that can transition most easily. You have already experienced requirements definition, client engagement, system integration, and on-site adoption—the very daily work of an FDE. There are two things to fill in: (1) shifting from a contractor mindset (building what you're told) to an in-house product mindset (delivering value through a product), and (2) adding AI/LLM skills. With these additions, you become a strong candidate who understands both technology and customers. The key is whether you can update the SIer culture mindset of "waterfall and multi-layer subcontracting" to the product company mindset of "ship fast and validate."

From Sales Engineer/Pre-sales: Add implementation skills

Giving demos in front of customers, answering technical questions, and backing up proposals with technology—pre-sales/sales engineer experience maps almost directly to an FDE's client-facing ability. What's missing is the implementation ability to "build and run it yourself after the contract." Since many can create demos but have little production implementation experience, build and complete a small product of your own using Python, API integration, and LLMs. Transitions that deepen the role one step from pre-sales (before the sale) to FDE (building and delivering after the sale) have been increasing notably in recent years. For how job titles relate, the Solution Engineer Jobs page is also a helpful reference.

From Consulting/Pre-sales: Bridge problem definition with implementation

Structuring problems, hypothesis building, stakeholder alignment, and presenting to executives are your strengths. By learning the missing implementation skills (Python, APIs, LLMs) and building a track record of completing something that works—even if small—you can become a rare professional who "can talk strategy and also get their hands dirty." Consulting tends to be a role of "propose and leave," whereas an FDE differs in that they "build what they proposed themselves and see it through to adoption." Understanding this difference and whether you can cross the implementation gap is the deciding factor. For details, see The Difference Between FDE and Consultant.

From Customer Success: Add technical depth

Continuously accompanying customers, expanding usage, and preventing churn—Customer Success experience is very close to the FDE role of "keeping the product in use after deployment." What's missing is technical depth. Going one step beyond changing product settings to being able to implement API integrations, data integration, and feature extensions using LLMs yourself will let you cross the boundary between CS and FDE. The challenge is filling the gap of "understands the customer's business better than anyone but can't build it themselves." The mindset of expanding usage after deployment (expansion) directly becomes one of the evaluation criteria for FDEs.

From Infrastructure/Data Roles: Expand into client engagement and application implementation

Infrastructure engineers, SREs, and data engineers already possess the cloud, data pipeline, and operations foundations that are essential for generative AI adoption. Since RAG and LLM applications ultimately succeed or fail based on data preparation and operations, this foundation is powerful. Two things are missing: (1) implementation at the application layer and LLM usage, and (2) experience listening to customers' problems and reaching agreement in front of them. If you can step out from behind the scenes into a role of "going to the customer's site and delivering value," you can build a unique position as a data-savvy FDE.

Skills to Acquire: The Two Wheels of Technology and People Skills

The skills required of an FDE are the two wheels of "technical skills" and "interpersonal/business skills." If you're aiming for this from scratch, prioritize filling whichever side you lack. Here we explain the five areas you should especially master. For a comprehensive list, see Skills Required for FDE.

Python and API Integration (The Foundation of Implementation)

The core implementation language for FDEs is Python, because the generative AI ecosystem (LLM libraries, data processing, automation) is most mature there. Start by being able to write Python scripts, call external APIs to fetch and process data, and return results—all with your own hands. In addition, being able to integrate with other systems using REST/Webhooks, handle cloud (AWS/GCP/Azure) basics, and use Git and simple deployments will give you the foundational strength of an FDE who can "just make it work" in the customer's environment.

LLM, RAG, and Evaluation (Eval) Design

This is the area with the highest market value for FDEs in the generative AI space: building applications with LLM APIs, implementing RAG (Retrieval-Augmented Generation) that references internal data, prompt design, and designing evaluations (Evals) to measure output quality. RAG and evaluation in particular are the core of solving the "PoC works but quality falls short in production" problem, and being able to implement these makes you a rare professional overnight. The fastest way to deepen your understanding of LLM implementation patterns and limitations is through official documentation from each provider (OpenAI, Anthropic, etc.) combined with hands-on verification. For why PoCs fail in production, see Why AI PoCs Fail in Production.

System Integration and Data Integration

The ability to connect AI with a customer's existing systems (core systems, CRM, databases, various SaaS) is frequently tested in real FDE work: data retrieval, cleansing, and integration; handling authentication and permissions; embedding into existing workflows—the skill here is bridging the gap between "a demo that works in your own closed environment" and "a system that works in the customer's complex production environment." In practice, most of the time spent on generative AI adoption goes to surrounding integration and data preparation rather than the AI model itself.

Requirements Definition and Problem Structuring

Customers arrive in a vague state of "we want to do something with AI." The FDE draws out the problems that truly need solving, structures them, prioritizes them, and narrows them down to what is feasible. Rather than "building what you're told," the upstream work of an FDE is "defining what should be built." Understanding the customer's business processes, discerning where AI will and won't help, and agreeing on a realistic scope—this upstream capability greatly determines whether you can deliver value in production.

Client Engagement, Expectation Management, and English

Interviewing stakeholders, managing expectations, sharing progress, explaining issues when they arise, and articulating value to management. FDEs are tested on their ability to translate technology into "the language of business." Since generative AI is not omnipotent, honesty in candidly communicating "what it can and cannot do" to build trust is also important. Additionally, if you're targeting foreign AI companies, English proficiency greatly expands the range of jobs you can apply to and salary ranges (see: Does an FDE need English skills?). Since the work centers on liaising with headquarters teams and reading documentation, starting with reading and writing is sufficient.

How to build achievements that get evaluated: Don't stop at PoC

What is valued most in FDE hiring is not titles or certifications but achievements where "you didn't stop at a PoC (proof of concept) but deployed to production, customers actually used it, and results were delivered." In other words, even without prior experience, creating even one achievement of this form will significantly advance your selection process. What matters is not "did you build it" but "was it used, and did it move operations or numbers." It can be for internal customers (your own or other departments), or side projects or personal development.

Get involved through production deployment and adoption

Demos and PoCs only prove that you "can build things." The value of an FDE lies in "running it in production, getting it adopted in the field, and iterating on improvements." If you have the opportunity at work, raise your hand to take stalled PoC projects through to production deployment. If not, pick one repetitive internal task, automate it with AI, have colleagues use it, gather feedback, and improve—even this small cycle counts as "production deployment and adoption" experience.

Speak about results quantitatively

"It became more convenient" is weak, but being able to speak quantitatively—like "reduced a task that took X hours per month by about X%" or "automated X% of first-tier inquiry handling"—makes a huge difference in persuasiveness. From the stage of building achievements, measure Before/After numbers. Be mindful of confidentiality; when discussing externally, omit specific names and express things in percentages and scale (e.g., "reduced technical document search workload by about 30% at a manufacturing company"). This quantification also becomes a weapon in salary negotiations (see: FDE salary benchmarks).

Create "customer-facing" experience through internal customers and side work

Even before changing jobs, if you have no external client projects, you can build customer-facing achievements by treating another department as your customer. Interview the sales department about repetitive tasks, define requirements, build and deploy a tool, and support adoption—this is a microcosm of FDE work. Completing one small AI implementation project through side work or freelancing is also effective. Experience running the full cycle of "interviewing → requirements → implementation → adoption → results" even once becomes the most compelling material in interviews.

Turn lessons from failure into assets

FDE work is a continuous series of things not going as planned. Stories of failure and course correction—like "my initial approach didn't fit the field so I rebuilt it" or "accuracy was lacking so I redesigned the evaluation"—are highly valued as evidence of ownership and problem-solving ability. People who can talk about their stumbles and what they learned from them are more likely to be judged as capable of delivering value in the field than those with only polished success stories.

How to build a portfolio

Once you've built achievements, package them in a form that hiring managers can understand. An FDE portfolio is not about showing "amount of code" but about showing the story of "how you structured the customer's challenge, how you implemented it, and what results you achieved." Structure it so both technical skill and business impact come through. For detailed guidance, see How to build an FDE portfolio.

Organize in case study format

Use the table from the previous chapter (challenge → scope → approach → results → adoption) as the template for each project. Ideally, one project per page, letting readers grasp "what was solved" in 30 seconds. Don't dwell on technical explanations; always make "how the customer/site improved" the star. Companies hiring FDEs want to see "achievements in solving business challenges" rather than technical flashiness.

Publish "small, working" code

Publishing small but complete projects using LLM/RAG on GitHub or similar serves as objective evidence of implementation ability. It doesn't need to be large-scale; practical, working things like "API integration with RAG to search internal documents" or "summarizing meeting minutes and extracting key points" are effective. Writing in the README "what it solves, how it was built, and what was clever about it" makes it a mini case study in itself.

Include results metrics and business context

Don't just list your tech stack; always include the business context of "whose challenge, what kind, and how much was improved." Express confidential figures as percentages and scale. Since FDE is a role that translates technology into business value, the portfolio itself becomes a demonstration of that translation ability. "Sharpness of problem framing" and "how results are told" are evaluated more than project scale.

Connect the story of "why FDE"

At the start of your portfolio, place a coherent story about the strengths of your background role and how you'll apply them to FDE work. Connecting the dots (achievements) into a line (career narrative)—like "honed customer-facing skills and requirements definition at an SIer, added generative AI implementation skills through self-study, and built production deployment achievements"—makes it easier for hiring managers to imagine "this person will grow as an FDE."

How to search for jobs: Mastering job title variations

The biggest pitfall in searching for FDE roles is job title variations. Even for the same work of "implementing and embedding AI at customer sites," companies use wildly different names, so searching only for "FDE" will cause you to miss many attractive openings. The iron rule is to include all the titles in the table below in your search. On our job listings page, you can search for these customer-embedded AI implementation roles across job categories.

Job title variations: FDE / SE / AI implementation / CE / DS

The main job titles are as follows: ① Forward Deployed Engineer (FDE) = Forward Deployed Engineer jobs, ② Solutions Engineer (SE) = Solutions Engineer jobs, ③ AI Implementation Engineer = AI Implementation Engineer jobs, ④ Customer Engineer (CE) = Customer Engineer jobs, ⑤ Deployment Strategist (DS) = Deployment Strategist jobs, ⑥ Professional Services = Professional Services jobs. Although the names differ, the core of the work is similar, so you should judge positions by the content of the job posting (customer-facing work, implementation, and adoption).

Judging by the content of the job posting

Don't be swayed by job titles; read the job description. To identify FDE-like roles, check whether all four of the following are present: (1) working deeply on-site with customers/users, (2) doing the implementation yourself (not just design), (3) taking responsibility for production deployment and adoption, not just PoCs, and (4) committing to the customer's business outcomes. Conversely, roles focused on "design only," "mainly accompanying sales," or "primarily maintenance and operations" differ from FDE. For guides on each role, see What is a Solutions Engineer, What is an AI Implementation Engineer, What is a Customer Engineer, What is a Deployment Strategist, and What is Professional Services.

Know the types of companies hiring

The five types of companies hiring FDE-type talent are: (1) top foreign AI companies (Palantir, OpenAI, Anthropic, etc.), (2) Japanese subsidiaries of foreign SaaS companies, (3) domestic AI startups, (4) AI divisions of SIers and consultancies, and (5) in-house AI departments at operating companies. Potential-based hiring tracks for those without direct experience tend to be found especially at startups and the AI divisions of SIers and consultancies. You can check hiring trends at Companies hiring FDEs and job listings by company at Jobs by hiring company.

Where to search and regular monitoring

Use a combination of each company's official careers page (Careers at Palantir / OpenAI / Anthropic, etc.), AI/engineer-specialized job boards, and this site, which specializes in FDE roles: FDE Jobs Overview. Because FDE job openings move quickly, regular monitoring is important. There are also many fully remote, nationwide positions, and the number of roles that can be applied for from outside major urban areas is growing. Bookmark companies you're interested in so you don't miss hiring waves.

Identifying no-experience-welcome and potential-based hiring

Because the FDE talent pool is small, companies actively pursue potential-based hiring, looking for "people who may not be perfect yet but can grow." If you're aiming to enter from a different background, identifying and applying to these tracks will improve your success rate. That said, some postings advertise "no experience required" while the reality is something different, so it's important to have a discerning eye.

Signs of potential-based hiring

Phrases like "either implementation experience or customer-facing experience required," "LLM/RAG experience preferred," "comprehensive training and onboarding after joining," and "career changers from adjacent roles welcome" are positive signs of potential-based hiring. Conversely, if a posting says "X+ years of FDE experience required" or "immediate contributors only," it will be difficult for those without experience. Learn to distinguish between preferred and required qualifications, and realistically target postings where you can meet most of the required qualifications.

Present your strengths as a "bridge"

In potential-based hiring, what gets evaluated is your ability to bridge the gap by explaining "why I'm a good fit even without FDE experience" using the strengths of your previous role. For example, "At an SIer I handled requirements definition and client negotiations for X years, and in addition I taught myself LLM implementation and deployed it to production." By connecting your existing strengths, the skills you've added, and concrete results, the hiring side can concretely imagine your growth potential.

Avoiding the "no experience required" trap

Even among "no experience required" postings, some positions are in reality just operations and maintenance, testing, or accompanying sales, with no implementation or customer problem-solving. Confirm specifically in the interview whether you'll have opportunities after joining to do FDE-style implementation and production deployment. Asking questions like "What kind of projects would I be entrusted with in the first six months?" and "What proportion of projects go beyond PoC to full production deployment?" will reveal the reality.

Choose your first company for an environment where you can build experience

For your first company coming from a different background, choosing based on "whether I can build a track record as an FDE" rather than salary is the better long-term strategy. In an environment with many production deployment projects, where you're entrusted with implementation, and where you can learn from senior FDEs, your market value will rise significantly within one to two years. If you build a track record early, salary and position will follow later (see also: FDE Career Paths and A Day in the Life of an FDE).

Interview preparation: what is being assessed

FDE interviews assess not only technical skill but also your thought process for "how would you solve the customer's problem" and your sense of ownership over outcomes. For applicants without direct experience, it's especially important to consistently articulate "why FDE" and "how you'll leverage your strengths." For expected questions and how to structure answers, see Common FDE Interview Questions.

Case studies and technical challenges: show your thought process

You may be given case problems such as "A customer has this problem. How would you solve it?" or simple implementation challenges. What gets evaluated is not the correct answer itself but your thought process: structuring the problem, confirming assumptions, and scoping it down to something realistic. The ability to ask back "What should I confirm first?" serves as evidence of your customer-facing capability in the field.

Digging into your track record: use STAR with concrete numbers

When asked "What project produced your best results?", answer in the order of Situation, Task, Action, and Result (STAR), including concrete numbers. Always include the three points of production deployment, adoption, and outcomes, and clarify your own scope of responsibility. If asked about lessons learned from failure, honestly telling the story of how you course-corrected conveys a sense of ownership. The achievements and portfolio you built in the previous chapters will serve directly as material here.

Consistently articulating "Why FDE?"

This is the question most scrutinized for applicants without direct experience. Prepare to tell a single coherent story: the strengths of your previous role, why you were drawn to FDE, the effort you've made to fill the gaps, and how you want to contribute going forward. The motivation of "I want to do both technology and customer work" aligns exactly with the essence of FDE. If you understand the target company's business and product and go as far as "what I could solve for them," you'll leave a strong impression.

Show ownership through your questions for the interviewer

The questions you ask at the end of the interview are an excellent opportunity to demonstrate ownership. Questions that dig into the work and outcomes, such as "What project would I be assigned first after joining?", "What proportion of projects go beyond PoC to production deployment?", and "How are FDE results evaluated?", leave a good impression. An attitude of trying to verify "how can I create value?" rather than just the conditions conveys exactly what an FDE should be.

Learning roadmap (rough timelines)

Below is a learning plan for becoming an FDE from scratch, along with rough timeframes. The durations below are only guidelines and will vary depending on your previous role and the time you can dedicate to learning. Those with a relevant background, such as former SIer or SE professionals, can shorten them, while those from completely unrelated industries should allow more time, including for hands-on implementation learning. The common goal is to "create one production deployment track record and compile it into a case study and portfolio."

PhaseRough timeframeWhat to do
Stage 1: Building foundations1–3 monthsLearn Python, API integration, cloud basics, and Git through hands-on practice
Stage 2: AI implementation2–4 monthsImplement LLM API usage, RAG building, prompt design, and evaluation (Eval)
Stage 3: Building a track record2–4 monthsComplete one production deployment project end-to-end through an internal client or side work
Stage 4: Application preparation1–2 monthsPolish your portfolio, search for jobs accounting for job title variations, and prepare for interviews

Stage 1: Building foundations (1–3 months)

Learn to write Python scripts and build the full workflow of calling external APIs to retrieve and process data yourself. Also get familiar with cloud basics (AWS/GCP/Azure), Git, and simple deployments. Those with implementation experience can shorten this stage and move on to Stage 2. Those from completely unrelated industries should spend extra time on this stage and make sure to gain the experience of "completing something that works from scratch on my own."

Stage 2: AI implementation (2–4 months)

Learn through hands-on practice: building applications with LLM APIs, implementing RAG, prompt design, and evaluating output quality (Eval). The fastest approach is to read the official documentation of each provider (OpenAI, Anthropic, etc.) as your primary source while building and testing small applications yourself. If you go as far as designing evaluations that solve the problem of "the PoC works but quality falls short in production," your market value will rise sharply at this point.

Stage 3: Building a track record (2–4 months)

Using the skills you have learned, take a small project from an internal client or side work and carry it through the full cycle (requirements gathering → requirements definition → implementation → adoption → measuring results). This becomes the "production deployment that was actually used" track record that carries the most weight in FDE hiring. Be sure to measure Before/After figures and, while being mindful of confidentiality, turn it into a case study. Also keep a record of failures and course corrections as an asset.

Stage 4: Application preparation (1–2 months)

Compile your track record into a portfolio, search for jobs while accounting for job title variations (FDE/SE/AI implementation/CE/DS), and prepare for interviews. Prepare to consistently explain "why FDE" based on the strengths of your previous role, and prepare questions to ask the interviewer as well. Monitor job openings regularly via FDE Job Listings Overview and Job Listings, and focus your applications on potential-based hiring slots.

Summary: The shortest path from no experience to FDE

The path to becoming an FDE from scratch is clear. (1) Determine whether your background lies in "implementation" or "customer-facing" work, (2) build on your strengths while filling the gaps (roadmap by previous role), (3) solidify both pillars of Python, LLM/RAG, integration, requirements definition, and negotiation skills, (4) don't stop at a PoC—create one production deployment track record, (5) compile it into a case study and portfolio, (6) navigate job title variations in your job search and apply through potential-based hiring slots, (7) prepare for interviews so you can consistently explain "why FDE"—if you build up these seven steps methodically, transitioning from an adjacent role is entirely realistic.

FDE is a rare role that bridges both technology and business, and it remains a seller's market with a persistent talent shortage. Now is an excellent time to take on the challenge from scratch.
