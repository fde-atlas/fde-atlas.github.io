Chapter 8 · Not a New Name, but an Old Role Converging at the Front Line

Original title: 第 8 章 · 不是新名字，而是旧角色在前线汇合

FDE 的来路 · 5 min read

Summary

This chapter argues that FDE is not a new skill set but a reconnection of responsibilities previously split across pre-sales, solutions architects, implementation consultants, product managers, and customer success. Each handoff loses context, so FDE keeps one person or small team holding context from problem definition through production and feedback. The author stresses that being on-site is not the same as being on the front line, notes that Chinese enterprise software already had similar hybrid roles, and cautions that not every project warrants an FDE team.

Key points

  • FDE reconnects fragmented responsibilities from old roles
  • Every handoff loses context and distorts the original problem
  • Front line means proximity to real work, production, and outcomes
  • China's implementation consultants already did similar hybrid work
  • FDE suits ambiguous, cross-system, high-stakes projects only

What is an FDE? This chapter compares the Forward Deployed Engineer with pre-sales, architects, implementation consultants, product managers, and customer success, and explains why old roles need to converge at the front line.

Since the FDE became popular, the most common debate has not been about what it should do, but about what it used to be called.

Some say, isn't this just an implementation consultant?

Some say, it's clearly a Solutions Architect.

Others think it's just a pre-sales person who can write code, or a full-stack engineer willing to be stationed on-site.

Each of these claims captures a part of the truth, but none is complete.

The FDE did not invent a new set of skills out of thin air. It is more like pulling several previously scattered roles in enterprise software to the same table, and then telling them: this time, don't rush to hand off—stay until the results are in.

Every old role guards a stretch of the road

Pre-sales is the first to engage with customers.

They need to understand roughly what problems the customer has, explain the product's capabilities clearly, and help sales judge whether an opportunity is worth pursuing. But once the contract is signed, the project is usually handed over to the implementation or delivery team.

The Solutions Architect is responsible for putting the system together.

They consider interfaces, data flows, permissions, performance, and deployment, ensuring the solution is technically sound. But how much the business actually adopts is often not their primary metric.

Implementation consultants know the customer's site best.

They configure systems, sort out processes, train users, and handle go-live issues. A good implementation consultant already looks a lot like today's FDE. The difference is that the boundaries of many implementation projects are still defined by feature checklists and acceptance milestones.

Product managers are responsible for commonality.

They must decide what goes into the product's mainline and what is just an individual customer's request. Too far from customers, and you build features nobody uses; too close to a single customer, and the roadmap may turn into a customization list.

Data engineers and software engineers turn the solution into a working system.

They are responsible for code, data, and production stability, but they don't necessarily get to participate in the earliest problem definition. By the time requirements reach them, the direction may already be set.

Customer success takes the final stretch.

After go-live, it's their turn to care about who is using the product, why others aren't, and whether the customer will renew. But by then, many product problems are already hard to redo.

This division of labor is not outdated. Large companies must divide labor; it's impossible for everyone to do everything from sales to operations.

The problem lies in the handoffs.

Every time a problem passes through a handoff, it loses a bit of context. What started as "reduce order disputes" becomes "build an anomaly dashboard" in the solution, becomes a few interfaces and pages in the engineers' hands, and customer success finally discovers that users are still handling things offline.

Every runner completes their leg as required, but the baton is no longer the original one.

The FDE temporarily connects several stretches of the road

OpenAI's public job description for the FDE is very specific: responsible from problem discovery, technical scoping, and system design, through building, all the way to production launch; success is measured by production adoption, measurable impact on workflows, and eval feedback that can in turn change the product and model roadmap.

In this description, what matters most is not which frameworks one knows, but that several actions normally kept separate are now connected.

The person who discovers the problem participates in building; the person who builds follows through to production; and production feedback flows back to product and research.

That is the basic shape of the FDE.

It does not require one person to permanently cover all roles. Complex projects still need business experts, security teams, product, research, and the customer's own engineering staff. The FDE's role is to ensure that no segment of this chain becomes "that's not my job."

Sometimes it is carried by one strong engineer, sometimes by a small team of two or three. The point is not heroism, but that someone on the chain must continuously hold the context.

"Front line" does not mean sitting in the customer's office

Being on-site is not the same as being on the front line.

An engineer who sits in the customer's meeting room every day writing code from requirement tickets may still be far from the problem. Another person who remotely examines real workflows, debugs alongside operators, and takes responsibility for launch outcomes is actually closer to the front line.

The front line means being close to three things:

  • Close to real work, able to see the exceptions that fall outside the documentation;
  • Close to production systems, knowing what happens when code goes wrong;
  • Close to outcomes, able to judge whether the delivery changed the business.

Missing any one of these, the role becomes distorted.

Close only to the work without touching production, you easily stop at consulting; touching only production without understanding outcomes, you easily become outsourced development; talking only about outcomes without engineering capability, you revert to pre-sales promises.

The difficulty of the FDE is precisely that none of the three can be fully pushed onto others.

In China, people have long been doing isomorphic work

Before the name FDE became popular, there had always been a group of cross-boundary people in China's enterprise software field.

Some implementation consultants could write scripts directly; some systems analysts sorted out processes together with the business; some data leads both did architecture and explained ROI to the boss. When a project ran into trouble, the person the customer sought first was often not the one with the highest title, but the one who knew how the system, the business, and the historical reasons connected.

They may not have held the title of FDE, nor possessed today's large model capabilities. But the shape of the work was already very close: blurred problem boundaries, the need to enter the field, and technology and business that could not be separated.

Acknowledging this history does not mean the FDE is just an old job with a new name.

The prototyping speed, probabilistic risks, tool calling, and continuous evals brought by AI mean that field roles must acquire new engineering methods. In the past, a consultant who understood the business but couldn't write production code could hand requirements to engineering; today, if prototypes change daily and model behavior must be tested on-site, the cost of handoffs becomes very high.

The old roles provide the skeleton; AI changes the tempo and the density of responsibility.

Not every customer project needs an FDE

The FDE can also be easily misused.

For projects where the standard product is already mature, customer requirements are clear, and configuration and training will suffice, there is no need to dispatch an expensive cross-functional team. Forcing the FDE model onto them may just add cost.

Scenarios better suited to the FDE usually share several characteristics:

  • The problem is important, but the customer cannot yet articulate it clearly;
  • Data, processes, and permissions span multiple systems;
  • Model capabilities need to be validated while building;
  • The cost of failure is high, so you can't just deliver a prototype and leave;
  • On-site discoveries offer opportunities to be distilled into product capabilities.

The last item here is crucial.

If every customer's problem is only useful to that customer, no matter how strong the FDE team is, it will be dragged down by endless customization. The front line must both serve the current customer and search for reusable patterns for the product.

This is a dual responsibility, and it naturally creates conflict.

The customer wants you to focus only on them; the product team wants you to bring back commonality. The FDE must make judgments: what is this customer's context, and what is a problem that recurs across the entire industry.

Judge well, and the field becomes the product's eyes; judge poorly, and the product becomes the project's warehouse.

What's new is not the skill list, but that responsibility can no longer be easily divided

Ultimately, it doesn't matter much which old role the FDE is classified under.

Companies don't get results because job titles become more accurate. What they care about is: when a problem moves from the meeting room to the production environment, is there someone who always knows why it's being done, what it should look like when done, and what to do when things go wrong.

The value of the FDE lies precisely in this continuity.

It picks up what used to fall through the cracks between handoffs: how the customer actually works, the constraints of technical implementation, post-launch adoption, and field feedback on the product.

And precisely because responsibility has been extended, evaluating an FDE cannot rely only on code, launch speed, or customer satisfaction.

The next chapter asks: if "launch" no longer means completion, what should this new delivery contract actually take as its endpoint?
