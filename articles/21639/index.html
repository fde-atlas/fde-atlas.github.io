Summary

This Japanese article by DeFactory's Shohei Tokumitsu explains the side-business market for technical advisors—senior engineers and CTOs who advise multiple companies part-time. Demand has grown because startups cannot afford or hire full-time CTOs but face high-stakes technical decisions. It breaks down monthly rates by engagement style: lightweight mentoring at ¥100k–200k, architecture and tech-selection review at ¥200k–400k, and organization-building support above ¥300k–500k, plus one-off technical due diligence at ¥500k–1.5M. It covers earning trust without authority, asynchronous communication rules, avoiding conflicts with one's main job, surging demand for evaluating LLM/RAG adoption, and a phased roadmap from side work to a full-time freelance CTO earning over ¥1.5M monthly.

Key points

  • Startup budget and hiring constraints drive spot-type technical advisor demand.
  • Rates range from ¥100k–200k/month for mentoring to ¥300k–500k+ for org-building work.
  • Three key hurdles: lack of authority, async communication, and conflicts with main employment.
  • Evaluating LLM and RAG adoption is a fast-growing, well-paid advisory niche.
  • A phased path leads from side advising to freelance CTO roles above ¥1.5M/month.

A technical advisor side job is a way of working in which active CTOs, VPoEs, and senior engineers provide technical advice, reviews, and decision-making support to other companies' development organizations while continuing their main job.

Against the backdrop of the surge in startups since the 2020s and the difficulty of hiring engineers, this role has rapidly become mainstream, driven by companies' need for "technical expertise that informs management decisions, without hiring full-time."

In this article, aimed at engineers at the active CTO/VPoE level and senior engineers who want to take on a technical advisor side job, we thoroughly explain, from a technical-advisor-level perspective: rate benchmarks by working style, the three common hurdles and their solutions, the rapidly growing demand for advisors in the generative AI era, and routes to winning high-rate projects.

The technical advisor side-job market has gone beyond being merely "extra income for engineers" and has formed its own rate benchmarks as a specialized profession that bridges management and development sites. If you jump into projects based on rate benchmarks alone, you are likely to fail — for example, "failing to earn trust despite the hours worked" or "unknowingly violating your contract due to conflicts with your main job." The biggest reason demand for technical advisor side jobs is expanding rapidly lies in startups' pressing need: "We don't have the budget or timing to hire a full-time CTO, but we want to avoid technical decision-making risks."

As of 2025–2026, centered on seed to Series A companies, "spot-type technical advisor" contracts of a few to a dozen-plus hours per week have come to occupy a major share of the project market.

Technical advisors are recommended mainly for three structural reasons: "① the difficulty and cost of hiring a full-time CTO," "② the risk that a wrong technical decision becomes fatal to the business," and "③ the frequency of cases where founders and management lack a technical background."

Below is a summary of the background factors behind technical advisor demand and the actual situation on the corporate side.

Non-technical management backgroundBusiness-background founders cannot judge technology selection on their ownThird-party validation of decision soundness

With these factors combined, companies have embraced the very idea of "procuring expertise that full-time hires cannot provide, only when needed," rather than "replacing full-time hiring."

The biggest difference between a full-time CTO/VPoE and a side-job technical advisor lies in "whether you bear responsibility for organization management, personnel evaluation, and daily management" versus "whether you remain purely an advisor on decisions."

A side-job technical advisor does not bear management responsibility; instead, it is a way of working that concentrates working hours on "the highest-value decision support."

Below is a summary of the main differences between full-time CTOs/VPoEs and side-job technical advisors.

Unlike a full-time CTO who "commits deeply to a single organization," a side-job technical advisor is a way of working that "engages only at key points in the technical decisions of multiple organizations."

There are mainly three conditions required for a technical advisor's scope of work: the ability to traverse different layers — "① hands-on technical advice at the development site level," "② support for organization and process design," and "③ strategic translation for the executive team."

Let's organize the actual scope of work a technical advisor handles.

  • Architecture and technology selection reviews: Advice on technology stack selection for new services
  • Technical debt inventory and prioritization: Identifying refactoring targets and formulating resolution roadmaps
  • Security and infrastructure audits: Reviewing vulnerability diagnostic results and advising on organizational readiness
  • Development process and organizational design: Support for introducing Scrum, establishing evaluation systems, and defining hiring criteria
  • DX strategy discussions with the executive team: Translating technology trends into business impact
  • Technical due diligence during fundraising: Reviewing technical explanation materials for investors
  • Support for technical interviews in engineer hiring: Evaluating candidates' technical skills and advising on offer decisions

The value of a technical advisor lies in accurately identifying "the support the company needs most right now" from this wide scope and allocating limited working hours accordingly.

The rates for part-time technical advisors are broadly divided into three tiers depending on working style: "mentoring/discussion type (¥100,000–¥200,000 per month)," "architecture design/technology selection type (¥200,000–¥400,000 per month)," and "organization building/hiring support type (over ¥300,000–¥500,000 per month)." The deeper the involvement and the broader the scope of responsibility, the higher the rate.

The mentoring/discussion type is defined as "an advisor who accompanies the decision-making of tech leads and junior CTOs for about 2–5 hours per month."

No hands-on work such as implementation or documentation is involved; the deliverable is "listening, organizing the issues, and presenting decision materials," making it the technical advisor style with the lightest workload.

Let's organize the typical duties of this style.

  • 1–2 one-on-one sessions per month: Handling consultations on organizational issues and technical concerns
  • Pre-decision discussions: Organizing decision materials for technology selection, hiring approvals, etc.
  • Career consultations: Advising junior CTOs and tech leads on their own career development
  • On-demand chat consultations: Responding to asynchronous questions via Slack, etc.

The range of ¥100,000–¥200,000 per month translates to an hourly rate equivalent of ¥20,000–¥50,000, making this the most efficient style in terms of return on working hours. However, it requires "the ability to answer instantly based on experience," so it is not suitable for those with limited practical experience.

The biggest difference between the architecture design/technology selection type and the discussion type is that deliverables such as "reviews of design documents" and "comparative analysis materials for technology selection" are produced.

Rather than mere consultation, involvement in concrete technical decisions is required, so the rate is one tier higher.

To evaluate a "modern technology stack," the advisor must continuously keep up with technology trends, and it is precisely this freshness of information that underpins the rate.

The organization building/hiring support type is defined as including "practical involvement in designing evaluation systems and career paths for engineering organizations, as well as hiring interviews," and has the broadest scope of responsibility among technical advisor styles. It requires not only technical correctness but also a deep understanding of organizational management.

Let's organize the specific support provided in this style.

  • Evaluation system and grade design: Establishing engineer evaluation criteria and salary/promotion logic
  • Hiring requirement definition: Documenting required skills and culture-fit criteria for each position
  • Conducting or attending technical interviews: Evaluating candidates' technical skills and participating in pass/fail decisions
  • Onboarding design: Building the ramp-up process for newly hired engineers
  • One-on-one system design: Teaching managers how to conduct one-on-one meetings
  • KPI design for engineering organizations: Establishing development productivity and quality metrics

Since this style effectively takes on a role close to that of a "part-time VPoE," a high-rate range of over ¥300,000–¥500,000 per month is formed. The hurdles faced in technical advisor side jobs can be summarized into three: "the wall of authority and responsibility," "the wall of asynchronous communication," and "the wall of conflict with your main job." All of these can be avoided through advance contract design and initial behavior, but overlooking them poses a risk of making it difficult to continue the engagement.

The key to overcoming the wall of authority and responsibility lies in "demonstrating from the start, precisely because you are in a non-implementation role, the effort to understand the development context more deeply than anyone else."

If you are perceived as a "talk-only external advisor," no matter how correct your advice is, it will not be accepted on the ground.

Here are practical actions for earning trust from the field.

  • Pre-reading the existing codebase and design documents: Review them to the extent possible before the first meeting
  • Individual interviews with on-site engineers: Listen first to the voices of implementers, not just management
  • A "don't start with criticism" stance: Respect the background of existing technology choices and design decisions before advising
  • Present small concrete proposals early: Offer one actionable improvement proposal at the first or second meeting
  • Serve as a translator of technical jargon: Consciously take on the role of building a common language between management and the field

A common failure of technical advisors who fail to earn trust from the field is the pattern of "wielding frameworks and theories while giving advice that ignores the organization's specific constraints (legacy systems, staffing, budget)."

An attitude that prioritizes "whether it is feasible in that organization" over technical correctness greatly influences your evaluation as a side-job technical advisor.

There are mainly three conditions for resolving the wall of asynchronous communication: "① securing a fixed slot for one synchronous meeting per week," "② establishing a rule to respond to asynchronous questions via Slack, etc. within 24 hours," and "③ documenting decision-making processes so they can be referenced later."

Here are the concrete mechanisms for making asynchronous work viable.

MechanismOperation MethodEffect
Fixed weekly synchronous meetingUse breaks or part of paid leave from your main job to secure 60–90 minutesPrevents important decisions from stalling
24-hour response ruleAlways respond by the next business day to questions on Slack or ChatworkEliminates anxiety that "the advisor is unavailable"
Sharing decision logsRecord the rationale for decisions in Notion, Confluence, etc. each timeMakes handover to successors and other members easier
Escalation criteria for emergenciesDefine in advance "situations that truly require urgent response"Prevents excessive demands for immediate response
Use of recordings and asynchronous sharingRecord regular meetings and share with absentees and those referencing laterResolves information gaps for those who cannot attend

Agreeing with the client at the time of contract that a technical advisor is "not someone who can always respond, but someone who reliably responds at agreed times" is the single most important point for making asynchronous work sustainable over the long term.

There are mainly three conditions for avoiding the wall of conflicts of interest.

"① Always check your main employer's side-job regulations and non-compete obligations before signing," "② Avoid engagements in business areas that directly compete with your main job," and "③ Clearly state confidentiality and mutual non-use of information clauses in the advisory contract."

These are the conditions.

Here are the checklist items to verify for choosing safe engagements.

  • Read your main employer's work rules carefully: Check the conditions for side-job approval and whether prior application is required
  • Confirm the scope of non-compete obligations: Check whether involvement in directly competing businesses is prohibited
  • Add a mutual non-use of information clause to the client's NDA: Clearly state that you will not bring in confidential information from your main job
  • Confirm in advance with your manager and legal department: Always consult beforehand on gray-area engagements
  • State working hour limits in the contract: Contractually guarantee a scope that does not interfere with your main job

Due to their position, technical advisors have many opportunities to access highly confidential technical information from multiple companies, so the risk of accidental information leaks is higher than in other side jobs. When in doubt, do not decide on your own; we strongly recommend consulting a lawyer or your main employer's legal department in advance.

The biggest change that generative AI trends bring to the technical advisor market is the surge in demand for "advisors who can evaluate the technical validity of LLM and RAG adoption." While companies wanting to incorporate generative AI into their businesses are rapidly increasing, in-house talent capable of judging its technical merits is lacking, and the need to consult external advisors is expanding exceptionally.

The reason advisory demand for LLM and RAG adoption evaluation is surging lies in a structural gap: "introducing generative AI tools itself is easy, but companies lack in-house talent who can judge the technical validity of accuracy, cost, and security."

Here are the concrete evaluation criteria required of technical advisors in this area.

Evaluation CriterionConcrete Check ItemsAdvisor's Role
Validity of model selectionWhether the model's size, accuracy, and cost are appropriate for the use caseCorrecting over- or under-specification
Validity of RAG designWhether retrieval accuracy, chunk design, and embedding model selection are appropriateIsolating the causes of insufficient accuracy
Security and information leak riskWhether the data handling policy for external API use is appropriatePointing out risks from a compliance perspective
Validity of cost structureWhether the expected token-based costs match business profitabilityImproving the accuracy of ROI estimates
Validity of the operational frameworkWhether the prompt management and evaluation framework can sustain ongoing operationsPointing out the risk of over-reliance on specific individuals

In generative AI adoption projects, many companies stumble at the stage of deciding "whether they should build it" before "whether they can build it," and the value of technical advisors who support this initial decision-making is rapidly increasing.

There are three main requirements for a technical advisor.

They are: "(1) the ability to continuously gather information on the latest AI tools and cloud service trends," "(2) the ability to translate how a technology contributes to solving a company's management challenges into ROI terms," and "(3) the composure to make decisions based on cost-effectiveness without being swayed by trends."

Here is a summary of practical points for translating into management challenges.

  • Converting technical trends into business impact terms: Speak in terms of "by what percentage the churn rate is expected to improve" rather than "accuracy improves"
  • Estimating implementation and operational costs separately: Evaluate not only initial implementation costs but also ongoing operational costs
  • Advising on the appropriate scale of PoCs (proofs of concept): Prevent wasting time and budget on oversized PoCs
  • Warning in advance about tool migration costs: Communicate the risk of vendor lock-in to management
  • Presenting the option of "not adopting": Make decisions based on cost-effectiveness rather than following trends

A common failure in the field is the case where "a trending generative AI tool was adopted on a whim, but operations never took hold and it stopped being used within a few months."

To prevent such "adopt and done" outcomes, technical advisors are expected to provide advice that looks ahead to ROI estimation before adoption and support for embedding the tool into operations after adoption.

The new role that AI-driven development efficiency brings to technical advisors is "redesigning the KPIs and evaluation metrics of engineering organizations on the premise of using code generation AI." Traditional metrics such as "lines of code" and "implementation speed" are ceasing to function as evaluation criteria in the era of AI utilization.

Here is a summary of key points for redefining evaluation metrics on the premise of AI utilization.

Traditional evaluation metricWhat should be reconsidered in the AI eraExample of redefined evaluation axis

Through such redesign of evaluation metrics, technical advisors are taking on the role of redefining the very "yardstick" by which engineering organizations adapt to the generative AI era.

Technical advisory side work, backed by two tailwinds—startup talent shortages and the generative AI trend—continues to see market growth as of 2026.

Clear rate ranges have formed: 100,000–200,000 yen per month for mentoring/brainstorming-type roles, 200,000–400,000 yen for architecture design and technology selection roles, and over 300,000–500,000 yen for organizational building and hiring support roles, with a diverse range of engagements matching different working styles.

Let us recap the key points for succeeding as a technical advisor on the side.

  • Choosing your working style: Select the format that matches your expertise from mentoring-type, technology selection-type, and organizational building-type roles.
  • Overcoming the three barriers: Overcome the authority barrier, the asynchronous communication barrier, and the conflict barrier through contract design and initial responses.
  • Adapting to the generative AI domain: Capture the new special-demand area of evaluating the technical validity of LLM and RAG implementations.
  • Leveraging multiple channels: Combine matching platforms with referrals through your network to increase the total pool of projects.
  • Long-term roadmap: Keep in mind a phased transition from side work to concurrent work to full-time freelance CTO/VPoE.

Working as a technical advisor is a highly distinctive option within a freelance career, precisely because being removed from implementation demands "expertise that connects management and the front lines."

Accumulating advisory track records at multiple companies while keeping your day job is the most realistic foundation for eventually going independent as a high-rate freelancer.
