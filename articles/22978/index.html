Working as a freelance Product Manager (PdM) is gaining attention as a new career path for engineers.

Monthly rates of 1.2 million yen or more are not uncommon, and the remote work availability rate is high. On the other hand, many people worry, "Isn't it difficult without PdM experience?" or "How do I find projects?"

This article explains everything from market trends for freelance PdMs to required skills and concrete methods for finding projects. If you are an engineer considering a career change to PdM, please use this as a reference.

A freelance PdM is someone who performs product management work under a service contract without belonging to a company.

Acting as a "mini-CEO" responsible for a product's success, they take on a wide range of roles, from strategy formulation to collaboration with development teams. As the IT talent shortage worsens, demand from companies without in-house PdMs is increasing year by year.

Limited to engineers with 5+ years of practical experience

High-rate, direct-contract project information

A PdM handles everything from product vision formulation to requirements definition, collaboration with development teams, and KPI design. They are in the position of deciding "what to build" and "why to build it," and serve as a bridge between engineers/designers and the business side.

Behind the recent increase in freelance PdMs is the lack of PdM know-how at startups. They want to build products but have no experienced person in-house. Even if they try to hire, PdM talent itself is scarce in the market. More and more companies facing these challenges are relying on external PdMs.

In addition, when non-IT companies embark on DX or new businesses, PdM expertise is also sought. The need for "we don't have a PdM in-house, so we want to rely on external help" will continue in the future.

According to the Ministry of Economy, Trade and Industry's "Survey on IT Talent Supply and Demand," it is estimated that there will be a shortage of up to approximately 790,000 IT professionals by 2030.

Against this backdrop, freelance projects for PdMs are on the rise.

Looking at rate benchmarks, the volume zone is 900,000 to 1.2 million yen per month. Rates vary depending on years of experience and skills, but for freelancers, an annual income equivalent of 10 to 14 million yen is a very realistic level.

There are several challenges behind companies seeking freelance PdMs.

First is the problem of "not having a PdM in-house." They have engineers, but no one to decide what to build. Business-side requests cannot be properly translated into development. Many companies struggle with these issues.

Next is the challenge of "building features but not getting results." Development is progressing, but users don't use it. Sales aren't growing. Even when they realize a product strategy review is needed, there's no in-house know-how.

There are also complaints that "communication with engineers doesn't go well." Even when the business side says "we want this feature," coordination on effort and schedules doesn't work out.

Freelance PdMs joining from outside can solve these challenges. The roles companies seek are diverse, including supporting new product launches, supporting growth of existing products, and process improvement consulting.

Salaried PdMs and freelance PdMs differ in how they work and the scope of their responsibilities.

If you are considering going freelance, it is important to understand the differences between the two and judge whether it "suits you." Independence is merely a means, and neither option is inherently the right answer.

The biggest difference between salaried and freelance PdMs is "who you work for."

As an employee, you act based on your company's product strategy. You are in a position to grow the product with a long-term perspective and contribute to achieving the organization's overall goals.

Freelancers, on the other hand, commit to a client's business success on a project-by-project basis. Engagement periods are often a few months to about a year, and you are expected to deliver results in a short time.

Contract renewal is strict if you don't produce results. You need a stronger sense of ownership than an employee and an attitude focused on results.

As a freelancer, you will also handle tasks other than product management yourself.

Everything from winning contracts, negotiating terms, executing tasks, to reporting results is self-managed. Situations where an employee would be supported by a boss or colleagues must be handled alone as a freelancer.

A "CEO of your own company" mindset is required. If a project succeeds, the credit comes back to you, but you also bear the responsibility when it fails.

Can you withstand this weight of responsibility? Can you act autonomously? These are points to consider before going freelance.

Freelance PdMs have advantages that salaried employees don't.

First, the freedom to choose projects. You can focus your work on industries and products that interest you. Being able to concentrate on fields where you can leverage your expertise—such as SaaS, FinTech, and healthcare—is a major attraction.

Also, according to FLEXY's research, 98% of their available projects support remote work. The flexibility to choose where you work is a strength unique to freelancers.

Another benefit is that working on multiple projects broadens your perspective. Seeing different products and organizations expands your toolkit as a PdM.

That said, there are points to be aware of. Lack of organizational support, loneliness, and a weak sense of belonging are challenges specific to freelancing. Some who returned from freelancing to full-time employment have said they "rediscovered the joy of working as part of an organization."

What does it take to continuously win projects and deliver results as a freelance PdM?

Beyond the fundamental skills of product management, the "self-driven" ability unique to freelancers is also essential. Here, we explain the five skills required of freelance PdMs.

【Reference】What Is an FDE? The Role and Essential Skills of Forward Deployed Engineers in the AI Era

Before getting into the main topic, let's understand why engineers-turned-PdMs are highly valued.

"Why do PdMs with engineering backgrounds have high market value?"—The answer is simple: PdMs who understand technology are rare.

Specifically, they have the following strengths:

  • They can see through engineers' "excuses for not being able to do something" and accurately judge technical feasibility
  • They can perform data analysis themselves using SQL, making their PDCA cycles fast
  • Their implementation effort estimates are accurate, allowing them to create realistic roadmaps
  • They can propose architecture choices and solutions for resolving technical debt

Because they have experience in development environments, they can talk with engineers on the same level. This is a strength that PdMs from business backgrounds don't have.

This is the biggest reason engineers should aim to become PdMs. Your technical experience will become a powerful weapon that greatly boosts your market value as a PdM.

Simply completing assigned roles is not enough. You need an attitude of digging deeper into the client's business objectives and market conditions.

"Why is this feature necessary?" "How can we maximize business results?"—The sense of ownership to act with these perspectives is the key to earning trust.

It's also important to have a "quick win" mindset: start small, deliver results, and build trust. Rather than aiming for big achievements right away, accumulate small improvements. That attitude leads to the client's trust.

Full-time employees have training programs and in-house study sessions, but freelancers need to keep learning on their own.

Deepen your knowledge of the industries you work in, learn new marketing methods, master data analysis tools, and follow trends in development frameworks. Constantly staying up to date is the driving force behind maintaining your market value.

There are many ways to learn: reading specialized books, taking online courses, attending industry seminars, and more. It's important to keep inputting knowledge continuously using methods that suit you.

In an environment without managers or colleagues overseeing you, self-discipline is required. When handling multiple projects simultaneously, prioritization, task breakdown, and schedule management become even more important.

Meeting deadlines is a fundamental requirement. Delays or quality drops can instantly destroy trust.

Maintain a healthy routine, secure an environment where you can concentrate, and execute work in a planned manner. These are the basic skills for surviving as a freelancer.

A PdM's role involves collaborating with stakeholders of different positions and specialties. Executives, business teams, engineers, designers—you need to communicate appropriately with each of them.

Simply communicating frequently is not enough. You need "high-quality reporting, communication, and consultation."

In progress reports, include not just facts but also current issues and future outlook. Avoid vague expressions and convey key points concisely. These abilities prevent misunderstandings and improve the productivity of the entire team.

To succeed sustainably as a freelancer, you need a strategy looking months to years ahead, not just at immediate projects.

"Become a specialist entrusted with projects paying 1.5 million yen per month in three years" or "Someday launch my own product and start a business"—Setting concrete goals like these and thinking backward about "what should I do now" is an important perspective.

Which area should you build expertise in? Which projects should you choose? Your daily choices shape your career.

To earn a high income as a freelance PdM, strategic project acquisition is essential.

Here, we explain the three classic routes: using agents, networking and referrals, and social media presence. For any of these methods, the prerequisite is to thoroughly prepare your resume and portfolio and keep them updated with your latest achievements.

Agents specializing in PdM projects hold many high-rate, non-public projects from major companies. Since they handle contract negotiations and rate adjustments on your behalf, the advantage is that you can earn stable income while focusing on projects.

Early in your career, agents can reduce your sales burden. Having them handle everything from project introductions to contract signing is reassuring.

However, while there are many agents for engineers, few are strong in PdM projects. We recommend registering with multiple agents and comparing them.

Also, when choosing an agent, pay attention to the "contract chain" (shoryu). The deeper the chain, the more intermediate margins are taken, reducing your take-home pay.

When asked how freelance PdMs find projects, the overwhelming majority answer "referrals from acquaintances."

The advantage of referrals is that you can start a project with trust already established from the beginning. There's no need to build a relationship from scratch, so you can get to work smoothly.

Also, referral projects tend to be advantageous in terms of conditions. Since there's no middleman margin, rate negotiations tend to be easier as well.

Doing your work with integrity every day and valuing your relationships will lead to future projects. Make a conscious effort to maintain relationships with former colleagues and business partners.

By consistently sharing information on X (formerly Twitter), note, LinkedIn, and other platforms, you can establish a brand as "the go-to person in the XX field."

If a company that resonates with your content reaches out, you're more likely to land a project with a strong fit. Projects you're invited into by name tend to come with favorable terms as well.

However, it's not easy. It takes time, but once your branding is established, it becomes a powerful weapon for continuously winning projects. The key is to steadily build up your content.

The reality is that it's difficult to go straight from no experience to becoming an independent freelance PdM.

Careful planning and step-by-step progression are required. Here, we explain a roadmap for successfully going independent in five steps.

Freelance projects demand people who can contribute immediately, so those with limited experience tend to be less likely to be hired.

You need at least 2-3 years of hands-on experience in a corporate PdM position to build a track record. Experiencing the full cycle from product planning through development to growth is a prerequisite for many projects.

If you're an engineer considering a move to PdM, the fastest path is to first gain experience in a PdM position within your company. Accept the "apprenticeship" phase and focus on systematically acquiring skills and knowledge.

Before making the big decision to go independent, test whether your skills hold up in the outside world through side work or job hunting.

One way to test the waters on a small scale is with PdM projects on weekends or 1-2 days per week. With 3+ years of practical experience, you can efficiently win projects by using side-job agents.

If you feel "I can do this," move on to the next step. If you feel "I'm not ready yet," choosing to gain more experience as a company employee is also a valid answer. Don't rush—move forward at your own pace.

Once your side work and market testing give you confidence that "I can make a living at this," shift the core of your working style from full-time employment to contract work.

The ideal pattern is to gradually shift your income base to contract work while expanding and continuing your side projects. We recommend a cautious transition to independence that avoids any gap in income.

While you still have the title of company employee, prepare your foundation as a freelancer—accounting and tax arrangements, insurance reviews, and so on.

To be stable as a freelancer, it's important to avoid depending on a specific client or a single agent.

If your only client's contract ends, your income drops to zero. Diversify your project channels by registering with multiple agents (2-3 companies), maintaining your network of contacts, and continuing to post on social media.

Diversifying your income sources also brings peace of mind. You'll be able to work boldly without anxiously worrying, "What if this contract ends?"

Even after going independent, continuous effort to raise your market value is essential.

Technology and trends in the IT industry evolve daily. If you neglect learning, your skills quickly become obsolete. Regularly review your goals, skill set, and portfolio to keep them up to date.

Continuously remaining a PdM with the latest knowledge and high expertise is the lifeline for staying in demand over the long term.

Freelance PdM is a career attractive for its high rates and flexible working style.

Projects paying over ¥1.2 million per month are not uncommon, and the remote-work compatibility rate is high. With engineering experience, you can enter the PdM market with the powerful weapon of technical understanding.

On the other hand, personal responsibility and continuous skill improvement are mandatory. There's no organizational support like at a company, and contract renewal is unforgiving if you don't deliver results. Remember that going independent is a means, not an end.

Considering your own career vision, which is the better fit—company employee or freelancer? It's important to compare both options and choose the path that suits you.
