Summary

This Japanese-language article from FDE総研 explains how forward deployed engineers (FDEs) use business interviews to uncover real problems at customer sites. What Is a Business Interview? Its Purpose and How It Differs from Requirements Definition

A business interview is an activity for hearing about field operations and pain points to identify the issues that need to be solved. Although it is often treated as the first step in system development or DX initiatives, its role goes beyond simply "listening." The essence of a business interview is to understand the business flow, decision criteria, exception handling, and even the power dynamics among stakeholders, and to determine "where intervention will have the greatest impact."

What Is the Purpose of a Business Interview?

The purpose is to verify, from facts on the ground, what the real problem is before deciding what to build. What the requester initially says—"I want a tool like this"—is in many cases a proposed solution, not the issue itself. For example, behind the request "I want to automate Excel aggregation" there may be a time constraint such as "I can't get the aggregated results ready in time for the next morning's meeting." A business interview can be described as the work of uncovering the constraints and objectives behind the requests.

How Does It Differ from Requirements Definition?

Requirements definition is the process of deciding the specifications of the solution, while a business interview is the preceding process of pinpointing where the issues lie. The two are continuous, but they frame questions differently.

PerspectiveBusiness InterviewRequirements Definition
Central questionWhat is happening, and what is the problem?What will be achieved, and at what level?
Main subjectsField staff, business flows, actual dataDecision-makers, IT department, development team
OutputIssue list, business flow diagrams, hypothesesFunctional and non-functional requirements specifications
Impact of failureSolving the wrong issuesSpecification gaps and rework

Speaking from my experience as a hands-on practitioner, I believe most rework stems not from how the requirements were written, but from misidentifying the issues during the preceding interviews.

How Do FDE Business Interviews Differ? What Is Palantir-Style Field-First Practice?

What sets FDE interviews apart is that they don't end with listening in a conference room; FDEs go into the field and dig into issues by alternating between observation and prototyping. An FDE (Forward Deployed Engineer) is an engineer who works close to the customer's site, and their distinguishing feature is handling interviews, data understanding, implementation, and operational adoption alone or with a small team. As a result, business interviews are not a "single pre-development step" but an activity repeated many times in parallel with development.

What Is Palantir-Style Field-First Practice?

Palantir-style field-first practice is an approach in which engineers embed themselves in the customer's site and define issues alongside real data and real operations. Through its official blog and other channels, Palantir describes how Forward Deployed Software Engineers work directly with customer organizations to identify issues and build solutions on the spot using the company's platform. What matters here is the sequence: rather than taking the issues back to headquarters to design after hearing them, you clarify the contours of the issues by showing working things in the field.

Comparing Traditional and FDE-Style Interviews

The traditional approach is fundamentally "listen and take back," while the FDE approach is "listen, observe, and build something small on the spot to verify."

ItemTraditional InterviewFDE-Style Interview
LocationConference rooms, online meetingsThe field (workplace, beside the screen)
Information sourcesStaff explanations, existing documentsExplanations plus observation, actual data, and logs
Verification methodReviewing minutes, review meetingsHaving people interact with prototypes
IterationConcentrated at the start of the processRepeated many times throughout development
Who conducts itConsultants, PMsThe engineers themselves who can implement

The advantage of having someone who can implement conduct the interviews is being able to make on-the-spot judgments like "that's technically easy" or "that's hard without the data." However, since engineers tend to think of solutions first, you need to watch out for the failure patterns described later.

What Is the Procedure for a Business Interview? The Flow from Preparation to Consensus Building

Conducting a business interview in four stages—preparation, field observation, deep-dive questions, and consensus building—reduces omissions. The purpose and output of each stage are as follows.

StagePurposeMain output
1. PreparationGather background knowledge and narrow down the questions to askQuestion list, stakeholder map
2. Field observationVerify gaps between explanations and actual workBusiness flow diagrams, exception records
3. Deep-dive questionsHear the facts of the work from those involved and identify the issues behind requestsInterview notes (facts and statements), issue hypotheses
4. Consensus buildingVerify hypotheses through prototypes and figures, and decide priority issues and next actions with stakeholdersVerification results, prioritized issue list, agreement notes

What to Do in the Preparation Stage

In preparation, review existing documents and stakeholders, and narrow down in advance to 3–5 key questions you want to verify in the interview. Going through business manuals, forms, system screens, and past improvement histories, and creating a glossary, will reduce follow-up questions on the day. Also create a stakeholder map distinguishing decision-makers, field leaders, actual operators, and adjacent departments, and decide in advance who to ask what.

How to Proceed on the Day and Observation Tips

On the day, the basics are to ask about the purpose, flow, and exceptions of the work in that order, and then have them show you the actual work alongside. An example of how to proceed is as follows.

  • Explain the purpose and duration of the interview and how notes will be handled at the start
  • Ask about the goal of their work (who they deliver what to)
  • Using a recent concrete example, have them walk through the work chronologically
  • Ask how they handle exceptions and problems when they occur
  • If possible, ask to see the actual screens and work in action
  • Summarize what you heard and read it back to confirm there are no misunderstandings

It is not uncommon for "documented procedures" and "actual work" to diverge. The places where they diverge are often where person-dependent processes and inefficiencies lurk.

The flow up to building consensus

In building consensus, you verify hypotheses about issues with small prototypes or numbers, then decide priorities together with stakeholders. In my experience, showing a simple dashboard or automation mockup and gauging reactions before discussing tends to make the conversation more concrete than presenting a list of issues immediately after the interview.

What should you ask in business interviews? What question patterns can you use?

By using four question patterns—"facts," "frequency and impact," "digging into reasons," and "exceptions"—it becomes easier to uncover issues.

Question patterns for drawing out facts

To draw out facts, instead of asking "What do you usually do?", ask about a specific past event: "What did you do in the most recent case?" In his book *The Mom Test* (2013), Rob Fitzpatrick argues that hypothetical questions and questions asking for opinions are unreliable, and that you should ask about specific past behavior. If you ask "Would you use this tool if it existed?", most people will answer "I think I would," but that is no basis for whether they would actually use it.

How to ask probing questions and about exceptions

For probing, stack "why" questions to trace back to the purpose; for exceptions, ask about "times when things didn't go well" to find them. The "5 Whys analysis" approach known from the Toyota Production System can also be applied to business interviews. However, asking "why" repeatedly can make the person feel interrogated, so it's better to rephrase as "What happens if you don't do that?" or "Who uses the results of that task?" Exception handling is the part most often overlooked in business automation and systemization.

Questions to avoid

Leading questions, yes/no questions, and questions premised on a solution narrow the information you can obtain, so avoid them. If you ask "Manual data entry is a hassle, right?", the other person will simply agree. Asking in an open form like "Which part of that data entry takes the most time?" makes it easier to surface unexpected facts.

What are common failures in business interviews? Tips for avoiding them?

Common failures boil down to three: talking about solutions first, interviewing only some people, and overlooking exceptions.

Common failure patterns

Most failures arise not from interviewing technique but from how the interview is designed and the interviewer's attitude.

Failure patternWhat happensCountermeasure
Talking about solutions firstThe other person adapts to you, and real issues never surfaceDecide not to make proposals in the first meeting
Interviewing only decision-makersIssue definition diverges from actual conditions on the groundAlways interview the actual workers individually as well
Not asking about exceptionsUnexpected handling proliferates in production operationAlways ask about "the most recent case that didn't go well"
Records contain only summaries of statementsFacts and interpretations get mixed, making later verification impossibleRecord facts, interpretations, and hypotheses separately
Finishing in one sessionMisunderstandings surface only after developmentShow a prototype and interview again

Tips for avoiding failure

The key is to reduce the time the interviewer spends talking and make observation and summary confirmation habitual. Interviewers with engineering backgrounds tend to think of solutions midway through the other person's explanation and blurt them out. In my experience, just jotting ideas down in notes and focusing on understanding the other person's business during the meeting is more likely to lead to good proposals. Also, simply confirming at the end of the meeting with "If I summarize what I heard today in three points, is that understanding correct?" can significantly reduce misunderstandings.

How should you organize interview results? How to connect them to next steps?

Organize interview results by recording facts, interpretations, and hypotheses separately, and agree with stakeholders on a prioritized list of issues. The quality of the summary largely determines the quality of the next steps.

Record facts and interpretations separately

The basic rule for records is to separate "what the person actually said and saw" from "the interviewer's interpretation." For example, "Three people do aggregation work at month-end" is a fact, "aggregation is person-dependent" is an interpretation, and "automating aggregation would reduce month-end overtime" is a hypothesis. If these three get mixed together, later readers on the team won't be able to judge which information is reliable.

A characteristic of the FDE approach is running a loop: build a prototype in a short period for high-priority issues, have the field try it, and interview again. The issue list should not be a one-time deliverable; treat it as a working ledger that you update with each loop.

A hallmark of the FDE-style approach is running a loop where, for high-priority issues, you build a prototype in a short period, have people in the field try it, and then conduct interviews again. The issue list should not be treated as a one-time deliverable, but rather as a working ledger that is updated with each iteration of the loop.

For FDEs, business interviewing is a skill that determines value as much as technical ability. If you can define issues rooted in facts on the ground, it becomes the foundation of the Palantir-style way of working: "build in the field, verify in the field." Start by trying the question "Could you walk me through the most recent case as an example?" in your next meeting.
