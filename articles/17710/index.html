How to Tell Real FDE Roles from Fake Ones: Five Tests

Original title: FDE 岗位怎么辨别真假：五项检验

FDE Times · 2026-10-08 · 6 min read

Summary

The article notes that FDE job postings grew over 800% between January and September 2025, making job titles unreliable. It proposes five tests to distinguish real FDE roles from pre-sales demo or support roles disguised as FDE: whether you build custom production code in customer systems, whether you engage after the contract is signed, whether you own running systems, whether success is measured by customer outcomes rather than revenue, and whether someone generalizes custom work back into the product. The author applies the framework to Anthropic's FDE posting and advises candidates to interrogate job descriptions like specs and ask in interviews where customer code ends up.

Key points

  • FDE postings grew over 800% in nine months; titles alone are meaningless.
  • Real FDEs engage post-signature and own running production systems.
  • The hardest test to fake: someone must carry custom work back into the product.
  • Anthropic's posting passes the tests, but post-launch ownership needs interview clarification.
  • Resumes should show generalization, e.g., modules reused by later projects.

Don't look at the title—look at what you can do and where the code ultimately lands; five tests can expose demo and support roles disguised as FDEs.

Key Takeaways

  • From January to September 2025, FDE job postings grew more than 800%, and a title alone can no longer tell you what the job actually involves.
  • A real FDE gets involved after the deal is signed, is accountable for running systems, and is measured on customer outcomes plus experience fed back into the product.
  • The hardest signal to fake is someone being responsible for bringing custom solutions back into the product. Without that loop, the role is consulting wearing an engineering costume.

According to data cited by Paraform, Forward Deployed Engineer (FDE) job postings grew more than 800% from January to September 2025. When a title becomes popular this fast, the job postings using it may not all describe the same job.

No one can count exactly how many of these postings are old jobs with a trendy new name. But a cautious inference is possible: titles are easy to change, while the structure of a role is hard to change. So job seekers shouldn't look at the title, but at the parts of the company that are hard to fake.

For developers looking to move from outsourcing work into FDE roles (and there are many of them in Vietnam right now), this is directly relevant. Pick the wrong role, and you could spend two years doing pre-sales demos or customer support while your resume still says "engineer." Fortunately, job descriptions usually give themselves away in five details—if you know where to look.

What are you allowed to build?

Tandem proposes what it calls the "output test": look at what each role is allowed to produce. Solutions Engineers build demos and architectures used to close deals. FDEs write custom code for a specific customer that didn't previously exist, then bring it back into the product.

Paraform adds a key point: FDEs write production code, but not for the core product. This line rules out two common misconceptions. An FDE is not someone who builds demos for show, nor a product engineer sitting at headquarters.

Apply this to a hypothetical job description. If the responsibilities say "build POCs and technical demos for prospective customers," your output is a sales tool—that's a Solutions Engineer's job. If it says "build production applications in customer infrastructure," you're looking at the right kind of posting.

Do you get involved before or after the contract is signed?

The second test is timing. Aced puts it this way: Solutions Architects work before the sale, while FDEs get involved after the deal is signed to make the product actually deliver value. The practical conclusion is simple: any posting that mentions quota, pipeline, or "supporting the sales team" is closer to an SA role, whatever the title says.

Aced draws an even finer line: SAs are responsible for designs, not for running systems.

So when reading a job description, look for words indicating you'll remain accountable after the system goes live, such as operations, monitoring, and incident response.

If the responsibilities stop at "proposing architecture," you're the person drawing blueprints, not the one building the house.

How success is measured exposes the real job

Timing and output can still be written vaguely, but success metrics are hard to blur. According to Tandem, Solutions Engineers are measured on revenue and deals closed, while FDEs are measured on customer outcomes plus experience brought back into the product.

If the job description has a section on what success looks like after a few months, read that section most carefully. If it's all revenue or renewal numbers, the role serves the sales team. If it describes a stable, running customer system and a deployment pattern that has entered the product roadmap, it's an FDE role.

The hardest test to fake: who brings custom code back into the shared product?

The four tests above can be glossed over in writing; the fifth cannot, because it requires the company to make an organizational decision.

From the employer's perspective, Valletta Software points out that any job description with no one responsible for turning custom solutions into reusable things is, in practice, a consulting role with an engineering title.

An article on Substack puts it more bluntly: if nothing gets generalized, an FDE program is just an ordinary support contract with a catchier name. The company is a services firm that happens to call its employees engineers.

Palantir's original model shows why this feedback loop is core. A historical overview citing Palantir's April 2019 article notes that the company split into two teams by scope: Dev built one capability for many customers, while Delta covered many capabilities for one customer.

The same overview describes the FDE model as a product development strategy that merely looks like services from the outside.

When reading a job posting, you can use this table to score it quickly:

TestReal FDE signalFake signal
OutputCustom production code running in customer systemsDemos, POCs, architectures used to close deals
TimingAfter the contract is signedPre-sales; mentions quota, pipeline
OwnershipAccountable for running systemsDesign only
MetricsCustomer outcomes plus experience fed back to the productRevenue, deals closed
Feedback loopSomeone brings deployment patterns back to the product/engineering teamNothing gets generalized

Dissecting a real job posting: Anthropic

Anthropic's FDE job posting, which has stopped accepting applications, is a perfect specimen for testing this table. It describes engineers embedded with strategic customers, working directly in customer systems and building production applications with Claude models. Output test: pass.

On ownership, the posting gives no direct answer. "Working in customer systems" tells you you're close to running systems, but not who operates them after launch. That's a question to ask in the interview—don't fill in the blanks yourself.

The most notable detail is the requirement to identify and codify reusable deployment patterns and feed them back to the product and engineering teams. This is exactly the feedback loop that Valletta and that Substack article treat as the dividing line between FDE and consulting. The posting passes the hardest test.

The posting also states travel of up to about 25%. This figure differs from the metric Valletta considers most worth publishing: the percentage of time spent working in customer environments. You can work in customer systems without ever leaving home, so travel is only a rough proxy—still ask for the real number.

By contrast, be wary of job descriptions that list a long string of required frameworks. Valletta notes that requiring as many as twelve frameworks suggests the employer doesn't know what the job actually needs. Faced with such a list, treat it as a prompt to ask what the day-to-day work really is.

For developers in transition: read job descriptions like a requirements spec

When a concept becomes trendy, some services firms rename their implementation or support teams as FDE. This is just as likely to happen in Vietnam as anywhere else. You don't need to guess at anyone's intentions—just read the job description the way you'd read a requirements spec: identify the inputs, the outputs, and who receives those outputs.

The table is only the first filter. The next step is the interview, where you ask the questions the job description didn't answer: where does code written for a particular customer ultimately land? Who decides to fold a deployment pattern into the product, and when did that last happen? If the interviewer can't answer, you have your answer.

The same table applies to your own resume. Employers seriously hiring FDEs look for people with experience generalizing, so don't just write "deployed a system for customer X."

Instead, write something like: solved the customer's problem, then extracted the data-processing portion into a module that was reused by three subsequent projects. That one line demonstrates both customer outcomes and experience brought back into the product.

FDE job openings will keep growing, and fake ones will too. Before applying, figure out who at the company turns code written for a single customer into a shared product.
