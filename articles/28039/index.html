An FDE (Forward Deployed Engineer) is an engineer embedded at the customer's site who turns AI models into production systems. They define scope, write code inside the customer's systems, push models into production, and are accountable for adoption. Job postings for this role have surged since 2025: according to analyses by Indeed and the Financial Times, FDE job postings grew more than 800% between January and September 2025.

If you want to first get an overview of generative AI, read The Complete Guide to Generative AI: From Principles to Use Cases. To judge whether this role is worth pursuing, you need to break down three things: what an FDE actually does, how to read the salary figures, and how the role differs from consultants or Solutions Engineers. How enterprises use FDEs is a question from another perspective.

The key point first: an FDE is a software engineer who places themselves at the customer's site—defining scope, writing code on the customer's infrastructure, pushing models into production, and being accountable for adoption. The backdrop to the role's surge in popularity is the mass failure of pilots. A report published in August 2025 by MIT's NANDA project concluded that roughly 95% of enterprise generative AI pilots showed no measurable impact on profit and loss, making engineers who can bridge the gap between demo and production system highly sought after. Salaries come in two kinds of numbers: base salaries listed in official job postings—OpenAI at $162,000 to $280,000 plus equity, Anthropic at $280,000 to $320,000 per year, verified in February and September 2026 respectively. Vendor-analyzed mid-to-senior total compensation packages are a separate range with a different basis and must not be mixed together. The boundary with consultants and Solutions Architects lies in how far the code goes: consultants give advice, SAs do consulting-style PoCs, and FDEs write production code directly on the customer's infrastructure. An action you can take today: compare your experience item by item against OpenAI's official job posting. Anthropic's original FDE posting was closed as of the October 2026 review, so instead find the currently open roles in its job overview and do the same comparison, list the skills you lack, and build them into your practice plan.

What Is an FDE: The Last-Mile Engineer Embedded at the Customer's Site

An in-depth report published by The Pragmatic Engineer on August 12, 2025 defines the FDE as "a software engineer who rotates between customer teams and core product engineering teams." OpenAI's official job posting describes its own Forward Deployed Engineering team as "working with customers to turn research breakthroughs into production systems, operating at the intersection of customer delivery and core platform development." Both statements point to the same thing: a model that works in the lab does not necessarily work inside an enterprise's data, processes, and systems, and the engineering in between is the FDE's territory.

Turning a model into a production system concretely means the work after the demo: connecting the customer's data to the model, embedding tools into existing workflows, building evals and guardrails, and monitoring and maintaining the system after launch. This work happens on the customer's side, using the customer's systems and data—which is exactly what the term "forward deployed" means in practice. Product engineers turn capabilities into a platform; FDEs install that platform into the real-world environment of company after company.

Etymology: An Analogy from Military Terminology

In a report published by tech media outlet The New Stack on January 30, 2026, the term "forward deployed" is explained through a military analogy: in the military, forward-deployed units are stationed at the front line and respond quickly; the tech version of the FDE is dispatched to customers or to internal developers. The report positions the role as "somewhere between a backend software engineer and a customer-facing software architect, similar to a field CTO." The analogy aids memory, but the actual content should follow each company's job postings and team descriptions.

Delta: Origins in the Early 2010s

The origin of the FDE is generally traced back to Palantir. Founded in 2003, the company's clients are mostly government agencies and private enterprises, and the data it handles is often sensitive. The Forward Deployed Software Engineer (FDSE) role was created at the company in the early 2010s, with the internal nickname Delta. According to The Pragmatic Engineer's summary, until around 2016 the company had more FDEs than regular software engineers; after the Foundry platform launched in 2016, more FDEs moved back to the product line, and to this day no company has ever employed more FDEs than it has.

The official blog distinguishes between the company's two types of engineers with the phrase "one capability serving many customers versus many capabilities for one customer": traditional software engineers build a single capability for many customers, while FDSEs enable many capabilities for a single customer; Delta belongs to business development, with the mission of achieving technical outcomes for customers. A First Round Review report records Karp's analogy: the waiter at a famous French restaurant is an extension of the kitchen team, understands how the kitchen works, and can recommend pairings or customized dishes based on diners' tastes—the inspiration for the FDE comes from exactly this kind of role.

Chinese Translations of the Term

In Simplified Chinese communities it is often translated as "前沿部署工程师" (Forward Deployed Engineer) or "前置部署工程师" (Forward Deployed Engineer), while discussions in Taiwan mostly use "前線部署工程師" (Forward Deployed Engineer). Job postings and official documents use the English term Forward Deployed Engineer as the standard; Chinese translations are only a reading aid, and when job hunting or verifying information you should search with the English string so you don't miss postings. One practical detail when searching: the same job carries different titles at different companies, and searching only for FDE will miss postings that only say Applied AI Engineer or Deployment Solutions Engineer, and vice versa. Filtering by responsibilities rather than job titles will surface a more complete set of opportunities.

Field Discipline from Official Interviews

An FDSE interview published on Palantir's official blog on November 2, 2020 describes the role as "a software engineer embedded directly with customers, configuring existing software platforms to solve the hardest problems." The interviewee, Brian, worked on projects spanning cyber, healthcare, and defense; before joining, his background was hardware engineering, and his COVID-19 project was required to go live and operational within days. The interview lists field disciplines including engineering reviews, code reviews, deployability optimization, and maintenance and monitoring of production systems, and also highlights the feedback loop: "some of the most valuable product additions have come from the field."

The Pragmatic Engineer also records a day in the life of two FDEs in 2019. One, based in Abu Dhabi, spent several days each week on-site at the customer in meetings, monitoring, debugging, and deploying, and spent the rest of the time writing small changes and reviewing code. The other, in São Paulo, saw a rhythm that shifted month to month—some weeks like a typical engineer, other weeks defining the project's future with the customer. This rhythm of switching between engineering and customer work is also the common thread across companies' job postings.

What an FDE Does: Two Official Job Postings and Three Case Studies

Job Responsibilities on OpenAI's Job Posting

OpenAI's Forward Deployed Engineering team was set up under Colin Jarvis. He joined in 2022 as a Solutions Architect, later became Head of Solutions Architecture, and then proposed the business case for the FDE team, which started with 2 people. The Pragmatic Engineer recorded in August 2025 that the team had grown to more than 10 people, with locations across New York, San Francisco, Dublin, London, Munich, Paris, Tokyo, and Singapore. A ZenML case study says there were 2 people in early 2024, 39 at the time of the interview, and a plan for 52 by year-end. The two accounts are at different points in time, and sources disagree on the year the team was founded; when citing, simply note the publisher and the time point.

The official OpenAI Financial Services FDE job posting, in a page snapshot from February 10, 2026, lists a role that leads end-to-end deployment of models within banking, asset management, and private capital organizations, from discovering needs, defining technical scope, and system design, through building to production launch. Success is measured by three things: production adoption rate, quantifiable process impact, and eval-driven feedback that changes the product and model roadmaps. Key responsibilities also include owning technical delivery across multiple deployments, building full-stack systems, embedding with customer teams to drive adoption, making trade-offs among scope, speed, and quality, writing code directly when necessary, hardening working patterns into tools or playbooks, and feeding field findings back to Research and Product. The working conditions are a hybrid model with 3 days per week in the New York office, relocation assistance provided, and travel of up to 50%.

This job posting is worth reading line by line. Taking things from the first prototype to stable production means one person spans three phases—discovery, building, and operations—without handoffs to other teams in between. Hardening working patterns into tools, playbooks, or building blocks means field experience must be distilled into reusable assets so that nothing resets to zero when the customer engagement ends. Feeding field findings back to Research and Product is what separates the FDE from pure custom services: problems observed at customer sites can change the roadmaps of the models and products themselves. The posting writes these three things as requirements of a single role, so the scope of work extends from requirements all the way to adoption.

The job responsibilities on the Anthropic posting

Anthropic's FDE posting (listed on Anthropic's official job overview on Greenhouse), as verified on September 10, 2026, places the FDE under the Applied AI team, describing itself as "directly embedded with the most strategic customers, driving transformational AI adoption." The technical items named in the posting are specific: MCP servers, subagents, and agent skills, for use in production workflows. The day-to-day work involves building production applications inside customer systems using Claude models, providing high-touch deployment support, feeding repeatable deployment patterns back to Product and Engineering, and building long-term relationships with customers. To understand the technical terms in the posting, you can first read up on MCP server background and how agent skills work. Travel is estimated at about 25%; locations are New York, San Francisco, or Seattle; at least 25% of the time is in the office; visa sponsorship is provided. When the original URL of this posting was checked on October 18, 2026, it redirected back to the job overview mentioned above—this is exactly how Greenhouse handles closed postings, so the original posting has been closed; whether a new FDE posting was opened at the time could not be confirmed. To track future openings, you can search that overview using "Forward Deployed" as a keyword.

The positioning statement of the posting is blunt: "You will sit at the very front line of enterprise AI deployment, as one of the founding FDEs shaping forward deployed patterns." The expectations for the person are to operate autonomously, thrive in ambiguity, and represent Anthropic in customer environments. In a founding role the systems are not yet settled, and you have to define the working methods as you go—this suits people who enjoy defining a role from scratch; those who need a clear operations manual will have a hard time.

Case one: Morgan Stanley's pipeline and pilot

ZenML's case study of the OpenAI FDE organization notes that Morgan Stanley was the first enterprise customer to deploy GPT-4 in 2023, with the application expanding from wealth management research to all financial advisors. The timeline was split into two phases: 6 to 8 weeks to build the technical pipeline, including retrieval tuning, guardrails, and an evals framework, plus 4 months of pilot and trust building. The results recorded in the case were a 98% adoption rate among financial advisors and 3x usage of research reports. The RAG technical details behind retrieval tuning are explained separately.

The timeline structure deserves a separate look: the technical pipeline took only 6 to 8 weeks, but the pilot and trust building took 4 months. Finishing the engineering does not mean adoption has happened—those 4 months were spent getting financial advisors to actually use it, and getting the organization to trust the quality of the output. The 98% adoption rate in the case comes from the sum of the two phases and cannot be read as a figure at the moment the pipeline was completed.

Case two: a debugging agent at a semiconductor company

The same case study records a European semiconductor company: engineers spent 70% to 80% of their time on debugging and compatibility maintenance; the FDE delivered 10 use cases, with early departmental efficiency gains of 20% to 30% and a target of 50%. The approach was to build a debugging investigation agent on Codex, first triaging issues by urgency, evolving from writing tickets that explain root causes to automatically fixing issues and issuing pull requests. The team's creed was eval-driven development: "No LLM code is done until verification exists," with a labeled evals set built from expert trajectories of 20-plus steps. For those who want to follow along, an introduction to evals engineering and a guide to using Codex are two starting points.

Case three: a supply chain scenario and the product loop

The APAC automaker in the case study used a scenario where goods from China to Korea were hit with a 25% tariff. The engineering principle was to use deterministic code wherever certainty is possible: critical parts like tires always keep dual suppliers and lead-time requirements, checked with 100% deterministic code, while the LLM is used only where probabilistic judgment is needed and can add value, running five optimization scenarios before giving recommendations.

Klarna's path shows how field work grows into a product. In 2023, Klarna found that hand-written prompts for 400-plus policies were unsustainable, and switched to parameterized instructions and tools, with an evals set attached to each intent, which grew into the internal framework Swarm. After open-sourcing, it was validated as generalizable in a T-Mobile customer service engagement, with business scale, policy count, and complexity 10x the previous case, and the Agent SDK was ultimately built by the product team. The team philosophy recorded by ZenML is "eat the pain, ship the product," explicitly modeled on Palantir, taking only problems worth tens of millions to billions of dollars, positioned as a zero-to-one team, not doing long-term services.

This path splits the FDE's output into two layers: the solution delivered to the customer, and the product capability that grows out of the solution. To judge the substance of an FDE posting, look at whether field experience has a chance to feed back into the product. A role with only the first layer is close to custom services; a role with both layers distills field experience into the product, and the accumulated assets travel with the product.

Why it blew up in 2025: the 95% pilot failure rate and the last-mile gap

How to read the 95% statistic

A Fortune report from August 18, 2025 noted that The GenAI Divide: State of AI in Business 2025, published by MIT's NANDA project, concluded that about 95% of enterprise generative AI pilots showed no P&L impact; the research was based on 150 executive interviews, a survey of 350 employees, and an analysis of 300 public AI deployments. This figure describes a collective phenomenon at the pilot stage—it does not mean every company failed, nor that generative AI is useless. A New Stack analysis from May 28, 2026 made the failure point concrete: AI pilots often die after the demo, when the model runs into messy data, undocumented processes, and production systems. Taken together, the two sources show that what the market lacks is precisely people who can push models through that stretch of the journey.

The way to read these three figures is to first look at the period and sample, then at who published them. The 800% figure rests on Indeed's job-postings database and reflects changes in posted jobs. The 2,100% is an estimate derived from interviews with recruiting executives; it speaks to future intent, not hiring that has already happened. The 1,000% comes from a single vendor's analysis of its own field, with sampling limited to English-language postings. The three figures can be cited side by side, but any comparison must carry their respective labels.

The timeline also lays out clearly: a16z's "hottest job" claim came in June 2025, the 800% statistic covers through September 2025, and the 2,100% estimate and elite-scarcity research appeared intensively in mid-2026. Within a single year, the conversation moved from venture capital hype to search-firm surveys and estimates—the role's visibility rose rapidly along this timeline.

2,000 out of 17,000

A TechCrunch report on July 30, 2026 obtained Christian & Timbers' "elite FDE scarcity study": there are roughly 17,000 FDEs in the US, a large share of whom work at Palantir—some clients even buy the company's technology just to gain access to its FDEs. People who combine industry knowledge, credibility in enterprise communication, hands-on applied AI experience, and the ability to keep generating AI returns for enterprises number only about 2,000 in total; in the researchers' words, "it's not that 2,000 people are available for hire—it's that there are only 2,000 in total." Jeff Christian described it as "companies raiding talent in the height of summer," with elite FDEs' returns measured in the tens of millions of dollars—examples include accelerating lead generation or replacing 2,300 document-processing staff.

Boundaries with adjacent roles: consultants, Solutions Architects, and Solutions Engineers

How it differs from consulting

The official interviews frame the difference as technical creativity and speed of delivery: Foundry and Gotham are ready-made playgrounds where most components work out of the box, so you don't have to reinvent the wheel for every client or spend years piecing together patchwork solutions. The Pragmatic Engineer's summary adds the timeline: consultants deliver one-off recommendations, while FDEs work with clients over the long term. A long-form First Round Review piece from February 2026 puts it even more directly: FDEs embed directly with customers to build the last mile of a product so it can run in production, and unlike traditional solutions consultants or sales engineers, FDEs remain genuine engineers who write code and debug production code.

Skepticism has always existed. For years some have viewed the role as "glorified consulting," and that long-form piece notes that Palantir's success in recent years—with a market cap exceeding $300 billion—has prompted a re-examination of the role. James Honsa, who helped Ironclad build its legal engineering team, warns of the other end: the model "gets framed as a panacea, but it's actually a fairly blunt tool"; once the company matured, it reserved legal engineers for high-ticket clients, and general counsels at Fortune 100 companies even nicknamed them The Backpacks. He draws the boundary clearly: scope can creep and pricing can follow workflows, provided the economics of the software itself benefit.

Some have also walked the path between consulting and engineering. Abby Bangser spent six years as a consultant at ThoughtWorks and is now a founding principal engineer at the startup Syntasso; in an interview she said her consulting career taught her to package high-value skills into a toolbox she could carry into different contexts—within weeks of joining a new team she was able to pitch platform patterns to specific teams. The advantage of a consulting background when moving to FDE work is speed of context switching; what needs to be added is the discipline of production code.

Inside OpenAI: the division of labor between FDEs and Solutions Architects

Solutions Architect (SA) is an existing role at OpenAI that coexists with the FDE role with a clear division of labor. Per The Pragmatic Engineer's summary: SAs lean consultative, rarely write code on customer infrastructure, and mainly build MVPs or PoCs with anonymized or offline data. FDEs write code directly on customer infrastructure using the customer's tools, face greater ambiguity, and align with research goals. One example of a product loop: a voice customer wanted to automate customer service; the FDE first built evals for the voice model, the model initially fell short, the FDE brought data back to the research team for improvement, and the customer later became the first company to launch the advanced tier in production—OpenAI's Realtime API improved as a result.

Relationship with Solutions Engineers and community skepticism

Solutions Engineer (SE) is the umbrella term for closely related roles, and in community discussions it is often mentioned alongside FDE. A common question in discussion threads on Reddit's engineering communities is whether FDE is just a rebranding of solutions engineers or sales engineers; the r/salesengineers subreddit even has discussions titled "if your company calls you an FDE," and some engineers see it as a hybrid "technical special forces" role. In a personal article by Baseten practitioner Trivedi dated June 2, 2024, he describes the role as a mix of "firefighter, plumber, and translator," the company's first line of defense, with time split roughly 60% model performance, 20% software engineering, 10% sales, and 10% customer support.

Prasad Rao, Principal Solutions Architect at AWS, wrote in a January 2026 LinkedIn post: "FDEs stay hands-on throughout the entire customer lifecycle, participating in day-to-day operations, fixing issues as they arise, and adjusting systems based on what's actually happening on the ground." Perspective AI's analysis offers a different estimate of time allocation from job descriptions: roughly 60% customer-facing, 30% deployment coding, and 10% internal matters. Unlike Trivedi's personal account, the two sets of numbers are best read side by side—doing so gives a better feel for the job's weighting than either set alone.

Job title variants and the product feature of the same name

Perspective AI's vendor analysis shows that 2026 job postings exhibit title fragmentation: Forward Deployed AI Engineer appears at Google, Scale AI, and Cohere; Applied AI Engineer at Anthropic and Mistral; Deployment-Solutions Engineer at Databricks and Snowflake; FDSE at Palantir and Hebbia; and mid-size startups have FDE-SA hybrid roles. These variants are not fully equivalent to FDE. Another easily confused name is the AI FDE in Foundry's official documentation: that is an interactive agent for operating Foundry through conversational commands, converting natural language into data transformations, code repository management, ontology creation and maintenance, and other operations. It requires AIP to be enabled and adheres to the user's existing permissions. It is a product feature that shares a name with the human role but sits at a different layer—keep them distinct when reading job postings or product documentation.

FDE salaries: two sets of numbers you shouldn't mix

Base salaries listed in official job postings

A page snapshot of OpenAI's Financial Services FDE job posting from February 10, 2026 lists compensation of $162,000 to $280,000, plus equity. Anthropic's FDE posting, verified on September 10, 2026, listed $280,000 to $320,000 per year. Both figures are ranges explicitly stated on official job pages; they are on a different basis from total annual compensation after adding bonuses and equity value, and actual terms vary with company policy and timing.

When reading salary figures, location and timing matter more than the numbers themselves. Both postings are based in US cities, and the figures cannot be directly converted to other markets. Official job pages list base salary ranges; equity value fluctuates with the company and timing; total package estimates are a different calculation that includes equity. When negotiating, ask how base salary, bonus, and equity are each calculated—don't compare the top of a total package against the bottom of a base salary.

Another set of estimates for total packages

The same Perspective AI vendor analysis offers another set of estimates for total compensation: mid-level $300,000 to $450,000, senior $450,000 to $550,000, staff and principal level at frontier labs $600,000 to $1.2 million or more, with equity accounting for 55% to 70% at top roles, versus 35% to 45% in 2024 estimates. These figures include equity and are on a different basis from the base salary ranges on official job pages; they must be labeled separately when compared and cannot be presented as a single salary range.

Who is suited to be an FDE: skills, seniority, and entry paths

The skills list reflected in job postings

The skill requirements in the two official postings concentrate on three layers. The engineering foundation requires production-grade front-end and back-end code in Python, JavaScript, or TypeScript. The AI application layer requires LLM application development, retrieval, prompt engineering, evals frameworks, and agent development. The customer layer requires requirements discovery, scope definition, and the ability to communicate with executives. Perspective AI's analysis offers its own estimates of skill frequency: core engineering appears in over 95% of sampled postings, AI-specific skills over 80%, and customer requirements discovery skills over 70% with the fastest growth. A systematic guide to AI agent development is a direct read for filling the second layer.

The three skill layers can also be inferred from failure modes. Pilots often fail on data and processes, so retrieval and evals frameworks are essential. After a pilot passes, someone has to push adoption to happen, so customer-layer communication and timeline management are unavoidable. By comparison, knowledge of model applications themselves is relatively easier to fill—model capabilities keep improving, and documentation and courses are plentiful—while judgment on the engineering and customer sides is the threshold that repeatedly appears in job postings.

Experience Spectrum and Interviews

Hiring criteria form a spectrum with no single threshold. A summary from The Pragmatic Engineer: Ramp's senior FDEs prefer 5+ years of experience, but also accept exceptional new grads. Palantir has even hired engineers with only one year of experience. Commure and Matta value end-to-end shipping experience; the latter's industrial AI work requires defining solutions on the factory floor. Anjor Kanekar, who spent seven years as an FDE at the source company, spent a long time on Airbus's final assembly line. Palantir, Commure, and two official job postings list travel ratios ranging from about 25% to 50%; on-site work is an explicitly stated fixed requirement at these companies.

First Round Review interviewed two recruiters with deep roots in this staffing model. Shilpa Balaji, a former FDE who once led FDE recruiting, said the company's FDE roster is dominated by new grads, and that FAANG veterans with 10+ years may actually be a red flag; standout FDEs are "people who can't help but keep building things," "willingness to endure hardship" is standard, the model is essentially "discovering new things in customer contexts and decentralizing product development," and the cadence is "prototype whatever you hear today, show it to the customer the next day." This same-day prototyping cadence aligns with the rapid iteration approach of vibe coding. Tiffany Siu, First Round's head of talent who also came from that company's recruiting system, added that FDEs go through the same interview process as software engineers.

Entry Paths and Company Systems

In his personal writing, Trivedi describes his entry path as publishing public articles: his blog post on using Truss to deploy open-source LLMs was noticed by a Baseten co-founder, and he joined the company as an FDE in January 2024, with work spanning pre-sales and post-sales. The three phases he describes: first answering executives' technical questions, such as caching model weights, concurrency, and throughput; then building and optimizing models, using TensorRT to reduce latency and request batching to raise throughput; and after the deal closes, running benchmarks alongside the customer and shifting to support.

Salesforce's newsroom introduction offers another reference. The FDE program is jointly composed of three internal teams—engineering, professional services, and customer success; the team tripled in size within six months; internal transfers account for 40% to 50% of FDE movement; and a six-week training program launched in September 2025, with two weeks of technical training plus four weeks of hands-on work. Sarah Khalid, Salesforce FDE Director, describes the division of labor as "implementation teams build systems, FDEs ensure those systems generate value," and for FDEs, deployment means "packing a laptop and heading to the customer site." Vice President Ruth Hickin describes the role as a mix of "hacker, customer value, and professional services," emphasizing "this is not typical software implementation": coding is the baseline, and the differentiators are judgment, pattern recognition, and the ability to tell executives the truth. Systemic changes include FDEs previously spending up to 40% of their time on administrative preparation, now largely automated, with the customer service AI agent using a 70% deflection rate as its north star metric; once met, customers expand to a second use case. The advice for outsiders is just one sentence: "First become the AI expert at your current job."

How Enterprises Use FDEs: Vendor Services, In-House Teams, and Joint Ventures

Buying Vendor FDE Services

On May 4, 2026, Anthropic released an official announcement on forming an enterprise AI services company with Blackstone, Hellman & Friedman, and Goldman Sachs, with General Atlantic, Leonard Green, Apollo, GIC, and Sequoia as investors. The announcement did not give a company name; the mission is to serve mid-sized companies across industries, with a customer base including community banks, mid-sized manufacturers, and regional healthcare systems—companies lacking the resources to build in-house. Anthropic's Applied AI Engineers work alongside the new company's engineering team to identify high-impact entry points, build custom solutions, and support customers long term. The example scenario in the announcement is a multi-site healthcare group's clinical documentation, medical coding, prior authorization, and compliance review, with the engineering team sitting alongside clinical staff and IT to build tools into existing workflows. CFO Krishna Rao's framing: "Enterprise demand for Claude significantly exceeds what any single delivery model can carry."

A TechCrunch report on July 30, 2026 called the company Ode with Anthropic, with CEO Chris Taylor, whose observation on the FDE market is "many FDEs can help you roll out Claude Code to employees; very few can build your flagship AI product features." Companies wanting to evaluate such services can first look at the Claude Code usage tutorial to understand what the tool can do, then judge the scope of services.

On the same day as Anthropic's announcement, Bloomberg reported that OpenAI raised $4 billion for The Deployment Company, with 19 investors and a $10 billion valuation, including TPG, Brookfield, Advent, and Bain Capital. TechCrunch's May 4, 2026 summary interprets both deals as asset managers funding in exchange for a preferred sales channel into portfolio companies, noting "the new capital also lets each customer get a larger share of engineering resources, embracing the FDE model that Palantir popularized." The Wall Street Journal reported the Anthropic deal at a $1.5 billion valuation, with Anthropic, Blackstone, and H&F each contributing $300 million. Background figures from the same report: OpenAI announced $122 billion in new funding at the end of March 2026 at an $852 billion valuation, and Anthropic was negotiating $50 billion at a $900 billion valuation. The joint venture structures and amounts are rolling, changing information; defer to the announcements at the time before making decisions.

The two joint ventures turn vendor FDEs from a staffing problem into a capital problem. The returns asset managers get for their funding include equity gains and a preferred sales channel into portfolio companies. For AI companies, the new capital lets each customer get a larger share of engineering resources, so the supply of FDE services is no longer capped by their own headcount. For buyers' procurement staff, the practical question actually becomes simpler: when will their company's turn come for these engineering resources?

Building In-House FDE Teams

TechCrunch reports that insurance, fintech, healthcare, and gaming companies are building internal FDE teams, keeping proprietary processes in-house while reducing dependence on AI companies. The build-vs-buy trade-off can be tested with three questions: can the process and data be handed to an external team, is the number of use cases enough to justify a full-time headcount, and does anyone internally own adoption? If all three answers point inward, building in-house is worth serious evaluation. Enterprises that obtain model capabilities through cloud services can view this path as AIaaS (AI as a Service); the FDE's job is to integrate models with existing systems, data, and workflows into production. The first tasks for an in-house team are often connecting existing workflows to models, and ChatGPT use cases in workflows are a common starting point. To put processes into an agent runtime the team controls, refer to Hermes Agent's data flow and security evaluation and first think through where the data will be sent.

One of the historical prototypes recorded in First Round is Looker: from 2013, the model was forward deployment during trials, "selling a product that felt like a custom service." Assuming an annual fee of $25,000 and 2,000 customers, that illustrates a path to $50 million in annual recurring revenue; the figures are a hypothetical calculation, not drawn from actual financial reports. Ironclad's legal engineering team shows the importance of scope discipline: the model's spread has prerequisites—software economics must benefit, and pricing must align with workflows.

Entering from existing roles

If you want to move in this direction, the requirements listed in job postings take long-term accumulation, but what you can do right now is within your current job: take on internal AI deployment projects and build up production LLM experience and a customer-facing track record—exactly the two things both official job postings require. Both postings specify days in office and travel ratios; actual conditions are subject to each company's announcements.

Internal projects, public work, and customer-facing experience can all be accumulated without changing companies. For an internal project, pick a process with real users, complete the whole pipeline of retrieval, evals, and deployment, and record the adoption rate. Public work means writing the deployment process up as an article or an open-source project; according to Trivedi's personal account, he got into the field by being noticed through public articles, and the Klarna case shows that on-site experience can be distilled into an open-source framework. Customer-facing experience comes from proactively taking on external communication and practicing explaining technical trade-offs to non-engineers.

Is it worth it: lifespan predictions and counter-evidence

Five-to-ten-year lifespan predictions

Recruiter Christian issued a warning about the long term: within two years, software agents may automate other software agents, with humans no longer driving in the middle; medium-term demand may shift to physical AI, meaning humanoid robots entering workflows, and within five to ten years the FDE role may "disappear." This is a recruiter's prediction, not yet reality, but the direction is worth noting. Perspective AI's analysis also cites Stack Overflow's 2026 developer survey: 41% of AI engineers spend more than 30% of their time facing customers, up from 12% in 2023. The boundary between engineering and customer work is shifting, and the engineering demand for pushing models into production is repeatedly pointed to by multiple sources.

Breaking the predictions apart, their certainty varies. Software agents automating within two years presupposes that agent tool reliability keeps rising, which remains unproven in customer environments. The demand shift to physical AI depends on when robot hardware matures, with the main variable lying in hardware. The disappearance of the role in five to ten years is an extrapolation assuming both prior conditions hold. Recruiters look at job supply and demand; personal career decisions must also factor in skill transferability: the combination of evals, deployment, and customer communication remains portable even if the title changes.

Criticisms and limitations

Beyond the "glorified consultant" label, practical limitations include travel burden—OpenAI's posting specifies up to 50%. Case selection also has a threshold: in ZenML's case study, the OpenAI team only picked problems worth tens of millions to billions of dollars, positioned as zero-to-one, with no long-term services; the deployment support most companies actually encounter may not look like this. Honsa's "blunt tool" reminder is equally practical: the model's spread has prerequisites—software economics must benefit.

Before committing, ask yourself one question: can you turn a vague requirement into a launched system in an environment with no existing playbook, and be accountable for the adoption rate. If the answer is yes, this role's skill set has a home on both the vendor and enterprise sides.

  • Starting out: compare your experience item by item against OpenAI's official job posting; after Anthropic's original FDE posting closed, search its job overview for current openings and do the same comparison, marking the skills you lack.
  • Six to eight weeks: build an end-to-end pipeline including retrieval, evals, and deployment. Use the technical pipeline from the Morgan Stanley case as a duration reference, and align with the technical-to-hands-on ratio of Salesforce's six-week onboarding.
  • Four months: find a real setting for a pilot and validate the adoption rate. Use the pilot period from the Morgan Stanley case as a duration reference.
  • After that: write the process up as a presentable public record; Trivedi's path of being noticed through public articles is a replicable approach.

Each of the four stages has a clear completion criterion. The first stage's output is a skills gap list, each item accompanied by existing evidence. The second stage's output is a presentable pipeline, including an eval set and launch records. The third stage's output is adoption numbers from real users—the sample need not be large, but it must be repeatable. The fourth stage's output is a public article or open-source project, so recruiters can find you.

Job requirements and salaries will change; skills and cases are what you can accumulate yourself.
