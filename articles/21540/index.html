Monthly rates of 1 to 2 million yen are the standard range, and for senior architects who can lead projects such as cloud migration, DX initiatives, and microservices adoption, projects exceeding 2 million yen per month genuinely exist.

However, to consistently earn as a freelance IT architect, you need not only "technical depth" but also the ability to "translate business requirements," "negotiate with senior management," and "continuously update your cloud knowledge" — all three working together. At the level of simply "being able to write design documents," you won't reach high-rate projects.

An IT architect is a specialist who designs a company's business strategy and requirements into the overall system structure (architecture). Unlike development engineers who handle individual feature implementation, IT architects define the skeleton of the entire system and are responsible for technology selection, configuration design, and ensuring non-functional requirements (availability, scalability, security, cost).

Their roles in a project can be broken down as follows.

The IT architect role is divided into the following specialties depending on the breadth of responsibilities.

  • Enterprise Architect (EA): The most senior role, designing the organization's overall IT strategy, business processes, systems, and infrastructure in an integrated manner. Works with CIOs and CTOs.
  • Solution Architect: Designs system solutions for specific business challenges. The most common role on a project basis.
  • Infrastructure Architect: Specializes in cloud platforms, networks, security, and DR design.
  • Application Architect: Specializes in application-layer design (microservices, API design, data models).
  • Cloud Architect: A design specialist focused on a specific cloud platform (AWS/Azure/GCP).

In the freelance market, "solution architect" and "cloud architect" projects are the most widely available, so having strengths in one of these two areas is a realistic starting line for going independent.

The fundamental reason demand for freelance IT architects is surging is the expansion of DX investment by Japanese companies combined with an overwhelming shortage of design talent capable of carrying it out.

From 2025 to 2026, the following three structural demands have overlapped, causing demand for IT architect talent to explode.

  • Legacy system modernization: In response to the "2025 Cliff" problem (the risk of delayed legacy system renewal highlighted in a METI report), a large number of on-premises to cloud migration projects have emerged. Architects capable of migration design are chronically in short supply.
  • Full-scale shift to cloud-native: More companies are moving toward containerization (Kubernetes) and microservices architecture. Since almost no in-house talent can design these, cases of hiring external specialists are rapidly increasing.
  • Mandated strengthening of security and governance: Due to tightened cybersecurity legislation and expanded disclosure obligations for listed companies, projects reviewing security architecture are increasing.

The biggest difference between IT architects, SEs, and PMs is whether they are the "decision-maker for technical decisions." SEs implement based on given designs, and PMs manage schedules, costs, and personnel, but IT architects present the technical rationale for "what to build" and "how to build it" and make the decisions.

The future prospects for IT architects are extremely high. In particular, professionals who can integrally design the three elements of "cloud native × security × generative AI integration" will maintain high scarcity even after 2030.

The relationship between technology trends and architect demand is summarized below.

  • Demand for generative AI integration design: Demand for architecture design that integrates LLMs into enterprise systems (RAG, agents, security guardrails) has surged since 2025.
  • Multi-cloud and hybrid cloud design: There is a shortage of professionals who can design composite configurations combining AWS, Azure, and GCP.
  • Platform Engineering (in-house development support): There is strong demand for platform design (IDP construction) when companies internalize AWS configuration management, CI/CD pipelines, and developer experience.
  • Zero-trust security architecture: Migration design from perimeter defense to zero trust is accelerating with the establishment of remote work.

Honing your expertise in any one of these areas directly contributes to maintaining long-term freelance market value.

Freelance IT architects are a profession where it is relatively easy to achieve 1.5 to 2 times the annual income of a salaried employee with equal or better skills. The annual income of salaried IT architects is typically ¥8–12 million at major SIers and around ¥12–20 million at foreign consulting firms, but for freelancers, income is calculated as a monthly rate of ¥1–2 million × months worked, making an annual income range of ¥12–24 million a realistic target.

The biggest advantages of going independent as a freelance IT architect are "no income ceiling" and "direct involvement in technical decision-making."

Advantage 1: Significantly higher income

It is not uncommon for an architect who delivered results equivalent to ¥700,000/month as a salaried employee to secure projects paying ¥1.2–1.5 million/month as a freelancer. This is because the portion that was previously taken as company profit is converted into their own income. By working on multiple projects simultaneously (e.g., 2 days/week × 2 companies), an annual income exceeding ¥20 million is within reach.

Advantage 2: Freedom in technology selection

As a salaried employee, technology is often chosen based on "the company's preferred vendors" or "past relationships." Freelance architects can evaluate and recommend the optimal technology for the client's challenges, making it easier to maintain technical integrity. This also contributes to market recognition as a "professional architect."

Advantage 3: Career discretion

You can build your own career portfolio, such as "only taking DX projects in this industry," "distributing 3 days/week of work across multiple companies," or "combining consulting with implementation." You can design your career in a direction that maximizes market value, rather than following organizational logic.

To hold market value that surpasses that of an IT architect, it is essential not only to "understand technology" but also to have "the translation skills to convert business requirements into system design." Engineers without this ability will never be chosen for architect projects, no matter how skilled they are at implementation.

The specific abilities required in the requirements definition and design phases are as follows.

  • Ability to define non-functional requirements: The ability to translate requirements such as "99.99% availability," "latency under 100ms," and "support for 100,000 RPS at peak" into concrete architecture choices (multi-AZ, CDN placement, auto-scaling design)
  • Cost design ability: The ability to estimate monthly AWS costs, development effort, and maintenance costs, and explain to executives in terms of "initial cost of ¥X, total cost of ownership after 3 years of ¥Y, and risks of Z"
  • Articulating trade-off judgments: The ability to support decision-making in the form of "I have organized the merits, drawbacks, risks, and costs of both options A and B, and I recommend A because..."
  • As-Is/To-Be analysis: The ability to structurally organize the issues of the current system and present multiple migration scenarios toward the target state (To-Be)
A real-world failure example: There was a case where a mid-level engineer joined an architect project but was told by the client, "The design document is fine, but you can't explain the rationale for choosing this architecture," and was replaced mid-project. What is required of an architect is not "the answer" but "the ability to explain the basis for your judgment." It is important to develop the habit of documenting the background of design decisions early on.

The most important soft skill for continuously winning high-rate projects as an IT architect is "the ability to explain the same content clearly to executives, business departments, and development teams alike."

The most important step in moving up from development engineer to IT architect is "building a track record of producing upstream-phase deliverables yourself." It is not about changing your job title; what gets evaluated is experience in leading the creation of design documents, architecture diagrams, and technology selection documents.

A realistic roadmap for stepping up:

  • Proactively create a "technology selection proposal" in your current project and present it to your manager and team
  • Carry out internal, side-project-style activities documenting "As-Is analysis and improvement proposals" for existing systems
  • Obtain advanced AWS/Azure certifications and become the in-house point of contact for cloud design consultations
  • Take on the leadership of small-scale migration or refactoring projects
  • Handle infrastructure design and cloud migration design for startups as a side job (starting at the 100,000–300,000 yen/month level)
  • Turn design documents, configuration diagrams, and technology selection documents into a portfolio that can be published on GitHub or a personal website
  • Publish technical articles on architecture design via Qiita or a blog
  • Register with freelance agents and start with entry- to mid-level projects (800,000–1,000,000 yen/month)
  • Demonstrate high deliverable quality on your first project and turn it into repeat orders and referrals from clients

The most reliable way to raise your rate by 30–50% is to shift your position from "contracted implementation and design" to "supporting technical decision-making."

Adding IT consulting capabilities

An architect who can "define system requirements from business challenges and present multiple solutions with ROI" commands a rate 200,000–400,000 yen higher than one who "just creates design documents." Specifically, the following approaches are effective.

  • Provide pre-PoC feasibility studies as deliverables
  • Provide cost-reduction estimates alongside proposals, such as "how much cost reduction can be expected three years after this system investment"
  • Include vendor selection criteria development and evaluation support in your scope of work

Elevating to a technical advisor position

Technical advisor engagements typically command 500,000–1,000,000 yen per month despite low working hours (10–20 hours per month). By holding advisor roles at multiple companies simultaneously, you can achieve high income while keeping total monthly working hours low.

The following are required to deliver value as a technical advisor.

  • Deep specialized knowledge in a specific technical domain, to the point that people think "if I ask this person, I'll get an answer"
  • The ability to translate into business language for direct conversations with executives
  • Problem-solving skills to identify essential issues in short meetings

To reach the point where work comes to you by name as a freelance IT architect, "client work that earns the trust of executives" is decisively important, not just technical skill.

Practical principles of client work that generate long-term, ongoing engagements:

Principle 1: Sell "peace of mind" through weekly reporting

Report technical progress weekly in a one-page executive summary format. Always include the three-part set of "risks, current status, and next week's action plan." Creating a state where the CIO can answer even when asked by subordinates becomes the foundation of repeat business.

Principle 2: Bring "solutions along with problems," not just problem reports

When an issue arises, always frame it as "Problem X has occurred. The causes are A and B, and we are considering C and D as solutions. I recommend C," rather than "Problem X has occurred." This is the fastest way to be evaluated by executives as "someone you can rely on."

Principle 3: Identify and propose "the next challenge" before the project ends

Starting two months before the end of an engagement, begin activities to discover and propose the next project phase or adjacent challenges. Make natural continuation proposals in the form of "once the current system is stabilized, shouldn't we next build out the data utilization platform?"

Principle 4: Create deliverables that solidify your technical "defense"

By maintaining operations documentation, incident response guides, and architecture decision records (ADRs), you build a relationship where clients think "I want to work with this person again" rather than "we'd be in trouble if this person left." Excessive dependence on a single person undermines trust.

The most effective strategy for maintaining high rates long-term as a freelance IT architect is "creating a one-of-a-kind position through the combination of industry × technology."

Rather than being a "generalist architect who can handle any project," a specialist recognized as "the person to go to for cloud migration in industry X" has overwhelmingly higher and more stable market value. This is because it more easily leads to technical advisor roles and work by direct referral.

Practical steps for positioning strategy:

  • Take inventory: List the industries you've worked in, the technologies you've handled, and the types of projects you excel at
  • Find the overlap: Identify areas where industry knowledge and technical expertise intersect
  • Articulate it: Create a one-sentence statement like "I am an architect who solves ○○ challenges in the ○○ industry using ○○ technology"
  • Communicate it: Keep publishing technical articles, giving talks, and posting on social media about your expertise to build recognition as "the go-to person for ○○"
  • Have the courage to say no: For projects outside your specialty, either raise your rate or decline. Generalizing leads to lower rates

You can go independent even if you've been away from hands-on development, but catching up on modern cloud technologies is essential before going independent.

For IT architects, design, decision-making, and communication are the main duties rather than implementation itself, so coding speed is not a requirement. However, practical knowledge at the level of "can write Terraform," "can explain architecture on Kubernetes," and "understands the characteristics and trade-offs of each cloud service" is necessary.

If you've been away from hands-on work for more than 3 years, we recommend obtaining advanced AWS or Azure certifications while working on personal projects and hands-on exercises, and setting aside a catch-up period of at least 6 months to 1 year. After that, it's safest to confirm whether you're "still viable as a working professional" through small-scale design projects as side work before fully going independent.

Mid-to-senior level architect positions increasingly offer full remote work, with 1–2 days per week in the office being the mainstream arrangement.

Since remote work became established after 2022, there is now a shared understanding that upstream design, requirements definition, and architecture reviews can proceed without issue remotely. In particular, cloud architect and technical advisor positions tend to be fully remote.

On the other hand, many clients require in-person attendance for initial kickoffs, presentations to key stakeholders, and whiteboard-based design workshops. The common arrangement is "basically remote, with 1–2 office days per month," and being too rigid about full remote may narrow your project options.

Yes. In fact, people in their 40s and 50s have the highest freelance market value as IT architects.

IT architecture is a profession where accumulated career experience translates directly into value. The "judgment gained from numerous project experiences," "experience communicating with executives," and "depth of industry knowledge" that engineers in their 20s and 30s rarely possess become strengths for those in their 40s and 50s.

Surveys of the freelance market also show that architect, PMO, and consulting positions are most readily filled by people aged 40–55. This is the opposite trend from hands-on development roles, where judgment based on experience is valued more as age increases. However, the condition is that you continuously keep up with the latest technologies (especially cloud, containers, and generative AI).

Even if you specialize in infrastructure, there are plenty of projects available, but understanding the application layer greatly widens your rate range.

Even as a specialized infrastructure architect (focusing on cloud platform design, security, and DR design), projects paying ¥1.0M–1.5M per month are readily available. In particular, the combination of AWS infrastructure design and security remains in high demand amid the wave of strengthened compliance requirements.

However, if you aim for higher-rate projects (over ¥1.5M–2.0M) as a solution architect or enterprise architect, you will need an understanding of application-side architecture (microservices, API design, data models). We recommend setting a mid-to-long-term goal of raising your skills to the level of "being able to make basic design decisions" in the application layer while maintaining your infrastructure strengths.
