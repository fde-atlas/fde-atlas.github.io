AI won't eliminate engineers, but it will split them into two kinds

Original title: AI 不会淘汰工程师，但会把工程师分成两种

xnnmfg.com · 2026 · 3 min read

Summary

The article argues that AI will not eliminate engineers but will split them into two groups: those amplified by AI and those replaced by it. As coding, research, and documentation become cheap, value shifts toward deciding what to build and making it work in real environments. The author highlights the Forward Deployed Engineer (FDE), a role pioneered by Palantir and now heavily recruited by AI companies, named the hottest tech job for 2026. FDEs embed at customer sites and own outcomes, not just software delivery. The piece outlines four AI-proof capabilities, six foundational skills, a practical path to develop FDE skills in one's current job, and four common pitfalls, noting that manufacturing domain experience is especially valuable.

Key points

  • AI makes coding and documentation cheap, shifting value to problem definition and real-world deployment.
  • FDE, originating at Palantir, works on-site and is accountable for actual customer outcomes.
  • AI cannot replace tacit field knowledge, problem judgment, trust-building, and orchestrating AI like a team.
  • Six skills: domain depth, full-stack breadth, AI toolchain, rapid prototyping, communication, and retrospection.
  • Practice on real pain points in your current job; avoid collector and one-shot-hero pitfalls.

Full text

Over the past two years, most engineers around me have shared a vague anxiety: AI is writing code faster and faster, and looking up information, making slides, and digging through documentation cost almost nothing—as if everything we know, machines learned overnight. But the anxiety points in the wrong direction. What AI really does isn't stealing jobs; it's redistributing "what is valuable." It has made a whole set of once labor-intensive capabilities fast and cheap, so value flows to both ends—upstream to "what exactly should be done," and downstream to "how to get it into a real environment and make it run." The role that sits precisely at these two ends was named the hottest tech job of 2026: the FDE, Forward Deployed Engineer.

The FDE model originated at Palantir, and today AI companies are scrambling to hire for it. It's quite different from the roles we're familiar with: it's not a Sales Engineer who presents a solution and leaves, not an implementer who configures systems by a standard playbook, and not an R&D engineer sitting at headquarters building generic features. An FDE dives straight into the customer's site, lives inside the customer's problems, builds things while learning the business, and is accountable for "actually running and delivering value." What's sold is no longer a piece of software, but "getting your problem solved." In manufacturing, this distinction is especially stark—factories have never lacked software; what they lack is the person who can make software actually work.

Why now? Because after AI made writing code, researching, writing documentation, and producing first drafts cheap, knowing how to use AI is no longer a bonus—it's the passing grade. What truly sets people apart are four things AI can't replace: understanding the unwritten tacit rules of the field, judging which of a pile of requirements is the real problem, earning customers' trust and getting departments to cooperate with you, and the ability to orchestrate AI like a team. For people in manufacturing, here's the good news: the hands-on experience and industry understanding you hold are exactly the fuel AI lacks most and values most. A manufacturing veteran plus an AI toolchain is, right now, the hardest combination to replace.

Break these capabilities down and you get six pillars: domain depth and full-stack breadth are the foundation, determining whether you can handle real, complex problems with no standard answers; the AI toolchain and rapid prototyping are the lever, determining whether you alone can match the speed of a former small team; communication and follow-through, plus reflection and knowledge accumulation, are the compound interest, determining whether each delivery leaves you "just more tired" or "one notch stronger." The foundation can't be patched by hiring; the lever is amplified by AI; the compound interest makes you more valuable over time. Others use AI to say "help me write this snippet faster"; an FDE uses AI to say "help me carry the whole thing."

As for how to train yourself, you don't need to change jobs. Right in your current role, pick a real small pain point and walk the entire path yourself, from understanding the problem to building something usable, even if it's rough; plug AI into every step—breadth comes from it, depth comes from your own accumulation; once it's done, translate the results into money or time saved and present them to the business side and your boss—with one trusted win, you've earned the next, bigger problem; finally, force yourself to distill every engagement into a template stored in your own playbook. After three to five engagements, you become the person "others come to when they hit this kind of problem." The most common pitfalls along the way are four: the collector who tries every tool but has never shipped a single thing; the jack-of-all-trades who knows a bit of everything but is trusted by no one; the silent builder who delivers but no one knows the value; and the one-time hero who starts from zero every time, exhausts themselves, and adds no lasting value.

In the end, AI won't eliminate engineers—it will split engineers into two kinds: those amplified by AI, and those replaced by AI. The difference isn't how many tools you know, but what remains that others don't have after AI has helped you.
