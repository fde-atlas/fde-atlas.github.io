Case Study: How Meituan Used an Agent Evals Approach to Manage a 310,000-Line AI Refactor

Original title: 案例复盘：美团用 Agent 评测思路管理 31 万行 AI 重构

FDE中国 · 2026-09-04 · 6 min read

Summary

A Meituan team running Agent evals applied its evaluation methodology to engineering governance, refactoring a system that grew from under 50,000 to 310,000 lines of code with over 90% AI-generated code, without pausing delivery. The core sequence: first align people on layering and domain modeling through a single strong decision-maker, then codify consensus into always-on AI Rules and Skills. Technical debt was identified via expert-scoped boundaries plus exhaustive AI scanning (3 P0, 2 P1 issues, 10 hidden performance problems) and repaid as side effects of feature work. The article notes preconditions: control over your own backlog, a backup owner for standards, and human-led testing since fully automated test generation failed. All figures come from Meituan's tech blog and are unverified.

Key points

  • Align people first, then codify AI Rules—order matters.
  • Expert-scoped boundaries plus AI scanning surfaced debt cheaply.
  • Zero-schedule refactoring requires owning your backlog.
  • Human-led testing beat fully automated test generation.
  • The strong alignment role is a single point of failure.

I. Background

The subject of this case is an internal Meituan team responsible for Agent evals (per the company's tech blog). Its Agent evals system has long supported multiple core business scenarios while handling data production, workflow orchestration, quality control, and multi-person collaboration: the underlying layer supports 6 types of multimodal data evals, the upper layer has multiple core task views and fine-grained business actions, and it comes with over ten quality-check mechanisms. The system grew from under 50,000 lines of code in June 2025 to 310,000 lines, sustaining a high-intensity iteration pace of 16 requirements per month on average (80% business requirements plus 20% technical requirements); the team grew roughly 3x in size within about a year, with members' backgrounds spanning high-concurrency systems, offline machine learning training, admin backends, and interns, and over 90% of the code was written with AI assistance.

II. Problem

The team's retrospective pointed to a counterintuitive fact: AI Coding does not automatically converge complexity — without unified standards, different people using AI produce code in wildly different styles, and the system actually rots faster. Three pain points erupted at once: the business model lacked extensibility, with nearly every new business form requiring new code, creating "silo-style" development; long-term "package-per-requirement" habits left complex logic like Controllers tangled in one package, and at 310,000 lines the "spaghetti code" made everyday development a matter of pulling one thread and unraveling everything; the team tripled in a year with diverse backgrounds, compounded by over 90% of code being AI-generated — without hard standards, the system would rot and accumulate new debt at an uncontrollable speed. Meanwhile the business was in an exploratory phase with urgent, vague requirements — there was no possibility of pausing for a dedicated project, nor of letting new debt grow unchecked.

III. Solution

The team migrated the Agent evals methodology from their day job to engineering governance, centered on one standard-alignment main thread: "human-to-human alignment → human-to-machine alignment." First, a strong single role aligned engineering standards across all roles — product, operations, algorithms, QA — "1 'dictator' beats 10 'democrats'"; then the consensus was codified into AI-executable constraints: the engineering layering standard, business-domain model conventions, and repository-layer conventions were implemented as always-level AI Rules, moved upstream into the pre-CR stage to constrain the coding process; the team unified the responsibility boundary between "orchestration-type" and "capability-type" code internally, and that consensus was distilled into Skills loaded progressively during coding. Order is critical: before the team formed consensus, the same specification would be interpreted into different versions by different people, and no matter how well the AI Rules were written, they'd be dead letters.

IV. Implementation

The refactoring proceeded in three phases (per the company's tech blog, February to April 2026).

Phase 1: Define the problem (February). Facing 310,000 lines of code no one could fully read, the team adopted "expert-guided targeting + AI-assisted troubleshooting": core developers scoped the high-risk inspection boundaries, and AI handled exhaustive scanning, quickly mapping out tech debt in business model flaws, database query performance, state management, and indexing — 3 P0 and 2 P1 tech debt items in total; engineers also used AI to locate, in a short time, 10 deeply hidden performance issues nearly impossible to find by eye.

Phase 2: Establish AI-friendly standards (completed late February). The standards were turned from documents into always-level AI Rules and moved upstream into the pre-CR stage, helping developers complete basic standards checks before submission; for the most contentious area — domain responsibility division — the team's internal consensus on "orchestration-type/capability-type" was distilled into Skills.

Phase 3: Incremental refactoring and quality assurance (March–April). First, engineering layering and decoupling: the spaghetti code was migrated to a four-layer architecture of Starter / Application / Infrastructure / Common; the refactoring lead personally migrated the two most complex packages and distilled a standardized migration SOP, after which the rest of the team guided AI through the remaining package migrations per the SOP, quickly completing structural migration of over ten core packages. Second, zero-schedule refactoring: tech debt was broken into "incidental actions" attached to business requirements — a brand-new business model was landed alongside core feature iterations, and the full migration of the quality-check business model was completed alongside a feature upgrade — without requesting a single day of dedicated refactoring time. Third, quality assurance: a Pre-PR mechanism was introduced, with AI performing multiple rounds of self-checks to fix standards issues before submission; human review shifted from "did you write this correctly" to focusing on technical solution compliance and business logic; higher-tier models reviewed lower-tier models' output, and models from different vendors cross-reviewed each other to broaden coverage; for testing, the team settled on a "human-led, AI-assisted" route — the fully automated test-case generation route was abandoned because it lacked holistic business understanding and easily missed hidden high-risk scenarios, and 100% of requirements were tested by developers doubling as testers.

V. Results

All of the following is per the company's tech blog and has not been independently verified. The refactoring was completed without halting business delivery: over ten core packages completed their engineering structure migration; the core data model and quality-check business model completed smooth upgrades and full migration (compatible with multiple business chains and cross-validation across multiple views and regions); tech debt triage located 3 P0 and 2 P1 items with limited effort, plus 10 performance issues, with no dedicated refactoring schedule requested throughout. The article also offers two qualitative judgments: the value of "experience" is shifting from "being able to see everything" to "being able to judge what matters"; and engineers' focus is shifting from "writing code" to "designing and maintaining an engineering environment that lets AI reliably produce code."

VI. Constraints

The preconditions for this playbook are worth understanding clearly: zero-schedule refactoring assumes the team controls its own demand pool and can break tech debt into high-priority requirements to be digested "incidentally," and the precision of that decomposition depends on strong technical judgment; standard alignment depends on one strong aligning role, which is itself a single point of failure; the value of standards is only fully amplified in scenarios where over 90% of code is AI-generated; Pre-PR and AI code review require the team to accept a redefinition of the human reviewer's role — this is process transformation, not just tool adoption.

VII. Reusable Lessons

  • Align humans first, then codify AI Rules. The order cannot be reversed: without team consensus on layering and modeling, any Rule will be interpreted into multiple versions. Precondition: there must be a role empowered to set and align standards.
  • Lead developer builds the prototype, SOP distributes it, whole team works in parallel. Having the most familiar person run through a complete exemplar first, then distilling the steps into an AI-executable SOP for replication, is faster and more stable than everyone fumbling on their own.
  • Break tech debt into incidental actions attached to requirements. Suited to teams that control their own demand pool; in delivery scenarios constrained by client cadence, consider an escalation mechanism for deferred debt items to avoid the ledger becoming a dead letter.
  • Move code review earlier. Once AI speeds things up, code review becomes the new bottleneck; Pre-PR self-checks let human review focus on business semantics — this step cannot be skipped.
  • Testing: human-led, AI-assisted. Fully automated test-case generation has been empirically falsified by this team's practice; human-in-the-loop is more stable — consistent with the Agent evals field's threshold of "only trustable when human-machine agreement rates are met."

China-context differences: the scenarios in this article involve a large tech company's in-house business teams; for FDE projects delivered to external clients, AI rules and standards must also accommodate the client-side codebase constraints and security approvals, so an extra layer of adaptation cost should be budgeted during implementation.
