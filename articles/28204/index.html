FDE Offer Equity: 8 Questions to Ask Before You Sign

FDE Times · 2026-10-18 · 7 min read

Summary

This article argues that equity now makes up 55–70% of total pay for FDEs at top employers, yet offer letters usually state only a share count with no denominator. It lists eight questions to ask before signing: the fully diluted percentage and its as-of date, the total preference stack, options versus RSUs with strike price and 409A date, vesting and cliff terms, the post-termination exercise window, acquisition acceleration, and early exercise with its 30-day 83(b) deadline. Worked examples show 20,000 options can mean 0.2% or 0.05% depending on dilution, and a preference stack can leave common stock with nothing in a modest exit. The author frames asking these questions as negotiation groundwork, noting same-level RSU grants can vary by $50,000–200,000 over four years.

Key points

  • Equity is now 55–70% of top FDE compensation.
  • A share count is meaningless without the fully diluted total.
  • The preference stack decides who gets paid first at exit.
  • One-year cliffs and 90-day exercise windows can erase equity value.
  • Same-level RSU grants vary by $50,000–200,000 over four years.

Equity may now make up 55-70% of top FDE pay, so treat any offer without the fully diluted share count as unfinished.

In brief

  • A share count on its own tells you almost nothing. Ask for the percentage, on a fully diluted basis, as of a specific date.
  • The preference stack, a one-year cliff, a 90-day exercise window and the absence of acceleration can each wipe out the value of your equity.
  • Grants at the same level vary widely, so asking detailed questions is also the first step in negotiating.

At top employers, equity can now make up 55–70% of an FDE’s total compensation, up from 35–45% in 2024. The figure comes from a 2026 FDE salary negotiation guide on a vendor’s blog, so treat it as an indicator only.

The largest part of the offer is also the hardest to read. Base salary is a number with a currency attached. Equity usually arrives as “X shares” or “X options”, with no denominator and no word on who gets paid before you.

If you are taking, or aiming for, an FDE role at a startup, this is where one missed question can be worth more than every round of haggling over base salary. The good news is that the questions are few, and asking them works exactly like the customer discovery a good FDE does.

A share count says nothing on its own

The first question is what percentage the grant represents, on which capital structure and as of what date, as The Startup Law Blog advises. The reason is simple division. The number of shares is the numerator; its value depends entirely on the denominator.

Suppose you are offered 20,000 options. If the company has 10,000,000 fully diluted shares, you own 0.2%. If the figure is 40,000,000, you own just 0.05%, a quarter as much. The same line in the offer letter, two very different values.

So if the company will not tell you the total fully diluted share count, treat the offer as incomplete until it does. That is Underdog.io’s firm rule. There is no need to be combative; simply say you cannot compare the offer without the denominator.

The “as of what date” question matters just as much. Dilution is not a theoretical risk but the default path for venture-backed companies that keep raising money, according to Underdog.io.

Nor does the company need employees’ consent to dilute them. Today’s 0.2% will shrink with every funding round, so build that scenario in from the start.

Who gets paid before you?

Knowing the percentage is still not enough, because not all shares are paid out in the same order. Ask for the total preference stack: the sum of the liquidation preferences held by preferred-stock investors (Underdog.io). When the company is sold, this is paid first, and employees’ common stock gets what is left.

Imagine a startup that has raised 100 units of capital in total, with an equivalent liquidation preference, and is then sold for 120 units. Investors take 100 first.

All the common stock, including your 0.2%, shares the remaining 20 units. If the sale price is only 90, your share is zero, even if the headlines still call it an “exit”.

That is why the FDE negotiation guide mentioned above recommends valuing a grant on a fully diluted basis, against both the 409A valuation and the preferred-share valuation. In other words, do not multiply your share count by a single price. Run the numbers at both and look at the gap between them.

Options or RSUs, and what will it cost you to buy?

Options and RSUs are different instruments. A stock option gives you the right to buy shares at a preset price, called the strike price, while an RSU is a share granted outright (Levels.fyi). With options, your value is the difference between the share price and the strike, not the full share price.

The strike price is what you pay to exercise, that is, to buy the shares. In the US it is anchored to the 409A valuation, so Ledgy advises asking for both the strike and the date of the most recent 409A. An old 409A may no longer reflect where the company stands today.

Anchoring the strike to the 409A is a US mechanism. If your contract is in another country, ask the company directly how the strike is set where you work and when you will owe tax.

The vesting clock and the 90-day window

The most common vesting schedule is four years with a one-year cliff, according to Ledgy. Leave before the one-year mark and you get nothing. If you are not sure you will stay the full first year in a new role, include a scenario where that equity is worth zero when comparing offers.

The second risk appears when you leave. The post-termination exercise period is typically 90 days for ISOs, but it can stretch to 10 years depending on the company’s equity policy (Qapita). Loom is one example: it gives a 10-year window to employees with two or more years of service.

Run the numbers. You have vested 5,000 options with a strike of 2 currency units per share. To keep them, you must put up 10,000 units within 90 days, before tax, to buy shares in an unlisted company.

If you cannot, the options are lost. A 10-year window turns a rushed decision into a choice you have time to weigh.

When the company is sold, and the 30-day deadline

Do not assume an exit means you get paid in full. An acquisition does not automatically accelerate the vesting of unvested equity unless the grant documents say so explicitly (The Startup Law Blog). Ask about acceleration and read the actual clause in the award document.

Early exercise has its own trap. If you exercise options before they vest, the statutory deadline for an 83(b) election is 30 days from when the shares are actually transferred, and it is generally not extended. This is a deadline to put in your calendar the moment you sign, not something to deal with next week.

Here is the list to send the recruiter before any numbers are discussed:

Question Why ask Warning sign
What percentage is the grant, as of what date? The share count is only the numerator They just repeat the share count
What is the total fully diluted share count? The denominator sets your real stake They refuse to disclose it
What is the total preference stack? It decides who is paid first at exit “You don’t need to worry about that”
Options or RSUs, what strike, what 409A date? Options are only worth the gap above the strike The 409A is stale
Vesting schedule and cliff? Leave before the cliff and you get nothing A longer-than-standard cliff with no explanation
Exercise window after leaving? 90 days can force you to give up your options Only 90 days, no exceptions
Is there acceleration if the company is sold? It is not automatic Verbal promises only, nothing in writing
Is early exercise allowed? It triggers the 30-day 83(b) deadline Nobody can explain the process

Ask the way you would ask a customer

Asking these questions is also the opening move of a negotiation. At the same level, RSU grants can differ by $50,000–200,000 over a four-year vesting period, depending on the compensation committee’s decision, according to the FDE negotiation guide mentioned above.

That spread is your room to negotiate. But you can only use it once you have converted the grant into a percentage and know who gets paid before you.

The same guide advises negotiating equity first, then everything else. That makes sense when equity is the largest part of the package. With the fully diluted percentage and the preference stack in hand, you are negotiating on data rather than on feel.

The skill here is the skill of the job. An FDE does not accept a request to “integrate the system” without asking about scope, which data and who signs off. You can make that point in interviews: the way you clarify a vague offer is the way you clarify a vague customer requirement.

Read the offer letter as you would a customer specification: wherever the numbers are missing is where you need to ask before you sign.
