The last mile of AI is still human work

Original title: AI的最后一公里，仍然是人的工作

新浪 · 2026-08-15 · 6 min read

Summary

This article explains the Forward Deployed Engineer (FDE) role, arguing it is not a new AI-era job but a delivery model long used by companies like Palantir, where engineers embed with customer teams to turn models into working business outcomes. As model capabilities converge, the author argues competitive advantage shifts to those who can integrate AI into real workflows. FDEs handle technical, business, and organizational languages, take responsibility for results, and convert one-off deliveries into reusable product capabilities. Palantir's AI FDE product shows AI can absorb standardized tasks, but judgment, coordination, and accountability remain human work. Chinese firms in finance, manufacturing, and other complex sectors need this working method, whatever the job title.

Key points

  • FDEs embed with customers, delivering outcomes rather than just code.
  • The role spans technical, business, and organizational languages.
  • AI widens the gap between model capability and enterprise reality.
  • True FDEs own results and distill one-off work into product features.
  • Palantir's AI FDE automates routine tasks, not judgment or accountability.

Full text

Source: Wu Xiaobo Channel CHANNELWU

In recent job postings and industry discussions, the abbreviation FDE has started to be seen by more people.

FDE stands for Forward Deployed Engineer, usually translated as "Forward Deployed Engineer" or "Forward Deployed Engineer".

It is not a new profession that appeared out of nowhere in the AI era, but a way of working that has long existed in complex software delivery. It is only after AI moved from the lab into enterprise processes that this role suddenly became more important.

Because no matter how powerful a model is, it will not automatically turn into business results.

What exactly does an FDE do?

Palantir is one of the most representative companies of the FDE model. In Palantir's definition, an FDE is directly embedded in the customer's team, using the existing software platform to solve specific problems.

Traditional software engineers focus more on "developing a general capability that can serve many customers", while FDEs focus more on "helping one customer truly solve multiple specific problems".

For example, a manufacturing company wants to use AI to predict equipment failures. The model itself may not be hard to obtain, but when it comes to actual implementation, a chain of problems arises: where is the equipment data stored? Are the fields consistent across different factories? Which data can be accessed? Who confirms the prediction results? Who handles alerts after the system raises them? If the AI makes a wrong judgment, who bears the responsibility?

What an FDE has to do is not just connect the model, but go deep into the customer's business site, collaborate with production, customer service, finance or supply chain teams, find problems truly worth solving, and then complete data integration, system modification, model deployment, permission setup, and continuous adjustments after going live.

What an FDE delivers is not just a piece of code, but a result that can run in real business and be accepted by the organization.

How is it different from an ordinary engineer?

FDEs are often understood as "software engineers who understand the business better", but in fact they face three languages at the same time.

The first is the technical language. An FDE needs to understand models, databases, APIs, workflows, permissions and deployment environments, be able to write code, and also be able to troubleshoot system failures.

The second is the business language. Customers usually don't say "please design a multi-agent orchestration architecture for me", but are more likely to say: "the daily reports are too slow", "customer service can't keep up", "the approval process often gets stuck".

An FDE has to translate these vague complaints into problems that can be solved technically.

The third is the organizational language. Once an AI system enters production, it involves budget, process, compliance and responsibility. An FDE needs to coordinate business departments, IT departments, management and frontline employees so that a technical solution is truly accepted by the organization.

Therefore, an FDE is neither purely a Sales Engineer nor a software engineer who only develops general products at headquarters. It is closer to a "hands-on, delivery-oriented technical business role".

Why does the AI era need FDEs again?

FDE is not a role that only appeared in the AI era. It has long existed in complex software projects in government, defense, finance, manufacturing and other fields.

AI has brought it renewed attention because a new gap has emerged between models and enterprises.

Model capabilities update quickly, but the actual environment of enterprises is very complex. Data is scattered across different systems, legacy software is still running, and business processes often have no standard answers. An AI application that performs excellently in a demo environment may immediately encounter permission, data, process and responsibility issues when it reaches the enterprise site.

What enterprises truly lack is often not a model, but someone who can put the model into their own workflows.

The value of an FDE lies precisely in compressing the distance in this process:

◎ Turning the customer's vague needs into executable problems;

◎ Integrating scattered data into the system;

◎ Adapting model capabilities into specific workflows;

◎ Pushing experimental versions into production environments;

◎ Bringing frontline users' feedback back to the product team.

It connects model companies and customers, and also connects product development with real business.

An FDE is not "on-site outsourcing"

This is also the most common misconception when understanding FDE.

If an engineer only modifies interfaces and fixes bugs according to customer requirements, or maintains systems for customers long-term, that is closer to traditional project delivery or outsourcing services.

A true FDE must be accountable for results.

He not only has to solve the immediate problem, but also judge whether the problem can be abstracted into a general capability. A customer's special need may eventually become a new product feature; an on-site failure may also expose flaws in platform design.

Therefore, an FDE usually sits at the intersection of two lines: one line points to the customer, requiring quick problem solving; the other line points to the company, requiring one-time deliveries to be consolidated into replicable product capabilities.

With only the first line, an FDE easily becomes a highly paid outsourced engineer; with only the second line, the product returns to the state of "works great in the lab, unusable at the customer's site".

The stronger AI gets, the more humans are needed to do what?

The most valuable aspect of an FDE may not lie in how much code it can write, but in the fact that it must constantly make judgments.

Which steps are suitable for automation, and which steps must retain human confirmation? When should the model be trusted, and when should a second verification be required?

When a customer says "I want an AI assistant", is the real problem low efficiency, messy processes, or the lack of unified data across departments?

These problems cannot be solved by model parameters alone.

AI can generate answers, but it does not necessarily understand an enterprise's real goals, processes and boundaries of responsibility. Only people who enter the business site can understand an organization's habits, concerns, power relationships and implicit rules.

Therefore, FDE is not a counterexample of AI replacing humans, but another requirement for humans after AI is implemented:

Humans are no longer responsible for completing all repetitive labor, but are responsible for defining problems, judging results and bearing consequences. This is also the true meaning of "AI must ultimately return to humans".

It does not mean AI has no value, but that AI's value must pass through human choices before it can enter the real world.

Will FDEs be replaced by AI?

Interestingly, Palantir has already launched a product called AI FDE. It can operate Foundry through natural language, performing tasks such as data transformation, code repository management and data model maintenance.

The AI FDE here is a product name, not a new human role.

It shows that some standardized parts of FDE work can indeed be taken over by AI: writing interfaces, checking logs, generating workflows, organizing documentation, testing models.

But the more complex the business scenario, the more humans are needed to handle exceptions, coordinate relationships and confirm responsibility.

A customer suddenly changes goals, the system suddenly shows anomalies, departments disagree on results, or a seemingly reasonable solution triggers new risks in actual operation. None of these problems can be solved by simply calling a model.

Judging from the current division of labor, AI is more likely to first reduce FDEs' repetitive work, rather than immediately replacing the parts where they are responsible for judgment, coordination and bearing results.

Do Chinese enterprises need FDEs?

Not every company needs to set up a separate FDE position.

In large tech companies, it may be called Forward Deployed Engineer, Solutions Engineer, Application Engineer or Industry Delivery Engineer; in small and medium-sized teams, it may also be jointly undertaken by product managers, technical leads and Sales Engineers.

What truly matters is not the job title, but a way of working: engineers cannot only be responsible for building the product, but must also understand where the product will ultimately be used; customer success cannot only be responsible for training and renewals, but must also participate in system building; product teams cannot only watch features go live, but must also observe whether customers have truly changed the way they work.

Especially in fields such as finance, manufacturing, energy, healthcare and government affairs, what AI faces is not a clean data table, but complex legacy systems, strict permission boundaries and real chains of responsibility.

Whoever can handle these problems well is the one more likely to push AI from "usable" to "valuable."

The last mile of AI is still human work

In the past, tech companies were used to packaging technical capabilities into products and then waiting for the market to figure them out on its own. But AI is changing this path.

As model capabilities become increasingly similar, what truly sets companies apart may not be parameter scale, but who can embed models into customers' business processes, who can get frontline employees willing to use them, and who can quickly identify responsibility and solutions when systems fail.

That is why the FDE has become a highly symbolic role.

It shows that the AI industry is moving from "who owns the stronger model" to "who can turn models into results."

And along this path, what may be hardest to replace is not any particular technology, but a person's understanding of real problems, judgment in complex field situations, and accountability for the final outcome.

AI can increasingly resemble an all-purpose assistant. But to truly bring this assistant into the world, someone still needs to take it into the field.
