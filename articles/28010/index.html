The FDE and the GTM Engineer Are the Same Job

JMC Go-to-Market Systems · 2026-07-27 · 10 min read

Forward deployed engineers and GTM engineers are the same job arriving from opposite directions. The interesting technical problems moved out of the product and into the customer's world. The org chart just hasn't caught up.

I keep seeing two job titles explode at the same time, posted by two hiring managers who will never sit in the same meeting.

The first is the forward deployed engineer. Product companies post it. It reports into engineering or a deployment team, and the pitch is some version of: sit inside the customer's business and make the product actually work there.

The second is the GTM engineer. Revenue teams post it. It reports into sales, ops, or occasionally the founder directly, and the pitch is some version of: build the systems that create pipeline, because the manual version stopped scaling.

Read the two job descriptions side by side and something strange happens. Strip the titles. Strip the reporting lines. You're reading the same job.

TL;DR: The forward deployed engineer and the GTM engineer are the same job arriving from opposite directions. A forward deployed engineer is a software engineer a vendor embeds inside a customer to make the product work in that customer's data and workflow. A GTM engineer builds the systems that create pipeline for their own company's revenue team. Both exist because the interesting technical problems moved out of the product and into the customer's context, data, and workflow. One walked in through the product door. One walked in through the pipeline door. The title is mostly an accident of which door it was. The org chart hasn't caught up, and the companies that notice first will hire for the builder, not the title.

#What is GTM engineering?

GTM engineering is the work of building the systems a revenue team uses to find, qualify and act on demand. The inputs are account data, buyer signals and the company's own context. The work is connecting those inputs to enrichment, routing, outreach and a feedback loop that tells the team what actually happened.

The distinction I care about is ownership. Buying another tool is a procurement decision. Making the tools work together, defining what qualifies, and keeping that system working is an engineering responsibility.

RoleWhere the work startsWhat the builder owns
GTM engineerThe revenue team's pipeline problemThe data, workflow and checks behind the motion
Forward deployed engineerA customer's deployment problemMaking the vendor's product work in that customer's systems

The boundaries overlap. That is the argument here.

#The door on the product side

The forward deployed engineer is Palantir's invention. Their contracts ran to eight and nine figures and everything was semi-custom, so instead of putting a business person who barely knew the product in front of the customer, they put engineers in the customer's building. The engineer's job wasn't to build the product. It was to make the product true in one specific company, with that company's data, politics, and plumbing.

For fifteen years that looked like an artifact of one unusual business model. Then AI made it everyone's business model.

Here's why. The gap between a great AI demo and a working AI deployment is where deals die. MIT put the failure rate of enterprise AI pilots at 95 percent, and most of that failure isn't the model. It's everything around the model: the customer's data, the customer's workflow, the customer's definition of good. So AI companies stopped shipping software over the wall and started shipping engineers with it. Sales lands the deal. An engineer makes it real.

Even the frontier labs run this play. Anthropic's $1.5B joint venture with Deloitte and OpenAI's $10B alliance with Bain are, underneath the framing, staffing deals for embedded engineers. When the model makers themselves decide deployment needs humans with commit access, that tells you where the hard problem lives.

#What a forward deployed engineer actually does all week

A forward deployed engineer embeds with a customer's team, scopes the problem, wires the product into the customer's systems, ships code to production, and feeds lessons back to product.

In practice that breaks into a few jobs. First, scoping: sitting with the people who do the work, not just the buyer, because the problem in the contract is rarely the problem on the floor. Then integration: connecting the product to the customer's data sources, APIs, identity, and permissions. Then building: custom code, configuration, and workflows for that one customer.

The part people underrate is that the work doesn't stop at a demo. The FDE ships to production and keeps iterating with real users until it holds up under daily use.

The last responsibility is the one I'd hire for. A good FDE carries what the field teaches back to the product team, so the fix they keep rewriting becomes a feature nobody has to write again.

#The door on the pipeline side

The GTM engineer walked in from the other side of the building, and mostly wasn't hired as an engineer at all.

The typical origin story is an ops person. They ran the CRM, managed the tools, kept the reporting honest. Then the workflows stopped fitting inside the tools. The interesting outbound motion became: pull signals from a dozen sources, score them, enrich the accounts, route the right message to the right person at the right moment, and do all of it continuously. That's not a settings page. That's software.

So the ops person started building. Enrichment waterfalls. Signal triggers. Agents that draft the first pass. Clay built a whole function around this person and gave the role its name, but the pattern is bigger than any one tool. The defining trait is that the person treats pipeline as a system to be engineered rather than an activity to be managed.

Nobody sent them to engineering school. The work demanded engineering, and they were standing closest to the work.

#Same job underneath

Now put the two side by side.

Both spend their days inside a specific business's messy reality instead of a product's clean abstraction. Both write code that no product engineer will ever review. Both are measured in revenue, not story points. Both live in the gap between what a tool can do out of the box and what this particular business actually needs. And both jobs exist for the same underlying reason: the hard technical problems in B2B moved out of the product and into the deployment.

I've written before that every AI workflow that survives is really a context problem. The model is interchangeable. The context isn't: the account data, the workflow, the definitions of good output. Look at who actually does that context work inside companies today and you find these two roles. The forward deployed engineer does it for someone else's business, on the vendor's payroll, after the contract. The GTM engineer does it for their own company's motion, on the revenue team's payroll, before there's ever a contract.

Same skill. Same temperament. Same tolerance for other people's messy data. The difference is which side of the transaction pays them, and the org chart treats that accident like a fundamental distinction.

#How is a forward deployed engineer different from a solutions engineer?

A solutions engineer helps win the deal, and a forward deployed engineer makes the deal work after it is signed. The solutions engineer, often called a sales engineer, lives pre-sale: demos, technical discovery, proofs of concept, security questions, whatever gets the buyer to yes. They mostly configure the product. They rarely write production code.

The forward deployed engineer mostly lives post-sale. They write and ship production code inside the customer's environment. They own the outcome: if the deployment doesn't work in that customer's data and workflow, it is their problem to fix.

The cleanest test I know is ownership. Ask who gets the call when the integration breaks months after signing. On small accounts some companies blur the two roles into one person. That's fine, as long as everyone knows which job is being done that week.

#What skills and background does a forward deployed engineer need?

A forward deployed engineer needs strong general software engineering plus real customer skills. The role breaks if either half is missing. The technical half means APIs, data pipelines, SQL and fluency in a language like Python or TypeScript. It also means comfort with messy customer data and debugging in environments you do not control. For AI products, add prompt and evaluation work.

The customer half is just as concrete. You scope the work, explain tradeoffs to people who are not engineers, manage expectations when reality disagrees with the demo, and tolerate travel.

The people I see doing this well rarely took the same path. Some are software engineers who liked customers. Some are consultants who learned to code. Others are solutions engineers who wanted to build. What they share is not a resume. It is a willingness to own the outcome inside someone else's mess, which is the same trait I look for in a GTM engineer.

#The org chart hasn't caught up

Because the two roles entered through different doors, they landed in different budgets. The forward deployed engineer is costed like delivery, benchmarked against software engineering bands, managed by people who think in sprints. The GTM engineer is costed like ops, benchmarked against ops bands, managed by people who think in quarters.

Same talent pool. Two prices.

That gap is an arbitrage, and I don't think it lasts. A GTM engineer who can make a revenue system compound is doing engineering work with a direct revenue signature, which is a rarer combination than most job ladders acknowledge. Right now you can sometimes hire that person into an ops band because the market hasn't repriced the title. The market will reprice the title.

The reporting lines are just as confused. Neither role reports to the VP of Engineering, even though both are engineering. Both get parked wherever the org had room: deployment, RevOps, a founder's direct line. Everyone senses these people are load-bearing. Nobody is sure whose org chart they belong on. It's the same lag I traced in The CRO Job Is Splitting: the org chart is always the last artifact to admit what the work has become.

#The Job Boards Already Merged It

The postings got there before the org charts did.

Attio, a CRM company, is hiring for a title that is both doors in one line: Forward Deployed GTM Engineer. The posting describes the job as working "post-sale with customers on complex GTM use cases, embedding deeply in their systems to turn complex requirements into working solutions." The experience it asks for is "2+ years as a developer, GTM engineer, or in a technical solutions role." Developer or GTM engineer. Either door gets you in. Read the posting and try to say which department it belongs to.

Clay went further and wrote the split into its org chart. In the writeup linked above, it describes two GTM engineering teams: forward-deployed GTM engineers who "work directly with customers," and internal GTM engineers who "build and maintain all our internal operations." Of the customer-facing team it adds that many "came from rev ops or growth roles." The ops person walked in through the pipeline door and ended up doing the job the product door was built for.

The budget line is starting to move too. TSIA, the Technology & Services Industry Association, reports that leading organizations are "reclassifying FDE spend from cost of goods sold (COGS) to customer acquisition cost (CAC)." Put that next to the two price bands above. The deployment engineer is being recosted as a cost of winning revenue, which is the side of the ledger the GTM engineer already sits on.

a16z has a useful test for whether a new title is real or just inflation: "does the new title describe work that would have been unrecognizable five years ago?" An engineer embedded in a customer's revenue system, building their pipeline machinery on the vendor's payroll, passes it.

#What This Means in Practice

Three readers, three moves.

If you're hiring either title, stop hiring the title. Hire the builder. The test is simple, and it's the same test on either side of the building: hand the candidate a real workflow from your actual business, messy and undocumented, and watch what they do. One kind of candidate asks for the process doc. The other starts pulling the workflow apart to see where software fits. The second one is the job, whatever your posting called it.

If you're the builder, on either side, understand that your price is set by the door you walked through, not the work you do. The move is to make the work legible: keep a record of the systems you've built and the revenue they touched, written down and checkable. The skill that transfers across both titles is context work, taking one business's specific reality and making software produce revenue in it, which is the same discipline JMC ships as a context system. A builder with a documented revenue signature gets benchmarked against the engineering band, not the ops band.

If you're running an AI-native company, you already employ the merged role. The useful move is to stop splitting it. The same builder can run the technical evaluation before the deal, stand up the deployment after it, and automate the motion that finds the next deal. Every handoff you add between those three is a place where context leaks.

#The Door Was Never the Job

I'm not going to coin a name for the merged role. The job boards are already trying one on. Naming it was never the useful move. Noticing is.

In three years I don't think these survive as two separate roles. The engineering keeps moving toward the customer, the revenue team keeps engineering its own motion, and somewhere in the middle the two titles meet. The person was always the same. The door was the only difference.

Jay
