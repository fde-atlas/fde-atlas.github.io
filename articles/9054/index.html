What capabilities does an FDE need: the six-dimension capability matrix

Original title: FDE 需要什么能力：六维能力矩阵

FDE 成长手册 · 2026-08-19 · 5 min read

Summary

This article presents a six-dimension competency model for FDEs: software and data engineering, AI application engineering, production reliability, customer discovery and product judgment, field delivery and adoption, and communication and influence. Drawing on job postings from Palantir, OpenAI, and Anthropic, the author argues that capability is not a list of frameworks. Each dimension is scored 0–3 based only on verifiable outputs, distinguishing between having heard of something, having done it, and having delivered it under real constraints. The piece offers a minimum score combination for beginners, notes that FDE hiring works like a constraint system where a zero in a key dimension matters more than averages, and provides a skill-gap worksheet template plus common pitfalls.

Key points

  • Six dimensions: software/data, AI applications, production reliability, customer discovery, field delivery, communication
  • Score each dimension 0–3 using only verifiable evidence, not potential
  • Beginner baseline: level 2 in software engineering, level 1–2 in discovery and communication
  • Hiring is a constraint system: fix the bottleneck blocking end-to-end delivery first
  • Pitfalls include stacking AI skills without engineering and treating certificates as deliverables

Direct answer: FDE = Forward Deployed Engineer (Forward Deployed Engineer); in this article it does not refer to full disk encryption. Core capabilities include software and data, AI applications, production reliability, customer discovery, field delivery, and communication influence.

Learning Objectives

Heading "Learning Objectives"

After completing this chapter, you should be able to:

  • Identify the common capabilities of FDEs from first-hand job requirements, rather than copying any single company's tech stack;
  • Take inventory of your own engineering, AI, production, discovery, delivery, and communication skills across six dimensions;
  • Score yourself based only on viewable outputs, distinguishing between "heard of it," "have done it," and "have delivered it under real constraints";
  • Identify the three gaps that most affect your target role, and rewrite them as near-term actions with clear acceptance criteria.

Capability Is Not a Tool List

Heading "Capability Is Not a Tool List"

The relevant job titles and business contexts at Palantir, OpenAI, and Anthropic are not identical, but their first-hand job postings repeatedly signal the same things: going deep into customer problems while building with your own hands; moving fast while making systems work reliably in real environments; handling technical details while helping different roles understand trade-offs and adopt solutions.

Therefore, this tutorial does not treat "how many frameworks you know" as the core metric, but instead uses six capability dimensions that can be verified through project evidence.

The Six-Dimension Capability Model

Heading "The Six-Dimension Capability Model"

Capability dimensionWhat you actually need to doExamples of acceptable evidence
Software and data engineeringWrite maintainable code, design APIs, handle data, and complete integrations using Git, testing, and containersCode repositories, automated tests, API documentation, data models, deployment records
AI application engineeringUnderstand model capabilities and limitations, design retrieval, tool calling, AI agent, or other solutions per scenario, and establish evalsEval datasets, baseline comparisons, failure cases, prompt and model version records
Production and reliabilityHandle identity and permissions, security, observability, performance, cost, failures, and rollbacksThreat models, log/metric dashboards, load tests, incident retrospectives, runbooks
Customer discovery and product judgmentInterview stakeholders, map workflows, identify root causes, define success metrics, and control scopeInterview guides, flowcharts, problem statements, metric trees, scope and assumption lists
Field delivery and adoptionIterate in uncertain environments, integrate with customer systems, train users, and complete handoverPilot plans, release records, user feedback, training materials, handover checklists
Communication and influenceExplain the same decision to engineering, business, and management audiences, and drive alignment on risks and trade-offsADRs, weekly status reports, demo recordings, decision minutes, cross-role feedback

AI application engineering is especially important for Forward Deployed AI Engineers, but not all FDEs center on large models. Choose weights based on the target role first; don't force trendy technologies into every project.

Grade with Evidence, Not Gut Feeling

Heading "Grade with Evidence, Not Gut Feeling"

Each dimension uses a 0–3 scale. What is being rated is your currently provable experience, not your potential:

LevelJudgment criteriaTypical statement
0 — No evidenceUnderstands the concept, but has no viewable output"I've watched related tutorials"
1 — Guided practiceCompleted exercises following explicit steps and can explain the basic approach"I followed the task to completion and recorded the results"
2 — Independent deliveryIndependently handled open-ended problems, made trade-offs, and passed acceptance"I defined the solution, implemented it, tested it, and explained the trade-offs"
3 — Real constraintsContinuously running, observing, and improving under real user or production constraints"People use it; I have metrics, incident records, and iteration evidence"

Having no public repository doesn't mean having no evidence. Sanitized architecture diagrams, metrics, decision records, demos, and retrospectives can also demonstrate capability; for anything that cannot be disclosed, clearly state the confidentiality boundary—never fabricate.

The Minimum Viable Combination for Getting Started

Heading "The Minimum Viable Combination for Getting Started"

For learners with "basic programming ability and zero FDE experience," set the initial targets as:

  • Software and data engineering at 2: able to independently complete a small but complete service or application.
  • Customer discovery and product judgment at 1–2: able to define problems and acceptance metrics through interviews or scenario research.
  • Production and reliability at 1: have at least done deployment, logging, basic permissions, and rollback drills.
  • Communication and influence at 1–2: able to explain choices, results, and risks in concise documentation.
  • Then, depending on the target role, strengthen AI application engineering or specific industry integration skills.

This is not a hiring bar, nor does reaching these scores mean you'll be hired. It is merely a starting sequence to avoid "learning lots of AI concepts without ever delivering a complete system."

How to Find Your Real Weak Spots

Heading "How to Find Your Real Weak Spots"

Don't compute a seemingly precise overall average score. FDE hiring is more like a constraint system: a 0 in one key dimension may matter more than an extra point in other dimensions.

Judge in the following order:

  • Pick 2–3 real target roles and mark which capability dimension each responsibility belongs to.
  • Give higher priority to dimensions that are frequently and explicitly required.
  • Attach evidence to each of your scores; if you can't provide evidence, downgrade.
  • Find the bottleneck that prevents you from completing end-to-end delivery, rather than picking your favorite technology to keep learning.
  • Turn the bottleneck into a task that can produce a portfolio piece within two weeks, then re-score.

For example, "not knowing Kubernetes" may not be your first weak spot; if you've never defined success metrics, completing a user interview, a flowchart, and acceptance criteria first may be closer to the core work of the target role.

Acceptance criteria:

  • Map at least 2 first-hand target roles that are still accessible;
  • All six scores have evidence links or clear explanations; "feeling good about yourself" cannot substitute for evidence;
  • The three priority gaps must be traceable to target roles, not to generic trending-skill lists;
  • Each next-step action can be completed in about two weeks and produce viewable documentation, code, tests, metrics, or demos;
  • Invite an engineering peer to review using the same rubric, and record scoring disagreements and reasons for revisions.

Common Pitfalls

Heading "Common Pitfalls"

  • Treating the number of languages and frameworks as capability. What problems you've independently solved is more persuasive than how many dependencies you've installed.
  • Only filling AI gaps, not engineering gaps. A successful model call is just the beginning; data, permissions, evals, observability, and failure handling determine whether a system can reach production.
  • Equating communication with public speaking. FDE communication also includes uncovering implicit constraints, writing decisions clearly, managing expectations, and driving adoption.
  • Waiting until all dimensions reach 3 before applying. Target roles, levels, and team needs differ; honest and clearly documented growth evidence is more credible than a fake perfect score.
  • Assuming certificates can replace deliverables. Certificates prove the learning process, but alone they cannot prove you can deliver results in ambiguous situations.
  • Dressing up projects with fictional users or metrics. It's fine to clearly state a project is a simulation; don't present test data as real business results.
