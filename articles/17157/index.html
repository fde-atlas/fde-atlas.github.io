How to Develop FDEs | Designing Training and Evaluations That Help Engineers Build On-the-Ground Skills

Original title: FDEをどう育てるか｜エンジニアが現場力を身につける研修と評価の設計

TIMEWELL · 2026-09-15 · 12 min read

Summary

In the final installment of TIMEWELL's FDE practice series, Hamamoto explains how the company trains forward deployed engineers. He argues that roughly half of the skills an FDE needs lie outside coding: listening, observing, being present, structuring, filming, and handing dictionaries to machines. Teachable routines are covered in training, but the eye for noticing gaps between what customers say and what screens show only develops through daily field practices: paired recording, a mandatory discrepancy column, weekly card clustering, and versioned dictionaries. Evaluation is based on patterns returned to the product, decreasing field hours, customer self-sufficiency, and reuse—not utilization rates. He contrasts this with outsourced development training, admits it takes about six months of shadowing, and notes not every engineer suits the FDE path.

Key points

  • Half of FDE skills lie outside coding: listening, observing, presence, structuring, filming, ontologies.
  • Routines are teachable in training; noticing field discrepancies only develops on site.
  • Four daily practices: paired recording, discrepancy column, Friday clustering, dictionary versions.
  • Evaluation uses pattern reuse and shrinking hours, not utilization rates.
  • Training takes about six months of shadowing and does not suit every engineer.

Hello, this is Hamamoto from TIMEWELL Inc.

This is the final installment of the FDE practice series. Over the previous nine articles, I have written about field techniques, how to run a week, market numbers, and contract templates. Finally, I will write about how to develop the people who acquire these skills.

Our company is an engineering company, and everyone is hungry for development. However, half of the skills required of engineers entering the field as FDEs are not coding skills. Listening, observing, being present, structuring, filming, and handing a dictionary to the machine. These six are not written in university curricula or programming textbooks. Our engineers have acquired these skills through external training and by repeating daily routines in the field. In this article, I will write about what is taught in training, what can only be learned in the field, and the design of evaluation. If you want to first check how much field capability your own engineering team has, please use the AI Literacy Diagnostic.

The skills required of an FDE lie outside the code

First, let us confirm what we are developing. A person who served as an FDE at Palantir for eight years lists three things required for this role: high resilience, social and political skills for embedding deeply within other companies to earn customer trust, and speed. In addition, he writes that the more quickly a person learns the customer's language and can dig into how their operations work, the better results they produce 1. The same person mentions, as books given to new FDEs, a practical guide to user interviews and a book on the theory of improvisational theater. The latter, he says, is a book that mechanically breaks down human behavior and social positioning, and was used as a textbook for the skills needed to earn trust at the customer's site 1.

There are no books about code on this list. An engineering company was handing new engineers books on interviews and theater. The reason is that coding skills can be verified at the hiring stage, but the skills of listening, observing, and earning trust in the field can only be developed internally.

A person who led Palantir's commercial business writes about the same thing from a different angle. Imitators, by narrowly defining the role at the product boundary, have lost the crucible that grows FDEs substantially, he says. Broad roles deeply engaged with the customer's mission are what develop people, he writes 2. The design of development cannot be separated from the design of the role. If the role is "someone who builds according to spec," the only skill that develops is building according to spec.

Let me rearrange the six skills written in this series in the order of development.

The reason the two right-hand columns are separated is that the design of development differs. The left can be taught in training. The right cannot. What develops the right is the daily routines in the field.

What is taught in training. Patterns are acquired once taught

Let me write honestly about the training. Our engineers received external training in interview techniques before entering the field. What they learned in training was the patterns: avoid leading questions, ask about past events rather than future opinions, stay silent for three seconds after a question, separate the person listening from the person recording. After the training, the way they crafted questions changed.

Patterns are acquired once taught. That is why our company makes sure to cover the pattern-based parts of the six skills before engineers enter the field. Interview question design, the five columns of observation, the three-layer notes of ethnography, the four steps of the KJ method, the six consent items for filming, the five extractions of ontology. These are skills that can be written as procedure manuals, and this series corresponds to those manuals. Our design is for new engineers to read these manuals in their first week, then accompany existing engineers in the field and sit in the recording seat.

External resources can also be used for pattern training. In the summer of 2026, a16z launched an eight-week fellowship for practitioners of FDE and applied AI. The format involves shipping frontier AI systems in real corporate environments while learning alongside experienced practitioners 3. In Japan as well, multiple courses for developing FDEs and programs for training in-house FDEs appeared in 2026. Entry points for learning the patterns are increasing.

However, patterns alone are not enough to stand in the field. What engineers who finish training inevitably experience in their first field engagement is a situation where, despite asking according to the pattern, the person in charge's answer differs from the screen. Whether they can think "huh?" at that moment is the next stage.

What can only be learned in the field. The eye that notices gaps

We call the skills that can only be learned in the field "the eye that notices gaps." It is the ability to see the name of a section chief in the approval field on the screen right after being told "the manager always handles approvals," think "huh?", and ask back, "Does the section chief sometimes approve as well?"

This eye did not develop in training. What developed it was four daily routines.

The first is working in pairs. New engineers sit in the recording seat at first. The senior engineer listens. The person recording writes down statements verbatim, as well as actions such as the person in charge pointing at the screen, looking at a sticky note, or confirming with a neighbor. By writing these records every day, the gaps between statements and actions become visible on the record. Moving to the listening seat comes only after one can spot gaps on the record.

The second is the discomfort column. Of the five columns of observation, the fifth is for writing "the difference between what was heard and what was seen." We have a rule that this column must not be left blank and at least one entry must be written every day. In the first week, most people cannot write anything. That is because they do not know what counts as a gap. In the second week they can write one, and by the fourth week, this column becomes the longest—that is the progression we have observed. The ability I wrote in the previous article was not acquired in training was developed by writing this column every day.

The third is the Friday bundles. This is the task described in the KJ method article: turning the week's fragments into cards, making bundles, and naming them. New engineers start on the card-making side, and the senior names the bundles. To be able to name a bundle, you need to know what that bundle is saying about the field. When an engineer can name the bundles, they have "seen" the field.

The fourth is the dictionary version. This is the task described in the ontology article: updating the dictionary of nouns and verbs every week and assigning it a version number. Can you explain how the definition of "approve" changed from last week to this week? When an engineer can explain the dictionary's diff to the person in charge, they have made the field's language their own.

What these four have in common is what Nonaka Ikujiro and others called socialization: the process of transferring tacit knowledge by being in the same place and sharing the same experience 4. The eye that notices gaps is passed from senior to junior not through lectures but through accompanying them. That is why we never send a new engineer alone to their first field engagement.

Designing evaluation. Not utilization rate, but the number of patterns returned to the product and the reduction in field hours

Training and evaluation are one. If the evaluation metric is monthly utilization, what grows is the skill of staying in the field a long time. If the evaluation is lines of code, what grows is the skill of writing a lot. To grow FDEs, evaluation must be aligned with the FDE's patterns.

We look at four metrics. The number of patterns from an engagement that became standard product features. Whether field hours decreased from week 1 to week 8. Whether the person in charge can now update the dictionary themselves. Whether patterns returned from the previous customer were reused at the next customer. This adds the person in charge's self-sufficiency to the three ratios described in the contracts article.

These four are the reverse of contracted development evaluation. In contracted development, the higher the utilization rate and the longer you stay, the better the evaluation. For FDEs, the earlier you leave, the more patterns you return, and the faster you can move at the next customer, the better the evaluation. If you don't reverse the evaluation, training will also be reversed. The test a16z cited for distinguishing genuine patterns—whether hours decrease at a mature customer 5—is both a company-level evaluation and the evaluation of an individual FDE.

There is one more thing we include in evaluation: the number of requests declined, and the reasoning behind them. Could you say "No" to a branch request with a stated rationale? This is not better when the number is high, but if it is zero, the engineer is either building everything or accepting everything. When the person who led Palantir's commercial business wrote that an FDE is the opposite of a role that shrinks customer requests to fit the product's current state 2, I read it as demanding both kinds of judgment: "Yes" to the mission, "No" to branches that violate product principles.

Differences from contracted development training

From a training perspective, here are the differences from contracted development.

PerspectiveContracted development trainingFDE training
Skills taughtLanguages, frameworks, designIn addition to the above: listening, seeing, being there, structuring, recording, handing over the dictionary
MaterialsTechnical books, certificationsProcedure manuals and practical books on interviewing and observation
Role in the fieldBuild to specDig into requirements, build, capture reactions, return them to the product
Where growth happensThe development roomThe customer's field (accompanying)
EvaluationUtilization rate, deadlines, qualityNumber of patterns returned, reduction in hours, self-sufficiency of the person in charge, reuse rate
Career outcomeProject leaderSomeone who grows the product

The biggest difference is where growth happens. Contracted development engineers grow in the development room. The customer's field is where they go to receive requirements. FDEs grow in the customer's field. The development room is where they return on Fridays. This difference is also a difference in training cost. Growing people in the field requires a senior's accompaniment and two people's worth of time. For a small company, this is not trivial. We still train through accompaniment because we want to avoid the scene of an engineer sent alone coming back locked inside a meeting room.

Another difference is the outcome. The outcome of contracted development training is someone who leads projects. The outcome of FDE training is someone who grows the product. Someone who can independently make the judgment to return patterns found in the field to the product. We are a small company, so the field engineer and the product engineer are the same person, but having one person do both is itself the outcome of the training.

Our practice and its limits

I will write honestly. This training takes time. Pattern training finishes in a few days, but for the eye that notices gaps to develop, I estimate it takes two or three accompanied field engagements, roughly half a year. During that time, a senior's time is consumed at double. For a small company, this is not light.

I will also write the limits. Sometimes people do not grow. Some engineers prefer writing over listening and cannot tolerate sitting in the field. We do not consider this a flaw. We need such engineers on the product-writing side. There are people suited to being FDEs and people suited to the product, and both are needed. If you try to make everyone an FDE, you lose both.

And there are the limits of training itself. The external training our engineers received taught them the interview patterns. But the patterns for observation, ethnography, the KJ method, video, and ontology had to be supplemented with internal procedure manuals. One reason I wrote this series is to release those manuals. If a company that wants to grow its own FDEs can use this series in place of a procedure manual, it was worth writing.

Summary

Half of the skills an FDE needs lie outside code. Listening, seeing, being there, structuring, recording, handing the dictionary to the machine. Of these six, the patterns can be taught in training, but the eye that notices gaps only grows in the field. We prepare the patterns before entering the field, and grow the eye through four daily practices: pairs, the column of discomfort, Friday bundles, and the dictionary version. Evaluation is not based on utilization rate but on the number of patterns returned, the reduction in hours, the self-sufficiency of the person in charge, and the reuse rate, and we also look at the rationale for declined requests.

The differences from contracted development training are that growth happens in the customer's field rather than the development room, and the outcome is someone who grows the product rather than a project leader.

This series is our procedure manual for growing FDEs, released to the outside. If you want to introduce this training into your own engineering team, or want to welcome an FDE grown in the field into your company, please see WARP's program or talk with us via individual consultation.
