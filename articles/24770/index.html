"What exactly should I do, and in what order, in the software development process?" "Waterfall or Agile—which should I ultimately choose?"—These are questions that every engineer or PM involved in development faces at least once.

If you start development with an ambiguous process, rework occurs with every specification change, causing missed deadlines and cost overruns. Conversely, teams that can properly define and operate their processes can maintain quality, speed, and cost management all at a high standard.

In this article, we systematically explain everything from the definition of the software development process and details of its six phases to comparisons of major development methodologies and how to create a roadmap, at a level you can immediately apply in practice.

The software development process is a framework that systematizes the series of activities from software planning through requirements definition, design, implementation, testing, release, and maintenance, in order to ensure reproducibility and quality.

It is not merely a "workflow," but something that defines "who does what, when, and how," serving as a foundation for increasing project success rates.

It is also defined in international standards such as ISO/IEC 12207 (Software Life Cycle Processes), and is applied regardless of scale or industry, from large-scale system development to small SaaS product development.

TermDefinition
Software development processA framework systematizing all activities from planning to maintenance
Development methodologyAn approach that defines how the process is executed (e.g., Agile, Waterfall)
Development life cycle (SDLC)All phases from a software's creation to its disposal

Proceeding with development without clarifying the process is like climbing a mountain without a map. The problem is that the following risks are likely to materialize.

  • Increased rework costs: The later a discrepancy in requirements understanding is discovered, the exponentially higher the correction cost becomes. According to IBM research, if the cost of fixing a defect found during the requirements definition phase is 1, it grows to 15–40 times at the testing phase and 40–1,000 times after release.
  • Inconsistent quality: Deliverable quality varies by person in charge, leading to dependence on individuals.
  • Difficulty in schedule management: When phases are unclear, progress cannot be visualized and management does not function.
  • Communication gaps between teams: Development proceeds without the business side and development side sharing a common "language."

Conversely, projects that properly define and operate their processes can control QCD (quality, cost, delivery) with high probability. The fact that project management standards such as PMBOK and ISO 21500 are structured around "process groups" also reflects this importance.

"Software development," "system development," and "product development" are similar terms, but their meanings differ depending on context. Let's organize the differences.

TermMain targetFocusExamples
Software developmentSoftware in general, such as apps and OSsCoding, quality, processWeb apps, business systems
System developmentEntire information systems including hardwareInfrastructure, networks, integrationCore ERP systems, internal information infrastructure
Product developmentProducts brought to marketUser value, PMF, business resultsSaaS, smartphone apps, D2C services

The software development process is a foundational concept applicable to all three, but in the context of "product development," the perspective of hypothesis-validation cycles (Build-Measure-Learn) is added, with a tendency to emphasize the speed of process iteration.

Software development is broadly composed of six phases. Understanding "what is done, by whom, and what deliverables are produced" in each phase is the foundation for managing development as a whole.

Requirements definition is the phase that clarifies "what the software should achieve." It sits at the very top of development and forms the foundation for all subsequent phases.

Main tasks:

  • Interviews with clients and stakeholders
  • Organizing business flows and As-Is (current state)/To-Be (ideal state)
  • Defining functional requirements (features the system should have) and non-functional requirements (performance, security, availability, etc.)
  • Creating the requirements definition document and RFP (Request for Proposal)

Examples of deliverables:

  • Requirements definition document
  • Use case diagrams
  • User story maps (in the case of Agile)
  • Wireframes (early stage)

Common failure pattern: If you proceed through the requirements definition phase without obtaining agreement from all stakeholders, "he said, she said" disputes arise in later phases. It is important to design a change management process for requirements (issue and approval flow for change request forms) from the very beginning.

The design phase is divided into "external design (basic design)" and "internal design (detailed design)."

External design (basic design) is the process of designing the system behavior visible to users. It includes screen design, report design, logical database design, and inter-system interface design.

Internal design (detailed design) is the process of elaborating the internal logic of programs before coding. It includes class design, module decomposition, algorithm design, and physical database design.

Design categoryMain focusMain deliverables
External design (basic design)User-visible functions, screens, and dataScreen design documents, ER diagrams, API specifications
Internal design (detailed design)Internal program logic and class structureClass diagrams, sequence diagrams, detailed design documents

The quality of the design phase's output directly affects coding efficiency and quality. In particular, API specifications and data models serve as a common language referenced by the frontend, backend, and infrastructure teams, so accuracy at the design stage is extremely important.

The implementation phase is the process of writing actual source code based on the design documents. It is the most core work for the development team and includes activities such as the following.

  • Writing program code based on the design documents
  • Compliance with coding standards (naming conventions, comment rules, etc.)
  • Source code management using a version control system (Git)
  • Code reviews (pull request-based reviews)
  • Performing unit tests (UT)

In modern development environments, it is common to build CI/CD (Continuous Integration/Continuous Delivery) pipelines so that automated tests run every time code is merged. This makes it possible to achieve both early bug detection and faster deployment.

Important practices in the coding phase:

  • Pair programming: Coding in pairs of two to prevent bugs through real-time review
  • Test-driven development (TDD): An approach of writing test code before implementation. It simultaneously achieves specification clarity and quality assurance
  • Feature flags: A mechanism that allows features to be toggled on/off in production. Effective for staged releases

The testing phase is a critical process for ensuring software quality. There are multiple types of testing, which are carried out in stages.

Test typePurposePerformed by
Unit testing (UT)Verify that individual functions and classes work correctlyDevelopment engineers
Integration testing (IT)Verify cooperation between multiple modules and componentsDevelopment engineers and test engineers
System testing (ST)Verify that the system as a whole meets requirementsTest engineers
Acceptance testing (UAT)Final confirmation that the system works as expected from the user's perspectiveClient and PO
Regression testingConfirm that changes and fixes have no impact on existing functionalityDevelopment engineers and QA
Load testingVerify performance under expected traffic and data volumesInfrastructure engineers and QA

In the testing phase, creating the test plan is especially important. By defining in advance "what to test, in what order, and at what coverage level," you can prevent test omissions and rework.

In recent years, with the spread of automated testing, it has become mainstream to automate regression testing and some system testing. By leveraging test frameworks such as Selenium, Playwright, Jest, and pytest, you can reduce testing effort and increase release frequency.

The release phase is the process of deploying tested software to the production environment. It is also called "production release," "deployment," or "cutover."

In the release phase, it is important to balance the release plan with the rollback plan. In case a serious bug is discovered in the production environment, you need to prepare procedures in advance that allow you to quickly return to the previous state.

Main types of release strategies:

  • Big bang release: Releasing all features at once. High risk, but simple to manage
  • Canary release: Deploying new features to a subset of users first, and expanding gradually if no problems occur
  • Blue-green deployment: Preparing two production environments (Blue/Green) and switching between them to release with zero downtime
  • Feature flags: The code is already deployed to production, but feature on/off is controlled server-side

In contracted development, delivery to the client takes the form of "delivery." At this time, deliverables based on the contract must be prepared, such as handover of source code, operation confirmation documents, and a list of deliverables.

The operation and maintenance phase after release is an ongoing activity to maintain and improve the value of the software over the long term. It often accounts for the longest period in the entire development process, and it is said that 60-80% of total costs are concentrated in this phase.

Main activities in operation and maintenance:

  • Incident handling and incident management: Detecting failures in the production environment and recovering while minimizing impact
  • Performance monitoring: Continuously monitoring response times, error rates, resource usage, etc.
  • Security patch application: Regularly applying patches to libraries and operating systems where vulnerabilities have been discovered
  • Feature additions and improvements: Extending and modifying features in response to user feedback and changing business requirements
  • Resolving technical debt: Refactoring legacy code, updating libraries, etc.

Modern development organizations are increasingly adopting DevOps and SRE (Site Reliability Engineering) principles, establishing structures where development and operations teams work together to improve system reliability.

Choosing a development methodology is a critical decision that directly affects a project's success or failure. Each methodology suits different project characteristics, so you need to correctly understand their features, advantages, and disadvantages before making a choice.

Waterfall development is a sequential development methodology in which the phases of requirements definition → design → implementation → testing → release are carried out in order, with each phase completed before moving on to the next. It is one of the oldest methodologies, having been used since the 1970s.

Features:

  • Clear completion criteria (reviews and approvals) for each phase
  • Easy to proceed according to plan, with high accuracy in cost and schedule estimates
  • Rich deliverables (design documents and specifications), making documentation easy to retain

Suitable projects:

  • Requirements are clearly defined from the start (e.g., systems compliant with laws and regulations)
  • Few specification changes are expected (e.g., internal business efficiency systems)
  • Large-scale systems involving many vendors (e.g., government agencies and financial institutions)

Disadvantages:

  • If specification changes occur in later phases, the rework costs are extremely large
  • Nothing visible is produced until the testing phase, so user feedback comes late
  • In fast-changing markets, requirements risk becoming obsolete by the time of release

Agile development is a general term for methodologies that continuously repeat development and improvement in short iterative cycles (iterations), based ideologically on the "Agile Manifesto" published in 2001.

Agile is a philosophy and set of values, with concrete practices including "Scrum," "XP (Extreme Programming)," and "Kanban."

Main Scrum practices:

ElementDescription
SprintA fixed development cycle of 1 to 4 weeks
Product backlogA prioritized list of features and tasks to be developed
Sprint backlogA list of tasks to be tackled in the current sprint
Daily scrumA daily 15-minute meeting to check progress and share obstacles
Sprint reviewA demo at the end of each sprint showing results to stakeholders
RetrospectiveTeam reflection and setting of improvement measures
Product owner (PO)The business representative who decides backlog priorities
Scrum masterThe role that facilitates the Scrum process and removes obstacles

Projects suited to agile development:

  • Requirements are uncertain and change frequently (e.g., new service development, startup products)
  • You want to quickly incorporate user feedback
  • The team can communicate closely in the same location

Lean development is a methodology that applies the Toyota Production System philosophy of "thorough elimination of waste" to software development. It was systematized by Mary Poppendieck and others, and is structured around the "seven principles."

The seven principles of lean development:

  • Eliminate Waste: Do not create unused features or unnecessary documentation
  • Amplify Learning: Shorten feedback loops to increase knowledge
  • Decide as Late as Possible: Make decisions once information is available
  • Deliver as Fast as Possible: Shorten lead times to speed up feedback
  • Empower the Team: Respect decisions made on the front lines
  • Build Integrity In: Build quality in from the start
  • Optimize the Whole: Improve the flow of the entire system, not just local optimization

Lean development resonates ideologically with agile in many respects, and in practice it is commonly used in combination with agile and Scrum in actual development settings.

MVP (Minimum Viable Product) development is a methodology in which a "product with minimal functionality that is still usable" is built as quickly as possible and released to the market to test hypotheses. It centers on the Build-Measure-Learn (build → measure → learn) cycle proposed in Eric Ries's book *The Lean Startup*.

The core of MVP development is deciding "what not to build." Before creating a feature-rich product, you narrow down to only the core features that users truly value and prioritize validation.

Concrete examples of MVPs:

  • Dropbox: Before building the actual system, they released only a feature demo video to measure user reactions
  • Airbnb: Started with a "manual MVP" in which the founders rented out their own apartment
  • Zappos: Validated demand by taking orders without holding shoe inventory, then buying shoes at actual stores to ship

Lab-type development is a development model in which a dedicated team of engineers is continuously assigned to a client as their exclusive team. It is close to a "quasi-delegation contract" in contracted development, and is characterized by securing a team as a fixed cost while allowing flexible changes to development content.

An increasing number of startups and companies pursuing in-house development use it as an alternative to hiring their own engineers.

Features of lab-type development:

  • An engineering team can be secured for a fixed monthly fee
  • Development content and priorities can be changed at the client's discretion
  • Development speed increases as the team accumulates understanding of the product
  • No recruiting or training costs required

Summary of how to choose based on project characteristics:

  • Requirements are fixed and the system is large-scale → Waterfall
  • New service with high uncertainty → Agile (Scrum) or MVP development
  • Prioritize speed while eliminating waste → Lean development
  • Market validation is the top priority → MVP development
  • Want to continuously secure a dedicated team → Lab-type development

After understanding the development process, the next important step is creating and managing a roadmap. A roadmap is a navigational chart showing "where the product or development project is headed," and it is the most important document for aligning the understanding of the entire team and stakeholders.

A development roadmap is a visual representation of mid- to long-term development plans and feature release plans on a timeline. It presents "what will be developed by when, and in what order of priority" in an at-a-glance format.

Why a roadmap is necessary:

  • Ensuring transparency for stakeholders: Non-engineers such as executives, sales, and customer success can also understand the development plan
  • Unifying the development team's priorities: Sharing what to build by when speeds up team decision-making
  • Visualizing dependencies: Dependencies, such as Feature A not being developable until Feature B is complete, can be identified in advance
  • Foundation for resource planning: You can predict when and what skills of engineers will be needed

Gantt charts and roadmaps are often confused, but while a Gantt chart is a tool for "managing task schedules on a timeline," a roadmap is a tool for "strategically sharing development direction, priorities, and milestones."

A high-quality roadmap includes the following seven elements.

  • Vision and goals (Why): The direction the product is heading and the business goals it aims to achieve
  • Themes and epics (What): Development themes organized as clusters of user value rather than individual features
  • Milestones: Important release points and achievement targets. Quarterly units are common
  • Timeline: A relative time axis such as Now/Next/Later, or an absolute time axis in months or quarters
  • Rationale for priorities (Why now): The reasoning behind why each feature is being developed at this particular time
  • Responsible teams and owners: Clarification of each theme's owner (PO/PM) and the teams involved
  • Assumptions and risks: Prerequisites for the plan to hold (e.g., completion of external API contracts) and risk factors

Steps for creating a roadmap:

Step 1: Confirm business goals and KPIs Clarify the business goals the product should contribute to (revenue, new customer acquisition, churn rate improvement, etc.). As a principle, roadmaps should be built by working backward from business goals.

Step 2: Identify user problems and needs Collect user problems to be solved through user interviews, support ticket analysis, NPS surveys, and so on.

Step 3: Identify development candidates and prioritize them Organize the collected problems and improvement requests as "epics (large clusters of features)" and determine priorities. Representative prioritization frameworks include the following.

FrameworkOverviewCharacteristics
RICE scoringQuantifies and compares Reach, Impact, Confidence, and EffortEnables objective, quantitative comparison
MoSCoW methodClassifies into Must/Should/Could/Won'tSimple and easy to reach consensus
Kano modelClassifies into must-be quality, attractive quality, and one-dimensional qualityCan be organized from the perspective of user satisfaction
ICE scoringEvaluates based on Impact, Confidence, and EaseSimpler than RICE and quick to use

Step 4: Map onto a timeline Once priorities are set, allocate them to a timeline (quarters or months) while considering team capacity.

Step 5: Review with stakeholders and reach agreement Share the draft with relevant parties and build consensus while incorporating feedback.

"We created a roadmap, but nobody looks at it anymore" — this is a failure experienced by many development organizations. The operational rules for preventing it from becoming a mere formality are summarized below.

  • Establish regular reviews: Set up a separate "roadmap review" in addition to sprint planning, revisiting the roadmap quarterly
  • Keep a change history: Recording what changed and when increases the transparency of decision-making
  • Also make the "Not doing" list explicit: Clarifying what you won't do helps the team stay focused
  • Keep it in a place everyone can access: Manage it with a document tool anyone can reference, such as Confluence or Notion
  • Link it to the backlog: When roadmap epics are directly connected to the Jira or Linear backlog, execution management goes smoothly

So far, we have covered each stage and methodology of software development as well as roadmap creation. Finally, we will touch on the careers of PMs and engineers who possess this knowledge and these skills.

There is a significant gap in market value between engineers who understand the entire development process and engineers who only handle coding. The reason is clear: talent capable of handling upstream processes (requirements definition, design, and roadmap planning) is scarce in absolute numbers, and their contribution to business outcomes is directly and easily visible.

Specifically, the following skills are highly valued by the market.

  • Requirements definition and specification writing ability: Able to translate business requirements into system requirements
  • Experience selecting and operating development methodologies: Able to run Agile or Scrum in practice
  • Roadmap planning and stakeholder coordination: Able to act as a bridge between management and the development team
  • Cost and schedule management: Able to estimate and manage progress for the entire project
  • Judgment in technology selection: Able to appropriately select cloud services, OSS, and frameworks

As of 2026, the shortage of IT professionals is becoming more severe, and demand in particular for "people who understand both business and development" is accelerating. Against this backdrop, freelance PMs and tech leads who can oversee the entire development process are in a position to easily land high-rate projects paying 800,000 to 1,500,000 yen or more per month.

Characteristics of projects where freelance PMs and tech leads are in demand:

  • Launching a startup's new product (from requirements definition through MVP development)
  • Supporting DX promotion and in-house development at large enterprises (development process design and Agile adoption)
  • Replacing or migrating existing systems (design and schedule management)
  • Engineer hiring and organizational design support (building development culture)

Freelancers with hands-on experience in Agile/Scrum or MVP development in particular receive many inquiries from startups and operating companies, and can expect a steady stream of projects.

To go independent as a freelancer and continuously win high-rate projects, it is recommended to systematically hone the following skill sets.

Technical skills (hard skills):

  • Hands-on experience with a specific development methodology (Scrum, Agile) and certifications (Certified ScrumMaster, PMP, etc.)
  • Basic design and operations skills for cloud services (AWS/GCP/Azure)
  • Experience building and operating CI/CD pipelines
  • Knowledge of API design and data modeling

Business and management skills (soft skills):

  • Requirements definition, RFP writing, and estimation skills
  • Stakeholder management and presentation ability
  • Practical experience in risk management and change management
  • Experience involved in hiring and organizational design

Example career path to going freelance:

  • Build practical experience as an engineer (3-5 years): Deepen expertise in a specific technical domain
  • Get promoted to tech lead or lead engineer (5-7 years): Gain experience in design, code review, and mentoring junior engineers
  • Accumulate PM/PL experience (7-10 years): Gain experience in upstream processes, stakeholder management, and team management
  • Go independent as a freelancer: Handle projects for multiple companies at once, focusing on high-rate upstream projects

People who can take a bird's-eye view of the entire software development process and engage end-to-end from requirements definition through operations and maintenance are highly sought after in the freelance market as well. A deep understanding of the development process is not merely a practical skill; it forms the foundation for building a long-term competitive advantage as a freelancer.

  • Definition of the software development process: A framework systematized from planning through maintenance, serving as the foundation for managing QCD
  • The six development stages: Each stage—requirements definition → design → implementation → testing → release → operations and maintenance—clarifies "what is done, by whom, and what deliverables are produced"
  • Comparison of the five major development methodologies: Waterfall, Agile, Lean, MVP, and lab-style development each suit different situations. It is important to choose the optimal methodology based on project characteristics (requirements certainty, frequency of change, and scale)
  • How to create a roadmap: Work backward from business goals, include the seven key elements, and review it regularly to prevent it from becoming a mere formality
  • Application to careers: PMs and tech leads who can handle the development process from upstream to downstream have high market value, and going independent as a freelancer can lead to winning high-rate projects
