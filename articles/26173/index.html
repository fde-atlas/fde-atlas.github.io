Who Writes the Eval in a Forward Deployed Deployment?

Falkster.AI · 2026-10-27 · 2 min read

Summary

This article argues that the eval is the core deployment artifact in forward deployed engineering: the FDE writes it before building anything, with the customer's domain experts supplying labels. Drawing on Colin Jarvis's account of OpenAI's FDE team and Saurabh Singh's handbook method, it describes experts labeling about 40 real cases, disagreements becoming a written rubric, and that rubric serving as labeling guide, LLM-judge prompt, and acceptance language in the statement of work.   • The FDE writes the eval before building; customer experts provide labels.
  • About 40 real cases are labeled independently; disagreements become a rubric.
  • The rubric doubles as labeling guide, LLM-judge prompt, and contract acceptance language.

Full text

THE SHORT ANSWER

The forward deployed engineer writes it, before building, and the customer's domain experts supply the labels. Colin Jarvis's October 22, 2026 account of OpenAI's FDE team has the engineers building evals with the customer's engineers before building the system, and a skill for writing fresh evals by the time a rebuilt deployment reached the next team. Saurabh Singh's method in the Vibe Engines handbook is the first-week version: two or three of the customer's experts label about 40 real cases independently, the disagreements become a written rubric, and the rubric becomes the labeling guide, the LLM-judge prompt, and the acceptance language in the statement of work. The eval is the deployment artifact. It is a frozen, versioned set of the customer's real cases with a target set against the human baseline, an error budget for the mistakes that are forbidden at any rate, the judge's agreement rate with human graders, and cost and latency limits. It travels into the handback to product, rides on the harness pull request, becomes the customer's independence test at month two, and is the customer's property in the contract. The product manager writes a second eval later, the one the generalized version has to pass, from the same rows. Aneel Bhusri's standard for AI running payroll, that almost right isn't good enough, only means something if someone wrote down the number for almost.

The eval is the deployment artifact, and the forward deployed engineer writes it before anything gets built. The customer's experts label about 40 real cases, their disagreements become the rubric, and the rubric becomes the statement of work. From there the rows travel: into the handback to product, onto the harness pull request, into the customer's hands at month two, and into the contract as the customer's property.
