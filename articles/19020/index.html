How to Answer FDE Interview Case Questions: The 45-Minute Practice Method

Original title: FDE 面试问题拆解题怎么答：45 分钟练习法

FDE Times · 2026-10-28 · 5 min read

Summary

This FDE Times guide explains how to practice problem decomposition interviews alone in a 45-minute session using a timer, blank page, and phone recording. It describes a six-step framework where decomposition comes only after clarifying the problem, defining success metrics, and mapping inputs. Using a fictional insurance AI claims scenario, the author demonstrates each step and lists four mistakes audible in playback: proposing solutions too early, long silences, going deep before an end-to-end path exists, and vague metrics. References to Cursor and UKG job postings show these steps mirror real FDE fieldwork.

Key points

  • Problem decomposition rounds run 45-60 minutes with large, ambiguous, open-ended prompts.
  • Decomposition is step 4, after clarifying the problem, metrics, and constraints.
  • Decompose along the data flow, not by technology, so each module maps to the bottleneck.
  • Recorded practice reveals four errors: early solutions, silence, premature depth, vague metrics.
  • Resumes should follow the framework: ambiguity, bottleneck found, metric set, first version shipped.

Key Takeaways

  • A problem decomposition interview gives you one large, vague question with no predefined scope, usually limited to 45-60 minutes, and the interviewer is watching how you arrive at a solution.
  • The six-step framework puts decomposition at step 4, on the premise that you have already clarified the question, agreed on how success will be measured, and mapped out the inputs.
  • The FDE job requirements at Cursor and UKG are precisely about doing these steps on-site with customers: finding the real bottleneck, setting metrics, and delivering a first version within days.

What you get is a large question drawn from a real-world scenario, vaguely stated, with no scope defined in advance. That is how an interview prep guide on aced.io (previously published on Exponent) describes the problem decomposition round. The question deliberately provides very little information, has no single correct answer, and usually allows only 45-60 minutes.

For a Forward Deployed Engineer (FDE), this is not a puzzle that exists only in the interview room. Cursor's FDE job posting requires candidates to lead requirements discovery alongside customer engineers, find the real bottleneck, and set clear success metrics. UKG describes the FDE's job as: turning vague customer needs into hypotheses, prototypes, and technical designs.

So practicing this round is practicing the job itself. This article explains how to complete a 45-minute exercise on your own, walks through the full process with a sample question, and explains what to check after each step.

What do you need before starting the timer?

Nothing to install. You need a 45-minute countdown timer, a sheet of paper or a blank text file, and a device that can record audio (a phone is enough). It's even better if a friend can play the customer: they only need to answer questions, no technical knowledge required.

Recording is mandatory, for a very specific reason. The aced.io guide emphasizes that interviewers will interpret your silence while thinking as being stuck. You must think out loud, and the only way to confirm whether you're doing that is to listen back.

The sample question used throughout this article is entirely fictional: an insurance company says, "We want to use AI to process claims faster." This sentence has all three characteristics of a problem decomposition question: large, vague, and unbounded.

Use the flowchart at the top of the article to control your pacing. The time allocations in the diagram are only practice suggestions, not rules for any specific interview.

Steps 1-2: Why ask before you draw?

According to aced.io, the most common mistake in this round is jumping straight to a solution. Faced with the insurance question, the wrong reflex is to immediately say, "I would use an LLM to read claim documents." You don't yet know where the claims process is actually slow.

Step 1 is asking questions that expose the real problem, just as UKG's job posting states: they want to see you engage with the problem itself, not just hear which features the customer wants. You can ask: How long does it currently take for a claim to go from submission to resolution? Where in the process does the time go? Who is waiting—the end customer or the claims adjuster?

Suppose the "customer" answers that most of the time is spent on employees manually reading attached documents. That is the real bottleneck, and a much narrower scope than "use AI for claims."

Step 2 determines how success is measured and what constraints exist. A reasonable metric is the time from submission to resolution. Constraints might include: final approval still requires a human; medical and financial data cannot leave the customer's systems.

Check after step 2: Can you write a sentence like "success means metric X goes down while constraint Y still holds"? If not, don't move on to step 3.

Steps 3-4: Decompose along the data flow, not by technology

Step 3 is mapping the inputs. For the sample question, list them out loud: forms filled in by customers, photos of receipts, PDF files, policy history, and payout rules. For each one, ask where it is stored and in what format.

This input map determines how you decompose in the next step. Step 4 is where you break the problem into subproblems.

One decomposition that is easy to explain clearly is to follow the path of a single claim: intake and classification, extracting information from documents, verifying against policy rules, entering the manual review queue, and measuring the time each stage takes.

Decomposing along the data flow is better than decomposing by technology ("the LLM part, the database part"). Each module has clear inputs and outputs, and you can say which module corresponds to the bottleneck found in step 1—in this case, the information extraction module.

Check after step 4: Can each subproblem be described in one sentence with its input and output? Is there any module not connected to the metric from step 2? Any unconnected module is a candidate to move out of scope.

Steps 5-6: Get end-to-end working first, then go deep

Step 5 is to sketch out the complete end-to-end path roughly before picking one module to go deep on. This is also the rhythm Cursor expects from an FDE: deliver a first version quickly, within days, then spend a few weeks polishing it into a reliable system.

In the sample question, the information extraction module is worth going deep on, because it is both the bottleneck and the highest-risk part. Say these things out loud: which documents are easy to extract, which are ambiguous or handwritten, and where do claims go when extraction fails? The natural answer is to route them back to the manual review queue, which also satisfies the constraint from step 2.

Step 6 is to list trade-offs and iterate. For example: the higher the automation, the faster it is, but the higher the risk of erroneous approvals; keeping a human for final approval is safer, but improves the metric less. UKG describes exactly this on-site loop with customers: build prototypes quickly, then validate hypotheses together with the customer.

Check after step 6: Did you state at least one assumption that needs customer confirmation, and what you would do next with one more week?

Four mistakes you'll hear when replaying the recording

The first is proposing a solution before minute seven. If the recording contains an "I would use..." before you've even asked where the bottleneck is, that is the most common mistake according to aced.io.

The second is long silences. Even if your brain is racing, the interviewer will think you're stuck. Practice voicing half-formed thoughts: "I'm weighing two ways to decompose this..."

The third is going deep too early—diving into details before the end-to-end path is complete, running out of time with one perfect module and four blank ones. The fourth is a vague metric, such as "customers are more satisfied," which cannot be measured even by step 6.

How does this skill show up on-site and on your resume?

On-site with a customer, this framework is the first week of a project. "We want AI to do X" is almost always a proposed feature request, and your job is to trace out the bottleneck behind it before writing the first line of code.

When reading job postings, watch for phrases like "ambiguous customer needs," "discovery," "success metrics," and "ship a fast first version." They signal that decomposing vague goals is core to the role, worth preparing concrete examples for both the interview and your first week of customer work.

On your resume, don't just write "built a document processing data pipeline." Write it in the order of the framework: how vague the initial requirement was, which bottleneck you found, what metric you set, how quickly the first version shipped, and what you hardened afterward. Written this way, recruiters can see you walked a real customer through every step of the framework.

Do three practice sessions on three questions, 45 minutes each, and listen back every time. By the third session, clarifying questions will naturally come before solutions, without you having to remind yourself.
