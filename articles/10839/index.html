FDE division of labor: What are Echo, Delta, and FDPM respectively?

Original title: FDE分工：Echo、Delta、FDPM分别是什么？

53AI · 2026-09-15 · 4 min read

Summary

The author argues that Echo, Delta, and FDPM—roles originating from Palantir—are best understood as three capabilities rather than fixed positions: judging which business problems are worth solving, building systems that can run in production, and driving projects forward. Whether one person can combine them depends on individual skills and project complexity. The article also explains how AI lets a single engineer cover more of these roles, while unfamiliar industries still require a genuine domain expert, and stresses that teams should turn field experience into reusable skills and templates.

Key points

  • Echo, Delta, and FDPM come from Palantir and represent capabilities, not mandatory roles.
  • Echo validates whether a scenario is real, frequent, measurable, and quickly backstopped by humans.
  • Delta, a full-stack engineer, must move demos toward production-grade systems with security and stability.
  • FDPM handles project management and is often a role combined with engineering.
  • AI enables one person to cover multiple roles, but unfamiliar industries still need domain experts.

When many people hear FDE, they immediately map it onto a set of role archetypes: Echo handles the business side, Delta handles development, and FDPM drives the project forward.

But in my view, these three are not necessarily one-to-one job positions; they are three different types of capabilities: who decides what should be done, who builds it, and who keeps the client and the project moving forward.

Whether one person can take on multiple roles depends on whether they possess these capabilities simultaneously, and on the complexity of the project and industry at hand—not on whether there are three names on the org chart.

First, a clarification: there is no single unified definition of FDE. Echo, Delta, and FDPM are three roles—or rather, three types of capabilities—first proposed by Palantir; they do not mean every company has these fixed positions.

Echo: First, confirm whether this is a scenario worth pursuing

Echo is more like a business expert.

Their job is not to write down every requirement the client mentions, but to first judge: is this a real business problem that exists and is worth investing in?

A high-value scenario usually has at least a few of these characteristics:

  • Frontline staff are actually doing it this way, rather than it being an imagined requirement passed through several layers of hearsay.
  • Errors either have sufficient tolerance, or can be quickly caught by human fallback.

The "quick human fallback" here is crucial.

If, after the AI produces output, humans still need to spend nearly as much time reviewing it as they would doing the work themselves, then it hasn't truly reduced the workload. A more reasonable situation is: people only need to make quick judgments and handle a few exceptions.

The value of a business expert is especially evident in unfamiliar industries.

For example, when working on a manufacturing project, if you can't understand industry jargon, don't know the default business common sense, and can't grasp the problem the other party really wants to solve, it's easy to ask the wrong questions and end up targeting the wrong scenario. Someone who truly understands the business can communicate frequently with frontline staff, keep digging into a problem, and identify which opportunities are truly high-value.

Delta: Turning a viable scenario into a system that can run long-term

Delta is more like a full-stack engineer.

What they receive is not a vague "build me an agent," but a business scenario that has already been translated: what problem to solve, what counts as success, and which steps must retain human involvement.

Delta's job is to first build a Demo, then gradually push it toward production.

A working Demo does not mean it can go live.

A Demo can temporarily skip permissions, data security, stability, performance, maintainability, and scalability; but in production, none of these issues can be avoided. In fact, even while building the Demo, you should judge whether this path could ever reach production. A Demo that can never be engineered into a real system is meaningless no matter how impressive it looks.

So Delta is not just "writing the features." They also need to consider system integration, testing, gradual rollout, exception handling, and how to minimize impact on the production environment.

FDPM: Keeping the project moving forward

FDPM is essentially a forward deployed project manager.

They are responsible for driving, coordinating, and managing the progress of the entire project, may proactively collaborate with and report to the enterprise side, and handle issues that arise during the project.

In many FDE teams, experienced engineers or developers already possess project management and execution skills. Therefore, a common collaboration model is: one business expert, plus one full-stack engineer who also serves as the project manager.

If one person can handle it, they can take on multiple roles; when they can't, split the work out.

What AI changes is not just development speed

In the past, a big reason for splitting capabilities across multiple people was simply that the workload was too large.

Writing code, communicating with clients, organizing documents, gathering information, creating proposals—all of these take a lot of time. Now, AI can help full-stack engineers develop faster, and can also help people unfamiliar with an industry build foundational knowledge in advance: looking up terminology, organizing materials, transcribing recordings into text, and summarizing questions from conversations.

This doesn't mean AI can instantly turn one person into an industry expert.

But it does allow someone who originally didn't understand a business domain to gain basic understanding faster and become a "half business expert." Some work that used to be distributed across different roles can now be covered by a single, stronger combination of individual capabilities.

I work in e-commerce myself, so I can combine all three of these things: understanding the scenario, doing the engineering implementation, and driving the project forward.

But when switching to manufacturing-oriented projects, because my industry domain knowledge is not deep enough, I must have someone who truly understands the business working with me. In that case, the better combination is a business expert plus a full-stack engineer who also drives the project forward.

The purpose of division of labor is not to make the org chart more complicated

Whether one person takes on multiple roles or multiple people collaborate, the ultimate goal is not just to complete a single client project.

Echo is better positioned to distill recurring business problems and industry judgments; Delta, on-site, will also discover which capabilities are worth reusing. Only by feeding these experiences back into Skills, templates, testing methods, or product capabilities can the next delivery be faster and more stable.

So for FDE teams, the more worthwhile questions to ask first are not "how many people do we need," but:

  • Is this scenario real, frequent, measurable, and able to be quickly caught by human fallback?
  • Does the team have someone who both understands the industry and can turn the problem into a working system?
  • Can what this on-site delivery leaves behind be reused for the next client?

AI will consolidate more capabilities into a single person.

But when facing unfamiliar industries, complex businesses, and real production environments, whether you can identify the right problem, build a working system, drive collaboration, and accumulate experience still determines whether this is an effective FDE deployment.
