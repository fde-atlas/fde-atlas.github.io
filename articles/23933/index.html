FDE Interview Preparation | Organizing Common Questions, Answer Frameworks, and Preparation by Career Background for Practical Use

Original title: FDE面接対策｜頻出質問・回答フレーム・職歴別の準備を実務で整理

FDE総研 · 2026-08-04 · 11 min read

Summary

This article from FDE Soken is a practical guide to interviewing for Forward Deployed Engineer roles. It argues FDE interviews assess three core abilities—implementation, customer handling, and structuring ambiguity—expanded into five evaluation axes including ownership and speed of judgment. It introduces a five-step answer frame (situation, constraints, own judgment, implementation, results and learnings) that improves on STAR by separating constraints and personal decisions. It maps preparation needs by current role (SIer, consultant, SWE, PdM), outlines a 2–4 week preparation process, resume and reverse-question advice, and catalogs common failure patterns, such as candidates who can only show one side of the technical-customer overlap.

Key points

  • FDE interviews weigh five axes: implementation, customer handling, structuring ambiguity, ownership, and speed.
  • The five-step frame adds constraints and personal judgment to the standard STAR method.
  • Candidates should prepare episodes covering their weak side based on their current role.
  • Recommended prep: inventory, shaping, technical articulation, and mock interviews over 2–4 weeks.
  • Common rejection causes: inability to explain technical decisions or over-reliance on external factors for failures.

What is asked in FDE interviews?

FDE (Forward Deployed Engineer) interviews evaluate three things: implementation ability, customer-facing skills, and the ability to structure ambiguity. Standard software engineer (SWE) hiring centers on coding ability, but the focus of FDE selection is whether you can get hands-on and solve problems in the customer's environment. This is because FDE, as defined by Palantir, is a role that stands at the boundary between technology and the customer. The role's definition is summarized in What is an FDE.

What criteria are evaluated in FDE interviews?

FDE Soken organizes evaluation criteria into five items: the three axes of "can you build it, can you draw it out, can you decide," plus ownership and speed. An FDE is responsible for talking with customers from a stage where requirements are not yet settled, all the way through to delivering something that works. So in interviews, you are also assessed on whether you can speak about each item with concrete episodes.

Evaluation criterionWhat is askedCommonly used questions
Implementation abilityCan you write and run code yourself"What was the most technically difficult implementation you've done recently?"
Customer-facing skillsCan you draw out problems and align expectations"What did you do when a customer's request was off the mark?"
Structuring ambiguityCan you move forward without defined requirements"How did you handle a project where the specs weren't decided?"
OwnershipCan you take responsibility through post-delivery"What is a project you saw through to the end yourself?"
Speed-based judgmentCan you choose progress over perfection"How do you prioritize when time is short?"

Where do interviewers spend the most time?

Since implementation ability is largely assessed at the resume stage, FDE interviews tend to spend more time on structuring ambiguity. Quite a few practitioners feel that "structuring ambiguity" is where the biggest differences emerge among the five items. However, this is just one perspective, and where the emphasis lies varies by company and selection stage.

What are the frequent FDE interview questions and answer frameworks?

Frequent FDE interview questions fall into three categories—technical, customer-facing, and ambiguity—and the FDE Soken five-step framework lets you answer them all with the same structure. Even if the wording of questions differs by company, the points they want to verify are largely the same. If you prepare material to talk about for each category, you'll be better equipped to handle questions you hear for the first time.

How to answer technical and implementation questions?

FDE interviews require you to go beyond "what you built" and answer all the way to "why you made that technical decision." What interviewers want to know is how you judged trade-offs within limited time and information. If you just list the languages and frameworks you used, you won't convey that you were the one actually responsible for the implementation.

  • What was a difficult implementation? → First define the nature of the difficulty (a technical constraint or shifting requirements), then tell the story
  • Why did you choose that technology? → Clearly show which of "speed," "maintainability," or "customer usability" you prioritized
  • What was an implementation that failed? → Concretely describe at what point you decided to pull back or rebuild
  • How do you use generative AI? → Talking about how you verified and evaluated outputs makes it easy to convey production operations experience

How to answer customer-facing and ambiguity questions?

Whether you implemented the customer's request as-is, or dug into the intent and reshaped it, is what separates evaluations in FDE interviews. An FDE is not an errand runner. Answers that end with "I built exactly what I was told" tend to be rated poorly.

Example questionWhat the interviewer wants to verifyPoints to touch on in your answer
How did you handle an off-target request?Can you push back on the customerDid you go beyond just objecting and connect it to proposing alternatives?
How do you proceed when requirements won't settle?Tolerance for ambiguityDid you build something small, show it, and drive decision-making?
Have you been caught between a customer and your company?Practical stakeholder coordinationHow did you reach a landing point that didn't crush either side?
What if problems arise after delivery?OwnershipDid you see it through to adoption rather than stopping at handoff?

How do you structure your answer framework?

Telling your story in five steps—"Situation → Constraints → Your judgment → Implementation → Results and learnings"—lets you convey your experience along FDE's evaluation criteria. You can also answer with the standard STAR method (Situation / Task / Action / Result), but in FDE interviews it's easier to get your point across if you separate out "constraints" and "your judgment" as independent steps. That's because what proves the quality of your judgment is the constraints under which you made it.

  • Situation: Share the customer, problem, and timeframe in about 20 seconds
  • Constraints: Which of time, data, permissions, or internal resources was lacking
  • Your judgment: What you gave up and what you prioritized, and why you decided that way
  • Implementation: What you actually built and how you delivered it (include specific technology names)
  • Results and learnings: Whether the results stuck, and what you would change if faced with the same situation again

Among practitioners, you often hear that how much of an impression you make depends on whether you can tell step 3, "your judgment," with yourself as the subject. If you keep describing team achievements with "we," it becomes impossible to read how deeply you yourself were involved.

How does interview prep change based on years of experience, current role, English ability, and company size?

The focus of FDE interview preparation changes depending on whether your current role is SIer, consultant, SWE, or PdM, since the side you need to strengthen differs. In FDE interviews, you must demonstrate both implementation and customer-facing experience through anecdotes. For this reason, your original role clearly determines which side is easier to talk about and which side will be thin.

What episodes should you strengthen for each current role?

In the FDE Soken comparison framework, each current role is divided into an "easy-to-talk-about side" and a "likely-deficient side," and you should prepare episodes for the deficient side first. How should you verify experience, English ability, and company size?

Requirements for years of experience, English ability, and interview format vary by company, so verify each company's official job posting and selection guidelines before applying for FDE roles. Mapping each must-have requirement in the job posting to an episode in the five-step framework makes missing experience clear. Whether the interview is conducted in English, and whether you may use generative AI tools during the selection process, should also be confirmed through official recruitment materials or recruiter guidance rather than guesswork. Challenges from non-technical backgrounds are covered in Can you switch to FDE without prior experience, and career-change routes by age in FDE career change in your 30s.

Where should you start preparing for the FDE interview?

FDE interview preparation starts with taking inventory of your experience, progressing through four stages over 2 to 4 weeks: inventory, shaping, articulation, and mock interviews. Rather than cramming in new knowledge, reworking the experience you already have into a form usable in interviews yields greater returns for the time invested.

What are the priorities and steps for preparation?

Narrow down to 3 to 5 episodes where implementation and customer interaction overlap, and finish them in order so they can be reused in later stages.

  • Experience inventory (2 to 3 days): Write out past projects in two columns: "what you implemented" and "what you decided with the client"
  • Shaping episodes (1 week): Write 3 to 5 episodes into full narratives following the five-step framework
  • Articulating technology (1 week): Organize why you chose the technologies you used, and how you would build it now facing the same problem
  • Mock interviews (a few days): Practice speaking aloud, keeping each episode within 2 minutes

How should you polish your resume and portfolio?

An FDE resume should not list tech stacks; structure it so the first line states "whose what problem you solved and how." Your resume becomes the basis of the interviewer's question list. Deliberately leaving some room that invites the interviewer to dig deeper makes it easier to steer the flow of the interview. For each project, include "client industry and size," "scope of responsibility," "technologies used," and "results," and keep your portfolio in a runnable state. Keep numbers to what you can reliably explain. Exaggerated figures will always unravel under probing questions.

What should you ask in reverse questions?

In reverse questions, ask things that reveal the reality of working as an FDE, such as proximity to clients, whether on-site work is required, and evaluation criteria. Reverse questions are a chance to assess the employer, and also an opportunity to show how well you understand the role.

  • How often and for how long do you go to client sites (on-site or mostly remote)?
  • Roughly how is time split between implementation and requirements gathering?
  • Is FDE performance measured by "delivery" or by "adoption and utilization"?
  • How do you collaborate with product teams?

What is a self-diagnostic checklist for the day before the interview?

The day before your FDE interview, use the following five items to confirm you have episodes ready for each evaluation criterion.

  • Have you assigned at least one episode to each of implementation ability, client-handling ability, and structuring ambiguity?
  • Can you talk about the "your own judgment" in each episode with yourself as the subject?
  • Can you state your reason for a technology choice together with alternatives you considered and rejected?
  • Can you close a failed project with what you changed, rather than external factors?
  • Can you explain "why FDE" in terms distinct from a motivation for SE or consultant roles?

How is the FDE interview different from SWE, consultant, PdM, and SIer selection processes?

The FDE interview differs in that it asks, on top of the SWE question of "can you solve it" and the consultant question of "can you present it," whether you can build it yourself after presenting. Typical SWE hiring centers on accuracy in algorithms and design, while consultant selection uses case interviews to assess structuring and proposal quality. FDE sits where both overlap, and you will be asked about "how you behaved in front of the client" and "what you implemented afterward" as a pair.

How is it different from SWE or consultant interviews?

There is an asymmetry: those from SWE backgrounds tend to have thin customer-interaction stories, while those from consulting backgrounds tend to lack implementation detail. Recognizing which side you come from and preparing episodes for your weaker side in advance is the starting point of preparation. Differences from each role are compared in FDE vs engineers and FDE vs consultants.

Who is a poor fit for FDE selection?

People who want to focus solely on writing code, or solely on making proposals, will find their inclinations misaligned with the criteria valued in FDE interviews. FDE is a role where being outstanding in only one of technology or customer work rarely leads to hiring. Differences from PdM and SIer roles can be checked in FDE vs product managers and FDE vs SIer.

What do people who fail FDE interviews have in common?

People who fail FDE interviews commonly lean too far toward either technology or client work, and cannot speak to experience where the two intersect. The reasons for failure fall into a few recognizable patterns.

What are typical patterns of failing on the technical side?

The most common way to fail an FDE interview is having built things but being unable to explain why you built them that way. Whether you were the owner of the implementation or merely handled assigned parts can be distinguished with two or three probing questions.

Typical patternHow the interviewer perceives itCountermeasure
Technology names appear but no reasoning behind decisionsNot involved in implementation decision-makingPresent selection rationale together with rejected alternatives
Sentences keep "the team" as the subjectIndividual responsibilities are unclearClearly indicate which parts you wrote yourself
Describing a perfectionist approachDoesn't match the pace of real-world workTalk about experiences of releasing small and iterating
Talking only about new technologiesNot connected to customer valueClose with who benefited and how

What are the typical patterns for failing on the customer-facing aspect?

If your stories are only about accepting customer requests as-is, with no experience of digging into their underlying intent, your evaluation in an FDE interview will struggle to improve. Another common pattern is closing your story with external factors, such as "the customer was uncooperative" or "the field team wouldn't move." Even if that was factually true, in an FDE interview it's safer to assume you're also being asked "what did you change in that situation?"

Where does lack of preparation become apparent?

A shallow understanding of the role shows most clearly in your questions for the interviewer and your answer to "Why FDE?" A motivation that treats FDE as the same role as an SE or consultant may give the impression that you haven't researched the role. Also note that rejection is often a matter of fit with the role, not necessarily a denial of your ability. Articulating which evaluation criteria you fell short on and addressing them before your next application is ultimately the fastest path forward.
