Shadow AI at clients: how FDEs turn it into use cases

FDE Times · 2026-11-17 · 6 min read

Summary

This FDE Times guide argues that a client's shadow AI use should be treated as a requirements list rather than a disciplinary matter. Citing surveys from TELUS Digital (68% of employees use personal AI accounts, 57% of those enter sensitive data), PagerDuty, and IBM (shadow AI adds $670,000 to average breach costs; 63% of breached organizations lack AI governance), the author shows bans cannot recover pasted data or remove the underlying work. The article offers a blame-free interview script, a five-question sequence, and an inventory template, then advises prioritizing high-risk, high-frequency tasks for the first deployment, building access controls early, and co-writing a data policy with the client. It closes with common trust-breaking mistakes and how to present the skill on a CV.

Key points

  • Shadow AI reveals both risk location and real user needs.
  • TELUS: 68% of employees use personal AI accounts; 57% entered sensitive data.
  • IBM: shadow AI adds $670,000 to average breach costs.
  • Run blame-free interviews and build a task/data/frequency inventory.
  • Replace the highest-risk, highest-frequency paste first; design access controls early.

A blame-free interview turns the risk of leaked data into the first list of use cases for the deployment.

In brief

  • The client's staff have almost certainly pasted data into public chatbots before you arrive
  • A ban cannot recover data already sent and does not remove the work that drove it
  • Every paste is a use case with a data type and a frequency, so inventory them and replace them with an approved tool

Take the first week at an insurance company. You are interviewing the claims team to design an internal assistant when the team lead says, quite casually: “We already do the report summaries. We just paste them into ChatGPT.”

The room goes quiet for a few seconds, and someone glances at the person from IT. How you react in those seconds decides whether discovery carries on. Look alarmed or reproachful, and nobody will tell you anything real from then on.

The scene is common. A TELUS Digital survey found that 68% of employees use personal AI accounts at work, and 57% of those admit to entering sensitive information.

PagerDuty’s 2026 shadow AI survey found that 34% of office workers had entered customer data or information into public AI tools, and 31% had entered financial information or confidential documents and strategy.

An FDE bringing AI into an enterprise almost always arrives after pastes like these.

A ban cannot recall what was pasted

The most familiar reaction is a ban. In 2023, after a Samsung engineer accidentally leaked sensitive internal source code by uploading it to ChatGPT, the company issued a memo banning generative AI tools. Its stated reason was that data sent to chatbots is stored on the servers of the companies running them.

The concern is legitimate, but a ban only stops future pastes. It does not recover what has already been sent, nor does it make the pile of reports that need summarizing every morning go away. Without a replacement tool the need remains, and people will probably just move to quieter workarounds.

Usually there is no rule to fall back on either. IBM’s 2025 Cost of a Data Breach report found that 63% of breached organizations had no AI governance policy to manage AI or prevent employees from using shadow AI. You may well be the first person to ask the question systematically.

When you need to persuade the sponsor, there is also a cost argument. The same report found that high levels of shadow AI added $670,000 to the global average cost of a data breach. Bringing AI use into view is worth more than banning it and letting it go underground.

Every paste is a use case

The core idea is simple: shadow AI tells you both where the risk sits and what users actually need.

Every time an employee pastes data into a chatbot, it gives you four things any workshop struggles to extract: which task is eating time, what data is involved, how often it happens, and what quality of output they already consider good enough.

Seen this way, your role changes. You are not there to catch anyone out. You are taking stock of what users already do, then moving it somewhere controlled.

Run the numbers first

Say the claims team has 40 people. Use the TELUS rates as a rough estimate, not a measurement: 68% of 40 is about 27 people using personal AI accounts. Of those, 57% have entered sensitive information, or about 15 people.

The figure is not for a report. It helps you ask the right questions. Once you assume as many as 15 people may have pasted sensitive data, you stop asking “does anyone use it?” and start asking “what is it used for, and with what kind of data?”

A script for the conversation

Before the interviews, agree boundaries with the project sponsor and the client’s security contact. You collect tasks and data types, and you record no names.

If you come across tightly regulated data such as medical records or account numbers, you report it to exactly one person agreed in advance. That boundary is what makes your promise of no blame credible.

Open with a line that lowers people’s defenses: “I’m not here to check up on anyone. I want to know which tasks are heavy enough that people have found their own tools, so the new tool takes on exactly those.”

Then ask five questions in order. “What do you most often ask AI to do?” “What do you paste in: reports, emails, spreadsheets?” “Roughly how many times a week?” “What does a result need to look like for you to use it?” “If you couldn’t use that tool from tomorrow, what work would slow down?”

The last question is the most valuable. It shows which tasks truly deserve priority, and it shows the sponsor what a ban would slow down. Record the answers in an inventory like this:

Task Tool, account type Data type Frequency Risk Next step
Summarize loss adjuster reports Public chatbot, personal Policyholder information Daily High First use case for the internal assistant
Draft customer email replies Public chatbot, personal Names, policy numbers A few times a week Medium Prewritten templates with data masking
Rewrite spreadsheet formulas Public chatbot, personal No customer data Occasionally Low Note it, no action yet

From inventory to approved tool

Once the inventory exists, sort the rows by risk and frequency. The row that is both high-risk and done daily is the scope for the first deployment. The reason is practical: replace it and you remove the biggest risk and gain daily users at once.

Data already sent out is for the client’s security team to assess and decide on. Hand them the inventory, and do not draw your own conclusions about consequences or promise anyone the data is safe.

When you build the replacement, design access controls in from the start rather than leaving them for the end of the project. According to IBM, 97% of organizations that had an AI-related security incident said they lacked proper AI access controls. That figure covers AI incidents in general, but it shows where the gaps tend to be.

The final step is to write a short policy with the client: which data types may go into which tools. A policy written from a real inventory fits reality far better than one drafted from scratch.

Mistakes that cost you trust

The most common mistake is recording names. A single name in a report to leadership, and every later interview yields only safe answers. The second is handing everything to security as out of scope, when this inventory is what defines the real scope.

The third is proposing a ban to be done with it, without offering a replacement tool. The fourth is the easiest to make because you cause it yourself: pasting client logs or data samples into a personal AI account to debug faster. At that point you become a row in your own inventory.

How this skill reads on a CV

If the FDE roles you are targeting mention AI governance, security reviews or working with a client’s security team, put this skill near the top. On a CV, write it as outcomes: for example, that you inventoried unapproved AI use across a team of a given size, and which use cases moved to a controlled tool.

In behavioral interviews it also makes a strong story: a sensitive situation, a no-blame promise you kept, and a deployment that came out of it. An interviewer will see that you can do the engineering and keep a client’s trust.

The few seconds of silence after “we already paste it into ChatGPT” are when you get the most honest information of the entire discovery. Do not let them pass with a frown.
