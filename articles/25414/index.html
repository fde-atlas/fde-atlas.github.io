How to Run a Weekly Client Demo: A 30-Minute FDE Agenda

FDE Times · 2026-11-09 · 6 min read

Summary

This guide argues that late-project surprises in FDE work usually stem from clients not touching the software for weeks, not from bad code. The fix is a fixed 30-minute weekly demo where the client operates real software on real data, priorities are set together, and the session ends with one written-down change for the next week. Drawing on the Agile Manifesto and Scrum Guide, the author provides a minute-by-minute agenda, stresses sending a three-part recap within an hour, and feeding feedback back to the product team. It also lists five mistakes that turn demos into ritual, and suggests using concrete demo stories as a hiring differentiator.

Key points

  • Week-eight surprises come from silence between kickoff and handover, not bad code.
  • Demos must run real software with the client holding the mouse on real data.
  • Every demo should end with one change written down and read aloud.
  • Check each demo against the client-signed brief to catch scope drift early.
  • Send a recap within an hour and route feedback deliberately to the product team.

A demo only pays off when the client uses the software on real data and both sides leave having agreed one change for the following week.

In brief

  • Late-project surprises usually come from the silence between kickoff and handover, rarely from bad code.
  • A 30-minute weekly demo must run on real software and end with a change that is written down.
  • Check every demo against the brief the client signed off, so scope drift is caught the moment it appears.

In week eight of a project, you share your screen to walk through a near-finished build, and the client’s head of operations asks a question that silences the room: “Why can’t it read attachments? Half our complaints come in as PDFs.”

That question rarely means your code is bad. It means that for seven weeks nobody on the client side touched the software. Each wrong assumption in that stretch was built on top of the one before, and by handover day undoing them is expensive.

For an FDE this is an occupational hazard, not bad luck. You sit right next to the client, where requirements can shift from one week to the next, so the cheapest defense against surprises is a fixed rhythm: 30 minutes every week in which the client gets to use what you have just built.

The idea predates the FDE role

The Agile Manifesto puts customer satisfaction first, through early and continuous delivery of valuable software. It recommends delivering working software every few weeks to every few months, with a preference for the shorter timescale. It also treats working software, rather than documentation, as the primary measure of progress.

The Scrum Guide adds a point many teams skip. The purpose of the Sprint Review is to inspect the outcome and decide on future adaptations. The guide also warns that it is a working session, and that the team should avoid turning it into a presentation.

Put the two together and a real demo needs three things: real software, a client who takes part, and an ending that produces a change. Miss any one of them and you are merely reporting.

Why 30 minutes, and why every week?

The Scrum Guide caps the Sprint Review at four hours for a one-month Sprint. Scaled down, a one-week cycle needs roughly a quarter of that. Thirty minutes is lighter still: small enough to fit in a client’s calendar without anyone asking their manager for permission.

The Agile Manifesto wants business people and developers to work together daily throughout the project. On a client site that is often hard, because the real users are busy with their actual jobs. A weekly demo is the minimum contact pinned in the calendar: even in a week when you never cross paths, you are guaranteed one round of feedback.

The rhythm still works for teams on longer sprints. PostHog’s FDE team runs two-week sprints, from Monday of one week to Friday of the next. Demo weekly and you get one check mid-sprint, while there is still time to change course, and another at the end.

A sample demo, minute by minute

Take an agent you are deploying to triage complaint emails for the customer service department of an insurance company. Before writing the first line of code, do what PostHog’s FDE team does: write the scope as a short brief and send it to the client for sign-off before committing. That brief becomes the yardstick for every demo that follows.

Minutes Activity Who holds the mouse
0–3 Restate the week’s goal from the brief and last week’s decisions FDE
3–18 The client runs 5 real emails taken from their own inbox Client-side user
18–25 Questions, pointing out errors, prioritizing together Both sides
25–30 Read the decisions aloud, lock in next week’s work FDE

The most important stretch runs from minute 3 to minute 18. You do not perform. You say: “Could you pick the five hardest emails from this week and paste them in here?” When the agent misclassifies one, do not explain straight away. Ask: “If this were yours, which category would it go in, and what tells you that?”

The answer to that question is the most valuable data of the week. If she says “you can tell from the attachment,” you have found in week two the problem that, in the scene at the top of this article, waited until week eight.

An FDE quoted by Insight Partners said a demo is worth far more than conversations. Those 15 minutes show why.

The last five minutes are for reading aloud: “Next week: add a PDF-reading step, drop the ‘other’ category because nobody uses it, postpone the CRM integration.” Anyone who disagrees says so in the room, rather than waiting for an email.

Feedback only counts when it travels

Within an hour of the demo, send a recap in three parts: what the client saw, what the two sides decided, and what comes next week. If a decision departs from the original brief, say so plainly and ask the client to confirm it again in writing. A small change left unrecorded today easily becomes a surprise on handover day.

The feedback also has to reach your own product team. PostHog’s FDE team sees itself as the fastest feedback loop between how customers really use the product and the roadmap.

Insight Partners also stresses that the feedback loop to product has to be deliberate. A single line, “insurance client needs to read PDF attachments,” attached to each weekly recap is what deliberate looks like.

Five mistakes that turn a demo into ritual

The most common mistake is showing slides instead of software. Slides show what you intend to do; working software is the evidence of progress. The second is demoing on sample data you wrote yourself: it was written to run smoothly, so it hides exactly the hard cases the client cares about.

The third is inviting only the project sponsor and not the people who will use the system every day. The sponsor approves the direction; the users are the ones who find the bugs.

The fourth is hiding what is still broken. Showing the client a half-built feature beats cancelling because “there’s nothing to see this week,” since one cancellation easily becomes a habit.

The last is the subtlest: the demo goes smoothly but produces no decisions. If three weeks of recaps in a row have nothing under “decided,” you are presenting to the client instead of working with them.

Exercise: write the recap for the sample demo

Go back to the insurance company demo. Suppose the outcome was this: the team lead ran 5 emails, the agent classified 3 correctly and got 2 wrong, both with the complaint inside a PDF attachment; nobody picked the “other” category; and she said the CRM integration was not urgent. Set a timer for 10 minutes and write the three-part recap before reading on.

A passing version looks like this. Seen: 3/5 emails in the right category, both misses caused by complaints inside PDFs. Decided: add a PDF-reading step, drop the “other” category, push CRM back. Next week: rerun these same 5 emails plus 5 new ones with attachments.

Then grade your version with three questions. Which decision departs from the brief, and is there a line asking the client to reconfirm it? Which line should go to the product team? Would someone who missed the demo understand what will be shown next week?

Turning the demo rhythm into a hiring advantage

If you want to prove you can iterate quickly with clients and not only write code, do not put “strong client communication” on your CV.

Instead, write one line that says what you did and where it led, for example: “Ran weekly 30-minute demos with end users; caught a scope change in week two and reconfirmed the brief in writing.”

Before an interview, have a specific story ready about a time demo feedback made you change course. When reading a job description, look for the passages about working directly with customers: that is where this story is worth telling.

You need nobody’s permission to start. Set up a recurring 30-minute meeting, invite the people who will use the system, and let them hold the mouse. The PDF question will come sooner or later, and it is far better to hear it in week two.
