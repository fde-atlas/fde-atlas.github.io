How to Run an FDE Enablement Workshop: A Two-Hour Course Plan

Original title: FDE 赋能工作坊怎么做：两小时课程方案

FDE Times · 2026-11-11 · 6 min read

Summary

This guide argues that a two-hour enablement workshop's success depends on the hands-on lab, not slides, and lays out a minute-by-minute 120-minute plan for handing an AI agent over to a customer team. It recommends limiting the session to two verifiable learning objectives, building labs around real anonymized errors with a backstory, having an outsider test the lab on a clean machine beforehand, deferring questions to a structured Q&A block, and ending with homework tied to the customer's own project, reviewed at the next check-in via pull requests. Common failure modes include cramming in too many topics and untested lab environments.

Key points

  • Limit a two-hour workshop to two verifiable learning objectives.
  • Have someone unfamiliar with the project test the lab on a clean machine beforehand.
  • Build labs from real anonymized errors with a backstory for motivation.
  • Pause demos every 3-5 minutes; collect questions for a final Q&A.
  • Assign homework on customer data, reviewed via pull requests at the next check-in.

Set only two learning objectives for two hours, have an outsider test-run the labs first, and make the homework land directly on the customer's own project.

Key takeaways

  • Two hours only fits two or three learning objectives; add a fourth and the lab time disappears.
  • The labs must be test-run by an outsider first, because running them yourself and the trainees' actual experience are two entirely different things.
  • The course should end with a concrete task based on real data, not a "any questions?"

Two weeks until delivery. The agent you built is already running on the customer's data, and their manager sends a message: "Could you run a workshop for our AI team? About two hours." The team has five or six engineers, and after this session, fixing prompts, adding tests, and handling failures will all be on them.

If your first instinct is to open PowerPoint, think again. Lecturing for two hours won't help anyone fix an agent alone at 2 a.m. The success of this session depends on the hands-on labs, and on what you prepare for the days that follow.

The importance of this skill is often underestimated. A deployment is only truly complete when the customer team can operate independently, and the enablement workshop is the step that hands that capability back to them.

Done well, you can move on to your next project with confidence; done poorly, a month later you'll still be the only person who dares touch the system.

How much can fit into two hours?

PreSales Collective notes that customer-facing technical workshops range from a one-hour lunch-and-learn to a full day of instructor-led training. Two hours sits in the middle: enough for everyone to do it hands-on once, but nowhere near enough to teach everything you know.

So the first thing to do is subtract. Maven's guide on how to run workshops recommends building each session around 2-3 core learning objectives. For two hours, two objectives is a safe number, because each one needs both explanation and hands-on time.

Imagine an insurance company's AI team about to take over a claims classification agent. You could teach them architecture, model selection, vector databases, cost monitoring.

But on delivery day, they only need two things. First, when the agent misclassifies a claim, they can fix the prompt. Second, they can add a test case to the eval set so the same mistake never happens again. Everything else can go into documentation.

Learning objectives should be written as things trainees can do, not things they "understand." "By the end of the session, everyone can fix a misclassifying prompt and prove the fix with evals" is testable; "understand prompt engineering" is not.

A 120-minute course plan, broken down segment by segment

PreSales Collective stresses that a workshop needs a thoroughly tested lab environment, and that the syllabus and learning objectives should be planned in advance. Below is a minute-by-minute sample plan designed for the insurance team above.

MinutesSegmentWhat trainees do
0-10Opening and backstoryLook at a claim misclassified by the agent and guess why
10-30Objective 1: Read and modify the promptFollow the demo, answer quick questions after each step
30-55Lab 1Modify the prompt for three misclassified claims and rerun
55-65BreakAnyone stuck finds a teaching assistant
65-80Objective 2: Add tests to evalsFollow the demo, line by line
80-100Lab 2Turn the errors from Lab 1 into test cases and run the eval set
100-108Hand out homeworkReceive the task, the data, and the deadline
108-120Structured Q&AQuestions collected during the session answered by topic

Note that even the demo segments must not become monologues. Maven recommends switching modes roughly every 3-5 minutes—asking a question, running a group discussion, or doing an exercise—to keep the session from sliding into one-way lecturing.

So in a 20-minute demo, you should stop four to six times and ask questions like: "If we change this line, what do you think happens to the output?"

Trainee questions are saved for the end. Maven recommends reserving a dedicated structured Q&A slot at the end of the workshop. During the labs, everyone posts questions to a shared channel; at minute 108, you group them by topic and answer them all at once, without interrupting the labs or missing any question.

Labs need a story, not just a checklist of steps

Skillable's material on hands-on lab design distinguishes two things: learning objectives, tasks, and expected outcomes are guidance for the lab author; the backstory is for the trainees—it gives them a reason to act.

For the insurance team, the backstory is ready-made in the project: "Monday morning, the claims department reports that the agent classified three flood damage claims under auto accidents. You have 25 minutes before your meeting with them."

These three claims should come from real errors that occurred during the deployment, anonymized. Trainees will see at a glance that this is exactly the work they're about to take over, not practice for practice's sake.

If you need a template, check out Anthropic's interactive prompt engineering tutorial on GitHub. It has nine chapters, each containing a lesson and a set of exercises. Each lesson ends with an "Example Playground" where trainees can freely experiment with the examples; there's also a separate answer key for instructors.

All three of these features are worth borrowing. The teach-then-practice structure gives each lab segment a clear start and end; the exercise area keeps early finishers busy; and the separate answer key means you or a colleague can help someone who's stuck without improvising on the spot.

One more detail. The tutorial also has a Google Sheets version, which the authors recommend as the more accessible option. If there are business experts or QA staff on site who aren't familiar with notebooks, running the labs in a tool they already know may be what decides whether they "do it hands-on" or just "watch."

Test the labs from a stranger's perspective

Skillable warns that internal self-testing is not the same as the trainees' actual experience, and that proper testing takes time and careful attention to every detail.

You know the system inside out, so you won't stumble where a newcomer would: the API key hasn't been issued yet, no read access to the bucket, mismatched library versions, an environment variable that only exists on your own machine.

The practical approach: hand the lab to someone who has never touched the project, on a clean machine, with accounts exactly like the customer's. Explain nothing. Every time they ask you something, that's a line to add to the documentation.

Do this at least a few days in advance, so there's still time to request permissions from the customer.

Another format worth knowing: UNC-Chapel Hill once ran a 90-minute hands-on workshop where participants played the role of an FDE, prototyping a solution for a real customer problem. The shorter the workshop, the more the environment must be prepared in advance, because there isn't a single minute to spare on setup.

Homework is the second half of the course

PreSales Collective recommends preparing a next step for workshop participants. For an FDE, that next step should be an assignment based on the customer's real data, with the deadline set for the day of the next check-in.

For the insurance team, the assignment could be: each person takes five claims misclassified within a week, fixes the prompt for at least one of them, adds the corresponding test case to the eval set, and submits a pull request.

The following week's check-in is used to review those pull requests. From them you can see who is ready to work independently and who still needs extra one-on-one coaching.

Common mistakes that waste two hours

The most common mistake is cramming too much into the goal: four or five topics, with hands-on experiments compressed to ten minutes, so nobody finishes. Almost as common is having run the experiment only yourself, so the first half hour of the course gets burned sorting out access permissions. Another is talking for twenty minutes straight without pausing—exactly the monologue Maven advises avoiding.

Two mistakes are less obvious. One is letting frequent questions interrupt and break the focus of people in the middle of an experiment. The other is assigning no follow-up task at the end of the course, so what was just learned fades before it's ever used for the first time.

From a career perspective: when reading FDE job descriptions, watch for items about training customers or handing off to customers—that's this very skill.

When writing your resume, don't just say "responsible for customer training." Be specific about which team you taught, what the learning objectives were, and which tasks they could independently complete afterward that previously required you.

What tests an enablement workshop isn't the feedback form afterward, but whether, the first time the AI agent breaks after you leave, the customer team fixes it before they even think to call you.
