How to Apply the Pyramid Principle: How FDEs Write Emails to Client Executives

Original title: 金字塔原理怎么读：FDE 如何给客户高管写邮件

FDE Times · 2026-10-09 · 4 min read

Summary

This article recommends Barbara Minto's Pyramid Principle to forward deployed engineers who write progress emails, pilot reports, and proposals for client executives. Using a logistics invoice-processing agent pilot as an example, it contrasts chronological engineer-style writing with conclusion-first writing. It explains Minto's three rules: state the answer up front, group only logically comparable ideas, and start from the question already in the reader's mind (Situation–Complication–Question). It also distinguishes the Pearson third edition from Minto's self-published version, suggests reading Antoine Buteau's summary first, and recommends rewriting one real email daily as practice, plus using a pyramid-structured document as an interview portfolio piece.

Key points

  • Executives may read only the first lines, so lead with the conclusion.
  • Three rules: answer first, group comparable ideas, start from the reader's question.
  • A week-two pilot example contrasts chronological and conclusion-first drafts.
  • Read Antoine Buteau's summary first, then the book, rewriting one email daily.
  • A pyramid-structured sample document can showcase client-facing skills in interviews.

  • For FDEs, this book is about how to think before writing emails, pilot reports, and proposals to client executives

This phrase is so common that Barbara Minto's method has become the industry's shared vocabulary.

A Forward Deployed Engineer (FDE) is not a consultant, but consider what an FDE might have to write: a progress email to a client VP, a report after a pilot ends, a proposal to expand the deployment. A safe assumption is that the recipient will only read the first few lines carefully before deciding whether to continue.

So even when the code works, a project can still be halted by one badly written email. That's why The Pyramid Principle deserves a place on the desk of every customer-facing engineer.

A book about thinking, not just writing

Barbara Minto is an American writer and consultant specializing in executive communication, with an MBA from Harvard Business School. Her course distills more than 30 years of teaching experience across multiple countries.

The book was first published in 1985. The 1996 edition was self-published by the author under the title The Minto Pyramid Principle: Logic in Writing, Thinking and Problem Solving, with four parts, 12 chapters, and three appendices, sold directly through barbaraminto.com. The more easily available option is the third edition, The Pyramid Principle: Logic in Writing and Thinking, published by Pearson Education.

First-time readers should start with the Pearson edition; read the author's self-published version when digging deeper into the problem-solving section.

This book helps you organize your thinking on any topic so you can explain it clearly to others, and it teaches you to use the pyramid structure itself to work out your points. Order matters: your points must be arranged in your head before you type the first word.

Why should the answer go at the top?

The first principle is simple: state the overall point first, then the supporting points below. This is top-down communication, where the main idea is a high-level summary of the ideas beneath it.

Imagine you are in the second week of a pilot: using an AI agent to process invoices for a logistics company.

A first draft written in a developer's habit tends to unfold chronologically: integrated the ERP API, hit timeout errors, fixed them, ran a trial of 500 invoices, found formatting errors on foreign invoices. Only in the last sentence does it propose delaying the launch by a week.

If the client's VP only has time to read the first two lines, all they'll learn is that the team is busy. Written the Minto way, the first line should be: "We recommend delaying the launch by one week to fix the foreign invoice issue; everything else is ready." The technical details stay in the email, but only as evidence for that recommendation.

Which points should be grouped together?

The second rule: points grouped together must be logically comparable, and each level must summarize the ideas immediately below it.

In the example above, three good supporting points are: the ERP integration is complete; domestic invoices have reached the required accuracy; foreign invoices still have errors. All three answer the same question: how ready is the system?

Conversely, if a group contains "we're using model X," "the client hasn't granted access yet," and "we should add a dashboard," three different kinds of information are mixed together, and the reader doesn't know what conclusion to draw. A quick test: if you can't write one sentence summarizing the group, the grouping is wrong.

What is the reader asking?

The third rule: start from the questions already in the reader's mind. This is the basis of the Situation–Complication–Question framework.

Applied to the pilot above: the situation is that the AI agent has been running for two weeks; the complication is that one type of invoice fails to process; the VP will naturally ask "can we launch on time?"—and the answer to that question is the top of the pyramid.

This rule connects directly to customer needs research. If you don't know what the client's executives are worried about, no matter how rigorously you build the pyramid, you'll be answering the wrong question.

Practice with your own emails

A reasonable order is: first read Antoine Buteau's summary article "Lessons from Barbara Minto" to grasp the rules, then read the original book. While reading, rewrite one real email each day using this method, rather than just highlighting.

For developers who want to move into an FDE role (including developers in Vietnam), this is a skill you can show others. Prepare a design document or project report written in pyramid structure with the conclusion in the first line, and bring it to interviews for customer-facing roles.

First exercise: take that jumbled group of points from earlier—"we're using model X," "the client hasn't granted access yet," "we should add a dashboard"—rewrite it yourself before reading on.

Check your work with three questions. What does the VP most want to know about the pilot right now? Which of the three points truly answers that question and deserves the top of the pyramid? Which is merely evidence, and which belongs in a different email entirely?

An employer can test your coding ability with a problem. But getting a client executive to nod after the first sentence is something you have to prove yourself.
