FDE vs Customer success engineer

Plank · 2026-09-11 · 2 min read

Summary

This article contrasts forward deployed engineers with customer success engineers: FDEs go deep into one or a few accounts writing production code, while customer success engineers manage portfolios of dozens of accounts, focusing on retention, adoption, and churn risk. A comparison table covers scope, metrics, code written, and reporting lines. The author argues the handoff between the two roles is where deployments most often fail, and that companies running only one role discover the gap painfully. Drawing on Plank's census of 1,262 verified FDE postings across 555 companies (observed 2026-10-18), only 0.5% blend both role types, including postings at Palantir, Clera, and Google. The piece closes with guidance on which role fits which situation.

Key points

  • FDEs build production systems for few accounts; CSEs keep many accounts healthy.
  • Metrics differ: deployment outcomes versus retention, adoption, expansion.
  • The handoff between roles is the highest-risk moment in an engagement.
  • Only 0.5% of 1,262 FDE postings blend both roles' language.
  • Choose an FDE when integration engineering is the blocker, not enablement.

A customer success engineer keeps a book of existing accounts healthy — onboarding, troubleshooting, adoption, renewal risk — across many customers at once. A forward-deployed engineer goes deep into a small number of accounts and writes the production software those accounts depend on. One is breadth and retention; the other is depth and construction.

Side by side

Forward-deployed engineer Customer success engineer
Accounts at once One to a few A portfolio, often dozens
Primary work Building the system Keeping systems and customers healthy
Measured on The deployment reaching its outcome Retention, adoption, expansion
Code written Production code in the customer's environment Scripts, configuration, occasional fixes
Time horizon The length of the build The life of the account
Reports into Engineering or delivery Customer success or support

What a customer success engineer actually does

A customer success engineer is the technical arm of customer success: onboarding new accounts, resolving escalations, advising on configuration and best practice, and watching for the signals that precede churn. They typically carry a portfolio of accounts and are measured on retention, adoption, and expansion.

Where they overlap

They meet at the handoff, and the handoff is where deployments go to die. A forward-deployed engineer builds the thing; a customer success engineer inherits it and lives with it. Companies that run both well make the transition explicit and documented. Companies that run only one usually discover the other's absence the hard way — either nobody builds the integration, or nobody supports it after the builder moves on.

What the hiring data shows

6 postings in Plank's census — 0.5% — blend forward-deployed and customer-success or enablement language, including at Palantir, Clera, Google. It is a low rate, and the pattern behind it is that companies tend to add the FDE title when a success role starts requiring real engineering.

From Plank’s first-party census of 1,276 verified open FDE roles across 547 companies, observed 2026-10-30. The census covers FDE-titled postings, so it describes the forward-deployed side of this comparison; it is not a survey of customer success engineer roles.

Which one do you need?

Forward-deployed engineer

  • The customer needs something built that does not exist yet.
  • The blocker is integration engineering, not enablement.
  • The deployment is complex enough to need a dedicated owner.

Customer success engineer

  • The product is deployed and the risk is adoption or churn.
  • You need coverage across many accounts rather than depth in a few.
  • The recurring work is configuration, training, and escalation.

Questions people ask

  • Is a forward-deployed engineer a support role?
  • No. Support and customer success respond to what already exists; forward deployment builds what does not. The confusion is common because both are customer-facing and both sit after the sale.
  • Who owns the customer after the forward-deployed engineer leaves?
  • Typically customer success or a support engineering team, and the transition is the highest-risk moment in the engagement. A deployment handed over without documentation, runbooks, and a named owner tends to decay quickly.
