FDE Career Transition to Startups: Can a Forward Deployed Engineer Become a Founder?

Original title: FDE 转型创业：前线部署工程师能当创始人吗

FDE Times · 2026-10-09 · 6 min read

Summary

The article asks whether years as an FDE prepare you to found a startup, and answers: only halfway. Skills like on-site discovery, demoing at the second meeting, and shipping small versions first transfer directly. What does not transfer is leverage—abstracting one client's solution into a reusable product. At large companies, product teams handle that; founders must do it alone or drift into a low-margin services business. Evidence for Palantir as a founder factory exists but is thin, and the author advises engineers to pick FDE roles that involve feeding patterns back to product teams.

Key points

  • FDE experience provides only half the founder skill set; leverage must be practiced deliberately.
  • Palantir's founder-factory reputation rests on weak samples, and its startup PMs outnumber named FDE founders.
  • On-site discovery, fast demos, and small releases transfer directly to founding.
  • Without high-value contracts and reusable insights, an FDE-style startup becomes a low-margin services firm.
  • Choose FDE jobs that involve writing shared code and feeding patterns back to product teams.

The ability to find the right problem is something you can take with you; turning a single customer's solution into a leveraged business is not, and that latter part is what requires deliberate practice.

Key Takeaways

  • Discovering needs one customer at a time, bringing a demo to the second meeting, and shipping a small version first—these habits transfer directly from FDE work to founding a startup.
  • Leverage does not come automatically. Without high-value contracts and reusable insights, the company slides into a low-margin services business.
  • At a large company, the FDE hands common patterns back to headquarters, where the product team handles them. As a founder, there is no headquarters; you have to close that loop yourself.

In June 2025, Y Combinator CEO Garry Tan advised founders to think of themselves as "Forward Deployed Engineers." In other words, YC holds up the way engineers embedded at customer sites work as a model for startup founders.

This advice raises the reverse question, which is more practical for many engineers: since founders should work like FDEs, can spending a few years as a Forward Deployed Engineer (FDE) turn you into a founder?

The short answer is: only halfway. What you can take with you is the method for finding problems worth solving. What does not come automatically is turning a solution built for one customer into a leveraged business. If you plan to use an FDE role as a springboard to founding a company, the second part is what requires deliberate practice.

The founder factory is real, but the evidence is thinner than the legend

The claim that "Palantir is a founder factory" is not baseless. The fund Concept VC analyzed founders who came out of Palantir and found that FDE was the most common background. Named examples include Sourcegraph co-founder Quinn Slack, who was an FDE at Palantir.

But the authors themselves admit the sample is not rigorous, may be incomplete, and that Palantir's job titles are not standardized. ElevenLabs' Mati Staniszewski appears on the same page listed as an FDE in one place and as a Deployment Strategist in another.

Nabeel Qureshi, who spent about eight years as an FDE at Palantir, appeared on an episode of the Lenny's Newsletter podcast whose title called Palantir a "founder factory."

The episode notes mention that nearly a third of Palantir's PMs later went on to found companies. Note that this refers to PMs, not FDEs, and it is a summary line on the page, not a direct quote from him.

Concept VC also left an important question open: with Palantir hiring aggressively in the coming years, it is unclear whether this wave of founders can continue. A role that hires in bulk struggles to maintain the quality of experience it offered when it was scarce.

So a title alone does not make founders. What truly makes founders is a set of specific habits that FDE work forces you to build.

What you can take with you: discovering needs one customer at a time

PostHog pinpointed the core difference: unlike a typical software engineer, an FDE does product discovery and product development with one customer at a time.

This is exactly the early founder's job. YC partners describe the playbook concretely: by the second meeting, the people on the customer's side should see a demo built from what they said in the first conversation.

The fund CRV also believes the FDE role is most valuable during the search for product-market fit (PMF), and that at this stage the founders and core team should be on-site themselves.

Imagine you are an FDE at a logistics company. In the first meeting, a warehouse manager complains that every morning they spend an hour reconciling orders across two systems. A week later you return with a reconciliation script running on their real data. It has no interface; it just prints out a list of orders that don't match.

The second meeting goes differently. Instead of describing the problem in general terms, they go through it line by line: this one is right, that one is wrong, because there's a rule you didn't know about. You learn more in this one session than in three customer interviews combined.

Gyani, a former Palantir engineer who became an AI founder, highlighted the companion habit: always build the small version of the problem first, because you can always scale later. Another Palantir alum, Pratap Ranade, added the market logic: solve one problem and you'll be asked to solve the next.

In the example above, the reconciliation script is a ticket that gets the warehouse manager talking to you about vehicle dispatch. Ranade put it in one sentence: great products are ultimately built in the field. Listening on-site, demoing quickly, and shipping small to earn bigger problems—these three habits carry over almost unchanged into the founder role.

What does not come automatically: leverage

Qureshi described Palantir's core model this way: solve one very large, very valuable problem for one customer, then abstract that solution into a product. That sentence has two halves. As an FDE at a large company, you mostly practice the first half.

The second half is usually someone else's job. The fund Flybridge described the division of labor: the FDE brings patterns discovered in the field back to headquarters, where the product team packages them into features other customers can reuse. When you start your own company, there is no headquarters to receive those patterns—only you.

What happens if you skip the second half? Flybridge warns that the FDE model is only profitable when it has leverage, meaning high-value contracts and reusable insights. Without those two things, the company slides into a labor-intensive, unscalable, low-margin model.

Back to the logistics example: suppose you've quit and gone solo. The first customer needs a reconciliation script, the second needs it adapted to a different data format, the third needs a new rule added. If you have to rewrite most of the code each time, you're selling hours, not a product.

The question a founder must ask is: which parts of these three jobs are the same. Only when you can distill that part into something the fourth customer can install in a day do you have leverage.

PostHog adds a blunt condition: the point of the FDE strategy is to earn the right to solve bigger problems. If the standard PMF playbook already works, you shouldn't use the FDE approach. Founders from an FDE background tend to make the opposite mistake: habitually sitting next to the customer even when the market is ready to buy a self-serve product.

Two columns within the same experience

Putting these views together, the same FDE experience splits into two very different parts:

The first three rows are capital you can take with you. The last three are what you must practice on top, and the earlier the better.

What engineers should be building now

If founding a company is the long-term goal, choose an FDE job by looking at the right-hand column, not just the title.

When reading a job description, look for wording that indicates the FDE will feed insights back to the product team or personally write shared code, not just "integrate and support customers." A role that only lets you do the first half teaches you the craft of the service industry, not the craft of founders.

In your day-to-day work, build a habit of keeping records: for each customer request, note whether it is specific to one customer or needed by several. A few months later, that list becomes a draft product roadmap, and it is exactly the abstraction exercise Qureshi sees as the core of the model.

On your resume, don't just write "deployed a solution for customer X." Tell the full loop: a customer's problem, the small version you delivered, and which part of it was later reused by other customers. When a recruiter or investor reads that line, they'll see you've done both halves.

Garry Tan's advice is valuable precisely because it describes a way of working, not a job title. If you've been an FDE but never had to answer "how long until the fourth customer gets this installed?", you've only learned half the founder's craft.
