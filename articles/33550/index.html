What is the Palantir clique: why FDEs produce so many founders

Original title: Palantir 帮是什么：为何 FDE 多出创始人

FDE Times · 2026-11-24 · 5 min read

Summary

This article examines why so many founders come out of Palantir's Forward Deployed Engineer (FDE) role. Concept VC counts over 111 companies founded by Palantir alumni, with FDE the most common background among these founders. Drawing on Nabeel Qureshi's recollections and a16z's analysis, the author argues that spending three to four days per week on-site at customers for months trains engineers to hear problems directly and to read power dynamics in meetings—skills founders need. The key caveat: the model works because custom work sits atop a real platform; without one, FDE roles become pure services work. The article closes with three signals for evaluating FDE job postings and interview questions to ask.

Key points

  • Concept VC counts 111+ companies founded by Palantir alumni; FDE is the most common founder background.
  • FDEs spend 60-80% of their time on-site, hearing problems directly rather than through tickets or intermediaries.
  • On-site work builds intuition for meeting-room power dynamics, needed for selling, hiring, and negotiating.
  • a16z argues the Palantir model works only because custom work sits above a real platform.
  • Job seekers should check for on-site time, deployment ownership, and a feedback channel to platform teams.

Key takeaways

  • Concept VC has counted more than 111 companies founded by Palantir alumni, and the most common background among these founders is FDE.
  • This role has engineers embedded at customer sites three to four days a week, reading the power dynamics in the room and deciding for themselves what to do.
  • a16z believes the Palantir model works because there is a real platform underneath the custom work; without a platform, the FDE role easily degenerates into pure services work.

Concept VC has counted more than 111 companies founded by Palantir alumni. When reviewing the résumés of the founders behind these companies, one title appears more often than any other: Forward Deployed Engineer (FDE).

a16z calls this group the "Palantir mafia," a cohort of founders and operators formed by people who left the FDE role over time. Anduril is one example, co-founded by two Palantir alumni, Brian Schimpf and Matt Grimm.

Whether Palantir was simply lucky doesn't matter that much to frontline engineers. The more worthwhile question is: when people in a job keep leaving to start companies, what exactly did that job train them in?

Whether or not you plan to start a company, these abilities are worth cultivating deliberately. Just as important is being able to tell when a job with an FDE title has stopped training them.

Most of the week spent at the customer

Former Palantir engineer Nabeel Qureshi recalls that FDEs often had to "go on-site," working at customer offices three to four days a week. What a16z observed from the outside is the same pattern: FDEs are embedded inside customer organizations, often for months at a time.

Do the math on a five-day work week. Being on-site three to four days means that 60% to 80% of the time, problems are heard directly from the people experiencing them, rather than through tickets or a product manager's summaries. Engineers at headquarters usually receive problems that have passed through several layers of intermediaries.

And this is exactly what early-stage founders lack. They need to know which problems hurt enough that someone will pay to solve them, and they need to know it fast. Few engineering roles offer this intensity of exposure to real problems: three to four days a week, sustained over months.

The hardest skills to teach aren't in the code

Qureshi is candid about why he believes former FDEs often make good founders. In his view, good founders have an intuition for conference rooms, group dynamics, and power, which is one reason people who have done FDE work tend to be good at starting companies.

There is reason to believe this ability is hard to build at headquarters, where the power structure is already drawn on the org chart. A customer site is the opposite: the person who signs the contract is not necessarily the person who decides whether the system gets used.

Take a hypothetical example. A VP supports the project, but the data sits with an operations lead who sees the project as a threat to her team.

An FDE who reports only to the VP would watch the data pipeline slow down without knowing why. An FDE who knows how to read the room would sit down with that operations lead in the first week and turn her into the first user.

This intuition is built meeting by meeting. Founders need it when selling to their first customer, hiring, and negotiating with investors.

Palantir screened for it at the hiring stage

The work itself doesn't explain everything. Qureshi believes Palantir's hiring screens for a particular type of person: people who can think independently. He also believes the company's culture of tolerating dissent was inherited from the "PayPal mafia."

a16z supplies the other half: Palantir spent over a decade recruiting and developing exceptionally strong generalist engineers. Hiring and daily work push in the same direction. The company selects people who are already self-driven, then puts them in a role that forces them to be self-driven every day.

No one can cleanly separate how much credit goes to selection versus the work itself. But for engineers planning their careers, the distinction is still useful: you can't choose whether you'd pass Palantir's hiring bar, but you can choose what kind of environment to put yourself in.

From FDE work to founder work

Every FDE habit described by Qureshi and a16z maps to a specific founder task. The last column gives ways to practice these habits before you land an FDE role.

FDE habitCorresponding founder taskHow to practice it in your current role
Embedded on-site 3-4 days a week for monthsFinding problems painful enough that customers will payAsk to shadow the support or sales team one day a month
Reading the room, the group, and the power dynamicsSelling to the first customer, hiring, negotiatingAfter every meeting, note who actually made the call
Self-driven, not waiting for work to be assignedNo one assigns you workTake on an ambiguous problem and write the scope yourself
Doing custom work on top of a shared platformDeciding what should become a productSplit each project's code into custom parts and reusable parts

The last row is the most overlooked, and the most important.

Not every FDE role produces founders

a16z raises a condition that many companies imitating the FDE model overlook: Palantir works because there is a real platform underneath the custom work. a16z is talking about the business model, but the same condition likely also determines what an FDE can learn.

One reasonable reading goes like this. With a platform underneath, every piece of custom work raises a question: which part is needed only by this customer, and which part is worth turning into a general feature? Answering that question is product judgment, which founders use every week.

Without a platform underneath, everything is custom, and what you learn is how to deliver on time, not how to see the product clearly.

So when reading FDE job postings, look for three signals: explicitly stated on-site or customer-facing time, ownership of deployment outcomes, and a concrete feedback channel to the product or platform team.

In the interview, ask directly when the last piece of code an FDE wrote made it into the platform. If the interviewer can't answer, you already have your answer.

When writing your résumé, don't just list the customers you deployed for. Spell out which part of that custom work was reused by the second and third customer. That one line shows the reader you can tell one-off work from work worth turning into a product.

The so-called Palantir mafia comes from a way of working: putting independently minded people on-site at customer offices for months, with a real enough platform behind them that every lesson learned has somewhere to go. You can go find such a role, or you can build it at your current company.
