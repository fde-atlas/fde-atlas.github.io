What is FDE outsourcing: the difference between FDE-as-a-Service and vendor FDEs

Original title: FDE 外包是什么：FDE 即服务与厂商 FDE 区别

FDE Times · 2026-10-21 · 5 min read

Summary

The article traces how the FDE role, once tied to vendors like Palantir, has become an outsourced service line. Companies such as &above and Entrans rent out FDEs, while OpenAI, Anthropic, and AWS have launched FDE initiatives. Its core argument: whoever pays defines the job. Vendor FDEs are measured by selling software and retaining customers; outsourced FDEs promise clients can own and extend systems after handover. Citing Gartner and Forrester warnings about vendor lock-in, overly broad access, and speed-driven blind spots, it proposes verifiable handover outcomes and access logs as practical tests for buyers and job seekers.

Key takeaways

  • Companies like &above and Entrans sell FDE as a service: they hire and manage the engineers, then embed them with client teams.
  • A vendor FDE is evaluated on helping sell software and retain customers; an outsourced FDE promises that the client can run the system without them.
  • Gartner and Forrester warn about vendor lock-in, overly broad access, and blind spots created by the pursuit of speed, making handover capability an advantage in the job market.

The services page of UK company &above sums up its business model in one sentence: it hires and manages engineers who build systems alongside your team.

What is being sold here is the Forward Deployed Engineer (FDE). A few years ago, this title belonged almost exclusively to employees of software companies like Palantir.

At first glance, this is just outsourcing with a trendy new name. But when FDE becomes a service line and companies emerge specifically to rent out engineers, the question "what does an FDE do" depends on another question: who pays the FDE?

For engineers with two to eight years of experience who want to pursue this career path, this distinction matters. Two job postings can carry the same title, but different employers will judge you by nearly opposite standards. The ability that makes you stand out at one employer may be worthless at another.

The FDE title is no longer exclusive to vendors

Tom Hollands of a16z believes that at Palantir, solutions and integration engineering was once seen as lower-status work, and the FDE title elevated it. A prestigious job title will sooner or later be put up for sale.

Today, many companies including Accenture, McKinsey, and Deloitte are partnering with AI labs to run, fund, or train FDE teams.

According to an AI newsletter, OpenAI and Anthropic have each set up FDE joint ventures in recent months, and AWS is also investing in an FDE unit.

Below the big companies, there is a layer of smaller firms, such as &above and Entrans, that sell FDE as a standalone service.

SD Times believes FDEs complement rather than replace traditional consultants, because they cover exactly the areas consultants often handle poorly. It also notes that FDEs can be direct employees of technology vendors like Salesforce. As a result, the title alone no longer reveals who an engineer works for.

Whoever pays determines what you optimize for

When comparing FDEs and consultants, Webfuse says the difference lies in the business model. An FDE works for a product vendor, so every deployment helps the vendor sell software and retain customers.

If a vendor FDE builds the system so well that the customer no longer needs the product, they are working against their own employer.

The incentives of an outsourced FDE are exactly the opposite. &above describes its service as embedding senior engineers with client teams to own a scoped workflow, with a commitment to transfer capability to client staff. It also promises that by the end of the engagement, the client team owns the system and can continue to scale it without &above.

Entrans distinguishes its service from staff augmentation. In its view, FDE-as-a-service must continue to build and deploy solutions rather than stop at providing billable hours. These three definitions lead to three very different success criteria:

Does the promise "you won't need us anymore" hold up?

A company promising handover does not mean handover will happen. According to The Register, guidance published by Gartner in March warned buyers to be wary of poorly designed FDE engagements, which can leave clients heavily dependent on the vendor.

A June report from Forrester also warned that the pursuit of quick wins easily creates blind spots, with considerable risk.

These are two sides of the same problem. The faster an FDE moves, the more system knowledge concentrates in the heads of those writing the code, while the client team just watches from the sidelines.

&above's promise that clients can scale the system without it directly addresses the dependency issue Gartner describes. But the promise only has value if it is turned into verifiable handover outcomes.

Imagine an insurance company hiring an FDE team to automate claims triage.

If, on the final day, the internal team has never independently deployed a change, never independently handled an incident, and cannot read the logs or figure out where the model went wrong, then no matter how well the code runs, the engagement has produced dependency.

A practical test: while the FDE is still on site, have the client team add a new claims type, but forbid the FDE from touching the keyboard.

Every step should be signed off by someone on the client side, not marked as done by the FDE itself. The client's technical lead confirms the deployment, the on-call operations engineer signs off on incident handling, and the workflow owner accepts the final testing.

Access: selling point or risk?

Some service providers promote engineers working inside the client's VPC as a security selling point, because data never leaves the company's infrastructure. Gartner looks at the same detail from the opposite angle: FDEs often hold full-stack access, able to enter both the client environment and the vendor's codebase.

Both views are correct, and the person who resolves this tension is usually the FDE. Working inside a VPC keeps data in one place, but it also means an engineer who is not on the client's payroll holds the keys to the client's most critical systems.

A responsible outsourced FDE treats access as part of the handover plan.

From the first week, they document what access they have been granted, why it is needed, and when it will be returned, and ask the client's security team to approve this list.

For the client, this list is more convincing than any copy on a services page.

If you want to enter the field via this path

If your target is a company that rents out FDEs, be clear about what you are being hired to do. In this model, the product is the capability you leave behind with the client, not the thing you built with your own hands.

How the two sides describe themselves is enough to tell them apart. &above writes about "senior engineers embedded with your team, owning a scoped workflow," with the goal of the client team owning the system. Webfuse describes a vendor FDE with a completely different goal: helping sell and retain software through deployments.

When reading job postings, see which side they lean toward. Phrases like "scoped workflow," "capability transfer," and "embedded with client team" mean you will be evaluated on handover. If the posting only talks about billable hours, start dates, and tech stacks, it is most likely staff augmentation with a new title.

Writing "built a document classification data pipeline" on your resume tells only half the story. Add the other half: how many people on the client team took over the work, and what they accomplished on their own after you left.

Runbooks, pair programming with client engineers, and an instance where you proactively revoked your own access are all evidence you can present when applying for these roles.

At vendors, a good FDE makes customers reluctant to leave the product; at companies that rent out FDEs, a good FDE makes customers no longer need them. The market needs both kinds of engineers.
