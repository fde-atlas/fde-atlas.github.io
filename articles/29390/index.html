What Is the Palantir Decomposition Interview? (How the FDE Round Works)

Design Gurus · 2026-11-14 · 4 min read

Summary

This article explains Palantir's decomposition interview, a roughly 60-minute, mostly code-free round common in forward deployed engineer (FDE) onsites. The interviewer plays a customer with a vague need, and the candidate must lead: ask clarifying questions, agree on scope, decompose the problem, and sketch a buildable first version. The author lays out a five-step method, the grading criteria (structured breakdown, user empathy, data reasoning, realistic scope, iteration), common mistakes, and prep resources including a free GitHub guide and practice problem banks.

Key points

  • The decomposition round is 60 minutes, code-free, and reportedly rejects many strong coders.
  • The interviewer acts as a customer with an unclear need; the candidate drives the conversation.
  • Five steps: clarify user and data, state assumptions, pick minimal scope, decompose, plan iteration.
  • Grading rewards structured breakdown, named users, explicit data sources, and shippable scope.
  • Top mistakes: designing before asking, hidden assumptions, over-scoping, and ignoring data schemas.

The Palantir decomposition interview is a 60 minute, mostly code-free round where you break a vague real-world problem into parts and propose a solution that could actually be built. It appears in nearly every Palantir forward deployed engineer (FDE) onsite, and prep guides describe it as the round that rejects the most strong coders. The interviewer acts as a customer with an unclear need. You lead the conversation: ask questions, agree on scope, then sketch the data, the services, and the first version you would ship.

Quick Overview

Part Time What happens
Clarify 10 to 15 minutes Questions about users, data, and what success means
Scope About 5 minutes Agree on the first problem to solve
Decompose 15 to 20 minutes Split the problem into parts and order them
Design 15 to 20 minutes Data model, data sources, APIs, and where logic or a model fits
Iterate 5 to 10 minutes What version two adds, and how you would measure it

Example Questions

Prep guides and candidate reports list questions like these:

  • Design a system to improve traffic in New York City.
  • Design a sync system between two employee record systems.
  • Design a way for several teams to query a shared dataset without seeing raw records.
  • Design an app to catalog species while exploring an unfamiliar environment.

None of these questions has a single correct answer. They are vague on purpose, because a real customer rarely arrives with a clear specification.

A Five-Step Method

Step 1: Ask about the user and the data first. Who uses the system, what decision do they make, and what data exists today? For the traffic question, the user might be a city planner who decides where to change signal timing, and the data might be signal logs, road sensors, and accident reports.

Step 2: State your assumptions. Say them out loud, for example: "I will assume we have sensor data every five minutes for the 200 busiest intersections." Interviewers cannot grade reasoning they cannot hear.

Step 3: Choose the smallest valuable scope. Propose one problem to solve first, such as finding the ten intersections with the worst delay at rush hour. Then ask the interviewer to confirm it. A narrow scope that works is graded higher than a complete system that never ships.

Step 4: Break it into parts. A typical breakdown has four parts: getting the data in, cleaning and joining it, the logic or model that produces an answer, and the screen or report the user sees. For each part, name the data it needs and the API it exposes. Point out where an algorithm or a model would help and which data would feed it.

Step 5: Plan the next version. Say how you would measure whether version one helped, such as average delay at the chosen intersections, and what you would add next. This shows you think like an engineer who stays with the customer after launch.

What Interviewers Grade

  • Structured breakdown. The problem is split into clear parts in a sensible order.
  • User empathy. The design serves a named user making a named decision.
  • Data reasoning. Sources, schemas, and joins are named explicitly.
  • Realistic scope. The first version could be built in weeks, not years.
  • Iteration. There is a clear path from version one to a better system.

Common Mistakes

  • Designing before asking. Jumping to databases and queues in the first two minutes is the most common failure.
  • Hidden assumptions. An assumption you did not say looks like a gap in your thinking.
  • Building everything. Proposing every feature at once signals poor judgment about time.
  • Forgetting the data. A design with no data sources and no schema cannot be built.
  • Ignoring pushback. When the interviewer changes a requirement, adjust the plan instead of defending the old one.

Where It Fits in the Loop

Decomposition is one of five Palantir onsite rounds, together with learning, coding, re-engineering, and system design, and Palantir usually picks three of the five. The full sequence is in What is the Palantir interview process like?, and the related design round is covered in What to expect in the Palantir system design interview.

How to Prepare

  • Practice with a timer. Pick one vague problem a day, spend ten minutes only on questions, then design for twenty.
  • Review the question patterns. The Decomposition Interview is a free guide on GitHub with a time budget for the 60 minutes, seven patterns that cover most questions, and a short worked example.
  • Learn the general FDE loop. What is the forward deployed engineer interview process like? shows how decomposition compares to other companies' versions.
  • Practice decomposition with a problem bank. Grokking the Forward Deployed Engineer Interview teaches decomposition patterns with a bank of practice problems, plus the learning and re-engineering rounds.
  • Strengthen data modeling. Schemas, joins, and pipelines appear in almost every answer. Grokking the System Design Interview covers data storage and pipelines in depth.
  • Practice speaking to a non-engineer. Explain your design to a friend outside tech, and notice where they get lost.
