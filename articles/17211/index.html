What's the difference between an FDE and AI-companion support? From the perspective of what's being sold, they're entirely different things

Original title: FDEとAI伴走支援は何が違うのか｜売っているものから見ると別物です

TIMEWELL · 2026-08-13 · 16 min read

Summary

A TIMEWELL author argues that AI advisory support and Forward Deployed Engineers (FDEs) are fundamentally different businesses, not just naming variants. Citing a July 2025 MIT NANDA report, he notes companies invested an estimated $30–40 billion in generative AI, yet 95% of organizations saw no measurable returns—the barrier is learning and implementation, not model quality. Advisory work sells billable hours (measured by utilization, deliverables owned by the client, read-only access), while FDE work sells product adoption (measured by time-to-value, assets retained on the vendor's platform, and write access to production systems, which demands permissions, simulation, human oversight, and audit trails). He traces FDE origins to Palantir, cites a16z's "trading margin for moat" thesis, OpenAI's new DeployCo (over $4 billion initial investment), and Anthropic's embedding of FDEs at FIS, warning that FDEs without knowledge feedback loops are just expensive contracting.

Key points

  • MIT NANDA: 95% of organizations get no measurable GenAI returns; the barrier is learning, not models.
  • Advisory sells hours; FDE sells product—opposite metrics and incentives.
  • Writing to production systems requires permissions, simulation, human oversight, and auditability.
  • OpenAI launched DeployCo ($4B+); Anthropic embedded FDEs at FIS; the role is spreading fast.
  • FDEs without knowledge feedback loops are just high-priced contracting; start with one painful workflow.

Hello, this is Hamamoto from TIMEWELL Inc.

"If you send engineers into the field, AI will reach production." Over the past year, I've been hearing this kind of talk more and more. The name is FDE: Forward Deployed Engineer, a forward-deployed type of engineer. It's a role that Palantir cultivated, and today both OpenAI and Anthropic officially use the same term.

In Japanese conference rooms, the next question usually comes up. Isn't that just a resident SE? How is it different from consulting-style AI accompaniment support?

I'll start with the conclusion. It is not merely a different name for similar work. What is being sold is different, the ownership of the deliverables is different, and whether things get written into the system is different. Our company is one of those whose core business includes accompaniment support. Precisely for that reason, I felt that unless we set aside our own offerings and organized the topic clearly, it wouldn't be useful to readers.

What is failing is not the model

The report "The GenAI Divide: State of AI in Business 2025" released by MIT NANDA in July 2025 has harsh numbers. Corporate generative AI investment is estimated at 30 to 40 billion dollars. On top of that, it states that 95% of organizations are not getting measurable returns 1. Of the pilots that were integrated, only 5% are extracting value at the scale of millions of dollars. ChatGPT and Copilot have been tried by 80% of organizations, with nearly 40% having deployed them. But that is individual productivity, and it hasn't reached the income statement.

There is another set of numbers, closer to the front lines. 60% of organizations considered dedicated enterprise tools. 20% made it to pilot. Only 5% reached production 1. The survey period was January to June 2025, covering over 300 public AI projects, interviews with 52 organizations, and a survey of 153 senior-level people. The report itself notes the sample's limitations, calling the findings preliminary. Even so, the direction is clear.

The report explicitly states that the cause is not insufficient model performance. The barrier is learning. Most generative AI does not accumulate feedback and does not improve in line with context. The larger the company, the more pilots it runs, yet the slower it is to transition to production. There is also a passage saying that partnering externally roughly doubled the success rate compared to building in-house 1.

We called this the implementation gap. MIT's term is the GenAI Divide. Either name is fine, but the form I see in the field is simpler. It works in the conference room. It stalls on the messy data of the actual workplace. And once it stalls, the people in charge never touch it again.

Traditional business systems could be built on the assumption that you put in A and get out B. Requirements definition, design, implementation, testing, delivery. If it doesn't work as specified, it's a bug. The moment you insert an LLM, the output wavers even for the same input. It's not a bug; that's just the kind of machine it is. Behind the single line "read the invoice amount" lies an infinity of handwritten text, missing stamps, multiple tax rates, and per-client layouts. None of this shows up in a conference room. Only by running it do you see the shapes of failure.

That's why you need a loop of observing, fixing, and observing again. And doing so while the business is actually running. Whether there is a person who can run this loop in the field—that is where a project's life or death is mostly decided.

What does an FDE actually do?

An FDE is an engineer who enters the customer's organization and takes on development and implementation in order to run their own company's product in the customer's production environment. The term "forward deployed" has military origins—the idea of placing troops at the front line rather than at headquarters.

Its origin lies with Palantir. In the early days of the company, customer data was dirtier than expected, business processes more complex than expected, and there was often no one who could even articulate the requirements. So they seated engineers next to customers and had them learn the business itself. Later accounts consistently say this is how it began. Since we haven't looked inside Palantir, we won't assert it as fact—it's a founding myth. But the shape of the role is there: don't wait for specifications; put something that runs on real field data in place first.

The emblematic practice is producing code on day one. Spending 90 days firming up requirements, demoing with clean sample data, and then collapsing when field data arrives just before production—that's the common way to die. The FDE model is the reverse: on day one, touch the field's real data and put something that works, even if incomplete, in front of the people in charge. People cannot articulate their own work when faced with a blank specification sheet. But when they see something running, they start saying things like "that's not right in this case" or "this needs the section manager's approval." The prototype is not a deliverable; it is a questioning device for drawing out requirements.

Technical skill alone is not enough. Being able to build distributed systems yourself. Learning aircraft maintenance regulations or export control annexes well enough to discuss them with customers within weeks. Drawing out separate requirements from three layers—management, division heads, and the front line—and consolidating them into a single design. The last one is the hardest. Management wants to change industry conventions, division heads want to cut this quarter's costs, and the front line doesn't want the current procedures changed. Finding the one point that satisfies all three simultaneously is, in effect, the job.

From 2025 to 2026, this role stopped being a Palantir monopoly. In a June 4, 2025 essay, a16z called the FDE the hottest job at startups, framing it as gripping implementation and buying a moat, even at the cost of temporarily lowering gross margin 2. The job posting growth figures do not appear in the a16z text itself. According to figures from Indeed Hiring Lab reported by the Financial Times and others, postings from January to September 2025 grew over 800% 3. Since the source is via media reports, read it with some latitude.

The movements verifiable from primary sources are even more recent. On May 11, 2026, OpenAI announced the establishment of the OpenAI Deployment Company, known as DeployCo. It acquired Tomoro, an applied AI consulting firm, and will start with roughly 150 FDEs and implementation specialists. Initial investment exceeds 4 billion dollars, with TPG at the head and consultancies and system integrators also listed 4. Anthropic, in a May 4, 2026 announcement from financial infrastructure giant FIS, stated that its Applied AI team and FDEs would be embedded into FIS to co-design agents for financial crime. It states that the aim is not to remain resident indefinitely, but to hand over knowledge so that FIS can run on its own 5. Compared to the "resident structure" we mentioned earlier, the official wording is one step more modest.

Accompaniment support and FDEs sell different things

This is the main point. If you only look at "entering the field" and "building together," you can't tell them apart. But when you break it down, they are different businesses.

Walk-along support and embedded development sell man-hours. Revenue is measured in person-months. The metric that matters most is utilization rate. Idle time is a cost, and a project dragging on is, at least in the short term, a plus for revenue. FDEs sell a product. An FDE's man-hours are not revenue; they are an investment to embed the product into the customer's operations. The metric is time-to-value for the customer. The sooner you make the customer self-sufficient and move on to the main contract and horizontal expansion, the more the seller gains. A structure where longer involvement means more profit, versus a structure where letting go sooner means more profit. The directions are opposite.

Ownership of deliverables also diverges. What embedded development builds becomes the customer's asset. Source code is delivered, and maintenance is handled by the customer or a separately contracted vendor. All that remains with the seller is the individual experience of the people involved. What an FDE builds is an application on top of their own company's platform. The design goal is that when the platform improves, the customer improves along with it. As a metaphor: embedded development lays a gravel road for each customer. It fits that customer, but it starts aging the moment it is laid, and it cannot be used for the next customer. An FDE lays gravel roads while bringing back the paving technology. They extract patterns from on-the-ground pain and promote them into standard features. Deployment costs for the next customer go down. An FDE operation where this feedback loop is not running is just high-priced contracting.

The deepest difference is reading versus writing. The deliverables of walk-along work and PoCs are almost entirely read-only. Analyze, visualize, offer insights. No permission to rewrite core records. Judgment and execution remain with humans. An FDE goes all the way to writing back. Allocate inventory, generate purchase orders, rebuild shift schedules. If a read-only system makes a mistake, a human will notice and stop it. If a writing system makes a mistake, reality breaks. Wrong orders, wrong shipments, wrong credit decisions. That is why writing without the full set—templates for permissible actions, permissions, pre-execution simulation, human intervention for critical decisions, auditable records—is unacceptable. It is rare for a project that started as "we'll walk alongside you" to go this far. Because writing means taking responsibility.

Where learning accumulates also differs. Walk-along knowledge accumulates in the heads of the assigned consultants. It disappears when they leave. When FDE works well, knowledge accumulates in the structure of meaning. What is a customer at this company? At what point is a shipment complete? Who experiences what when a delay exceeds a threshold? Fixed in a machine-readable form. It survives even when people leave. The name for this is ontology. It sounds difficult, but what you are doing is teaching the company's nouns and verbs to AI.

Our WARP is work closer to the left column of the table. Hiding it would be found out immediately, so I'll state it upfront. This is not an article that denies walk-along work itself. What I deny is walk-along work that stays read-only and leaves nothing but beautiful reports. Walk-along work that does not build in the right column—that is, writing, feedback loops, and evaluation on the customer's business metrics—leans toward the 95% side that MIT described.

Without teaching nouns and verbs, agents cannot run

The first thing an FDE does on-site is establish the structure of meaning. Define scattered data as entities—customers, invoices, parts, workers—and define actions such as issuing a refund, placing an order, or rebuilding a shift schedule.

Many companies say, "We already have a data platform." But conventional normalized models are designed around a current snapshot, with little temporal depth. Star schemas are strong at aggregation and pre-joining, but the transformation logic hides inside the ETL. Both are well suited for humans looking at graphs and making judgments. They fall short for machines that traverse and execute. I wrote about making relationships themselves traversable from a different angle in What Is Graph Engineering?.

Some point out that this is a different thing from academic ontologies like RDF or OWL. Those are strong at static knowledge representation and retrieval. What the field needs is a cycle of executing, writing back, and learning. In terms of layers: a layer for nouns, a layer for actions, a layer for permissions and simulation. Operations defined in the action layer become tool calls from the LLM's perspective. Things like ordering a nonexistent part number become structurally impossible. The idea is to suppress hallucinations through structure, not prompt engineering. The argument that agents break down on long tasks because of the environment more than the model is continuous with Introduction to Agent Engineering.

For case studies, I separate what I could verify from what I cannot write about as raw material.

Airbus's Skywise appeared in the official announcement of June 20, 2017 as a data platform for the aviation industry built with Palantir 6. The sequence was opening up what had been used internally for manufacturing and maintenance to airlines. The figure that A350 deliveries became 33% faster is in Palantir's public materials 7. Airbus's announcement does not contain that percentage. So I treat it as Palantir's claim, and in terms of official announcements I write only up to the launch of Skywise. The story in the source material about "shipping a digital twin in 3 days" could not be traced to a primary Airbus source. I have not written it.

Regarding John Deere, in a conversation OpenAI published in May 2025, John Deere said that See & Spray targets only weeds and reduces herbicide use by up to 70% 8. That is about the product. The story in the source material about an FDE going into an Iowa farm and making a harvest deadline could not be drawn from that same conversation. I think the point itself—that evals, plumbing, and controls are the three essentials needed in production—is correct. But this time I have no basis for writing it as a John Deere FDE case.

What can be verified is the fact that the role is spreading. OpenAI has spun out an implementation company, and Anthropic has FDEs within FIS. a16z's "trading gross margin for a moat" is a business argument. In the 2010s, SaaS won through self-service adoption and high gross margins. Generative AI that replaces core workflows requires deep integrations and permissions, and is hard to overcome with self-service alone. You staff heavily, temporarily lower gross margin, and seize the entry points to data and operations. ServiceNow's gross margin at IPO was 63.2%, Workday's 54.1%. By 2024 they had recovered to 79% and 75% respectively, a16z writes 2. Can you withstand short-term criticism over gross margin? The decision to deploy FDEs is a matter of management more than technology.

An FDE in name only is worse than outsourcing

It is not a panacea. An FDE who does not feed learnings back is just an expensive person-month. What percentage of the features created in the field this quarter made it into the standard product? If your organization is not tracking that, it should not call itself FDE.

If you keep piling up customer-specific exceptions, the codebase will break within a few years. Is this a pattern that should be standardized, or a branch for this customer only? The ability to say no is also a skill. If you keep writing scripts in the field without capturing the structure of meaning, the moment the person in charge leaves, no one can touch it anymore. The essence is not being in the field, but turning the field's tacit knowledge into structure. Using engineers as a way to close deals becomes unpaid customization. Does that customer's pain also apply to many other customers? Deployment without an investment hypothesis is just a substitute for sales.

The prescription for Japanese companies is not to import job titles. Narrow it down to the single most painful business process. If you start with company-wide DX, you end up back in the conference room. Write out the nouns and verbs of that process in the field's own words. Translating them into system terminology comes later. On day one, put something incomplete but working in place. Measure not 95% accuracy, but whether 3 hours became 20 minutes, or how many points rework dropped. Before allowing writes, put in permissions, simulation, human intervention, and audit logs. Never connect something lacking these four to core systems. I wrote about recording what agents read and constraining the exit in Why is Cloudflare OS open source?. The thinking points in the same direction.

Advisory support is work that helps the customer make the decision. You provide insights and lay out options. The customer bears final responsibility. This is necessary work, and our company sells it too. An FDE is work that carries, all the way to the field, the responsibility our own product bears for the customer's reality. That is why they write. That is why they build governance. That is why they are measured on the customer's business metrics. What they learn there is brought back into the product, becoming the pavement for the next customer.

The 95% stall is not because the models are weak. It is because no one carried responsibility all the way to the field. The capabilities of generative AI will keep growing. The implementation gap cannot be closed by capability alone. What closes it is people who go down into the field, and a structure that turns the insights they bring back into product.

When our company takes on field design, it is WARP. For preserving internal knowledge as relationships, it is ZEROCK. If you want to decide how far to go with a single business process of your own, rather than importing job titles, tell us about your situation via individual consultation.
