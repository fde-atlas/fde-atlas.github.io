Characteristics of People Suited for FDE | How to Identify Aptitude

Original title: FDEに向いている人の特徴｜適性を見極める

日本Fde協会 · 2026-10-01 · 6 min read

Summary

This article from the Japan FDE Association argues that technical skill is necessary but not sufficient for success as a Forward Deployed Engineer. Drawing on author Maekawa's experience in finance, e-commerce, and manufacturing, it offers a self-assessment framework covering personality, work habits, and interests. Suitable candidates can articulate vague customer problems, push back on unnecessary requests, show rough prototypes early, back proposals with numbers like ROI and hours saved, tolerate unglamorous compliance work such as access controls and audit logs, and find the tech-business boundary interesting. The article stresses that aptitude is not fixed and can grow through field experience, and points readers to the association's skill check.

Key points

  • Technical skill alone does not determine FDE aptitude.
  • Strong candidates verbalize vague customer needs and question unnecessary development.
  • Showing rough prototypes early beats waiting for perfect requirements.
  • Comfort with controls, logs, and documentation matters for long-term trust.
  • Aptitude is learnable; the association offers a skill check for self-assessment.

In this article, FDE refers to Forward Deployed Engineer (a technical professional who embeds at the customer's site and implements and operates AI and software tailored to their business), not Full Disk Encryption, which means disk encryption. When asked what kind of person is suited to FDE, the answer tends to be "someone with strong technical skills." But from what we see in the field, that alone doesn't determine aptitude. By reading this article, you can self-assess whether you or a candidate is suited to FDE from concrete perspectives—personality traits, work style, and areas of interest—and decide which qualities to develop next and what first step to try. Aptitude is not a fixed trait; it can be developed later. Let's think through this together.

Technical skill alone doesn't determine who is suited to FDE

When we consider FDE aptitude, we see technical skill as a necessary condition but not a sufficient one. There is a surprising distance between being able to write code and delivering results at a customer's site.

Can you put vague problems into words?

The requests customers bring at first are usually vague. Drawing out the real problem and articulating it, starting from "Could AI do something here?" or "We want to make our operations more efficient," is the entry point for an FDE. Maekawa came through experience as a trader at a financial institution and launching an e-commerce business; when he first moved into e-commerce, he had technical skills but lacked marketing knowledge, and sales were nearly zero for the first six months. He grew only after abandoning the assumption that "if you build something good, it will sell" and thoroughly interviewing customers. This interpersonal orientation—digging into requests the other party can't put into words—can't be learned from technical books.

Can you say, "Do you really need that?"

An FDE's job isn't just implementing customer requests as stated; it also includes making the call to stop unnecessary development. In fact, we sometimes hear people say they were shocked the first time someone asked them, "Do you really need that?" We find that people who can step back and question against the purpose tend to perform better in this role than those who are comfortable simply building what they're told.

Three tendencies that show up in how people work

Aptitude shows up not only in personality but also in daily work style. Here are three tendencies commonly seen in people suited to this field.

Showing something that works first, without waiting for perfection

Rather than someone who wants to perfect the requirements specification before starting, FDEs are better suited to people who want to show a rough but working prototype first and get feedback. Although a PM, Maekawa has always worked hands-on, from coding and implementation to operations and maintenance. His end-to-end experience from upstream to downstream across industries—shipping and receiving management for petrochemical complexes, core systems for an entertainment company—underpins this "show something that works first" attitude. Whether you enjoy working with your hands is one key dividing line.

Do you have a habit of speaking in numbers?

Having a habit of backing proposals with ROI, cost, and effort estimates lets you influence customer decision-making. People who can measure and demonstrate impact with KPIs, rather than saying "it seems good somehow," tend to earn trust in the field. Even at manufacturing clients, we've seen many times that when improvement effects are translated into numbers for reduced work hours, executive decisions suddenly speed up.

Do you mind unglamorous control work?

This is a part that general discussions of aptitude rarely cover. FDE work includes unglamorous tasks such as separating permissions, logging operations, designing around segregation of duties, and documenting for handover. In work close to the financial domain, designs that insert human approval before irreversible actions are indispensable. People who can steadily accumulate this kind of control and audit-trail work without finding it a chore tend to earn lasting trust, more than those focused on flashy implementations.

Self-assessing aptitude through your areas of interest

Finally, the perspective of what you're drawn to. This is also a quality that can be developed later.

Do you find the boundary between technology and business interesting?

The consistent thread of Maekawa's career has been "embedding in different environments and solving problems from both technical and business angles." At a major engineering firm, he led a development team of eight and more than twenty stakeholders, handling everything from interdepartmental coordination to reporting to executives, and came to appreciate firsthand that neither technology alone nor business alone is enough. People suited to this role are those interested not just in one side but in the friction that arises at the boundary between the two.

Do you find yourself wondering if something can be automated?

When you spot a process running on manual work, you can't help thinking, "Couldn't this be automated?" This habit is the very essence of FDE aptitude. His experience planning and developing social media auto-posting and lead generation systems also grew from this mindset of working backward from customer business problems. Whether you can take a pragmatic view—use AI where it's useful—is another good indicator.

Don't give up even if you feel you're not suited

After reading this far, some of you may feel, "I lack interpersonal orientation" or "I'm bad at control work." But aptitude is not a fixed trait; it's a quality that can be developed later through field experience and learning. From startup experience, it's not uncommon for people who were initially purely technical to become skilled at articulating problems as they accumulate interviews. For the full picture of aptitude and career options, also see FDE Career Paths | Three Options Five Years Out, and for an overview of required skills, see The Full Range of Skills Needed for FDE | Explained in 8 Areas.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q. Is there a chance I'm suited to FDE even if my technical skills are still weak? Yes. FDE aptitude isn't determined by technical skill alone; interpersonal orientation for articulating vague problems and a willingness to do unglamorous control work also have a big influence. These are qualities that can be developed later. Technical skill can be built on top of them.

Q. If I feel I'm not suited, should I give up on pursuing it? No. Aptitude is not a fixed trait; it changes through field experience and learning. Even if some elements feel difficult now, this is a role where much can be developed by trying things little by little.

Q. Is there a way to objectively check my aptitude? The Association publishes a skill check that verifies knowledge and practical domains related to FDE. You can use it as one input for self-assessment. For details, see Skill Check.
