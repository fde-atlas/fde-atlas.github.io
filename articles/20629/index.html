The Rapidly Spreading "FDE" Overseas and the Higher Level of Expertise Required of Engineers in the AI Era

Original title: 海外で急速に広がる「FDE」と、AI時代のエンジニアに求められるより高い専門性

Qiita · 2026-08-17 · 4 min read

Summary

Prompted by a16z naming FDE a rising job category, the author investigates the role. FDE, which originated at Palantir in the 2010s, describes engineers embedded at customer sites who gather requirements and build systems iteratively on-site. Companies including OpenAI, Anthropic, Salesforce, and in Japan ExaWizards and SoftBank are hiring, driven by the fact that AI systems vary greatly per customer. The author's key insight: FDEs are not generalists but teams divided into implementation, field-facing, and UI/UX roles, each requiring deep expertise. The conclusion favors mastering one strength over shallow breadth.

Key points

  • FDE originated at Palantir: engineers work on-site, listening and building iteratively.
  • OpenAI, Anthropic, Salesforce, ExaWizards, and SoftBank are hiring FDEs.
  • AI systems differ per customer, requiring ongoing on-site customization.
  • Palantir splits FDE teams into implementation, field, and UI/UX roles with varied backgrounds.
  • The author concludes deep expertise in one area beats shallow breadth.

Nice to meet you. I'm Sumomo🍑, an engineer at PRUM Inc. I share tips and ways of thinking about points that are easy to get stuck on in programming study and daily work.

Have you been seeing the term "FDE" a lot in the IT industry lately? Apparently, American investment experts (a16z) are calling it "a popular job that will be in demand going forward," so I got curious and looked into it.

※a16z (Andreessen Horowitz) is an investment firm whose assets under management reached approximately $90 billion (about 13 trillion yen) as of January 2026, making it one of the largest fundraisings in Silicon Valley history.

What exactly is an "FDE"?

FDE is an abbreviation for Forward Deployed Engineer.

To compare it with everyday work, here's the image:

A regular engineer: someone who builds systems within the company according to fixed specifications. An FDE: someone who visits the client's company directly, listens to their needs, builds systems on the spot, and takes care of them until they're actually usable.

For example, suppose there's a client who says "our order management isn't working well." A regular engineer would proceed with development based on a fixed specification document, but an FDE starts by going to the site and asking "what exactly is the problem?" Then they build something simple on the spot, have the client try it, and gradually shape it into a working solution.

In other words, it's a job where one person, or a small team, handles both "listening to people" and "building things."

Why is this role spreading so rapidly now?

FDE is said to be a role created by the US data analytics company Palantir in the 2010s. Recently, companies like the following seem to be gradually hiring FDEs.

Overseas companies: OpenAI, Anthropic, Salesforce. Companies for the Japanese market: ExaWizards, SoftBank

The background seems to be the fact that "AI-based systems are used very differently, and have very different pain points, from company to company." Rather than just building fixed features, the role of continuously building solutions tailored to each individual workplace seems to be becoming increasingly important.

An FDE can be one person or a team

An FDE may be a single person handling everything, or a small team dividing up the roles. At Palantir, the originator of the FDE role, responsibilities are divided as follows.

※Palantir is an American company founded in 2003 that provides software supporting data integration and AI utilization for government agencies and large enterprises.

Implementation lead: the person who actually writes code and turns systems into reality. Field lead: the person who listens to clients, organizes what their biggest problems are, and coordinates so everyone can use the system. UI/UX lead: the person who builds the parts related to usability, such as screens and buttons.

It seems the structure is designed so that coding skills are combined with listening skills and the ability to turn usability into reality, allowing the team to dig deep into a single workplace. Looking at the backgrounds of people actually working as FDEs, implementation leads tend to come from software engineering, while field leads tend to be people with experience interacting directly with clients, such as customer success, product managers, and consultants. Rather than a role with a fixed mold, it seems to be a role that takes shape by bringing together the accumulated experience of each member.

What the skills required of FDEs tell us

Honestly, at first I thought "FDE is a job for so-called 'jack-of-all-trades' people who can handle both technical skill and on-site adaptability."

But after learning that roles are divided based on career foundations, like implementation lead and field lead, I feel it might be a bit different.

I think the FDE team works well not because everyone can do everything at a shallow level, but because each member deeply brings their own strongest skill to the table.

Someone strong in technology, someone strong in understanding the field, someone skilled at making things easy to use. Rather than "broad but shallow," it's a structure where a team that can be entrusted with a single workplace is formed only when people with the kind of strength that makes others say "this area is their specialty" come together.

Things you can start doing today

Identify one area in your current work where you feel you are strongest. Try to deepen that area not "broadly and shallowly" but to the level where people rely on you. Instead of trying to fill in your weak areas yourself, think with the premise of relying on people who are strong in those areas. Think about which role you would be most relied upon for within your team.

Summary

FDE is a team made up of people who have deeply mastered their respective fields—technology, field understanding, usability, and so on—bringing their roles together to dig deep into a single workplace.

Rather than being "shallowly broad," mastering a single point of strength may be what holds value in engineering going forward.
