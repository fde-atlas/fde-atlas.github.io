What is the difference between an FDE and a Sales Engineer?

Original title: FDE 和销售工程师的区别是什么

FDE Times · 2026-10-11 · 5 min read

Summary

Drawing on sources like Scaler, Christian & Timbers, The Pragmatic Engineer, and Paraform, the article distinguishes FDEs from sales/solutions engineers: the dividing line is contract signing. Sales engineers prove the product fits and finish at signature; FDEs own production outcomes afterward, writing production code on customer infrastructure with customer tools and handling post-launch incidents like 2 a.m. outages. It also warns that the FDE title is being applied to sales roles—Bloomberry found FDE job postings grew 1,165% year over year—so candidates should read job descriptions for terms like go-live and on-call, and offers resume and interview tips.

Key points

  • The contract signature marks the handoff: sales engineers finish, FDEs begin.
  • FDEs write production code on customer infrastructure and own the results.
  • When systems fail after launch, the FDE gets the call, not the SE.
  • FDE postings grew 1,165% year over year, and titles are often misused.
  • Look for go-live, on-call, and end-to-end ownership in job descriptions.

Key takeaways

  • The dividing line between the two roles is contract signing: by that point, the Sales Engineer's main work is essentially done, while the FDE's work is just beginning.
  • FDEs write production code directly on the customer's infrastructure, using the customer's tools, so they are accountable for the results there.
  • The FDE title is also used for sales-leaning roles, so look at the scope of work in the job posting rather than the title.

Three months after the system went live, it went down at 2 a.m. Who has to get up and fix it? When comparing FDEs with Solutions Engineers, Scaler answered without hesitation: recovery work belongs to the FDE, not the SE.

This answer captures nearly the entire difference between two jobs that are often conflated. Both roles deal with customers, both require solid technical skills, and both involve communicating with people who don't write code.

If you want to move into a Forward Deployed Engineer (FDE) role, or you're already in one but unsure where your responsibilities end, the question to ask isn't "will I meet with customers?" but rather: when a live system breaks, who gets woken up?

The dividing line is the moment of signature

Both roles are customer-facing, but their core work falls on opposite sides of the signature. According to Scaler's description, the Solutions Engineer works within the sales cycle, while the FDE's main work only begins after the contract is signed.

FDE Academy draws a similar line: FDEs primarily come into play after the product is sold, while SEs operate pre-sale or in the early stages of implementation.

Christian & Timbers puts it more bluntly: the Sales Engineer's core task is to prove the product can solve the customer's problem, and by the time the customer signs, that task is essentially complete. The FDE's responsibility extends all the way to results in production.

So what changes hands at signature isn't the customer, but responsibility for the live system. Christian & Timbers made this point right in the title of their analysis: the difference lies in who owns production.

Why demos work but production breaks

The Pragmatic Engineer emphasizes that FDEs are far more hands-on than sales-type roles: they write code directly on the customer's infrastructure, using the customer's tools. The gap between demo and production shows up exactly here.

Imagine an agent that reads internal documents and answers employee questions. In the demo, it reads a sample folder of documents, answers fluently, and the customer nods along. In production, the agent must respect permissions: employees in Department A cannot read contracts from Department B.

In this scenario, the documents also live in a document management system that has been running for years, whose API no one remembers how to use. The customer's security team needs to know where the data flows and where logs are stored. Then one day someone uploads a scanned document with no text layer, and the agent's answers go completely wrong.

Paraform describes the FDE as the person who puts production code into the customer's environment and is accountable for the results there. A demo only needs to succeed once in front of the customer. Code with an FDE's name on it must keep running even on days when no one is paying attention to it.

After the sale, who delivers on the promise?

If the Sales Engineer sells a promise, then someone has to turn that promise into a system, and someone has to keep it running over the long term. When advising startups on which role to hire, Paraform summarized this chain in three stages: the SE pitches the vision, the FDE builds it, and the Customer Engineer (CE) maintains it.

Sitting in the middle of that chain shows that the FDE is not just a Sales Engineer who writes more code. According to The Pragmatic Engineer, what makes the FDE unique is working with customers while also feeding insights back into the product itself, something sales-type roles don't do.

At the company that pioneered this model, deployment is hardly a niche role. Valletta Software tallied Palantir's open positions on September 8, 2026, and found that 77 of 310 openings were forward deployed roles, roughly a quarter.

The title alone is no longer enough

The most practical impact of this dividing line shows up in job postings. Bloomberry analyzed 1,000 job postings and found that FDE postings grew 1,165% year over year, mostly from small companies. Getperspective found that this hiring wave has spread from frontier labs to vertical AI startups and hyperscalers like Google.

Once a title gets hot, it gets slapped onto many different jobs. Getperspective notes that employers are quietly renaming Solutions Architect roles to FDE to compete for the same pool of candidates; its conclusion is that the wording describing the scope of work matters more than the title.

Christian & Timbers looks at the problem from the company's side: bad hires often start with the job description, not the candidate pool. When a job posting uses the FDE label to describe a Sales Engineer's job, applicants get misled too.

So look for words like go-live, production, integration, on-call, or "own end-to-end." If the posting mainly talks about demos, proofs of concept (POCs), supporting the sales team, and win rates, then whatever it's called, it's most likely a sales engineering role. That role isn't bad, but it builds a different set of skills.

Rewriting one line of your resume

Don't write "worked directly with customers" on your resume, because a Sales Engineer could write that too. Take the hypothetical document retrieval agent above: the line everyone writes is:

Before: "Worked directly with customers to deploy AI solutions."

After: "Took an internal document retrieval agent to production, integrating with a legacy document management system and department-level permissions; joined the on-call rotation after launch and personally resolved an incident where scans without a text layer caused incorrect answers."

If you've ever fed customer-side problems back into the core product, add a line about it, because that's exactly the point The Pragmatic Engineer uses to distinguish FDEs from sales-type roles.

Questions to bring into the interview

Ask the interviewer one question in the final round: three months after launch, if the customer's system goes down at 2 a.m., who answers the phone? Does that person have permission to fix code on the customer's infrastructure?

If the answer is that the incident gets handed off to another team, then whatever the title says, you won't be responsible for production. If the answer is "you," and you have the access to fix it yourself, that's the FDE's seat.
