FDE Project Scope Management: How to Handle Scope Creep

Original title: FDE 项目范围管理：如何应对范围蔓延

FDE Times · 2026-10-08 · 6 min read

Summary

Drawing on PostHog's FDE handbook and Tandem's definition of scope creep, this article argues that forward deployed engineers should treat scope as the first lever to adjust on customer sites. Deadlines and environments are set by clients, and quality has a floor, so scope is what FDEs can proactively negotiate daily. Projects should start with a brief containing measurable outcomes, a timeline, and an agreed definition of done. New requests must be classified as clarifying an existing outcome or adding a new one; additions without adjusting time or resources constitute scope creep. When core requirements cannot fit the deadline, renegotiate early rather than cut quality.

Key points

  • Deadlines and environment belong to the client; scope is the lever FDEs should pull first.
  • PostHog requires a pre-project brief: measurable outcome, timeline, and definition of done.
  • Expanding a narrow scope is cheaper than narrowing a wide one; overbuilding is waste.
  • Classify new requests: clarifying an outcome is exploration; additions without trade-offs are scope creep.
  • If core work cannot fit the deadline, renegotiate time or resources instead of lowering quality.

Key takeaways

  • Deadlines and environment are decided by the customer, while quality depends on scope, so scope is the lever an FDE should adjust first.
  • Narrow the scope from the start: expanding a narrow scope costs less than reining in a broad one. Doing more than the problem requires is waste.
  • A new request is not necessarily scope creep. First ask whether it clarifies an existing outcome or adds a new one; if it adds one, time or resources must be adjusted accordingly.

PostHog's FDE handbook contains a very short sentence that captures an entire way of working: keep scope small, because expanding a narrow scope is always cheaper than reining in a broad one.

At first glance, this looks like routine project management advice. In fact, it answers the hardest question on the customer site: when the customer wants everything, where should you concede?

Customers rarely say "a bit slower is fine" or "rough around the edges is okay." They want every feature, delivered early, running reliably. Every Forward Deployed Engineer (FDE) has to trade off among these three. The real difference is whether the trade-off is made proactively by you or happens passively.

For developers transitioning into the FDE role, this tension shows up in the first week on site. There are three levers in front of you, and scope is the one to pull first. The skill to practice is pulling it without damaging the customer relationship.

Three levers: which one to pull first?

Anyone who has done project management knows the project triangle. Wikipedia describes one of its core ideas: the quality of work is constrained by the project's budget, deadline, and feature scope. Quality is not a knob that can be adjusted on its own; it is determined by the other three.

On the customer site, most of these constraints come from the customer. Ben Griswold argues that an FDE's work is constrained by the customer's own timeline, tech stack, and environment, and ultimately ends with handing ownership back to the customer.

So the deadline is effectively an input. You rarely get to decide the customer's internal launch date, and you cannot change the infrastructure the customer runs on.

Quality has a floor. Griswold writes that the goal is to transfer capability, not to create long-term dependency. A system built hastily that the customer cannot operate independently is a failure even if it ships on time.

That is why scope is the lever an FDE should pull first. Deadlines and resources can still change, but that requires negotiating with the customer. Scope, however, is something you proactively propose to narrow or expand every day.

Small scope does not mean doing less

PostHog requires every project to have three things before work begins: a measurable outcome, an estimated timeline, and a mutually agreed definition of "done," written up in a short brief. Those three lines are the project triangle on paper: the outcome is scope, the timeline is speed, and the "done" criteria are the quality floor.

The handbook goes further: doing more than the problem requires is not thoroughness, it is waste. If you habitually do a few extra things to please the customer, this sentence is worth writing down. Every feature beyond the brief either eats into the agreed time or drags quality below the floor.

PostHog also offers a subtler filter: a solution can be specific to one customer, but a useful insight should not be trapped there.

When weighing whether to take on extra work, ask: does this serve only this one customer, or can the company learn something reusable from it? The latter kind of work is more worth expanding scope for.

Exploration or creep: two questions worth remembering

The difficulty is that an FDE cannot freeze requirements the way old-style fixed-scope contracts do. Tandem points out that the job requires practitioners to continuously gather requirements and calibrate with the customer. Changing requirements are the norm; the question is which kind of change it is.

Tandem defines scope creep as: scope grows, but time, cost, or resources do not change accordingly. This definition matches the lever logic: scope can grow, as long as another side of the triangle adjusts with it. What causes damage is added scope that no one pays for.

Of the four tests Tandem proposes, two can be used directly. First: is this request clarifying an outcome the customer has already bought, or adding a new one?

Second: can you point to what was agreed, what was said, and what was delivered? The first question classifies the request; the second determines whether you have enough grounds to negotiate.

Applied to a concrete case

Imagine a scenario: you have four weeks to build a low-inventory alert data pipeline for a retailer, covering 10 pilot stores. The three-line brief confirmed by the customer in week one might read:

Outcome: Reduce stockouts at the 10 pilot stores. Timeline: Four weeks. Done criteria: Alerts run daily on real data from the 10 stores, and the customer's operations team can handle them independently.

In week two, the customer's operations manager adds two requests: seasonal alert thresholds, and a revenue dashboard for management.

Apply the first question. Seasonal thresholds are precisely clarifying the outcome already bought, because they make the alerts more accurate, so they count as requirements exploration and are reasonable to include in scope. The revenue dashboard is a new outcome. Taking it on without adjusting the deadline or adding headcount is exactly the scope creep Tandem defines.

The second question lets you say "not for now" without damaging the relationship. Open the brief and reply with one sentence: "Seasonal thresholds are within the agreed outcome, so we'll do them now. The revenue dashboard is a new outcome—your choice: put it in the next phase, or do it now and push the deadline back a week?"

Note that the second option pulls the deadline lever, which is entirely legitimate, because the customer makes the decision. The conversation shifts from "you don't want to help" to "which trade-off do we choose?"

When the levers get stuck

This model has limits, as Wikipedia itself acknowledges: in reality, constraints cannot always be traded against each other. Some scope cannot be cut. If the customer needs integration with a payment system, that integration is core—remove it and there is nothing to deliver.

At that point, "keep scope small" becomes "cut in the right place." What you trim is the number of edge cases, pages, and reports handled in the first round, not the core of the outcome.

If even the core cannot fit into the deadline, that is a signal: tell the customer early and renegotiate time or resources, rather than lowering the quality floor.

Griswold also makes an easily overlooked point: what a product team delivers in a quarter, an FDE can deliver in weeks, but the difference lies not in speed—it lies in fitting the constraints of one specific customer. If you cut scope to move faster and the product no longer fits the customer's environment, you pulled the wrong lever.

What to practice

If you have done outsourcing work, you are probably used to change requests. But if someone else is still negotiating those requests on your current project, that is a gap worth deliberately closing. Because calibrating requirements with the customer never stops, an FDE can hardly avoid standing personally at the point of trade-offs.

When reading job descriptions, watch for phrases like "scoping," "trade-offs," "definition of done," or "customer discovery." They signal that the company expects you to define scope yourself, not just execute. For interviews, prepare a real story: a time you said "not now" to a requirement, and what that bought you.

On your resume, replace sentences like "developed the reporting module" with wording that shows trade-offs: which part of the scope you deferred, which deadline you protected, and which quality standard you never lowered. Don't try to prove you can do everything. Show that you know what to set aside, and that you can explain the reasoning to the customer.
