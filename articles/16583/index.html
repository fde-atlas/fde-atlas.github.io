PoC Acceptance Checklist: A Passing Demo Does Not Mean a Passing Validation

Original title: PoC 验收清单：演示通过不等于验证通过

FDE中国 · 2026-09-04 · 4 min read

Summary

This article addresses a common failure mode in PoC acceptance meetings: everyone is carried along by a smooth demo, and issues that later surface in production go unchecked. The author offers a sixteen-item, four-group checklist covering data authenticity, boundary coverage (failure cases and long-tail inputs), performance and cost, and handover deliverables. The core argument: passing a demo only proves the system works on ideal inputs, while validation requires meeting committed levels across the agreed input range. The checklist should not be used only on acceptance day—data sourcing and boundary definitions must be locked before the PoC starts, with vendor self-review a week ahead. Unmet items must be explicitly classified as resolved-before-acceptance or follow-up with owners and deadlines. In the Chinese context, disputes should be tied to payment milestones and documented in meeting minutes.

Key points

  • A sixteen-item checklist in four groups: data authenticity, boundaries, cost/latency, handover.
  • Demo success only proves performance on ideal inputs, not validated reliability.
  • Data sourcing and boundary definitions must be settled before the PoC starts.
  • Unmet items need explicit classification with follow-up owners and deadlines.
  • The single most revealing question: who prepared the test data?

The problem this piece addresses: at PoC acceptance meetings, both sides get carried along by "the demo went smoothly," and nobody systematically checks for the issues that only blow up once the system reaches production. The most valuable takeaway is the review logic behind the four groups of check items: first check who prepared the data (the authenticity of customer data determines the validity of the PoC), then check the boundaries (failure cases and long-tail inputs), then check money and time, and finally check the handover deliverables. The most common misuse is treating the checklist as a one-time tool for acceptance day—the first two groups of questions should be asked before the PoC even starts; by the time the acceptance meeting comes around, the answers are already predetermined. Compared with the in-site content on evals and expectation management, this piece's increment is "executable check actions for the acceptance meeting itself"—every item can yield a conclusion on the spot. What the domestic context adds: acceptance is often tied to commercial payment milestones, so disputed items should be classified as "resolve before acceptance / follow up after acceptance" and written into the minutes, avoiding a binary standoff. The first thing to do after reading: send this checklist to the technical counterpart at your current project's client and ask them to go through it independently, in addition to your own acceptance self-check. — FDEChina Editorial Team · Sharp Review

Bottom line first: a passing demo only proves "the system performs well on its best inputs," whereas passing validation requires "the system reaches the promised level on inputs within the agreed scope." The gap between these two is the distance between PoC and production. This checklist contains sixteen check items organized into four groups, each of which can be checked off. We recommend the delivery team run a self-check one week before the acceptance meeting, and then go through the items one by one with the client on acceptance day. It builds on the scope boundaries agreed in customer expectation management, uses the numbers produced by eval baseline building as the judgment criteria, and connects the handover deliverables directly to the project handover checklist.

Group 1: Data authenticity—can the PoC data withstand questioning?

  • □ Test data was sampled by the client from real business data, with the sampling ratio and sampling method documented in writing, rather than "clean data" prepared or cherry-picked by the delivery team.
  • □ Data distribution matches production: the proportions of high-frequency and low-frequency input types have been confirmed, and the proportions of each type in the test set have been verified—no uniformly standard samples.
  • □ Test data has been confirmed to meet desensitization requirements: the handling of sensitive fields has been approved in writing by the client's information security or data owner, and the eval set can be stored and circulated safely.
  • □ Test data includes difficult samples recognized by the client (cases where manual handling historically failed, inputs with abnormal formats), with the count and proportion stated in the acceptance documentation.

Group 2: Boundary coverage—have you seen what failure looks like?

  • □ At least five out-of-scope inputs have been tested live at the acceptance meeting: garbled text, overly long inputs, questions unrelated to the business, questions with false premises—and the system's behavior (rejection, clarification, degradation) matches the agreement rather than crashing or hallucinating.
  • □ A failure case list has been handed over: scenarios the current version demonstrably handles poorly are listed item by item, each with trigger conditions and a fallback plan, and the client has read and confirmed acceptance.
  • □ The manual fallback path has been tested: the complete flow of transferring to a human after the system determines it "cannot handle" a task has been walked through successfully at least once, including how the task is routed and how the human takes over.
  • □ The definition of "pass" within boundaries has been confirmed by both sides: what counts as success, what counts as partial success, and what counts as failure—three definitions written into the acceptance documentation, each mappable to test results.

Group 3: Performance and cost—how much money and time does a single item cost?

  • □ The per-invocation processing cost of core scenarios has been measured along the real call chain (including model calls, retrieval, retries), with a per-item mean and variance range given, consistent with the budget baseline confirmed by the client.
  • □ Latency metrics were measured under conditions close to production load: not single-request serial testing, but P50 and P95 latency measured at the agreed concurrency level, with test conditions written next to the results.
  • □ A monthly cost estimate has been produced: total monthly cost projected from the real business volume provided by the client, including peak scenarios, with the shape of the cost curve as volume grows clearly stated.
  • □ Known performance shortcomings and capacity limits are documented: at what scale the current architecture will break down, and what the direction for scaling or refactoring will be at that point—so the client doesn't mistake the PoC architecture for a production architecture commitment.

Group 4: Acceptance and handover—can the validation process itself be reused?

  • □ The eval set and eval scripts have been handed over and run independently: the client's technical counterpart has run them successfully once, either at the acceptance meeting or within a week, with results matching the numbers reported by the delivery team.
  • □ The acceptance report contains complete metric figures (layered breakdowns, not a single overall score), a description of the test environment, and a list of known issues, signed or confirmed by email by both parties.
  • □ Disputed items have been classified: check items on which agreement was not reached are each marked "resolve before acceptance" or "follow up after acceptance," with the latter having a clear follow-up time and owner—no ambiguous gray areas left unresolved.
  • □ Deployment and operations handover deliverables have been listed: environment configuration, parameter documentation, common failure handling, upgrade procedures—checked item by item against the project handover checklist, clarifying which are delivered in the PoC phase and which in the production implementation phase.

Usage Notes

The right way to use this checklist is not to read it aloud item by item on acceptance day. Half of the items in Groups 1 and 2 are questions whose "answers are predetermined before the PoC starts"—whether the data comes from the client or is fabricated in-house must be settled before running; if you only discover at the acceptance meeting that the data isn't real, the only conclusion is to rerun. So we recommend using it at three points in time: go through it once at PoC kickoff to lock down Group 1 and the boundary definitions; delivery team self-check one week before acceptance; and item-by-item confirmation by both sides on acceptance day, so meeting time is spent on disputed items rather than basic facts.

The sixteen items don't all have to pass for acceptance to succeed, but each one must either pass or be explicitly classified as "follow up after acceptance" with a time and owner written down. The most dangerous state is neither passing nor anyone admitting it didn't pass—it will reappear six months later in the form of a production incident. If you're allowed to ask only one question in the review, ask the first item of Group 1: "Who prepared the test data?" The answer to this question usually predicts the quality of the entire acceptance.
