Summary

Written by Shohei Tokumitsu, CEO of DeFactory, this Japanese article explains how freelance engineers in Japan can calculate take-home pay, which typically runs 65–85% of gross annual income. It provides simulations from 3 million to 20 million yen, breaks down income tax, resident tax, national health insurance and pension costs, notes how quasi-mandate versus contract work affects business tax, and suggests tax-saving measures like blue-form filing, small business mutual aid and iDeCo, plus strategies for raising rates.

Key points

  • Freelance take-home pay is roughly 65–85% of gross income, declining as income rises.
  • Examples: 5 million yen gross yields about 4.17 million net; 10 million yields about 7.7 million.
  • Take-home calculation requires four inputs: revenue, expenses, taxes and social insurance.
  • Quasi-mandate contracts often avoid the 5% business tax that contract (deliverable-based) work incurs.
  • Blue-form filing (650,000 yen deduction), small business mutual aid and iDeCo can save hundreds of thousands of yen yearly.

For freelance engineers, take-home pay is roughly 65-85% of annual income. For example, with an annual income of 5 million yen, take-home pay is about 4.2 million yen; even at 10 million yen, it's around 7.7 million yen.

Unlike company employees, freelancers must calculate and pay taxes and social insurance premiums themselves. Many say, "My income went up, but I'm left with less than I expected."

This article comprehensively explains the basics of take-home pay calculation, simulations by income level, and concrete methods to increase your take-home pay. Whether you're considering going independent or are an active freelancer looking to optimize your income, please use this as a reference.

Take-home pay calculations for freelancers are completely different from those for company employees. For employees, taxes and insurance premiums are withheld from salary, but freelancers must deduct various expenses from revenue themselves.

This section organizes the fundamental knowledge needed for take-home pay calculations. By understanding average income data, typical take-home rates, and the four elements required for calculation, the simulations later in this article will be much easier to follow.

Limited to engineers with 5+ years of practical experience

High-rate, direct-contract project information

Take-home pay is the amount you can actually freely use, after deducting taxes, insurance premiums, expenses, and so on from your income.

Company employees benefit from an employment income deduction, and the company covers half of social insurance premiums. Freelancers, however, must bear all of these costs themselves.

Even with the same annual income of 5 million yen, a company employee's take-home pay is about 3.93 million yen, while a freelancer's is about 3.44 million yen—a difference of nearly 500,000 yen in some cases. Correctly understanding this gap is essential when deciding whether to go independent.

When transitioning from employee to freelancer, it's important to compare based on "the amount actually left in your hands," not just gross annual income.

Surveys show the average annual income of freelance engineers is about 5.76 million yen. However, income varies greatly by skill and experience: 31% earn less than 3 million yen, while nearly 30% earn 8 million yen or more.

Take-home pay is typically around 65-75% of annual income, but this varies depending on expense ratios and how deductions are utilized.

The average monthly rate for freelance projects is around 700,000 yen, so with full-time (5 days/week) work, an annual income of 8-9 million yen is well within reach. However, the amounts deducted for taxes and insurance premiums also grow accordingly, so be sure to understand them thoroughly.

To calculate a freelancer's take-home pay, you need

  • Revenue (annual income)
  • Expenses
  • Taxes
  • Social insurance premiums

—these four elements.

The formula is simple: "Take-home pay = Revenue - Expenses - Taxes - Social insurance premiums."

Expenses include PC purchases, communication costs, travel expenses, and so on, typically around 20-30% of revenue. Taxes consist of four types: income tax, resident tax, sole proprietor business tax, and consumption tax. Social insurance premiums are the total of National Health Insurance and National Pension contributions.

By accurately grasping these four elements, you can calculate a more realistic take-home amount.

Freelancers pay a wide variety of taxes and insurance premiums. Many people rarely looked at their pay slips as employees, but freelancers must calculate and pay these themselves, so accurate knowledge is essential.

This section explains the calculation methods and key points for income tax, resident tax, National Health Insurance premiums, National Pension premiums, sole proprietor business tax, and consumption tax. We also explain in detail the tax differences based on contract types, which are easy to overlook.

Income tax is applied on a progressive scale of 5-45% based on taxable income. The higher your taxable income, the higher the tax rate.

The formula is

(Revenue - Expenses - Blue Tax Return Special Deduction - Income Deductions) × Tax Rate - Tax Credits

For example, if taxable income is 1.95 million yen or less, the tax rate is 5%; if it's over 3.3 million yen and up to 6.95 million yen, it's 20%.

In addition, a Special Reconstruction Income Tax of 2.1% of the income tax amount is levied until 2037.

By claiming the 650,000 yen deduction under the Blue Tax Return, you can significantly reduce taxable income on the same revenue and potentially move into a lower tax bracket. As a first step in tax savings, be sure to consider applying for the Blue Tax Return.

Resident tax is calculated based on the previous year's income and consists of the municipal tax and prefectural tax combined.

The tax rate is generally 10% income-based tax (6% special ward tax + 4% metropolitan tax) plus a per-capita levy (about 5,000 yen per year).

The formula for the income-based tax is

(Taxable Income - Income Deductions) × 10%

Unlike income tax, resident tax is levied the following year. So while freelancers have a light resident tax burden in their first year, from the second year onward they receive a large bill based on the previous year's income—be careful of this.

If your income grows significantly in your first year of independence, set aside funds to prepare for the following year's resident tax payment.

National Health Insurance premiums are calculated differently by each municipality of residence and consist of an income-based portion and a per-capita portion.

In Setagaya Ward, Tokyo, the income-based rates for the basic and support portions total about 9.44%, with a per-capita levy of about 55,000 yen per year. With an annual income of 6 million yen (30% expenses), the annual burden is roughly 350,000 yen.

National Pension premiums are a fixed amount regardless of income: 16,980 yen per month (about 200,000 yen per year) for fiscal 2024.

The National Pension premium is a fixed amount regardless of income: 16,980 yen per month (about 200,000 yen per year) for fiscal 2024.

These add up to an annual burden of around 550,000 yen, which in most cases is higher than when you were a company employee. Be sure to factor them into your monthly expenses.

The individual business tax is levied when business income exceeds 2.9 million yen. Freelance engineers may be subject to a 5% tax rate as "contracting business," but those working on-site under quasi-mandate (jun'inin) contracts are often exempt.

Consumption tax obligations arise when your taxable sales two years prior exceed 10 million yen. The invoice system was introduced in October 2023, and registering as a qualified invoice issuer now requires paying consumption tax even with sales of 10 million yen or less.

As your income rises, it's important to calculate your take-home pay with these additional burdens in mind.

Freelance engineer contracts fall broadly into two types: "quasi-mandate contracts" and "contract (ukeoi) agreements." Did you know that this difference determines whether you are subject to the individual business tax?

A quasi-mandate contract is a form of work where you are stationed at the client's site and perform tasks under their direction. Because it resembles being a company employee, it is often exempt from the individual business tax.

On the other hand, a contract agreement promises the delivery of deliverables and falls under "contracting business," one of the legally designated business types. In this case, an individual business tax of 5% is levied.

For example, with an annual income of 7 million yen (taxable income of 3.37 million yen), a contract agreement incurs an individual business tax of about 24,000 yen, reducing take-home pay compared to a quasi-mandate contract.

Check the contents of your contract and know which type applies to you. Projects via agents tend to be quasi-mandate contracts, but outsourced development or projects you acquire yourself may be contract agreements.

This is an income level reachable even by side-business freelancers, making it a good time to seriously consider going independent.

Take-home pay at 7 million yen is about 5.57 million yen (a take-home rate of about 80%), and at 8 million yen it's about 6.34 million yen (about 79%).

At this income level, the 20% income tax rate applies, and national health insurance premiums approach their cap. Also, since taxable income exceeds 2.9 million yen, cases arise where the individual business tax (about 50,000-80,000 yen) is incurred.

This is also the time to consider hiring a tax accountant; with professional tax-saving measures, take-home pay can sometimes improve by 100,000 yen or more. This income level is also when you should consider incorporation.

Take-home pay at 10 million yen is about 7.7 million yen (a take-home rate of about 77%), and at 20 million yen it's about 14.3 million yen (about 71%). The higher your income, the heavier the tax burden due to progressive taxation, and the lower your take-home rate.

Above 10 million yen, a consumption tax obligation also arises starting the year after next. If you are registered for the invoice system, you also need to account for roughly 10% of sales being deducted as consumption tax.

With incorporation, the corporate tax rate is only 23.2%, so if your income tax rate exceeds 33% (taxable income over 9 million yen), the tax savings from establishing a company become significant. At this income level, working with a tax accountant and labor consultant is essential.

To increase take-home pay, it's important to approach from both sides: "increasing income" and "reducing expenses."

From here, we'll explain how to use deductions, record expenses, save on taxes while preparing for the future, and strategies for raising your rates. In particular, the blue tax return special deduction and the small business mutual aid system are must-use programs; using just these can sometimes yield annual tax savings of over 1 million yen.

By filing a blue tax return, you can receive an income deduction of up to 650,000 yen.

The requirements are threefold: "bookkeeping using double-entry accounting," "attaching a balance sheet and profit-and-loss statement," and "filing via e-Tax or using electronic record-keeping."

For example, with an annual income of 6 million yen and expenses of 1.8 million yen, the 650,000 yen deduction brings taxable income down to 1.55 million yen, with income tax of about 58,000 yen. Under a white tax return (no deduction), it would be about 115,000 yen, a difference of about 57,000 yen.

Accounting software (freee, Money Forward, etc.) can automate double-entry bookkeeping, so freelancers should always choose the blue tax return.

Missing expense deductions directly increases your taxable income.

The main expenses freelance engineers can claim include PCs and peripherals, internet and communication costs, coworking space fees, technical books and seminar costs, and travel and meal expenses for meetings.

If you work from home, you can also deduct part of your rent and utilities through "home office allocation." It is common to claim around 20-30% of these costs, depending on the proportion of your workspace.

Since receipts must be kept for 7 years, developing the habit of saving them digitally with a scanning app will make your final tax return easier.

The Small Business Mutual Aid Program (Jigyosho Kyosai) allows monthly contributions of 1,000 to 70,000 yen to be fully deducted from income, with the amount received as a retirement payment upon closing your business. You can deduct up to 840,000 yen per year, which for someone with a 20% income tax rate means tax savings of about 170,000 yen.

iDeCo (individual-type defined contribution pension) also allows full deduction of contributions, with a maximum annual contribution of 816,000 yen.

By combining both programs, you can achieve income deductions of over 1.65 million yen per year, realizing both future asset building and tax savings simultaneously. Since freelancers have no retirement benefits, it is important to use these programs to prepare for the future yourself.

Since tax savings have their limits, raising your rates is essential to fundamentally increase your take-home pay.

To land high-rate projects, it is effective to acquire in-demand skills (cloud, AI/machine learning, modern frameworks, etc.), get involved in upstream processes (requirements definition and design), and accumulate PM/leader experience.

Registering with multiple freelance agents helps you understand market rates, and you should also consider negotiating rates with existing clients. There is even data showing that the average annual income of Levtech Freelance users reaches 8.76 million yen.
