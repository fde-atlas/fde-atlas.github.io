The Forward Deployed Engineer Playbook for AI Startups

Forward Deployed Engineer (FDE) roles have increased 10x in the past few years, fueled by the rapid advancement of AI agents.

One of the most insightful discussions on this business model comes from the Y Combinator Lightcode podcast episode featuring Bob McGrew. He traces the origins of the FDE model at Palantir and explains how the same pattern is now powering a new generation of AI-native startups. I’ve watched and summarized the episode for those who want the 80:20 information.

Below I’ve broken down Bob’s insights into two macro sections: novel concepts introduced and core takeaways for future FDEs.

Novel Concepts

The Echo and Delta Teams

When Palantir built its FDE teams, they divided the field organization into two distinct roles that operated together at the customer site.

The Echo Team (Embedded Analysts)

  • Domain experts acting as embedded analysts & account managers.
  • Core Role: Understand the user’s workflow and identify the key problem to solve.
  • Hiring Profile: “someone from the domain you’re working in... possibly a former army officer or someone who worked deeply in healthcare”

An interesting point Bob emphasizes is that a crucial characteristic of a good echo team hire is they needed to be “rebels” or “heretics” who recognized that the current way of doing things was broken. If they were perfectly happy with the status quo, they would never “figure out the step function change that the new software has to be able to make”.

The Delta Team (Deployed Engineers)

  • Software engineers deployed to the field
  • Core Role: Build prototypes and deliver outcomes on a tight timeline.
  • Hiring Profile: Someone “very good at writing code extremely quickly, eating a lot of pain”.

Something unique about hiring for this role is that Bob explicitly warns against hiring “craftsmen”. You do not want someone focused on “making sure the abstractions are…going to be maintainable for a dozen years”.

The Gravel Road vs The Paved Superhighway

This is the central framework for how the FDE team interacts with the internal product engineering team. It defines the division of labor between custom implementation and scalable product development.

The Gravel Road (FDE Team)

The FDE goes to the customer site and builds a “gravel road to where the product needs to go”. They write the custom, unscalable code necessary to fill the gap between the existing product and the user’s immediate need.

The Paved Superhighway (Product Team)

The internal product team looks at the gravel road built by the FDE and figures out “how that should generalize to the next five customers or the next 10 customers, and then turn that gravel road into a paved superhighway”. They must hold the product vision and guess the correct, generalized problem to solve.

Core Takeaways

Below I summarize 3 core takeaways that generalize for firms adopting the “forward-deployed” business model.

1. The Core Purpose: Delivering Outcomes over Software

The foundational premise of the FDE model is that you are not selling a software installation, instead you are selling a guaranteed outcome. In the context of large enterprises or complex government deployments, the customer often does not believe a solution is possible because “they’ve had many large projects that have failed”.

To overcome this, the startup must take on the execution risk. As McGrew explains, the startup must say:

“We’re gonna just believe in our own execution and we’re gonna take on the risk and you pay us if it works or you pay us when we’re actually able to expand.”

This outcome-driven approach relies heavily on demo-driven development. Instead of building features in isolation based on internal capabilities, the FDE team builds a prototype from the customer’s perspective. The goal of this demo is not just to show functionality, but to elicit a visceral reaction:

“A really good demo is something where you show it to the customer and you are creating desire in that customer for what you’re doing. They have to see what you’re doing and just want to reach out and grab it and bring it into their life.”

2. The Economics: Contract Size vs. Custom Work

A common criticism of the FDE model is that it resembles an unscalable consulting business. McGrew addresses this by contrasting the FDE economic strategy with traditional SaaS product-market fit.

In a traditional SaaS model, the goal is to drive down costs by doing less work per customer while keeping the contract size the same. The FDE strategy flips this dynamic:

“In the FDE strategy, you want to drive the contract size up. So you’re doing more and more valuable work for this customer and also for future customers. And because you’re doing more valuable work, it’s okay. You can leave the amount of customization you do per customer the same.”

Early in an engagement, profit margins may be negative as the startup invests heavily in product discovery and custom engineering at the customer site. Only as the FDEs deliver the initial outcome do they “earn the right” to tackle more critical, higher-value problems within the enterprise. This “land and expand” motion drives the contract size up, making the custom work economically viable.

3. The Deployment Reality: Executive Buy-In and IT Blockers

Deploying into a large enterprise, especially on-premise, requires navigating significant organizational friction. McGrew highlights that the primary blockers are often internal IT teams or other gatekeepers whose incentives are “not aligned with the end user”.

Because these internal groups “do not think like startups,” overcoming them requires top-level executive support. The problem the startup is solving must be so critical that the CEO or top leadership is willing to intervene:

“This is part of why it matters that you’re working on one of the CEO’s top five problems. Because you need to be able to bring in someone from the top to say, yes, give them authority to operate... let them do what they need to do.”

Without this level of executive buy-in, the customer will lack the energy required to force the deployment through the organization’s bureaucratic hurdles.
