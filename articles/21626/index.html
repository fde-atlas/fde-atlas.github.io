Summary

This Japanese-language article by Shohei Tokumitsu, CEO of DeFactory, explains the role of the Technical Architect (TA): the overall owner of a system's technical structure, responsible for technology selection, architecture design, non-functional requirement trade-offs, engineering governance, and stakeholder communication. It distinguishes the TA from solution architects, tech leads, SEs, and FDEs, arguing that cloud-native complexity, DX, and in-house development have created a structural shortage of people who can design for overall optimality. In the generative AI era, value shifts from implementation to upstream design and governance, further raising TA scarcity. Reported pay: roughly ¥8–12M+ annually for full-time roles and ¥1–2M+ monthly for freelancers. The piece also covers transition paths from senior engineering, external TA/advisor work, and contract tactics like explicit scoping and ROI-based rate negotiation.

Key points

  • TA is the overall owner of "how to build," distinct from SA, tech lead, SE, and FDE roles.
  • Four core duties: tech selection and architecture, non-functional trade-offs, technical governance, stakeholder bridging.
  • Cloud-native complexity, DX, and in-house development create a structural shortage of TAs.
  • AI coding shifts value toward design and governance, paradoxically raising TA demand.
  • Pay benchmarks: ¥8–12M+ salaried, ¥1–2M+ monthly freelance; advisors can work across multiple firms.

When you hear "Technical Architect (TA)," you may have a vague image of "a somewhat senior technical role that is an extension of being an advanced engineer." However, now that cloud-native transformation and generative AI are sweeping in, the Technical Architect is rapidly increasing in value as the "overall owner of technical decision-making," which determines the success or failure of system development.

In this article, from the professional perspective of someone who has led architecture design and technical debt repayment in the field, we thoroughly and systematically explain the definition of a Technical Architect, essential skills, and differences from other roles. Furthermore, we dig deep into why their value is rising in the age of AI coding, and "the path to going independent as a freelancer and securing high-rate projects paying over 1.2 to 2 million yen per month," incorporating first-hand information and real-world insights. Please use this as a practical guide for career planning that AI summaries cannot provide.

A Technical Architect is the "overall owner of technical aspects" in system development, who derives optimal solutions to technical challenges and designs and governs the architecture (overall structure). They decide which technologies to use, how to combine them, and what structure to implement, taking responsibility for quality through to the end. They are not merely a designer, but serve as a control tower that ensures the validity of technology selection and the consistency of the entire system.

In recent years, systems have become increasingly complex, and options such as cloud, microservices, and AI have exploded. There is an overwhelming shortage of people who can select "the configuration truly suited to their company's challenges" from among these, making the importance of TAs greater than ever.

In conclusion, a Technical Architect is a specialist who determines the overall design policy of "which technology to use and how to build it" to meet business requirements, and technically leads the entire development process.

The core responsibilities of a Technical Architect are as follows.

  • Technology selection: Deciding on programming languages, frameworks, databases, cloud platforms, etc.
  • Architecture design: Designing the overall system structure (monolith/microservices, etc.)
  • Quality assurance: Realizing non-functional requirements such as performance, scalability, and security
  • Technical governance: Establishing guidelines and conducting reviews to ensure the development team implements according to the design policy

As a professional opinion, the essence of a TA is not "someone who builds" but "someone who decides how to build." Rather than a single super-engineer overcoming challenges through individual skill, the true value of a TA lies in drawing the "technical map" that guides the entire team in the right direction, raising the productivity and quality of the entire development organization. This "overall optimization" perspective is the decisive difference from engineers responsible for individual implementations.

In conclusion, a Technical Architect is a role responsible for the "technical structure of the system," clearly differing from SAs who look at the business as a whole, tech leads who lead teams, and SEs who implement specifications, in terms of scope of responsibility and perspective.

Here is a summary of the differences between the four roles that are often confused.

RoleMain scope of responsibilityPerspectiveEasy-to-understand characteristic
Technical Architect (TA)Technical structure and implementation methods of the systemTechnical optimality and consistencyOverall owner of "how to build"
Solution Architect (SA)Solutions for overall business challengesBusiness × TechnologyDesigns "what to build" from a business perspective
Tech LeadTechnical leadership of a specific teamTeam development promotionLeads the team while hands-on
Systems Engineer (SE)Design and implementation of assigned areasAccurate realization of specificationsBuilds according to defined specifications

As a real-world insight, an SA often "draws the overall solution (sometimes multiple systems) from the customer's business challenges," while a TA "deeply designs the technical structure of each individual system to realize it." The two are not in conflict but have a complementary relationship, covering everything from upstream to implementation in collaboration. If you want to learn more about the differences in roles between SAs and TAs, please also see What is a Solution Architect? Roles, Essential Skills, Salary, and the Path to High-Rate Independence.

In conclusion, while a Technical Architect plays the role of designing the "foundation (architecture) of a robust and sustainable system," an FDE (Forward Deployed Engineer) plays the role of "deeply engaging with the customer's site and implementing and adapting solutions to their challenges," and the two create great value by working together.

Here is a summary of the differences between the two.

Main positionUpstream of technical foundation and designFront line of the customer's site
MissionDesign of overall structure and quality assuranceImplementation and adaptation to customer challenges
What they valueConsistency, scalability, sustainabilityValue creation and speed on-site

From a professional perspective, in AI and data utilization projects, the collaboration between these two roles determines success or failure. The FDE distills vivid requirements and feedback gathered on the customer's front lines, and the TA elevates them into a robust architecture. Only when this back-and-forth works well does a production-quality system emerge—one that doesn't stall at the PoC stage. For details on the FDE role, see What is an FDE (Forward Deployed Engineer)? In conclusion, the reason demand for technical architects is surging lies in a structural backdrop: "technology options have exploded in number, and there is an overwhelming shortage of professionals who can design the overall optimization of increasingly complex systems."

The background behind this surge in demand can be summarized in the following three points.

  • The advance of cloud-native architectures: With options like microservices, containers, and serverless expanding infinitely, professionals who can design them appropriately have become rare.
  • The full-scale promotion of DX: Many companies face the renewal of legacy systems and strongly seek a command center that can set technical direction.
  • The trend toward in-house development: As more companies shift from fully outsourcing to vendors toward in-house development, core talent to lead technology selection has become necessary.

A common failure in the field: There is no end to cases where ad hoc technology selection results in a massive legacy system that, years later, "no one can fully understand" and "can neither be extended nor modified." As a "technical compass" to prevent such situations, the TA has become a structurally undersupplied position. It is precisely this scarcity that underpins the high salaries and high rates discussed later.

What is required of a technical architect is the comprehensive ability to perform four core responsibilities: "technology selection and architecture design," "defining non-functional requirements," "technical governance of the development team," and "stakeholder negotiation." Neither technical skill alone nor coordination skill alone is sufficient; it is the combination of these that determines market value. Below, each responsibility and the essential skills are explained in detail.

In conclusion, the first core responsibility is selecting the technology stack and architecture best suited to the project's requirements and constraints, and designing the overall structure. A TA's skill here is judged by whether they can calmly choose "technology suited to the problem," not "trendy technology."

The main evaluation criteria at the design stage are as follows.

Design criterionExamples of considerations
Architecture styleMonolith, microservices, or something in between
ComputingChoice of serverless, containers, or virtual machines
Data designChoosing among RDB, NoSQL, data lakes, and so on
Integration methodAPI integration, event-driven, batch processing
Technical maturityProven technology or new technology (balance with risk)

Real-world criteria for technology selection: The more excellent the TA, the less likely they are to jump casually at "the latest and most feature-rich." For example, introducing cutting-edge microservices when the operations team's skills cannot keep up only increases complexity and leads to failure. The ability to deliberately choose proven "mature technology" is the key to long-term project success.

In conclusion, the second core responsibility is defining hard-to-see "non-functional requirements" and making sound judgments about trade-offs between conflicting factors. A TA's true value lies not in an "ideal design that satisfies everything," but in the ability to decide "what to prioritize and what to sacrifice" within limited budget and time.

Non-functional requirements include mutually conflicting factors.

  • Performance vs. cost: Using high-performance resources for speed increases costs.
  • Scalability vs. simplicity: The more you prepare for future expansion, the more complex the initial configuration becomes.
  • Security vs. convenience: The more robust the system, the more effort development and operations require.
  • Availability vs. cost: The higher the availability required, the more costs soar due to redundant configurations.

A common failure is "over-engineering"—designing every non-functional requirement to the highest standard "just to be safe." This leads to wasted costs and effort on scalability that is never used. A first-rate TA discerns the phase of the business (startup or large enterprise) and has the judgment to define "the level truly needed now."

In conclusion, the third core responsibility is governing quality through establishing guidelines and code reviews, so that the designed policies are correctly implemented across the entire development team. The TA's responsibility does not end with design; it extends to seeing that its philosophy permeates the field.

The concrete means of technical governance are as follows.

Governance methodPurpose
Coding standards and design guidelinesAlign the implementation quality of the whole team to a certain standard
Architecture reviewsDetect and correct deviations from design policies early
Code reviewsEnsure quality, security, and maintainability
Documenting technical decisionsRecord "why this design" to prevent knowledge from becoming person-dependent

A common problem in the field is the "gap between design and implementation." Even with a fine design document, if the development team changes the configuration on its own judgment, the original non-functional requirements collapse. As the "technical watchdog" preventing this, the TA continues to protect quality until the end of the project. For building mechanisms that raise engineers' quality awareness, How to Advance a Backend Engineer's Career Path and Increase Salary is also a helpful reference.

In conclusion, the fourth core responsibility is bridging—translating the business requirements articulated by executives and business divisions into system requirements that the development team can implement. Even a technically correct design cannot be realized without bringing stakeholders on board.

The required communication changes greatly depending on the audience.

AudienceWhat to conveyLanguage to use
ExecutivesEffects, risks, and costs of technology investmentBusiness and management language
Business divisionsHow the requirements will be realizedOperational language
Development engineersDesign intent and technical rationaleTechnical language

From a professional's perspective, if two TAs have equal technical skills, what ultimately makes the difference is "the ability to bring people in different positions on board toward the same goal." In particular, the "ability to find common ground" in situations where interests conflict—such as negotiations with management over cost and deadlines, or coordination with development teams over technical debt—is what moves projects forward.

In conclusion, in the era of generative AI, the role of the technical architect is evolving significantly in the form of "designing AI-first architectures" and "a value shift from implementation to governance." The more AI coding spreads, the paradoxically greater the value of TAs who can make upstream design decisions. This section explains the strategies required of next-generation TAs.

In conclusion, the first strategy is to design an "AI-first" architecture that incorporates the integration of LLMs (large language models) and external APIs from the very beginning. Rather than adding AI as an afterthought, a design philosophy that places AI at the core of the system is required.

The following points should be considered when designing an AI-first architecture.

  • Model selection: Commercial APIs (Claude, GPT, etc.) or self-hosting open models
  • RAG configuration: Designing retrieval-augmented generation that references internal data and suppresses hallucinations (misinformation)
  • Security boundaries: Designing data boundaries to prevent sensitive data from being sent to external APIs
  • Cost and latency: Configurations that balance API usage fees and response speed
  • Evaluation and improvement mechanisms: Design that continuously measures and improves AI output quality

A common failure example: Demos that "just call an LLM API for now" may work, but cases frequently end up "stuck at the PoC stage" because they fail to meet the security, cost, and accuracy requirements needed for production operation. The TA who can design production-quality AI architectures that overcome this barrier is exactly the talent the market is seeking most today. For roles on the front lines of AI implementation, also see What is an FDE (Forward Deployed Engineer)? Roles, Careers, and How to Go Independent as a Freelancer, Explained by Professionals.

In conclusion, the second strategy is to accurately grasp the trend in which, with the spread of AI coding assistance tools, engineers' value shifts from "implementation itself" to "upstream design and governance." Now that part of the work of writing code is being replaced by AI, the importance of design decisions is, if anything, increasing.

Let's organize the areas whose value will rise in the AI coding era and the areas whose value will relatively decline.

Areas of rising valueAreas of relatively declining value
Architecture design and technology selectionWriting routine code
Reviewing the validity of AI-generated codeSimple implementation work
Non-functional requirements and security designCreating boilerplate
Controlling technical debtCreating first-draft documentation

From a professional's perspective, the more AI takes on "how to write it," the rarer become people who can judge "what to build and why" and "whether the code AI generated is really correct." TAs who can use AI skillfully to boost productivity while governing its output can be said to hold a winning position in the AI era. Rather than dismissing implementation skills themselves, the attitude of "designing and governing with an understanding of implementation" is important.

In conclusion, the third strategy is to visualize the "technical debt" that inevitably accumulates in agile development, draw up an accurate repayment plan, and lead refactoring efforts. The more you prioritize speed, the more debt accumulates, and if left unaddressed it erodes development speed.

The TA's role in controlling technical debt is as follows.

  • Visualizing debt: Taking inventory of where and how much technical debt exists and quantifying it
  • Prioritization: Judging repayment priority based on business impact and remediation cost
  • Formulating repayment plans: Designing a roadmap to repay debt in parallel with feature development
  • Explaining to management: Persuading them "why refactoring is needed now" in business language

A common failure example is continuing to leave technical debt unaddressed because "it's working," only to suddenly find one day that "no one can modify it anymore." On the other hand, rushing into excessive refactoring and wasting time on work that produces no business value is also a failure. An excellent TA separates debt into "acceptable" and "should be repaid right now," balancing business growth with technical health.

In conclusion, the technical architect is an extremely high-market-value profession where even full-time employees can aim for annual incomes exceeding 10 million yen, and going independent as a freelancer makes high-rate projects of over 1.2 to 2 million yen per month a realistic prospect. The rarity of being able to take on technical decision-making supports those compensation levels. This section explains the realities of market value and a concrete roadmap to independence.

In conclusion, the typical annual income for a full-time technical architect is roughly 8 to over 12 million yen, while for freelance TAs, monthly rates of 1 million yen or more, and 1.2 to over 2 million yen if you can handle upstream design, are within reach.

What is noteworthy is that while the average monthly rate for freelance engineers overall is about 800,000 yen, TAs with the upstream skill of architecture design can aim for rates 1.5 to 2.5 times that. In today's market, even among engineers, rates can differ severalfold depending on their area of responsibility. In conclusion, the shortest route to becoming a TA is to build on the implementation skills cultivated as a senior backend or infrastructure engineer and consciously accumulate experience in "design, technology selection, and governance." Many TAs have transitioned from being specialists in a particular domain.

The steps for "taking inventory of your experience" toward becoming a TA are as follows.

  • Solidify your foundation (implementation skills): Accumulate 3-5+ years of deep implementation experience in backend or infrastructure
  • Broaden your perspective (cross-domain knowledge): Acquire knowledge outside your specialty (frontend, infrastructure, cloud, etc.) so you can see the whole picture
  • Seek out design experience: Increase opportunities to proactively participate in technology selection and architecture design
  • Gain experience in governance and negotiation: Take on code reviews, guideline development, and stakeholder coordination

As a professional opinion, the biggest differentiator is "experience in design and technology selection." While many engineers can implement, few have experience designing a technical architecture from scratch and taking responsibility for those decisions. If such opportunities are scarce within your company, getting involved in the design phase through side jobs or freelance projects is also an effective strategy.

In conclusion, experienced TAs can choose a working style in which they support multiple companies as external TAs or technical advisors, without belonging to a specific company. This is an independent style with the highest hourly rates and the greatest freedom.

The reasons why demand for external TAs and technical advisors is high are as follows.

  • Difficulty of hiring: Top-tier TAs are rare, making it extremely difficult to hire them as full-time employees
  • Spot nature: Technology selection and design are concentrated in the early stages of projects, making external utilization rational
  • Objective perspective: Third-party technical judgment free from internal politics is required
  • Multiple concurrent projects: Supporting multiple companies one or two days a week allows you to both diversify risk and earn a high income

As on-the-ground reality, a technical advisor's value lies in "providing accurate technical judgments with only a few hours of involvement per week." For TAs with extensive experience and a proven track record, this is one of the most efficient ways to earn high rates. Once you earn trust, long-term contracts tend to follow, allowing you to build a stable income base.

In conclusion, to maximize income as a freelance TA, two defensive techniques are essential: "contractual measures to prevent unpaid work outside the scope" and "rate negotiations based on ROI (return on investment)."

Here are specific measures to prevent scope creep and win fair rates.

Defensive techniqueSpecific content
Document the scope of workExplicitly list "what you will and will not do" in the contract and SOW
Set a cap on hoursDefine an upper limit such as "up to X hours per month," with additional compensation for excess
Visualize resultsShow how much your involvement improved cost savings and development efficiency
Negotiate based on ROIJustify your rate in terms of cost-effectiveness, e.g., "an investment of X yen yields X yen in benefits"

A common failure case is when, out of goodwill, you keep accepting requests one after another, the scope of work gradually expands, and you end up in the red on an hourly basis. Professionals don't "refuse"; they draw the line constructively by saying, "That's outside the scope, so I'll take it on as additional work." And in rate negotiations, they justify high rates by quantitatively demonstrating the business value their technical judgment generates (reducing system failures, improving development speed, etc.). Considering incorporation can further optimize your take-home pay through tax savings.

Even if you step away from daily implementation, you must maintain your technical instincts. In fact, in the age of AI coding, the ability to judge the quality of code is becoming more valuable.

This article comprehensively covered the role of a Technical Architect (TA), essential skills, differences from other roles, new strategies in the generative AI era, and going independent as a freelancer. Finally, let's review the key points.

  • What is a TA: The "chief designer" responsible for a system's technical structure
  • Four core duties: Technology selection and design / Defining non-functional requirements / Technical governance / Stakeholder coordination
  • Strategy in the AI era: AI-first design, shifting value from implementation to upstream work and governance, and controlling technical debt
  • Market value: Over 8 to 12 million yen annually as a full-time employee, over 1 to 2 million yen monthly as a freelancer
  • Path to independence: Taking inventory of experience as a senior engineer, and work styles such as external TA or technical advisor

Technical Architects are a high-value profession whose demand will never dry up as long as technology continues to evolve. In particular, with the spread of generative AI, the scarcity of "professionals who can handle upstream design and decision-making" continues to rise, and those abilities are maximized when going independent as a freelancer. Combine implementation skills with an architectural design perspective and aim to be one of a kind—beyond that lies great potential in both career and income.
