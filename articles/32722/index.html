Berkeley Agentic AI Course FDE Learning Sequence

Original title: 伯克利 Agentic AI 课程 FDE 学习顺序

FDE Times · 2026-10-09 · 4 min read

Summary

The article lays out a reading order for the UC Berkeley CS294/194-196 Agentic AI course (fall 2025, taught by Dawn Song) for developers aiming to become FDEs. It recommends starting with Clay Bavor of Sierra on deploying agents in real environments, then the evals and safety lectures, followed by 2024's enterprise workflow content, leaving training lectures for last. Three takeaways: agents are products, not just technology; reliability matters more than occasional brilliance at scale (a 99% accuracy rate means 10,000 wrong answers over a million conversations); and agents can evaluate other agents, as in the AgentX–AgentBeats competition's green agent setup. The MOOC series has over 40,000 enrollments.

Key points

  • Watch Clay Bavor's Sierra lecture on real-world deployment first.
  • Suggested order: deployment, evals, safety, then enterprise workflows.
  • Treat agents as products: ask who uses them and who is accountable.
  • At scale, reliability beats occasional brilliance—99% still fails 10,000 times.
  • Build eval agents that score your agent automatically; more valuable than certificates.

Key Takeaways at a Glance

  • The Fall 2025 session (CS294/194-196) is taught by Dawn Song. Xinyun Chen only participated as a co-instructor in the Fall 2024 and Spring 2025 sessions.
  • The content most relevant to FDE work is the lecture by Clay Bavor (Sierra) on deploying agents in real environments, along with the lectures on evals and safety.
  • Three points worth remembering: agents are products; reliability matters more than occasional brilliance; use agents to score agents.

In the Fall 2025 session of UC Berkeley's CS294/194-196 Agentic AI course, there is a lecture on November 10 that Forward Deployed Engineers (FDEs) should watch before all other lectures.

In this lecture, Sierra's Clay Bavor shares practical experience deploying AI agents in real environments. One learner summarized their biggest takeaway on dev.to: agents are shifting from being a technology to being a product.

Turning agents from demos into products that customers use every day is also the core of what FDEs do at customer companies.

This course belongs to the MOOC series that Berkeley RDI considers its flagship program. The institute's education page shows enrollment has exceeded 40,000.

If you are a developer with a few years of experience looking to transition into an FDE role, don't treat this course as a theory course on agents. You'll get more out of it by treating it as a manual for making agents run reliably inside enterprises.

Who teaches each session?

The MOOC series has three sessions: Large Language Model Agents (Fall 2024), CS294/194-280 Advanced Large Language Model Agents (Spring 2025), and Agentic AI (Fall 2025). UC Berkeley's Dawn Song teaches the Fall 2025 session, and also taught the Fall 2024 session.

Google DeepMind researcher Xinyun Chen participated as a guest co-instructor in the Fall 2024 and Spring 2025 sessions. Her name does not appear on the official Fall 2025 page; to hear Chen lecture, you'll need to watch the first two sessions.

Besides the Sierra lecture, the Fall 2025 session also includes lectures on agent evals and agent training, including one by Noam Brown (OpenAI) on multi-agent systems, and one on agent safety by Dawn Song. The Fall 2024 session had more content on agents in enterprise scenarios, including AI Agents for Enterprise Workflows by Nicolas Chapados (ServiceNow).

In what order should you study?

Berkeley recommends that its own students have a foundation in machine learning and deep learning before enrolling. The course page sets no separate requirements for MOOC learners. Even so, if you've never trained a model, the lectures on agent training may be challenging.

If you build agents for customers, watch the Clay Bavor lecture first, then the Fall 2025 lectures on agent evals and safety.

Then go back to the Fall 2024 session and watch the lectures on enterprise workflows. Save the lectures on agent training and multi-agent systems for last, and watch them when you have an actual agent that needs improvement.

The Fall 2024 session has ended. At the time, certificates had multiple tiers: each tier required completing 12 quizzes and submitting a qualifying article; the Mastery tier added 3 labs, and the Ninja and Legendary tiers added a hackathon. This structure still works well for self-study: watch lectures, write articles, do labs, and finish with a real project.

Point One: Agents Are Already Products

The dev.to learner's observation may sound obvious, but it changes the questions you ask in your first meeting with a customer.

If you view an agent as technology, you'll ask which model is strongest. If you view it as a product, you'll ask: who is using it? Who is accountable when it gets things wrong? Is there an escalation path to humans? When you're on-site with the customer, write down the answers to these three questions before writing your first prompt.

Point Two: Reliability Matters More Than Brilliance

The learner also wrote that when an agent handles millions of conversations, reliability matters more than a few standout performances. This is the learner's personal view, not that of the course organizers. A simple calculation shows why it holds.

Suppose a customer service agent answers 99% of questions correctly. In a 20-question demo, it will likely get none wrong. But across 1,000,000 conversations, a 1% error rate means 10,000 customers receive wrong answers, each one potentially becoming a complaint ticket.

So when demoing to customers, don't only pick the questions the agent answers best. Show its error rate on a sufficiently large test set, and explain what it does when it's unsure of an answer.

Point Three: Use Agents to Score Agents

The Fall 2025 session is paired with the AgentX–AgentBeats competition. In the competition, the benchmark is built as an agent responsible for evals, called a green agent, which scores the competing agents. This idea can be applied directly to customer projects.

Suppose you're building an agent for an e-commerce platform to handle refund requests. Instead of having the customer's QA team read logs line by line, build an eval agent that plays the buyer: sometimes it requests refunds for overdue orders, sometimes it reports incorrect order numbers.

Every time you modify a prompt or swap a tool, rerun all the scenarios and immediately get comparable numbers. A set of evals like this also carries more weight on a resume than a certificate.

A line like "built an automated eval suite for agents that catches failures before release" shows recruiters that you know how to measure agent quality. When reading FDE job descriptions, if you see terms like evals, deployment, and safety, go watch the lectures on those topics in the course first.

Course certificates will eventually lose their value. The habit of scoring agents with numbers before delivering them to customers will serve you for a long time.
