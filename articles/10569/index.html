Microsoft, ByteDance, and Ford Hiring at High Salaries! AI Gives Rise to Brand-New Job Roles

The whole internet talks about AI replacing human labor, but the reality is that big companies are hiring like crazy.

Microsoft just had news of laying off thousands of people, and then it dropped $2.5 billion to establish Frontier Company, integrating about 6,000 engineers, technical consultants, and sales staff, and stationing them on-site at enterprise customers like Unilever and Novo Nordisk. Ford went even further — it just spent big money to bring back 350 veteran engineers, because the AI-automated design system produced a pile of errors, with quality slipping to the point that manual rework became unavoidable.

Isn't AI supposed to replace human labor? How did it end up more dependent on people than ever?

Microsoft drops $2.5 billion, shifting from "selling CDs" to "on-site installation"

Microsoft's 6,000 on-site staff were drawn directly from existing teams. Judson Althoff, president of the commercial business, publicly admitted back in June that "binding only to OpenAI models when building Copilot three years ago was a mistake." Now Microsoft is pushing the platform-neutral Frontier Company to help customers "flexibly switch between different large models."

The core point Althoff is making is really just one thing: previously, when selling Copilot licenses, companies assumed they could take it back and use it themselves, only to find they couldn't get it working at all.

The ideal for software companies is "selling CDs" — customers take them home and install them on their own, which is also the traditional SaaS mindset. Now Microsoft is sending people on-site to install, debug, and teach customers how to use it, which shows that the productization of AI tools hasn't been figured out yet. Customers don't know how to use it, don't dare to use it, or can't use it well, so vendors have no choice but to send people over. The $2.5 billion is initial funding covering salaries, travel, platform building, and operations — sustaining this team is a considerable expense at scale.

Palantir did this twenty years ago, but Palantir was born with consulting DNA. The fact that Microsoft, a pure software giant, is also stepping in shows that the self-service level of AI products is far below expectations. Enterprises find they don't know how to embed it into business processes, how to integrate it with legacy systems, or how to calculate the return — and the whole "efficiency boost" story becomes empty talk. Licenses become mere decorations, and naturally aren't renewed.

Chinese big tech companies are heading in the same direction. ByteDance offers Forward Deployed Engineers (FDE) a monthly salary of 35,000 to 70,000 yuan with 15 months' pay, up to an annual salary of 1.05 million. Alibaba Cloud Intelligence FDEs get 20,000 to 50,000 yuan per month with 16 months' pay. LinkedIn's 2026 Workforce Report shows that from 2023 to 2025, global FDE job postings grew 42-fold — measured by job postings, with a low base, mainly because the role is an emerging category. Over the same period, AI engineer postings grew only 13-fold.

What big companies are competing for isn't coders, but "translators." The core capability of an FDE isn't writing code — it's on-site diagnosis. When a customer says "I want to adopt AI," the FDE must first figure out: is your data usable, where can the business process be cut in, and how is the return calculated? You have to be on-site, understand both the tech stack and the customer's business, and be able to translate "I want this button in red" into system architecture on the spot.

On June 30, AWS just announced a $1 billion investment to establish a similar forward-deployed engineering division; OpenAI and Anthropic had earlier set up their own deployment joint ventures. All the big players are doing the same thing — the bulk of enterprise AI revenue has shifted from API call fees to service fees for "putting people on-site."

Ford brings back 350 veteran engineers to fix its AI

Microsoft is sending people out; Ford is different — Ford is bringing people back.

On June 25, Ford executives publicly admitted that the AI-automated design system had caused quality to decline, forcing the company to recall more than 350 senior engineers to fix the errors. VP Charles Poon's exact words: "We mistakenly believed that simply introducing AI would produce high-quality products."

Once the veteran engineers left, all the AI inherited was blank space. The experience never written into documentation — like why a veteran engineer would add an extra weld pass on a certain seam — never made it into the database, leaving the AI as an intern blindly guessing. Ford also added 100,000 AI-automated tests and a 40-person QA team, and its quality ranking has rebounded significantly.

Ford hasn't given up on AI — it's making up for a missed lesson. AI's effectiveness depends on the quality of its training data; if the data lacks the tacit knowledge of veteran engineers, what it produces is garbage. AI can write perfect code and draw beautiful design diagrams, but it doesn't understand the unspoken rule that "this legacy system must not be touched."

Put bluntly, well-diggers are now worth more than water-sellers

Deloitte's "2026 China Manufacturing AI Adoption White Paper" shows that among a survey sample covering 200 manufacturing enterprises above a designated size, 91% fell short of expectations. Manufacturing is the hardest nut for AI adoption, and this figure says it all.

Getting an AI API is like getting a Swiss Army knife — lots of functions, but you don't know which screw to turn first. After buying the API, you need to clean data, tune prompts, connect internal systems, redesign business processes, and train employees. None of this work can be done by the API — it takes people.

This also explains why companies like Citi and Adobe have recently restricted employees from using flagship large models. Compute costs can be saved by downgrading models, but the labor costs of implementation services cannot be saved by a single cent. The total cost of AI has never been just the token bill — it's just that many companies never did this math before.

Some will surely say this is just a temporary phenomenon of AI's early stage — once agents mature and products become foolproof enough, this many people won't be needed anymore.

That statement is only half right. Standardized, generalized scenarios will indeed gradually be solved through productization. But the core pain point of enterprise-level scenarios has never been that models aren't strong enough — it's that every company has its own legacy systems, unspoken business rules, and undocumented historical experience. AI can never fully learn these things, and they can't be standardized either.

Human roles will upgrade from "execution roles" to "translation, debugging, and oversight roles." People will always be the last mile between AI and real business.

Microsoft is dispatching 6,000 people to customer sites, ByteDance and Alibaba are competing for million-yuan-a-year "translators," and Ford is bringing veteran engineers back to fix its AI. The three companies are worlds apart, but they're doing the same math: AI-to-B can't be accounted for by API call fees — it has to be counted in "person-days."

The API itself isn't valuable — what's valuable are the well-diggers. Those shouting "AI replaces humans" are mostly tool sellers; those actually using AI are still busy hiring people.
