Full text

One year after leaving Alibaba, former Alibaba Vice President and DingTalk President Ye Jun has finally settled on his entrepreneurial direction: enterprise-grade AI agents. His new company, Shenzhou Zhixu (Deep Space Order), was founded about three months ago and has already raised tens of millions of RMB in funding, led by Guangzhou 4399 Information Technology Co., Ltd., with two paying customers.

For Ye Jun, this may carry a sense of continuing unfinished business.

Leaving DingTalk came as something of a surprise to Ye Jun. At the time, DingTalk was fully preparing for its 8.0 launch event, planning to release a version that would fully pivot to an Agent foundation, which he called DingTalk's "AI version." On March 31, 2025, he received notice of a position change.

Three months later, Ye Jun left Alibaba, where he had worked for 19 years.

That launch event, which never took place, became a regret on Ye Jun's AI journey. During his time at DingTalk, he firmly believed that "Agents are the window to the next era," and starting in 2022 he pushed the business to fully pivot toward AI.

Ye Jun knew clearly that what he once wanted to do could only be done on the platform of a big tech company. Now, as an entrepreneur, what he can do is use his past resources and experience to reach the gaps that big companies cannot or will not touch: building enterprise agents, digging into specific industries, and going deep into the business processes of company after company.

Foreign trade and cross-border e-commerce are the specific industries Ye Jun chose first. He found that many unsolved problems exist there. For example, helping overseas buyers more accurately match with Chinese suppliers, and how to use AI to improve the quality of responses to inquiries from overseas customers, thereby influencing deal closures.

One of Ye Jun's assets comes from the experience and resources accumulated over the past five years driving enterprise-level business at DingTalk. He values the commercialization of To B business, and his usual working state was running between all kinds of enterprise clients. "I meet more than 100 enterprise clients a year," he said, describing his level of commitment at his busiest.

The funding Shenzhou Zhixu has received so far comes mainly from the industry side. One business owner who knows him well said that whatever he does, they will invest.

Of course, Ye Jun also faces skepticism.

During our conversation, Ye Jun once joked about the outside world's bias against big-company executives starting businesses: "After all, when executives start companies, most of them fail."

When Ye Jun left Alibaba, there was a common assessment: he was more of a professional manager, good at taking something from 10 to 100 rather than from 0 to 1. As AI redefined collaborative work, DingTalk needed someone willing to start from zero again. Now, running his own startup, Ye Jun has arrived at the position of going from 0 to 1.

In his five years at DingTalk, Ye Jun accumulated the trust of a large number of B-end clients; within one month of the new company's founding, it had two paying customers.

But Ye Jun knows clearly that the real 0-to-1 problem has not been solved yet. Can these orders, built on personal resources, experience, and trust, be consolidated into standardized products? For example, can a solution that requires an FDE to work on-site to get running be replicated from one company to an entire industry, and then from one industry to more industries?

Ye Jun was once considered good at going from 10 to 100, but today he admits that if this startup fails, the problem is more likely to lie in the "10 to 100" part—after all, a startup lacks the resources and talent density of a big company.

Below is the conversation between Ye Jun and "Future Human Lab" (hereinafter "Future Human"), edited for clarity.

Ye Jun

Ye Jun: I spent many years at Alibaba, fully responsible for product and R&D, sales, marketing, services, and HR, finance, and legal—a role with independent P&L. That experience gave me a lot of training. The company assigns different roles at different stages, and I gladly accepted the change at the time. I had worked for five years and was genuinely exhausted, so I was happy to take a break. Future Human: DingTalk was preparing for its 8.0 launch event at the time. What was that version originally supposed to release? Ye Jun: A fully AI version. The product was supposed to continue the roadmap laid out at the 7.5 launch event, open up the Agent foundation capabilities, and rebuild the AI software and hardware ecosystem. Future Human: DingTalk's AI momentum and business results were both good at the time. In your analysis, why did the group still decide to change leadership? Ye Jun: The group's strategic needs in different cycles. Perhaps from the group's perspective, AI is so critical to Alibaba that they wanted someone more battle-ready and more entrepreneurial. I could understand and support that. Future Human: Looking back, how do you evaluate your achievements at DingTalk? What did you truly accomplish? Ye Jun: One thing I consistently did was make the product create deep value that customers were willing to pay for. The essence of commercialization is value exchange. A To B product without value cannot last long on stories and narratives or subsidies. I spent four and a half years on commercialization after taking over. In terms of ecosystem and openness, customers gained access to data on attendance, video conferencing, documents, HR, and more, and began feeding their own enterprise data to AI. I believe that for enterprise-level products, enterprise customers should own their data sovereignty. To stay attuned to the market's rhythm, I would meet over a hundred entrepreneurs a year and have deep conversations with clients—clients all care deeply about data. On the product side, we explored early: AI code writing, AI generation, AI search, super assistants, and AI knowledge bases. The AI product paradigm in the market has shifted at least three times. We connected seven external models into the Agent marketplace—we were among the first in China to pioneer this open paradigm—but regrettably we never built a powerful general-purpose Agent. We worked hard to bring the business into the AI era, pushed SaaS partners to shift from a traditional software ecosystem to an AI ecosystem, and built more business data collaboration. We did solid work, not storytelling. Future Human: During your tenure, DingTalk built so many AI assistants, and you once judged that with 80-90 million daily active users, getting 10 million people to use AI daily would not be difficult. Why did neither a star Agent emerge nor the expected user scale materialize? Ye Jun: At least in complex enterprise task scenarios, no To B product with that kind of user scale exists in the domestic market even today. The first reason is that model capabilities at the time were not mature enough compared to today. We built memory, Skills, workflows, and knowledge bases, but model capability is the prerequisite. The models back then could not support Agents for complex enterprise workflow tasks. The second reason is that both enterprise customers and users need time for market education. To B is not To C—you cannot achieve scale overnight by running ads and handing out subsidies. Employees' main task is not to use advanced tools but to get their work done. Employees are already accustomed to their existing workflows, and even the best tools must go through an adoption process. You have to wait for that point in time. The third reason is the limits of product perception. Even today, the AI products seeing widespread use are things like Coding and video creation—these grew directly out of the models. The perception of products like OA and online collaboration is about facilitating person-to-person collaboration. Changing that perception takes a long time. Having 80-90 million users at an office entry point does not mean you can immediately convert that into 10 million AI daily active users. Platform traffic cannot directly become AI usage—the product must find real, high-frequency scenarios that continuously create value, while also waiting for models and customers to mature. Future Human: Why did you ultimately choose enterprise agents as your startup direction? Ye Jun: Over the past year, several companies approached me hoping I would join as a senior business executive. I'm truly grateful, but I declined them all—I wanted to do something of my own. I want to own the complete outcome and let customers and the market test the product. Over the past year, I talked with founders in different directions such as computing centers, data collection, and robotics. I spent nearly 19 years at Alibaba—early on in cross-border e-commerce, then long-term in enterprise services. These experiences made me more familiar with foreign trade and enterprise-level business. After the non-compete period ended, I formally founded the company, starting from one person to build the team, find customers, and find investors. Future Human: Why did 4399 invest in you? They're a gaming company. Ye Jun: Early-stage investment is ultimately about investing in people. They recognized my judgment on the path to AI implementation, as well as the way I conduct myself. At the same time, they also have needs in enterprise-grade AI agents.

Finding the gaps big companies won't touch

Future Person: Why did you choose foreign trade and cross-border e-commerce first? What industry pain points did you discover that urgently need solving? Ye Jun: We chose to go deep into the foreign trade vertical first because we had accumulated resources there, and we found a large number of unsolved problems. For example, at a foreign trade company, dozens of employees sit at their computers every day replying to inquiries from overseas customers. After AI scans the historical communications, it can suggest how to reply to each message, what customers care about most, and whether there is a risk of losing them. Foreign trade customers rely mainly on remote communication, and reply quality directly affects deal closure—this is exactly where AI can create incremental value. AI amplifies human judgment and work output. Future Person: Alibaba.com also has similar AI features. What makes yours different? Ye Jun: Platform products first need to cover broader, more common needs, and the starting point of decision-making is often still platform value. I want to build a small company that helps customers solve a series of needs in vertical scenarios and becomes part of the platform ecosystem. Alibaba.com wants to serve the customers on Alibaba.com, but a foreign trade company may simultaneously use Alibaba.com, Amazon, Facebook, TTS, and other channels. Nowadays, besides the fragmentation of information context caused by multiple channels in foreign trade, there is also a trend of orders getting smaller and long orders becoming short orders, which leads to scattered enterprise information and increased workloads for employees. As a third party, we can take the customer's perspective and connect different platforms, different systems, and the customer's entire business processes. AI is well suited to handling large amounts of scattered information. Future Person: What exactly is Deep Space Order building as a product? What stage are you at now? Ye Jun: It is not a single agent, but an AI work platform for foreign trade. The platform handles model scheduling, Token Planning, memory management, tool calling, and long-horizon task decomposition, and on top of that, multiple agents for specific industry scenarios are layered on, such as marketing, ad buying, and customs declaration. The company was founded about three months ago and now has a dozen or so people, mainly in product R&D, FDE, and new media marketing, with no marketing or sales teams. By my standards, we are still in the product polishing stage, still far from a mature product. Future Person: You need to enter companies to sort out their processes and provide customized solutions—this sounds more like an FDE or a project company. Won't heavy delivery drag down later productization and scaling? Ye Jun: Enterprise-level vertical scenarios definitely require FDEs, and there will definitely be customization. The job of an FDE is to translate the customer's business language into language that agents can understand. Without understanding how a company works, it is impossible to build a product that truly solves business problems. But one company's custom needs may not be custom within an industry. For example, customs declaration is something every foreign trade company has to do; using agents to help sellers find buyers is also a common need; connecting ticketing, order, and customer service systems is not unique to any one company either. We first do it well for one customer, then distill the common parts into Skills and products, which other companies can use directly. I believe customization is a necessary stage before productization in To B startups. If a company hasn't truly gone deep into any business and immediately tries to build a general-purpose platform, that actually has no moat. AI has lowered the cost of customization, and today's customization is not entirely equivalent to the project-based model of the traditional software era. Precisely because it requires entering the front lines of a company's business and understanding the industry, big companies are unwilling to do it, and small teams may not be able to—this is where a market gap forms. Without this process of positioning judgment, most To B startups in the AI era would lose their moat, and big platforms and large models would quickly cover you. Ye Jun: In my plan, the company's first step is to enter through specific industries and customer scenarios, truly understanding business processes through FDE work. The first scenario we chose is foreign trade—going deep into scenarios like inquiries, customs declaration, and marketing, then distilling one company's needs into industry-generic Skills and agent products. The second step is building industry-specific models and doing some post-training. Problems that are stable and suitable for cost reduction through models will go into vertical models; fast-changing, highly granular industry rules stay in the application layer. Further on, we will distill capabilities like model scheduling optimization, memory management, tool calling, and long-horizon task decomposition into a platform, then expand from foreign trade to other industries. Ye Jun: Currently it's prepaid credits plus pay-for-results. Besides fees from model calls, we also charge by specific task, such as generating a customs declaration or producing a video and running ads on Meta and TK. In some scenarios, we can also charge based on final impressions or business results. We don't want to set fixed subscription fees or seat fees—customers pay only when they use it, and don't pay when they don't. I believe the business model of the AI era is not entirely the same as SaaS; ultimately it should be billed by usage and results. A good business model should be tied to a naturally growing base. Future Person: A year from now, what criteria will you use to judge whether this path has worked? If it fails, where are you most likely to get stuck? Ye Jun: With no sales team, relying mainly on organic online customer acquisition, if we can serve 100 paying customers we've never met and generate revenue over 10 million yuan, I consider the first stage a success. Reaching that number means the company has survived. But the real challenge is scaling. Is the product good enough? Do unfamiliar customers choose it on their own? Are we solving typical business problems? Can we replicate from 10 customers to 100 customers? These still need to be validated as we go. From 0 to 1, I honestly have no experience, and I've been very busy lately.

Not competing head-on with big companies, but becoming part of their ecosystem

Future Person: WorkBuddy, Feishu, and Doubao are all pushing hard on office agents, and WorkBuddy has even opened up its ecosystem. Do you see them as competitors? Or will Deep Space Order become an ecosystem partner of WorkBuddy? Ye Jun: We won't compete; my position in the ecosystem is to be their complement. The office agent space suits big companies, and having come out of a big company myself, I'm not capable of building an entry point either. What we solve is industry business processes, not general office work. WorkBuddy, Feishu, DingTalk, and WeCom can all become platforms we connect to. They provide entry points, basic capabilities, and relationship chains, while we enter specific industries, understand customer businesses, and go deep into processes. I don't think we are in a competitive relationship. I think WorkBuddy got it right in building an ecosystem. Tencent did not directly hand the responsibility for AI-era product innovation to WeCom, because WeCom and WorkBuddy are two different species. Its ecosystem strategy also matches the To B model. The moat and compounding returns of To B come from the ecosystem; no single platform can cover all vertical scenarios. WorkBuddy letting external teams bring in industry know-how is also good news for small companies like us—we applied to become part of their ecosystem. By the same logic, I think Doubao and Feishu are also better suited to cooperation. Internal collaboration in large teams is often slow, and market-based cooperation can sometimes be more efficient. Of course, this is my personal judgment; I don't know their internal strategic considerations, and I can't casually pass judgment. Future Person: But for Doubao to build an office agent, it needs the enterprise context Feishu has accumulated, and companies' files and communications are already in Feishu, making it easier to open data and permissions to Feishu. Isn't that exactly the advantage of the two merging organizationally? And for startups, isn't it rather difficult to get a company to trust you, especially when it involves their own data security? Ye Jun: Doubao Office can certainly access Feishu's context more easily. But there are many customers who don't use Feishu, and customers won't switch to Feishu just because of this convenience. The To B market is highly fragmented, and without a To C data flywheel, Doubao Office will surely need to deeply connect with different platforms and industry partners. Office context is only part of enterprise context; what many companies really need to solve are inquiries, production, tickets, orders, and equipment issues—an office entry point alone cannot solve these business processes. A lot of business data lives in other SaaS systems. To B is fundamentally a long-term business built on trust. Future Person: You just said you will start from one vertical industry and may eventually grow into a platform, while big companies build platforms from day one. Will you ultimately still compete, and what are each side's advantages in that competition? Ye Jun: Because AI has leveled technical capabilities, the talent density and resource advantages of big companies have been diluted to some extent. Entering a vertical industry is like entering a jungle—big companies have abundant resources and strong individual capabilities, but they may not be good at fighting a scattered battle that requires staying close to customers for the long term. Many projects showing a market of only a dozen or so million yuan at strategy meetings might be abandoned on day one. But for a startup, that is already a big opportunity. I believe To B will ultimately be fairly fragmented, because it has industry and regional characteristics, giving rise to many industry-specific products and platforms. Big companies can easily replicate general-purpose platforms but are unwilling to replicate vertical industries. Our opportunity lies in our understanding of vertical industries and customers, and in deep service to customers. It's too early to talk about the endgame. The most important thing right now is to first prove that these scenarios can be replicated from 10 customers to 100 customers.
