The difference between an FDE and a TAM: when things break, who fixes the system and who liaises with the customer

Original title: FDE 和 TAM 的区别：故障时谁修系统、谁对接客户

FDE Times · 2026-11-04 · 5 min read

Summary

This article uses a scenario—a retailer's order system failing on the eve of its biggest promotion—to distinguish the FDE and TAM roles. FDEs write production code daily, fix outages, and own delivery through measurable results; TAMs rely on customer relationships and coordination, mobilizing experts and keeping client stakeholders informed. Drawing on AWS Enterprise Support and Palantir's Forward Deployed Enablement Engineer role, the author proposes a phase-by-phase division of labor, a three-line status update format, and pre-agreed responsibilities to prevent overlap. It also advises TAMs seeking to become FDEs to demonstrate shipping code to production.

Key points

  • FDEs build and deploy production code; TAMs create value through relationships and coordination.
  • During outages, a workable split: FDE fixes the system, TAM coordinates experts and updates the client.
  • AWS ties TAMs to Enterprise Support; Palantir has an FDE variant on call 24/7 for critical incidents.
  • Both roles handle escalations and product feedback, so responsibilities must be agreed in advance.
  • TAMs moving to FDE roles usually lack proof of deploying code to production themselves.

Key Takeaways

  • An FDE builds code and deploys it to production every day; a TAM can read code, but creates value through customer relationships and coordination.
  • One workable division of labor during an incident: the FDE troubleshoots while the TAM mobilizes experts and keeps the customer updated in real time. Palantir has a type of FDE role that requires 24/7 on-call availability.
  • The two roles overlap in handling escalations and giving feedback to product teams, so you need to agree in advance on who sends which type of feedback.

It's the eve of the customer's biggest promotion of the year, and the order processing system you deployed for them starts throwing errors. In the incident channel there are two people from the vendor side. One opens a terminal; the other opens their contacts, ready to call the customer's head of operations.

If both open terminals, no one tells the customer what's happening. If both pick up the phone, no one fixes the outage. The line between the Forward Deployed Engineer (FDE) and the Technical Account Manager (TAM) may sound like a matter of job titles, but on nights like this, the difference is most visible.

For an FDE in the role, understanding the TAM position means knowing when to hand the phone to someone else and go back to writing code. For a TAM who wants to become an FDE, it means figuring out which skills they're still missing.

What's the difference between the two roles?

A role comparison article on fde.academy puts it concisely: the FDE builds and delivers, while the TAM coordinates and advises. Specifically, a TAM can read code but rarely writes production code; an FDE writes production code every day.

The same blog post points out that a TAM's strengths come from the depth of customer relationships and cross-functional coordination, not from personally delivering technical products.

The work rhythms also differ. A TAM stays with the same customer long-term and conducts quarterly business reviews. An FDE works in units of deployment projects, each lasting weeks to months.

The executive search firm Christian & Timbers emphasizes that an FDE's responsibility doesn't end when the customer signs the contract—it continues until the system is in production and delivering measurable results.

AWS offers a simple example for understanding the TAM role. AWS lists a dedicated TAM as a benefit of its Enterprise Support plan. The TAM comes with the paid support plan; it is not an engineer working inside the customer's systems.

During incidents, the line is easiest to blur

The two roles share one important commonality: both handle customer escalations and both give feedback to product teams. So once something goes wrong, the two can easily pile onto the same task.

AWS describes the TAM's work around critical business events quite clearly. Before an event, the TAM works with the customer to check whether systems are ready and can handle the expected load. During the event, the TAM provides support to the customer.

According to the Enterprise Support page, the TAM is responsible for coordinating AWS experts, maintaining an overall view of the situation, and leading the post-event review to identify improvements. AWS's Countdown program calls this type of support "TAM-led."

On the FDE side, look at Palantir, where the role originated. The company has a variant role called Forward Deployed Enablement Engineer, which belongs to a centralized customer success team.

The job posting for this role requires the incumbent to act as first responder when incidents occur and to be on call 24/7 for the customer's critical outages. In other words, their job is to keep the customer's systems running.

Example: the same promotion, two task lists

Back to that promotion night. Suppose you're an FDE who deployed an AI agent that automatically handles return requests for a retail chain, and your TAM colleague has managed this customer for years. The table below suggests one way to divide the work.

PhaseWhat the FDE doesWhat the TAM does
Two weeks beforeLoad-test for expected traffic, fix bottlenecks, write the runbookVerify readiness and expected load with the customer, confirm who on the customer side has decision authority
During the incidentFirst response: check logs, roll back or patch, write technical updatesMobilize internal experts when the FDE needs them, brief the customer's management
After the eventWrite the root cause analysis, deploy the long-term fix to productionLead the post-incident review, compile feedback for the product team

The most fragile link is the handoff of information between the two during the incident. One effective practice is for the FDE to send the TAM a three-line status update at fixed intervals, for example:

故障：30% 的退货申请在调用库存 API 时超时。
处理：已回滚今天下午的部署，正在监控错误率。
下次更新：23:30。

The TAM then turns those three lines into what the customer's management needs to know: the impact on shoppers and when the next update will come. The FDE doesn't have to leave the terminal for a conference call, and the TAM doesn't have to promise the customer a timeline without asking the person fixing the outage.

Hands-on: divide the roles before something goes wrong

Start while things are calm. Write down who is responsible for the system and who communicates with each person on the customer side, then send it to both teams. If your company has no TAM, the communicator can be an account executive or your team lead. The key is that this role is assigned to a specific person.

Next, agree on the update cadence and message template before an event, not in the middle of an outage. Schedule a readiness review before each of the customer's business peaks. The FDE brings load-test data; the TAM brings the list of customer-side people who need to be notified.

After the event, sit down and analyze together before sending anything to the product team. Since both roles give feedback to the product, a joint analysis carries more weight than two emails that each tell the same bug story from their own perspective.

Common Mistakes

A trap FDEs fall into is taking on the TAM's work too: the outage isn't fixed yet, but they're already sitting in the customer's management meeting. The TAM's mirror-image mistake is promising the customer a resolution timeline without asking the person reading the logs. Both mistakes stem from not assigning roles in advance.

The third mistake is treating the TAM as a simplified version of the FDE. The two roles rely on different strengths: on one side, customer relationships and coordination; on the other, production code. Someone good at one is not necessarily good at the other.

How to read job descriptions and write your resume

When reading a job posting, watch the wording. Terms like "quarterly business review," "designated," or "relationship" indicate a TAM-leaning role. "Production code," "on-call," or "first responder" indicate an FDE-leaning role, even if it sits within a customer success team like Palantir's.

On your resume, don't just write "worked with customers." Describe an incident you personally handled: what you fixed, how long the system took to recover, and which long-term fix made it into production.

If you're a TAM looking to become an FDE, what's usually missing is proof that you've personally deployed code to production. The next time an escalation comes up, volunteer to take on a small patch instead of handing everything to the engineering team.

A good vendor needs both roles on outage night. As long as everyone knows their responsibilities in advance, the customer sees the system recover and receives updates from the vendor on the agreed schedule.
