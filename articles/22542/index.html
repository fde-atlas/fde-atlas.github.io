How to Evaluate Boutique AI Consulting Firms: 6 Steps

FWD Deployment · 2026-11-11 · 8 min read

Summary

This guide argues that the "boutique" label for AI consulting firms carries no formal standard, so buyers should evaluate firms on verifiable written evidence: named staff, contract terms, and independent audit documents. It lays out six steps covering staffing commitments, pricing structures (using FAR Part 16 definitions of time-and-materials, labor-hour, and fixed-price), code and data ownership, security clauses, SOC 2 report verification, and reference checks. It leans on NIST's AI RMF as a checklist while stressing that the voluntary framework does not assign ownership or set confidentiality terms, so contracts must. Procurement should be run by people with financial, legal, or policy authority.

Key points

  • Judge firms on documents, not the boutique label.
  • Put named staff and substitution clauses in the statement of work.
  • Compare quotes under at least two pricing structures, per-role rates.
  • NIST AI RMF is voluntary; contracts must cover ownership and breach terms.
  • Check SOC 2 scope: criteria, entity, and systems must match your data.

Evaluate a boutique AI consulting firm by checking four things in writing: who will do the work, how you will pay, what the contract says about code and data, and which independent documents back up the firm's claims. Ask for a SOC 2 report, which covers up to five criteria (security, availability, processing integrity, confidentiality and privacy), and read the contract before you read the pitch deck.

In this guide, "boutique" means a smaller specialist firm rather than a large global consultancy. There is no formal standard for the label, so it tells you little on its own. The six steps below work on any firm, whatever it calls itself.

What separates a boutique AI firm from a large consultancy?

The difference you can test is evidence, not size. For any firm you consider, ask what it can put in front of you: named people, written terms and audit documents.

Start your shortlist with our page on top AI consulting firms, with their investments, partners and teams. Then run every firm on that list through the same checks below, so the comparison is like for like.

Step 1: How do you find out who actually does the work?

Do not accept a team description from the sales call. Put the staffing in the statement of work.

  • Ask for names and roles. Request the people who will be assigned, their titles and what each will own.
  • Ask to meet them. Interview the engineers who will be in your systems, not only the partner who sold the deal.
  • Write in a substitution clause. Require your written approval before a named person is replaced.
  • Ask how senior people are involved after kickoff. Get the answer as a written commitment, such as review cadence or hours per week, not as a general assurance.

What share of work goes to senior versus junior staff depends on the firm and the deal. Only the contract can fix it for your engagement.

Also decide which engagement shape you need. Dplyz, for example, places senior engineers inside a business and offers three ways to work: an embedded team, project delivery or technical leadership. Our embedded team vs project delivery comparison can help you pick before you request quotes.

Step 2: How do the pricing models compare?

Use the Federal Acquisition Regulation (FAR) as a shared vocabulary for the structures. It is a federal procurement source, and your private agreement sets its own terms. Under FAR Part 16, time-and-materials and labor-hour contracts are not fixed-price contracts, and a fixed-price structure is a separate category.

Structure What you pay for What to pin down
Time-and-materials Direct labor hours at specified fixed hourly rates and actual material costs. The rates include wages, overhead, general and administrative expenses, and profit. The rate for each named role, a cap or approval threshold, and how materials are billed.
Labor-hour A variation of time-and-materials in which the contractor does not supply materials. The same rate and cap terms. Confirm who pays for any tools or services.
Fixed-price A set price for defined work, a structure distinct from the two above. Written scope, acceptance criteria and how change requests are priced.

Ask each firm to quote the same scope under at least two structures, then compare. Because a time-and-materials rate already bundles overhead and profit, ask for a rate per role rather than a single blended figure.

Some firms do not publish prices. Dplyz, for instance, doesn't publish a price list, so you request a quote. For a worked comparison, see FDE pricing models: time-and-materials, fixed and outcome-based. As of November 2026, check each firm's current terms rather than relying on any rate you read online.

Step 3: What should the contract say about code ownership?

Do not assume a default. NIST describes the AI RMF Playbook as suggested actions, not a mandatory certification or contract standard. Nothing in it assigns who owns what, so you must write it down.

List each deliverable and name the owner of each one:

  • Custom code and configuration
  • Prompts and agent workflows
  • Models and trained artifacts
  • Evaluation sets and test results
  • Documentation and runbooks

NIST recommends documenting risks of intellectual-property or other-rights infringement involving third-party AI components. Ask the firm to list every third-party component it plans to use, and get its position on infringement in writing. NIST's AI RMF Core also says organizations should have policies addressing risks associated with third-party entities, including intellectual-property infringement risks.

Step 4: What data security terms should you require?

Write data terms into the agreement itself. NIST's AI RMF is voluntary and does not set confidentiality, breach-notification, data-retention or subcontracting terms. A lawyer should read those clauses, since this article is general information and not legal advice.

Use these NIST recommendations as your checklist:

  • Apply procurement, security and data-privacy controls to all acquired third-party technologies.
  • Map risks for all components of the system, including third-party software and data.
  • Review third-party data and models for bias, privacy and security-vulnerability risks.
  • Review the firm's release schedules and change-management plans, including patching and compatibility commitments.
  • Agree contingency processes for failures or incidents involving high-risk third-party data or AI systems.

Also decide what access the engineers get on day one. Our guide on securely granting forward deployed engineers access covers that part.

Step 5: What does a SOC 2 report tell you?

A SOC 2 examination evaluates controls at a service organization relevant to security, availability, processing integrity, confidentiality or privacy. Those are the five AICPA Trust Services Criteria.

When a firm hands you a report, check these items:

  • Which criteria are covered. A report that covers only security does not speak to confidentiality or privacy.
  • Whether the report covers the firm that will do your work. Look at the entity and systems named in the description.
  • Whether it reflects the environment your data will touch. Match the systems described to where your data will actually sit.
  • Whether anything in the report conflicts with what sales told you.

Privacy deserves its own look. The AICPA notes that service organizations may possess personal information belonging to user entities, and that customers want confidence it is protected from disclosure after a security event. If the engagement touches personal data, require the privacy criterion or equivalent evidence.

A firm with no SOC 2 report is not automatically out, but you then need other written evidence of its controls, and the gap should shape your contract terms.

Step 6: How do you check references and past deployments?

Ask for documents you can read yourself, not only a client who will vouch for the firm. NIST recommends reviewing third-party audit reports, testing results, product roadmaps, warranties, terms of service, end-user license agreements, contracts and related documentation when assessing third-party AI providers.

  • Request the document set. Ask for the audit report, sample testing results, standard terms and a draft contract before you pick a finalist.
  • Ask for references that match your project. Match the kind of work, such as agents, integrations or data work, and the kind of system.
  • Ask each reference the same questions. Cover who was on the team, whether the named staff stayed, how change requests were handled and what happened when something broke.
  • Ask for evidence of the deployment. Look for test results, vendor documentation or an agreed description of what is running in production.
  • Compare claims with documents. Any claim in the pitch that you cannot tie to a document stays unverified until the firm supplies one.

Who should run the evaluation?

NIST states that procurement tasks for third-party AI models, products or services should be conducted by personnel with financial, legal or policy authority. Engineering leaders should judge technical fit, but contract terms should go through people who can bind the company.

If you want a structure for the whole review, NIST's AI RMF Playbook is organized around four functions: Govern, Map, Measure and Manage. Use them as headings for your internal review notes.

Common questions

What is a boutique consulting firm in the AI space?

In this guide, it means a smaller specialist firm rather than a large global consultancy. The label has no formal standard, so judge a firm on its named staff, written terms and audit documents.

How do small AI consulting firms structure their pricing?

Ask each firm which structure it is quoting. Time-and-materials pays direct labor hours at specified fixed hourly rates plus actual material costs, labor-hour is a variation without materials, and fixed-price is a separate structure. Some firms, such as Dplyz, don't publish a price list, so you request a quote.

Who owns the code when working with boutique AI firms?

It depends on the contract. NIST's AI RMF Playbook is not a contract standard and does not assign ownership, so list code, prompts, models, evaluations and documentation in the agreement and name an owner for each.

What security standards should boutique AI consulting require?

Ask for a SOC 2 report, which evaluates controls against the AICPA criteria of security, availability, processing integrity, confidentiality and privacy. Add NIST-recommended steps: apply procurement, security and data-privacy controls to third-party technologies, and agree incident contingency processes.

How are boutique consulting companies using AI internally?

It varies by firm, and we have no verified information on it, so ask each firm directly. Treat any AI tool that touches your data as third-party software and data, which NIST identifies as a supply-chain risk area that needs policies and procedures.
