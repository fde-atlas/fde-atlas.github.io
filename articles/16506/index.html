Forward Deployed Engineer Interview Questions (With Answers)

Forward Deploying · 2026-11-03 · 3 min read

Summary

This article presents a set of interview questions for Forward Deployed Engineers, organized around six scorecard dimensions: production engineering, LLM systems, evals and reliability, data and integration, customer judgment, and communication and ownership. The author argues that generic coding puzzles fail to test whether a candidate can ship production software inside a customer's environment, so every question should push for a real system, a real customer, and a real number. Each question includes markers of strong versus weak answers, such as knowing per-request cost or having written evals with known blind spots. The piece closes by recommending a 60–90 minute deployment case with ambiguous requirements and messy data, to observe how candidates scope, build, and evaluate rather than just how they talk.

Key points

  • Interviews should test building production software in customer environments, not generic puzzles.
  • Questions span six dimensions, each with strong and weak answer signals.
  • LLM questions probe architecture choices, per-request cost, and model upgrade handling.
  • Eval questions require candidates to show real evaluation sets and their blind spots.
  • A 60–90 minute deployment case reveals actual working style beyond verbal answers.

Published · 3 min read · By Vishal Wadhwani

A good Forward Deployed Engineer interview tests two things at once: can this person build production software, and can they do it inside a customer's world, with someone else's data, constraints and deadlines? Generic coding puzzles test neither. These are the questions we use, grouped by the six dimensions of our scorecard. Ask for specifics every time: a real system, a real customer, a real number.

1. Production engineering

  • "Walk me through a system you built that other people still run. What would you change?" Strong: names the users, the failure modes and the trade-offs, and is candid about what they got wrong. Weak: describes a demo or a hackathon.
  • "Tell me about the last time something you shipped broke in production." Strong: how they found out, how long it took to fix and what they changed so it would not happen again. Weak: "nothing I built has broken".
  • "How do you deploy into an environment you do not control?" Strong: talks about packaging, configuration, access, rollback and who owns what. Weak: assumes their own cloud account.

2. LLM systems

  • "Why did your last system use retrieval, an agent or plain prompting, and not the alternatives?" Strong: a reason tied to the data, cost or reliability. Weak: "agents are the future".
  • "What did one request cost, and how fast was it?" Strong: knows the order of magnitude and what they did to bring it down. Weak: never measured it.
  • "How do you handle a model upgrade that changes behaviour?" Strong: pins versions, reruns evals, rolls out gradually. Weak: upgrades and watches.

3. Evals and reliability

  • "How did you know your system worked before the customer did?" Strong: a fixed evaluation set, a metric and a threshold agreed with the customer. Weak: "we tested it manually".
  • "Show me an eval you wrote. What did it miss?" Strong: knows its blind spots. Weak: has never written one.
  • "What guardrails did you put in place, and why those?" Strong: guardrails linked to a real risk for that customer. Weak: a generic list.

4. Data and integration

  • "Describe the messiest customer data you have worked with." Strong: specifics about format, permissions and quality, and what they did about it. Weak: only clean public datasets.
  • "How did you get through a customer's security review?" Strong: knows what security teams ask about (identity, data residency, logging) and prepared for it. Weak: someone else handled it.
  • "How do you respect a customer's document permissions in a retrieval system?" Strong: permissions enforced at retrieval time, tested. Weak: hasn't thought about it.

5. Customer judgment

  • "Tell me about a time you told a customer no." Strong: said no for a good reason and kept the relationship. Weak: never has, or did it badly.
  • "A customer asks for a feature in a meeting. What do you do in the next 24 hours?" Strong: clarifies the problem, sizes it, checks with product and replies with a plan. Weak: starts building straight away.
  • "How do you decide what to build first in a new deployment?" Strong: the smallest thing that proves value on real data. Weak: the most interesting technical piece.

6. Communication and ownership

  • "Explain your last system to me as if I were the customer's CFO. Now as if I were a staff engineer." Strong: two genuinely different explanations. Weak: the same answer twice.
  • "When did you last give a customer bad news?" Strong: early, with a plan. Weak: hoped it would resolve itself.
  • "Who from a previous deployment would vouch for you, and what would they say?" Strong: names people quickly. Then call them.

Run it as a case, not only questions

Questions show how someone talks about their work. A short deployment case shows how they do it: an ambiguous brief from your domain, a sample of messy data and 60–90 minutes. Watch how they scope it, what they build first and which eval they write. Our hiring guide covers the full loop.
