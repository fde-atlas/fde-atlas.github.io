How teams are configured, hired, and evaluated

Original title: 团队怎样配置、招聘与考核

pafa.github.io · 2019-04-08 · 10 min read

Summary

This chapter covers how to staff, hire, and evaluate FDE teams. It argues that reporting-line choices (Palantir placing field engineers under business development, Baseten keeping them in engineering) have no inherent winner; what matters is converting responsibilities into named people with real hours. A teaching example of a repair-recommendation pilot shows how to refuse requests that exceed capacity. Hiring should target specific team gaps, onboarding requires mentor time in the schedule, and evaluation should rely on evidence of customer outcomes and knowledge transfer rather than single metrics like signings or launch counts. The chapter also borrows Google SRE's practice of reserving time for improvement work.

Key points

  • Reporting lines matter less than ensuring field commitments get product support.
  • Team commitments should be named, with hours budgeted including waiting and coordination.
  • Diagnose team gaps before hiring; avoid one all-rounder job description.
  • New hires add capacity only after tracked, reviewed, and handed-off work.
  • Evaluate on customer outcomes and knowledge transfer, separating contribution from luck.

When a company decides to adopt the FDE model, the easiest first step is to rewrite the job posting, putting programming, communication, industry knowledge, project management, and business awareness on the same page. The skill bar can be high, but the organization also needs to state: who pairs with whom to complete this work, how much real time is available, and what gets dropped when conflicts arise.

Buyer and Seller: Who Decides Goals, Authority, and Outcomes already drew the boundaries of decision-making. Now put responsibility onto people and schedules, so that customer problems have someone continuously accountable, while the team does not depend on any single person being always online.

After the job title, ask one more question

In Palantir's 2019 description, core R&D sits in the product development department while field engineers, Delta, sit in business development; the head of FDEs at Baseten explained that they kept engineers within the engineering department while acknowledging that coordination with the commercial side would increase. 12

Neither arrangement has a natural winner. The side closer to the customer's business must ensure field commitments get product resources; the side closer to core engineering must ensure product priorities do not leave customers waiting forever. This is a management problem derived from organizational differences, and reporting lines alone cannot prove who delivers better. Palantir's description of Deployment Strategists also shows that business understanding and technical work overlap, and real responsibilities do not fully follow job boundaries. 3

For identifying role boundaries, see Responsibility Boundaries. When forming a team, convert responsibilities further into committed people and time: who responds to field issues, who receives product changes, and who decides scheduling conflicts.

OpenAI's FDE job materials place requirements discovery, system design, building, and production launch within the same scope of responsibility. Such a role design puts engineering outcomes in the context of customer adoption, but a hiring description is only the responsibilities the organization hopes to establish, not a result every employee has already achieved. 4

Use one change of work to form the team

The following uses a teaching example. This is not a reconstruction of any real customer's staffing. An equipment service company wants to turn repair reports into maintenance recommendations, with the maintenance supervisor deciding how to dispatch work orders. Currently the materials are scattered across email, equipment records, and tickets. The team plans to pilot with one type of equipment in one service area first.

The business, frontline, engineering, data, and operations responsibilities from the previous chapter do not have to map to five new positions. When staffing, first check who already has the capability to take them on, then check whether combining roles would eliminate necessary oversight or time.

Returning to the maintenance pilot, you can start with three commitments assigned to named individuals: the maintenance supervisor serving as both business owner and frontline representative, the FDE responsible for development and troubleshooting, and the customer's existing systems engineer covering data interfaces and operations handover. This describes three arrangements with names attached, not a recommendation that every company hire three people.

In this teaching arrangement, the supervisor sets aside two real ticket reviews per week; the FDE does not take on another launch during the first trial period; the systems engineer attends every design session that could change the interface and personally rehearses recovery before the first go-live. The customer's existing permission approver and vendor product owner need not be on site full time, but help windows must be agreed in advance. If the customer has no engineer who can take over, the third position cannot just name a department.

Such role combinations have boundaries. The maintenance supervisor can raise requirements and judge usefulness, but cannot solely approve expanded data access that they themselves requested; the developer can modify the system, but cannot substitute their own demo for frontline acceptance. A pilot within one small area's working hours also does not mean three people can provide round-the-clock service. Putting commitments into hours per week makes this boundary clearer.

Suppose a second region now demands a launch this week as well. The assessment requires at least 16 more FDE hours, 6 systems engineer hours, and 2 local supervisor hours. These are not idle slots: the incident reserve cannot double as new development time, and the local supervisor has not confirmed. The project lead therefore rejects a dual-region launch this week and keeps the original region; if the business urgently needs insight into the second region, with the supervisor's approval, 4 hours can be pulled from the 18 development hours for requirements observation only, with 4 hours of the original plan explicitly deferred. It still receives no launch commitment.

The alternative is to add someone qualified to take over, but hiring or secondment does not mean effective capacity on day one; time for learning the system and handover must also be reserved. Resource decisions should state both what is added and what is delayed. Weekly Resources and the Second-Region Request provides a filled-in schedule and alternatives; the hours in the table only illustrate the conflict and do not prescribe industry staffing ratios.

Who to hire depends on which segment the team is missing

Baseten's lead explained that they require candidates to pass the company's engineering bar while also looking at product judgment and willingness to work with customers. 2 For how their hiring focus shifts, see Capability Map: From Doing Tasks to Owning Outcomes. This is that company's hiring experience; it does not mean all industry knowledge can be quickly filled in after onboarding. When equipment, safety, or professional judgment is involved, willingness to learn cannot substitute for the qualifications and experience currently required for the work.

The organization can first take one piece of real work to check for gaps. If no one can trace a cross-system fault from repair information, the gap is engineering capability; if the system can be built but no one understands why the supervisor rejects a recommendation, the gap is business understanding and usage research; if permission and product requests keep waiting, the gap may be a decision interface. Only when the first two categories are not covered by the existing team does hiring or training become more directly relevant; the third usually cannot be solved by adding one more executor.

Interviews should likewise leave evidence for each. Ask candidates to explain how a piece of material enters the system and how errors are discovered; present a scenario where a user request conflicts with a known constraint and see what they ask and how they narrow the commitment; then observe whether they can hand a problem to product or operations colleagues rather than keeping all responsibility to themselves. These are observation methods for organizations choosing people; how individuals practice and showcase their work is covered in Learning and Portfolio: Proving Capability with Checkable Results.

You cannot require every candidate to have the full team's entire skill set. Someone with solid engineering fundamentals who is willing to verify things on site can be paired with an industry expert; someone who knows the business well also needs an engineering partner who can modify and maintain the system. Hiring everyone with the same "all-rounder" description tends to produce identical strengths and identical gaps.

Hiring, internal development, and external collaboration can proceed in parallel. The UK Government's AI Playbook also considers new hires, third parties, and upskilling existing staff together, noting that the capabilities needed change across lifecycle stages. 5 For the maintenance team, external experts can help with complex interfaces in the short term, but the customer must still have someone learn to judge and maintain it, or explicitly continue purchasing that service.

When a new person joins, when does capacity actually increase

Adding a name does not immediately add a deliverable commitment. Getting familiar with customer data, understanding past decisions, obtaining necessary permissions, and learning failure recovery all require existing members' time. When onboarding someone, the original team's mentoring and review time must also go into the schedule.

You can have the newcomer first track the inputs, decisions, and outcomes of an existing piece of work, then complete one small change under someone's review, and finally handle one controlled incident and hand it off to a colleague. The lead expands their independent scope based on these performances, rather than automatically granting all permissions after a set number of days. This is the development sequence this book recommends; there is no fixed training period, and newcomers are not required to independently take on high-consequence actions first.

If the team needs to cover an urgent service immediately, people still in the hiring process cannot be counted as available; for those on secondment, verify their available time and the conditions for taking over. When staff are genuinely short, you can defer new scope, lean on partners who already have the capability, or renegotiate service commitments. Writing the gap down clearly is more reliable than having a new hire, on day one, shoulder the organization's pre-existing debts.

Don't use full utilization to mask the organization's overdraft

A team whose schedule is packed every day may look like full resource utilization, but it may also mean there is no capacity to absorb change. When a customer has a serious outage, a new project needs urgent investigation, or a member takes leave, the original plan slips item by item. If recovery depends only on overtime, the so-called high utilization is actually borrowing elasticity from people's personal lives.

SRE stands for Site Reliability Engineering, which focuses on how software services run reliably. A chapter of Google's book proposes a management target: averaged over several quarters or a year, each SRE should spend at least half their time on engineering work that reduces future repetitive toil or improves the service. This ratio is not an industry standard for FDEs; what is borrowed here is the idea of reserving time for improvement, not a mechanical daily fifty-fifty split. 6

An FDE team should also distinguish three kinds of busyness: getting to know a problem previously unknown; making an improvement with long-term value; and repeatedly performing manual work to maintain existing systems. The first may be research investment, the second may accumulate capability, and if the third keeps growing, it will crowd out the first two.

A timesheet alone cannot tell these three apart. An operation performed a hundred times may be deliberately collecting data under different conditions, or may simply reflect a missing automation step. The person in charge should ask what each piece of work left behind: new knowledge, reusable tools, clear rules, or only a completion record. Operations that require long-term repetition should have a budget, service commitments, and an owner; they must not remain permanently assigned to the FDE just because the FDE did them first.

Scheduling must also account for waiting and coordination. If data access has not yet been granted, the engineer is not thereby free; if the customer's business representative never has time to give feedback, the design will not mature on its own. Writing these constraints into the plan is more useful than questioning individual efficiency after the project slips.

Whom you evaluate determines how everyone works

If field staff are evaluated only on contract value, they will tend to agree to more; if evaluated only on launches, they will tend to leave problems until after launch; if evaluated only on code reuse rate, they may prematurely build complex frameworks to showcase generalization. Every metric says something, and every one can mislead when used alone.

Better evaluation revolves around several kinds of evidence. Has the customer's target work actually changed? Are serious problems detected and handled in time? Does the team leave key knowledge to successors? Which field experiences made it into maintained products? How do project resources differ from the original commitments, and why?

In the maintenance example, where auto-dispatch was deferred because the withdrawal conditions were not met (permission checks covered in the previous chapter), the person in charge should not merely record it as "one fewer feature launched." He should also check whether the manual confirmation approach helped supervisors find grounds faster, and whether the team caught the unacceptable mis-dispatch risk early. If the benefits of finding information do not offset the review burden either, the business owner can stop the pilot and leave the reason for non-adoption to the next procurement. Such a conclusion may reduce the launch count by one, but it avoids having frontline staff maintain a worthless new step indefinitely.

Individual evaluation should also separate contribution from luck. One person's customer happens to have mature data, a strong owner, and ample budget, so the project moves fast; another faces legacy systems and frequent changes, so results come slower. When comparing, look at what constraints they identified and what choices they made; do not credit the customer's conditions entirely to personal ability.

When these observations enter personnel evaluation, it must also be clear who made the decisions, who provided the evidence, and which outcomes were affected by customer conditions. A team should not reward commitments it cannot sustainably honor.

Before changing people, make time for shared memory

The records and rehearsals in a week's schedule are not spare-time work. They give the customer's system engineers a chance to actually take over the actions, and let the product contacts judge field requests. Without this time, the team is merely swapping its only point of contact from one capable person to another. After Delivery: Real Use and Departure provides a handover rehearsal; the management responsibility here is to allocate real capacity for hands-on practice and coverage arrangements, not just to demand "good documentation."

The UK AI procurement guidance places training, ongoing support, and risk allocation among procurement considerations. It reminds us that these arrangements are not just good habits within the team; they should also enter the working agreements between customer and supplier. 7

When you look at that job posting again, you can keep those high capability requirements. But beyond the position, you also need an organization that can deliver on them: someone on the customer side who decides, engineers who can act, a product team that can respond, and operators who can take over. The value of FDEs comes from being close to the problems; the value of team management is making that closeness produce long-term capability, not just long-term dependence on a few capable people.
