How to judge whether an FDE offer is worth accepting? Look at four things

Original title: 怎么判断一个 FDE offer 值不值得接？看四件事

FDE中国 · 2026-09-07 · 3 min read

Summary

This article argues that evaluating an FDE offer should go beyond comparing total compensation, since identical salaries can differ greatly in real value depending on the environment. It proposes four verifiable dimensions to probe in interviews: client and industry quality (renewal rates reveal delivery quality), team mechanisms (mentoring, retrospectives, how often senior people code), scope and accountability boundaries (who locks scope, who answers to the client when things break), and growth paths (where former team members went). It lists three red flags, including vague job descriptions with no delivery boundaries, and advises asking these questions late in the process. Its conclusion: an offer with two dimensions passing and no red flags deserves serious consideration.

Key points

  • Salary is an outcome, not the premise; anchoring on it is the most common mistake.
  • Ask for client names, industries, tenure, and renewal rates.
  • Probe onboarding, mentoring, retrospectives, and senior coding ratio.
  • Clarify who sets scope and who is accountable to clients.
  • Three red flags warrant caution; no offer is perfect—know what you give up.

Full text

This piece is the final link in job-hunting decision-making, upgrading the question of "is it worth it" from salary comparison to a four-dimension evaluation. The most valuable takeaway is the verifiable ways to ask about each of the four dimensions: ask about the client list and renewal rates, ask about mentoring and retrospective mechanisms, ask who defines scope and who takes responsibility when things go wrong, and ask where the previous person in the role ended up—each question can get you a real answer in the interview. The most common mistake is being anchored to the total package number while ignoring the hidden discount that comes with low-quality clients and an environment without mentoring. Compared with on-site interview guides, this piece is aimed at decision-making rather than exam prep, and together with the pieces on career transition and salary it forms a complete loop. Finally, a tip on timing: the four-dimension information can only be genuinely obtained in the later stages of the interview process; the early rounds should focus on showcasing yourself and assessing the company, and you should concentrate your questions on the final interview or offer negotiation stage—this is both appropriate and more likely to get you honest answers. One more reminder on mindset: the purpose of evaluating an offer is to reduce the probability of regret, not to find a zero-risk option; if two of the four dimensions pass and there are zero red flags, it is an offer worth seriously considering. After reading, do one thing: copy the four-dimension checklist into your interview notes and use it to ask questions in your next interview.— FDEChina Editorial Team · Practical Edition

How do you judge whether an FDE offer is worth accepting?

Direct answer: look at four things—client and industry quality, team mechanisms, scope and accountability boundaries, and growth paths. Salary is the result of these four things, not the premise; making a decision based solely on the total package number is the most common mistake among job seekers for this role. The actual returns of an FDE role (skill growth, reusable assets, long-term negotiating leverage) depend heavily on the environment you join—at the same salary, the real value of two offers can differ by a factor of two.

First, client and industry quality. Ask directly: which clients are currently being served, in which industries, how long the partnerships have lasted, and whether there have been renewals. Client complexity determines your growth slope, industry conditions determine role stability, and renewal status reveals delivery quality. A team with only one or two shaky clients is a high-risk asset no matter how high the salary. Before the interview, you can also research public information and case studies yourself; see the industry context in Domestic Private Deployment Cases.

Second, team mechanisms. Ask three specific questions: how are new hires' first three months arranged, and is there a mentor and a shadowing period; are project retrospectives a routine practice, and what did the last retrospective change; what proportion of the time does the most senior person on the team personally write code. A team with mentoring, retrospectives, and a talent pipeline treats you as an asset; a team without these mechanisms will most likely consume you as a gap-filling resource billed by the person-day—the difference between the two is exactly the difference between outsourced on-site staffing and FDE. Third, scope and accountability boundaries. Ask: who participates in locking down project scope, how are requirement changes handled, how are client acceptance criteria defined, and who is accountable to the client when problems arise. Getting clear answers to these questions lets you anticipate your future workload and degree of influence; see the reverse application of the Expectation Management Handbook.

Fourth, growth paths. Ask: how did people two levels above you on the team get to where they are, and where did the last person who left go. This question reveals both the team's promotion pathway and its reputation in the talent market. At the same time, compare it with your own two-year plan: is the asset you want industry depth, reusable components, or client relationships, and which of these does this team provide. For how to compare salaries scientifically, see the conversion approach in Why FDE Salaries Are High.

Three red flags—any one of them means proceed with caution: first, the interview avoids questions about who the clients are and what the projects are, talking only about the platform and tech stack; second, the FDE role is described as "do everything, go wherever needed" with no delivery boundaries; third, the interviewer cannot give specific answers to questions about overtime and on-call expectations. One final honest word: there is no perfect offer—you usually have to give up one of the four dimensions, but you should know which one you are giving up and be able to accept its cost.
