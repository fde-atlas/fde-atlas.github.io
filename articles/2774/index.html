FDE (Forward Deployed Engineer) Becomes AI's Hottest New Role: Monthly Pay of 100K but Full of Challenges

Original title: FDE（前沿部署工程师）成AI最火新岗位：月入十万但挑战重重

虎嗅 · 2026-08-05 · 10 min read

Summary

This report follows three independent forward deployed engineers (FDEs) in China, profiling a role that has become AI's hottest new job. OpenAI, Anthropic, Google Cloud, and Chinese giants like ByteDance, Alibaba, and Tencent are all hiring; Silicon Valley FDEs start around $170,000, top out near $500,000, and independent contractors in China can charge up to 100,000 yuan per project. But the reality is harder than the hype: clients can't articulate their pain points, system-switching risks stall projects, cost savings are limited when replaced workers earn little, and projects rarely lead to repeat business. All three conclude that independent FDE work is transitional, and that training in-house FDEs inside companies is the more sustainable business.

Key points

  • FDEs work on-site to deploy AI; OpenAI, Anthropic, and Chinese tech giants are competing for them.
  • Silicon Valley FDE salaries start at $170,000; Chinese independents charge up to 100,000 yuan per project.
  • Key difficulties: vague client needs, risky system migrations, and limited cost savings.
  • Projects are often one-off deals; entering a new industry means starting over.
  • All three interviewees see independent FDE work as transitional, favoring corporate in-house roles.

While AI replaces people, it is also creating new jobs. The FDE (Forward Deployed Engineer) has become the hottest new AI role of the year.

The job's mission is to be stationed at customer sites, solving the problems of putting AI into practice. Companies that make money by helping enterprises deploy large models have already made it a standard part of their business.

From overseas large model companies like OpenAI and Anthropic to domestic giants like ByteDance, Alibaba, and Tencent, everyone is competing for talent. On social media, stories like "earning 100,000 yuan a month on solo gigs" and "switching careers with zero experience to a million-yuan salary" are everywhere.

We found three solo contractors. Their feedback, however, was: this job is not easy. Customers can't articulate their pain points, the risk of switching systems is too high, and the room for cost reduction is limited. Projects often end up being one-off deals, and switching to another industry means starting from scratch.

What makes them even more anxious is that the solo FDE is only a transitional form—companies are incubating such roles internally.

Below are their stories.

A monthly salary of 100,000 yuan

Before taking his first FDE gig, Lawted had never seen such a "backward" company.

In April 2026, Lawted was invited to a freight forwarding company in Shenzhen to AI-transform its workflows. After walking in, he stood behind the nearest employee—whose screen was covered with WeChat chat windows and email pages in both Chinese and English.

After watching for a while, he noticed that this employee kept opening shipping PDFs sent by customers via WeChat and email, extracting the port and sailing schedule, replying to the customer for confirmation, and then manually typing all this information into the company system, entry by entry.

Lawted walked around the office. More than thirty employees were all doing the same thing.

"So outdated," Lawted grumbled to himself. The scene made him realize that using AI to transform traditional workflows had huge potential. That same day, he packed his bags and moved to Shenzhen.

At the time, he didn't know there was a specific name for what he was doing. Later, while chatting with an American friend, the friend blurted out upon hearing about it: "This is FDE." He searched it up and discovered that what he had stumbled into was precisely the hottest role in Silicon Valley right now.

Photo | Lawted in the US

FDE is not a new concept. Twenty years ago, the data platform Palantir built its business on it. After taking on military contracts, it would send engineers directly to the frontlines in Iraq and Afghanistan. When a soldier said "this road looks suspicious," the engineer would turn it into an annotation tool on the map on the spot, distilling useful features back into the platform.

In the traditional software industry, the closest equivalents are Sales Engineers and implementation consultants. The difference is that Sales Engineers only present solutions, implementation consultants install software following manuals, while FDEs write code on-site at the customer's location, personally delivering and launching the system.

In the AI era, this role has come back into vogue. The reason is easy to understand: large models are standardized products, but enterprises' data, workflows, and systems vary enormously. Over the past year, many companies bought models and AI agents, but most stalled at the demo stage, hitting walls of permissions, data, and compliance the moment they entered production. The "last mile" from demo to something truly usable requires someone on-site to smooth it out.

OpenAI, Anthropic, and Google Cloud are all competing for talent. In May this year, OpenAI teamed up with 19 PE firms to pour over $4 billion into a dedicated deployment company; the same day, Anthropic announced a $1.5 billion enterprise services joint venture with Blackstone, Goldman Sachs, and others. Domestically, ByteDance, Alibaba, Tencent, and Zhipu are following suit. On compensation, Silicon Valley FDEs generally start at $170,000 a year, with senior total packages reaching $500,000; domestic giants offer up to a million-yuan annual salary.

The demand for this role falls roughly into two camps. One is AI companies serving governments, enterprises, and major clients, which set up dedicated positions—FDEs are the supporting delivery for selling models. The other is individuals or small teams like Lawted, who mostly come from big tech engineering backgrounds, understand both engineering and business, and can work on-site, serving small and medium-sized enterprises and traditional companies that can't afford or make good use of AI—charging up to 100,000 yuan per gig.

Lawted's deal came about quite dramatically.

He is 26 years old and took the standard tech career path. After graduating with a computer science degree from Beijing University of Posts and Telecommunications, he joined Alibaba. In his second year of work, Cursor blew up, and he recommended it to everyone he met, but his colleagues advised him not to use AI to write code—"your brain will degenerate."

At that moment, he felt "this place doesn't belong to the future." After securing a research assistant position at Stanford, he resolutely quit and moved to the US, and started a company focused on an AI browser.

One day in October 2025, he had an appointment with an investor at 2 p.m. to discuss the project. He had just opened his laptop to confirm the meeting location when a news alert popped up in the bottom right corner of his screen: OpenAI had released the AI browser Atlas. That was exactly what he was building. With a big AI company entering to grab the business, his project was dead.

Later, through an introduction, Lawted met the CEO of a listed company. When he mentioned wanting to let Stanford students try his AI browser first, the CEO laughed: "Do you know that many people in China pronounce DeepSeek as 'Deepsuck'? Go back and make money off them."

In April 2026, OpenClaw "Lobster" went viral in China. That CEO approached Lawted: "There's a deal to be made." At a dinner over drinks, he met the freight forwarding boss who made tens of millions a year.

"I found a Harvard master's graduate to install Lobster for you," the CEO introduced him.

A few drinks in, Lawted asked the boss: "Have you ever used Doubao?"

The boss shook his head, bewildered. Lawted took his phone, downloaded it on the spot, and typed: "What is a freight forwarder?" Doubao quickly gave a series of specific answers. The boss leaned in, read them, took the phone back, and asked several more industry questions—all answered correctly.

"Let's work together," the freight forwarding boss decided on the spot. And then came the scene at the beginning.

Jolie Ni knew the term FDE early on. Now 27, she worked as a programmer for three years at a major tech company in Silicon Valley after graduating from college. She had always had the entrepreneurial itch, but the stable, high-paying job kept her from making up her mind. Until this year, when she watched one AI startup after another rise, and friends around her who started companies turned three-person shops into unicorns. She finally decided to step out.

At first, she just helped a friend's moving company build an AI customer service agent for free. Unexpectedly, the friend was very pleased with the results. As soon as the first-phase project ended, she went full-time as an FDE.

In June this year, she became an Anthropic Claude Partner.

This is a partner program Anthropic just launched in March this year. The company makes money selling AI services to enterprises and needs a group of knowledgeable partners to help customers deploy Claude into their businesses, for which it has invested $100 million in training and certification.

The certification path isn't complicated. Apply to join the Claude Partner Network under a company name, self-study and prepare at the official academy, and finally pass a 120-minute, 60-question online proctored exam to earn the CCAF certificate. Jolie Ni reviewed for only three or four hours and passed the exam smoothly.

For her, this is a weightier professional endorsement. Since becoming an FDE, she and her team have signed three major deals in a row.

Her team has only three people, keeping labor costs very low. After becoming an FDE, Jolie Ni's average monthly income exceeded 100,000 yuan.

Xiao Tang, who also works as an FDE but in China, faces a different pricing landscape.

Xiao Tang is 24, a liberal arts graduate with an economics background, working in data analysis at a state-owned enterprise. His feelings about AI are complicated—both excitement and anxiety. The first time he used Manus, watching the command lines flickering on the screen, he felt he could be optimized away at any moment. At the end of January this year, he started learning vibe coding and posted his learning process on Xiaohongshu.

Three months later, a company that runs a booking platform reached out via private message, wanting to use AI to replace part of their workflows, offering a price well below the industry average.

Xiao Tang knew peers charged 100,000 yuan easily. But to get a real project for practice, he took it.

The hard part is communication

After the contract was signed, the client sent Xiao Tang the business documentation. It detailed the business processes he was supposed to replace with AI: pre-sales and after-sales customer service, account managers pushing projects forward, and IT staff working on SaaS.

After that, every day after work, Xiao Tang would spend over an hour discussing with the AI, over and over, how exactly to implement these tasks.

Two months later, the first phase was complete. Customer service and account management had been fully handed over to AI digital humans, with real people involved in only 5% of the entire process.

The boss was very satisfied. Previously, communicating with clinics and pushing projects forward all required his personal involvement. Now, in the gaps between camping and cycling trips, he just needed to glance at the data on his phone.

"This is amazing, so freeing," the boss messaged Xiao Tang. Two days later, they signed a contract for the next phase, and this time Xiao Tang changed his pricing model.

New problems soon emerged.

After the digital employees first went live, Xiao Tang thought the deal was pretty much done. One day, as he was about to shut down his computer, messages from the boss popped up one after another: "When I ask questions, which tools does the AI call? What data does it use?"

After Xiao Tang explained, the client pressed further: "Why can't I see exactly what it did?"

He typed up a long explanation and sent it over, but the boss seemed somewhat disappointed. Only then did Xiao Tang realize that the boss actually wanted to see with his own eyes what the AI was doing.

For people unfamiliar with AI, invisible things are always unsettling. So Xiao Tang added visualization to the process, letting the boss see which data the AI called, which steps it took, what it did each day, and whether there were any anomalies.

After this version was delivered, the boss replied with a thumbs-up.

In her six months as an FDE, the most important lesson Jolie Ni learned was "educating clients."

Often, when companies come knocking, their demand boils down to three words: "improve efficiency." But when she asks, "What pain points does your business have?" the other side often stammers, saying things that miss the point entirely.

Some processes are inherently unreasonable, but once you point out the problems and suggest redesigning them, the client gets defensive: "I've been in this industry for 10 years. What gives you the right to lecture me?"

After hitting walls a few times, she gradually understood that most clients don't actually know where their business is going wrong—they just want things better, faster, and cheaper.

So she changed her approach and started with demos. When taking on a project for a law firm, she discovered that the firm's paralegals spent hours every day deciding whether to take on a case. That's a job AI could finish in minutes.

She didn't say it directly. Instead, she made a demo video using projects from other law firms. In the video, it was crystal clear how the AI judged whether to take a case according to the rules, whether additional information was needed, and then automatically sent acceptance or rejection emails. The effect was far better than repeatedly explaining things to clients.

As an FDE, technical skill is the entry barrier. Getting clients to actually listen to your advice—that's the real skill.

Facing communication problems, Lawted found a more "convenient" route. Having taught a freight forwarding boss to use Doubao, Lawted quickly understood that many bosses in traditional industries are still riding horses—when you talk to them about AI, they just want a faster horse. A large part of FDE work, he said, "is telling them that cars already exist in this world."

After joining the freight forwarding company, he quickly ran into problems. To be an FDE, you first have to understand the business processes. He went to the boss, who waved him off and told him to go ask the employees himself.

He went around the office asking one by one. The employees he asked would think for a long time, then point at the send button on the screen: "This button is a bit too small." Another one pointed at the recall button: "I think this button should be changed to red, more eye-catching."

He couldn't get any process information out of them. So he simply leaned over the employees' computers, went through their past work records, pieced the processes together bit by bit, and asked questions wherever something seemed off.

After a month of shuttling between the boss and the employees, Lawted got the hang of it.

When talking money with the boss, never mention "subscription fees." That phrase reminds people of video site memberships. Talk about the digital human's annual salary instead—one digital human can replace dozens of real people, requires no social insurance or housing fund, and only needs token fees to "feed" it.

With employees, the message is "I'm here to help you." He ran the numbers for one employee: currently handling 100 orders a month for 5,000 yuan; in the future, 400 orders a month with quadrupled pay. The employee nodded repeatedly, saying his order volume had indeed plateaued for a long time, and his salary hadn't gone up in ages. From then on, this employee proactively reported to him every day.

At this point, Lawted had gotten his foot in the FDE door. But watching this employee talk with growing enthusiasm, he suddenly felt a pang of sadness—the more detailed the employee's account, the higher the probability of being laid off.

Cost reduction is a logic every boss buys into. The money saved by AI-driven efficiency gains comes mostly from labor costs, and FDE is no exception.

He knew before starting that AI would impact employees. His own company cut customer service staff this year, and 80% of his own work had already been handed over to AI.

Making money off anxiety

Two months after taking on the freight forwarding project, Lawted had built a complete AI system that could integrate all of the company's processes and achieve full AI transformation.

The boss was delighted when he saw it. Upload files like Excel or PDFs into the system, and it automatically completes the entire workflow—parsing, creating orders, filling in orders, exporting—with no human intervention needed, just a final check and confirmation.

But switching systems takes time. Just then, the situation in the Middle East improved, and this export-oriented freight forwarding company was flooded with orders. At the critical moment, employees had no time to learn how to use the new system.

Lawted wanted to wait, but the boss told him the slow season would last until February of next year. He was stunned—the original plan was to finish this project this month, then roll the system out to other companies in the freight forwarding industry, monetize this industry before moving to the next. Now that was completely impossible.

Soon, the boss dropped the final straw that broke the camel's back: the company's gross margin was only 8%. Of the 300 million in orders shown in the system, only about 10 million actually landed in hand. One miscalculated order would wipe out an entire year's work. Switching immediately from an old system that had run safely for six years to a new one he'd written in two months—the risk was too great.

The boss's enthusiasm dropped to freezing point. Lawted had to settle for second best: instead of replacing the system, use digital employees to plug into the old system and cut costs first. But when he ran the numbers, the digital employees' token consumption was substantial, while the employees being replaced earned only 4,000 yuan a month. There was simply no point in replacing them.

Xiao Tang ran into a similar problem. After finishing his first project, he realized that without productization, an individual FDE is essentially trading time for money. Every new industry means re-understanding the business, re-communicating with clients, and rebuilding the whole process from scratch.

The biggest dilemma for FDEs is labor costs. Jolie Ni found that doing a project requires going deep inside a company, getting familiar with a completely unfamiliar industry, and afterward teaching employees how to use it. The time and energy costs in between are very high. If the positions being replaced don't pay much to begin with, even optimizing away all the people doesn't mean much.

Another dilemma is that requirements are too specific and change too fast.

Her team once built a marketing and customer acquisition workflow for a hardware startup, and only learned on a follow-up visit that the company's target customer base had changed, making the original workflow completely unusable. With current technology, completely reshaping a company's workflows isn't realistic. What's more, many companies' problems lie not in technology but in the processes themselves, and as an outsider, an FDE can hardly grasp all the intricacies involved.

As for the future of FDE, Jolie Ni's judgment is that companies will eventually set up internal positions. Unlike the current model where big companies offer FDE as a bundled service with product sales, it might become a role permanently stationed inside a company, continuously running AI processes. So she plans to steer her company toward training internal FDEs for enterprises.

Lawted has found the same path. After completing two projects, he started building an FDE community: on one side, young people looking to try a new kind of work; on the other, traditional companies urgently needing AI transformation. He wants to twist these two forces together.

"Right now, most bosses are just afraid of missing the AI wave, and this anxiety will eventually pass," Jolie Ni concluded. "Training talent who can be directly embedded on-site—that's the truly reliable business for FDE."
