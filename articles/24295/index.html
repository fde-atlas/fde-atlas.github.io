How FDEs Turn a Client's IT Team Into Allies

FDE Times · 2026-10-30 · 7 min read

Summary

This article argues that client IT teams resist forward deployed engineers not out of rudeness but from fear of being replaced or inheriting a system they cannot maintain. The recommended response is to state openly, from the first meeting, that the goal is for the client's team to run and extend the system without the vendor. It proposes a three-step playbook for a hypothetical inventory agent deployment: a pre-coding meeting that lets IT set boundaries, bringing security in during week one with a one-page data-flow description, and co-designing and pair programming with decision logs. It also lists five common mistakes, including escalating to executives and deferring security reviews, and suggests how to present handover outcomes on a CV.

Key points

  • IT teams resist FDEs because they fear replacement and unmaintainable systems.
  • Declare early that the client's team should eventually run the system without you.
  • Bring security in during week one; late changes can block go-live.
  • Pair programming and decision logs make handover continuous, not a final event.
  • Five mistakes: escalating over IT, over-broad access, exclusive demos, no documentation, late security.

What the contract sold rarely matches the real data, and the client's IT team knows where it breaks, so bring them in as co-owners from day one.

In brief

  • IT teams are wary of FDEs because they fear being replaced, or being left with a system they do not understand, not because you are good at the job.
  • Say plainly from the start that the goal is for them to run and extend the system without you.
  • Invite the security team in during week one, pair program with the client's engineers and hand over gradually throughout the project.

The ticket asking for read access to the order database has gone three days without a reply. In the technical meeting, the client’s IT lead sits with arms folded and answers every question the same way: “We’ll have to check.”

This is not rudeness. From where the IT lead sits, a stranger has been brought in by senior management to do work that should have belonged to the IT team.

Palantir compares the job of a Forward Deployed Software Engineer to being the CTO of a startup: a small team, end-to-end responsibility, often working on site with the client. With a remit that broad, an FDE ends up doing precisely the work that would otherwise fall to the IT team.

Even when a client likes you, you remain an outsider. First Round Review reports that the General Counsel of a Fortune 100 company affectionately called the engineers Ironclad sent over “The Backpacks”. The nickname sounds friendly, but the point is that people who arrive with backpacks will leave with them too.

Why can’t you do the job without them?

You could route around the IT team by asking the executive sponsor to lean on them. That backfires quickly. A former Palantir FDE recruiter told First Round Review that what you discover on site will be very different from what was sold in the contract.

OpenAI’s head of forward deployed engineering made the same point to The Pragmatic Engineer: what clients describe during scoping often fails to match their real data and systems.

The people who know where that gap lies, such as which table is full of junk data or which job runs at 2 a.m. and locks a table, are almost always on the IT team.

Push them out and you lose the very source of information you need most.

So the question is not how to get past the IT team. It is what they are afraid of, and how you answer that fear.

Change the story: you are there to leave

IT teams usually fear two things. They fear being replaced, and they fear being left with a system they do not understand, so that when it breaks at midnight they are the ones who carry it. Both fears have the same answer.

A guide to FDE project handover on Simpliaxis sets a clear goal: the client’s team must be able to run and keep developing the system without needing to contact you.

The guide also turns the usual view around: making yourself unnecessary does not reduce your value, it is the proof of it. A CIO.com article on FDEs in the age of agentic AI likewise advises designing for the transfer of ownership from the outset, so the client’s team can operate and extend the system.

Say this in the first meeting and act accordingly, and you are no longer someone who has come to take their place. You become the person helping them take ownership of a new system.

Example: an inventory agent and a four-person IT team

Take a hypothetical deployment of an agent that answers inventory questions for a retailer. The agent has to read data from an ERP system managed by a four-person IT team. Three things need to happen, in order.

Step one: a 30-minute meeting before writing a line of code. Do not open by asking for access. Ask about the real system: “Is the inventory table updated in batches or in real time? What caused the last time the numbers were wrong? Is there anything on the system you don’t want anyone touching?” The last question matters most, because it gives them the right to draw the boundaries.

Close the meeting with a commitment: “By the end of the project, your team should be able to fix and extend this agent without calling the vendor. The schedule will be built around that goal.”

Step two: bring security in during week one. The CIO.com article stresses that governance in agentic AI cannot be a compliance checklist pulled out at the final stage. ElevenLabs writes that for its FDE team, security, data control and regulatory compliance are mandatory from the start.

So bring a one-page description of the data flow: which three tables the agent can read (read-only), where the logs live, and whether prompts contain client data.

Let the security team strike out whatever they like. A change requested in week one costs a few hours; the same change in the final week can block the go-live date outright.

Step three: design together and code together. The Simpliaxis guide lists three concrete practices: involve the client’s engineers in design decisions, pair program with them during the build, and explain your reasoning as you work. ElevenLabs describes its FDEs working side by side with the client’s technical teams, inside the client’s own infrastructure.

In the inventory example, this means a decision log kept in the shared repo, plus two pairing sessions a week, each with a different member of the four-person team. A log entry can be as short as this:

D-03: Cache inventory query results for 15 minutes
Reason: avoid calling the ERP directly every time the agent answers
Rejected option: read the inventory table directly in real time
Decided with: [client engineer's name]
To change it, edit: the cache config file; the IT team can do this themselves

The “decided with” line is the most valuable detail. It turns your decision into a shared one, and tells future readers whom to ask once you have gone.

The Simpliaxis guide explains why this works: engineers who build alongside the client’s team hand over continuously throughout the project, so the final transfer is very small. By the last week, there is no three-day “handover training” left to run.

What wording puts an IT team at ease?

Most of the wariness starts with how you open. You can still ask for exactly what you need; only the wording changes:

Instead ofSay
“Give me admin access to the ERP so I can move faster.”“I only need read-only access to these three tables. Is there any you’d rather leave out?”
“I’ll handle this part, you don’t need to worry about it.”“Let’s pair on this, since your team will be the one maintaining it.”
“Demo for leadership on Friday.”“Dry run with the IT team on Thursday, then your team co-presents on Friday.”
“Security can wait for a later phase.”“Here’s the data flow diagram. Could the security team review it before we build?”

Five mistakes that turn allies into blockers

The most common mistake is going over the IT team’s heads to the executive sponsor whenever they are slow to respond. You win one ticket and lose the whole team. The second is asking for broader access than you need, which confirms exactly what the security team fears.

The third is demoing to leadership without inviting the IT team, so they hear about a system running on their own infrastructure secondhand.

The fourth is writing code only you understand: no decision log, no runbook, everything in your head. The fifth is leaving security until the end, the very thing both CIO.com and ElevenLabs warn against.

How does this skill look on a CV?

When reading FDE job descriptions, watch for phrases such as “work side-by-side with technical teams”, “handoff” or “enablement”. Where they appear, the company cares about handing over to clients, not only about building.

For interviews, prepare a story about a time you brought a wary team into the work. Be specific about what you asked in the first meeting, when you brought security in and whom you paired with.

On a CV, write about the outcome of the handover: how many engineers the client’s team had, how long after you left they were running the system on their own, and which features they added themselves.

Those numbers must be your real ones. They map directly onto the goal that FDE handover guides set: the client’s team runs and extends the system without you.

The final test is simple. The system is still running after the day you pick up your backpack and leave, and the person maintaining it is the IT lead who sat with arms folded in that first meeting.
