A Palantir Alum Explains the "FDE" Implementation Power — Why Generative AI Rollouts End Up Stuck in the "Experiment Phase"

Original title: パランティア出身者が語る「FDE」という実装力。生成AI導入が「実験止まり」で終わる理由

Business Insider Japan · 2026-07-01 · 5 min read

Summary

Business Insider Japan interviews Katsuki Kitazawa, who led the FDE team at Palantir and is now CTO of Space Data, on why generative AI rollouts stall at the pilot stage. He argues that although technical barriers have fallen, the flood of new tools and models has made the market chaotic, and the real challenge is getting frontline staff to actually adopt systems. He says successful deployment requires both top-down and bottom-up optimization, and that FDEs bridge users and technology by engaging before requirements are fixed, aiming to improve business processes rather than just build what was requested. He cautions that FDEs are not suited to high-reliability systems, where waterfall development remains appropriate.

Key points

  • Kitazawa led Palantir's FDE team and is now CTO of Space Data.
  • Proliferating AI tools and models have made the market chaotic.
  • Deployments fail when only top-down or bottom-up optimization is pursued.
  • FDEs engage before requirements are fixed to improve business processes.
  • High-reliability systems still suit waterfall, not FDEs.

The use of generative AI is shifting from the experimental stage to production deployment. AI companies and major consulting firms are also strengthening their systems to support companies on-site, from implementation through to adoption.

One role attracting attention in this context is the FDE (Forward Deployed Engineer). An FDE is a professional who stands between on-site users and technology, identifying challenges while implementing systems.

Katsuki Kitazawa is someone who has tackled this FDE role at Palantir Technologies. At the company, he led the FDE team and was also involved in hiring interviews and certification. He currently serves as CTO (Chief Technology Officer) of SpaceData, a startup working on businesses that combine space and digital technology.

Kitazawa explains that the FDE is "a role that connects users and technology."

What does it take to ensure AI adoption doesn't end as a mere "PoC" and becomes integrated into actual operations? We asked Kitazawa about the role of the FDE and how companies should make use of external support.

Technical hurdles have lowered, but the market has become "chaotic"

The emergence of generative AI has dramatically changed access to technology.

"Anyone can now build a simple app, and if you ask AI about technical matters, it gives you fairly good answers. It's true that the hurdles keep getting lower" (Kitazawa)

However, Kitazawa points out that this doesn't mean corporate AI adoption has become easy. Options like "ChatGPT," "Claude," and "Gemini" are rapidly increasing, and when the model changes, so does how you use it.

Moreover, challenges such as usage-based billing and token management (managing costs incurred according to AI usage), security, and connecting with existing systems only become visible after implementation.

"New AI tools appear almost every day, and the market has become quite chaotic. The hurdles to AI adoption may actually be higher than before" (Kitazawa)

In Kitazawa's view, the difficulty lies not so much in the performance of the models themselves, but in whether the implemented system can actually be used on-site and drive real change in operations.

Technology adoption requires both "global optimization" and "local optimization"

Kitazawa explains that a typical way technology adoption such as AI stalls is "leaning too heavily toward either top-down (global optimization) or bottom-up (local optimization)."

"With bottom-up alone, a huge volume of minor issues and complaints come up from the field, and implementation ends once those are built. With top-down alone, policies come down but lack substance, resulting in systems that the field never uses" (Kitazawa)

What makes this tricky is that the "problems" users on-site report often don't align with what is actually consuming their work time.

"Even when field staff answer the question 'What are the challenges in your work?', it's often unclear whether that's really the challenge. Sometimes only after collecting and analyzing all the logs do you see where time was actually being consumed" (Kitazawa)

Simply gathering requirements and building on top of them, Kitazawa says, ends up just picking up surface-level issues and rarely reaches the essential problems.

"The goal for a company is not to implement a system. It is to improve operations through implementation and achieve business objectives" (Kitazawa)

As a role that meshes top-down and bottom-up approaches, what Kitazawa confronted at Palantir was the FDE.

An FDE is not just a "technology implementation specialist"

Kitazawa explains that the essence of the FDE is "connecting users and technology."

"No matter how good the technology, it's meaningless if it isn't used. If users can't use it, it's a treasure left to waste. At Palantir, we always used the term 'mission-driven' (an approach that starts from the purpose or mission that should be prioritized). What is the mission, and what is needed to achieve it? Connecting those is the most important thing" (Kitazawa)

The FDE role overlaps in some ways with on-site resident engineers and implementation support. However, Kitazawa says the roles they play differ.

In conventional development, what matters is implementing clearly defined requirements while ensuring quality and stability. An FDE, on the other hand, gets involved before requirements are even settled, going as far as understanding what a request means in terms of actual business operations.

"When someone says 'I want to do something like this,' I ask back 'What does that mean specifically?', understand the business, and then proactively offer our own input" (Kitazawa)

The goal is not to implement what was requested, but to improve the business workflow itself.

Another difference is the speed of decision-making.

In conventional development, requests are often taken back, organized, and implemented with role division across the team. In the FDE approach, priorities are judged on-site, and implementation may begin right then and there. Kitazawa says this is also a characteristic of the FDE.

That said, Kitazawa does not consider the FDE a universal solution.

"Systems like bank account management, where mistakes are unacceptable and robustness is required, don't suit the FDE approach. It's better to do those properly with waterfall (a traditional development method that sequentially fixes each phase from requirements definition to design, development, and testing)" (Kitazawa)

Kitazawa's view is that it's not about which method is superior, but about choosing the right one according to the objective.
