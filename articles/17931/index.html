From "Strong Models" to "Painful Scenarios": Lessons from ICBC and China Merchants Bank's Large-Scale Scenario Deployment

Original title: 从「模型很强」到「场景很痛」：工行与招行大规模场景落地的启示

Summary

This article examines what large-scale LLM delivery looks like in Chinese banking, using public disclosures. ICBC's GongYin ZhiYong platform covers over 400 scenarios with a 90% end-to-end success rate and over 1 billion cumulative calls; China Merchants Bank has deployed 856 scenarios with a fully in-house tech stack. The author argues that scenario counts measure coverage, not impact, and that coverage, quality, and usage metrics must be read together. Notably, FDE-type capabilities appear as internalized roles within banks rather than vendor on-site deployments common internationally.

Background: banking is the industry with the densest large-model scenario deployment

In the domestic toB landscape for large model deployment, banking is one of the industries with the fullest disclosure and the highest scenario density. According to a September 2025 Xinhua report, ICBC built the enterprise-level hundred-billion-parameter financial large model "ICBC Zhiyong," empowering over 20 major business domains and 200+ scenarios, and launched the "Pioneering AI+" initiative. On the other side, according to a March 2026 MobilePayment report, China Merchants Bank has deployed 856 large model application scenarios and announced plans to build "the industry's first intelligent bank." Hundreds of scenarios being built and operated simultaneously within a single institution means large model delivery has moved from "pilot thinking" into "engineering thinking."

This background deserves another layer of explanation. Banking is a typical heavily regulated, process-driven, multi-system industry: core banking, credit, risk control, customer service, and wealth management each have independent systems and data domains, and any AI capability entering a business process must pass through multiple gates such as data permissions, auditing, and review. Therefore, the scenario count and call volume achieved by banks represent a ceiling-level sample of "last mile" engineering capability—the gap in model capabilities across providers is narrowing, and it is the organizational capability to roll capabilities out across hundreds of business touchpoints that creates differentiation. The kind of work represented by the Forward Deployed Engineer (FDE)—translating model capabilities into runnable business scenarios—is in massive demand at this scale; it just takes a different form than in international markets, a point elaborated below.

The problem: delivery pain after scenario scaling

A demo for a single scenario has long ceased to be a problem; the real difficulties come with scaling. First is scenario selection: which business touchpoints are worth putting a model on, and in what order—a wrong choice causes resources to dissipate quickly. Among hundreds of candidate touchpoints, those with high repetition, clear decision rules, and controllable fault tolerance should go first, consistent with the selection logic in the on-site scenario discovery playbook. Second is data and system integration: hundreds of scenarios mean hundreds of integration paths with in-bank systems, data permissions, and compliance review; integration workload grows linearly with scenario count, while coordination costs grow super-linearly with inter-system dependencies.

Third is quality and operations: post-launch success rates, rollbacks, and eval regressions face strict audit requirements in the financial industry, and any customer-facing scenario failure can constitute a risk event. Fourth is measurement: management needs to know the input-output metrics, otherwise building hundreds of scenarios cannot sustainably secure budget. None of these four problems is a model problem—they are all engineering and organizational problems. The distance between "the model is strong" and "the scenarios are painful" is precisely the sum of these problems.

The solution: enterprise-level platform + rolling scenario expansion + agent system

From public disclosures, ICBC's path can be summarized as "platform as the foundation, rolling scenario expansion, convergence toward agents." The platform layer is the enterprise-level hundred-billion-parameter financial large model ICBC Zhiyong, serving as a unified foundation supporting reuse across business domains; the scenario layer started from over 20 major business domains and 200+ scenarios and expanded in rolling fashion; in terms of capability form, it converges toward agents—according to an October 2025 China Electronic Banking Network report, ICBC has built over a thousand domain-specific agents. Abstracting similar business problems into agents rather than developing scenario by scenario is a typical convergence strategy at the scaling stage.

CMB's path, by contrast, is "full-stack in-house development": according to information released by the Shenzhen Financial Regulatory Bureau, CMB built a full-stack in-house large model technology system, pushing large models from technological innovation to the business front line; MobilePayment's report shows it advancing its "intelligent bank" positioning on the basis of 856 deployed scenarios. The common point of the two paths: both treat large models as enterprise-level infrastructure to be built, rather than procuring solutions scenario by scenario. The difference lies in the source of the technology stack—ICBC's path emphasizes unified enablement through an enterprise-level platform, while CMB's emphasizes full-stack in-house development, reflecting the two banks' different technology organization traditions, but the core of delivery engineering is isomorphic.

Implementation: what kind of roles does scaled delivery require

Rolling out hundreds of scenarios relies not on a one-time mega-project but on continuous scenario engineering. Looking at the implementation form against public figures: according to an August 2025 Sina Finance report, ICBC Zhiyong's cumulative call volume exceeded 1 billion, application scenarios grew 67% year over year, call frequency rose 120%, and APIs were opened to over 30 small and medium banks. A 67% year-over-year growth in scenario count means dozens of new scenarios are added every quarter on average—at this pace, delivery work must be broken down into a replicable pipeline: requirement definition, data integration, eval baselines, launch acceptance, continuous monitoring. The on-site eval baseline playbook and POC acceptance checklist describe the key steps in this pipeline.

What deserves attention is the organizational difference in where these roles sit. In international markets, this capability is usually provided by model vendors' FDEs on site—labs send engineers to customer sites to do delivery; whereas at leading domestic banks, public information shows scenario delivery is completed jointly by in-bank technology teams and external vendors, and FDE capability manifests here as "internalizing the role": what banks need internally is a hybrid role that simultaneously understands model capability boundaries, data architecture, and business processes. Meanwhile, domestic vendors are also filling in this type of role—ByteDance, Tencent, and other companies have directly posted FDE-type positions (see the on-site FDE China localization analysis); simultaneous expansion on both the buyer and vendor sides shows this capability is an industry-wide gap, not a special need of a single institution.

Results: scale evidence from three interlocking metrics

ICBC's publicly disclosed results can be presented through three interlocking metrics. Coverage metric: large models cover over 400 scenarios (China Electronic Banking Network, 2025-10-20); compared against the 200+ scenarios reported by Xinhua, the expansion trajectory over roughly half a year is clearly visible. Quality metric: 90% end-to-end success rate (same source). Usage metric: cumulative call volume exceeding 1 billion, scenario count up 67% year over year, call frequency up 120% (Sina Finance, 2025-08-01). There are two additional pieces of granular evidence: according to the China Finance Net special feature "Top 10 Typical AI Application Cases in Banking 2025," ICBC added over 100 application scenarios such as the AI wealth assistant, and its enterprise-level intelligent risk control platform covers all domestic branches and more than 130 risk control scenarios.

On CMB's side, the public figures are 856 deployed scenarios and the "intelligent bank" positioning (MobilePayment, 2026-03). It is important to emphasize the boundaries of these metrics: scenario count measures coverage, not returns; success rate measures quality, not business value; call volume measures activity, not effectiveness—only the three together form a complete picture, and citing any one alone may overestimate or underestimate delivery quality. This "multiple interlocking metrics" disclosure approach is itself worth learning; it is a reflection of the measurement layering emphasized in the FDE delivery ROI framework on a real institution.

Constraints: the delivery boundaries of the financial industry

Delivery constraints in banking are harder than in most industries. First is compliance and audit: model outputs need a clear review mechanism before entering business processes, and the flip side of a 90% end-to-end success rate is that some parts still require human fallback—delivery design must spell out the handling path for the "remaining 10%." Second is data permissions: the more scenarios there are, the higher the coordination cost of cross-department data authorization, and permission negotiations often take longer than technical integration; see the on-site Data Compliance Checklist for related check items.

Third is long-tail operations: thousands of agents mean thousands of monitoring and regression testing paths. Without a platform-based evals and observability system, scaling up actually amplifies risk—which is why evals capability is given such a prominent place in financial delivery. Fourth is ecosystem constraints: opening APIs to more than 30 small and medium banks means delivery standards must extend from internal engineering to external service standards, placing higher demands on stability, version management, and documentation quality; in effect, the internal delivery team takes on the responsibilities of an external product team.

Reusable Lessons

First, platformization is the prerequisite for scaling: build the enterprise-grade foundation and evals system first, then roll out scenarios—getting the order wrong leads to loss of control at a few hundred scenarios. Second, establish three measurement dimensions simultaneously: coverage (number of scenarios), quality (success rate), and usage (call volume); reports presenting only one number won't withstand scrutiny. Third, agents are the right form after scenarios converge: abstracting similar scenarios into domain-specific agents is easier to maintain and reuse than stacking solutions scenario by scenario. Fourth, delivery processes should be pipeline-ized: requirements definition, evals baselines, acceptance, and monitoring each have clear procedures and deliverables—at scale, quality is guaranteed by process rather than individual heroes; see the on-site FDE Project Kickoff Template and Delivery Handover Checklist for procedure references.

For practitioners, the trend of FDE-type roles being internalized by large clients deserves serious attention—doing AI scenario delivery on the client side is becoming a career path parallel to vendor on-site work; see the on-site FDE Career Path and FDE Competency Model.
