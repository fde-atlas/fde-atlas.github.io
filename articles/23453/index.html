What does an FDE do: three Forward Deployed Engineer roles

Original title: FDE 是做什么的：前线部署工程师三种岗位

FDE Times · 2026-11-04 · 5 min read

Summary

Drawing on Bloomberry's analysis of 1,000 job postings, the article argues that the FDE title covers three very different jobs, with coding time ranging from 30% to 90%. Builders make up 60% of postings and code 70-90% of the time; about 30% are really sales engineering roles with quotas and commissions; the remaining 10% build internal tools like CRM integrations with little client contact. The author advises candidates to classify a posting before asking whether the job is technical, using signals like quota and commission wording, and to probe interviewers with concrete questions about hours spent coding and what was recently shipped into customer systems.

Before asking whether an FDE role is technical enough, first figure out which kind of job the posting in front of you actually describes.

Key takeaways

  • Bloomberry analyzed 1,000 job postings and found that the FDE title actually covers three different jobs; Builder-type roles account for 60%, with 70-90% of time spent coding.
  • About 30% of postings are actually sales engineering (30-40% coding, with a quota attached); the remaining ~10% only build internal tools and barely interact with customers.
  • In Builder roles, customer work doesn't eat into coding time—it feeds the code: the software gets written into the customer's own systems.

Bloomberry read 1,000 Forward Deployed Engineer (FDE) job postings and found a strange phenomenon: under the same title, some roles require 70-90% of time spent coding, while others require only 30-40% and come with a sales quota. The gap is so large it's hard to call them the same job.

Bloomberry isn't the only one to notice this. DataCamp wrote that two jobs both called FDE at two companies may have almost nothing in common. Scaler put it more bluntly: the share of time spent coding has nothing to do with the title—it's a range each employer sets for itself.

If you're a software engineer considering a move to FDE, the question "is this job technical enough?" is the wrong one to start with. The better question is: which category does the posting in front of you belong to? Between the 30-40% and 70-90% groups, the two largest types can differ by up to three times in weekly coding hours.

Most postings describe Builder work

Bloomberry split the FDE title into three types of jobs. Builder-type roles account for 60% of postings—the majority—with the defining traits of 70-90% coding time and frequent travel. Scaler's independent analysis likewise estimated that about 60% of postings are build-focused roles.

Converted to a 40-hour week: 70-90% means 28 to 36 hours of coding, roughly the same as a regular software engineer's week.

Are customers eating your time, or feeding your work?

PostHog, a company that hires FDEs, puts it simply: FDEs still do software engineering, just with far more time spent talking to customers. Perspective's survey of 1,500 FDEs found a median of 47% of the week spent collaborating directly with customers, with only about 31% spent coding.

31% seems to contradict the Builder's 70-90%, but that survey didn't break down by job type, and the median mixes sales engineers together with Builders.

In other words, Builder-type postings promise lots of coding, while the real average FDE week leans more toward customers. That's one more reason to ask several pointed questions before accepting an offer.

In Builder roles, the two don't necessarily conflict. Read PostHog's description with this in mind: time spent with customers is precisely the input to the code—it's when you figure out what to build.

Big companies' postings say the same. Palantir's Forward Deployed Software Engineer role requires strong coding ability. Anthropic's FDE Applied AI role ties building production systems together with regular on-site stints working side by side with customers.

Imagine developing on-site at a customer: the tech stack is rarely your choice, you have no way of knowing what the data looks like in advance, and when something breaks the user is standing right next to you. This makes coding as a Builder-type FDE far harder than the percentages suggest.

What about the other two types?

Bloomberry classified about 30% of postings with the FDE title as Sales Engineer+. The markers are 30-40% coding time, salary including commission, and a quota to hit. On a 40-hour week, that's 12 to 16 hours of coding—less than half the Builder's upper bound.

The remaining ~10% are Internal Tools Builders: serving only the company internally, mainly doing CRM integrations, with almost no travel. Here the FDE name has drifted almost entirely from its original meaning, because there are no external customers to "deploy" to at all.

Sales engineering is not lesser work, and neither is building internal tools. But once you carry a quota, revenue becomes part of how your work is measured. If you're moving to FDE because you want to build real things for real customers, mistaking these two types for Builder roles is the easiest trap to fall into.

Read job postings like you'd read a requirements spec

The clearest signals are the words "quota" and "commission." If either appears, no matter what the title says, you're most likely reading a sales engineering posting. "Internal" plus "CRM" and almost no travel point to the internal tools type.

The travel figures in the table are useful references but not decisive on their own. Palantir is clearly a coding-heavy role, yet its listed travel percentage is below the Builder's typical range and adjustable to personal preference. Judging by that single number alone could lead to a misclassification—read it together with the quota signal and what you're asked to build.

Once you're in the interview, ask directly: how many hours did the FDEs on the team spend coding last week? What was the most recent thing shipped into a customer's system? If the interviewer answers with revenue rather than a concrete system, you have your answer.

What do you need to prove?

For Builder-type roles, employers want to see two things at once. First, that you can actually write code: Python first and foremost—it appears in 66% of the postings in Bloomberry's analysis; even better is having a project running in an environment you can't fully control. Second, that you can find problems yourself rather than waiting for requirements to be handed to you.

So don't write "strong communication skills" and "proficient in Python" as separate sections on your resume. Tell one story: what problem a user ran into, how you discovered it, what you shipped into their system, and what changed as a result.

A small project that solves a real problem for real users, running on their data, with a README that explains the problem clearly, is more persuasive than any certificate. And it happens to prove exactly the two things Builder roles require.

The work of a Builder-type FDE is actually more technical than many people think, because the code has to run in the messiest place: someone else's systems. Before applying, what you need to do is read the job posting carefully and confirm that it describes exactly this job.
