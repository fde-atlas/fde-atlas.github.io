What is context engineering?

Context engineering is the discipline of designing and managing the entire set of information passed to an LLM so it can make the right next move.

When generative AI is used in business, you often see a gap where "the same model works well in one setting but is unusable in another." This gap is often caused not by model performance but by "what you show the model." Context engineering is an approach that treats this "way of showing" as a design target rather than leaving it as personal know-how.

Definition and how the term spread

An approach that treats the entire context as a design target, popularized around mid-2025 by posts from Andrej Karpathy and others.

In June 2025, former OpenAI researcher Andrej Karpathy posted on X that he endorsed the term "context engineering" over "prompt engineering." In it, he described it as a "delicate art and science" of filling the context window with just the right information needed for the next step. Around the same time, Shopify CEO Tobi Lütke made a similar statement, and the term spread rapidly.

Later, in September 2025, Anthropic published the technical blog post "Effective context engineering for AI agents." This article frames context as a "finite resource" and emphasizes the importance of selecting the minimal, high-density information needed to elicit the desired behavior.

PerspectiveDescription
TargetThe entire set of tokens passed to the LLM (instructions, knowledge, tool definitions, history, etc.)
PurposePassing exactly the information needed for the next decision or action, with no excess or shortage
Key proponents/organizersAndrej Karpathy (June 2025), Anthropic technical blog (September 2025)
PremiseContext is finite, and more is not always better

Why it matters for FDEs now

Because AI agents are increasingly deployed in business, and the difference in accuracy is increasingly determined by "what you pass" rather than model performance.

An FDE (Forward Deployed Engineer) is a role that embeds in the customer's environment and makes the product work in line with the customer's business. In this way of working, established at Palantir, the core value lies in bridging generic products and specific business operations. In the era of generative AI, much of that bridging work becomes context design itself. Turning implicit knowledge—customer-specific terminology, exception rules, internal system specifications—into a form AI can handle overlaps strongly with the FDE's job.

How does it differ from prompt engineering?

Prompt engineering is about crafting instruction text; context engineering is about designing the entire information environment, including instructions.

The difference lies in the "unit" of design. Prompt engineering focuses on how to write a single input. Context engineering, on the other hand, deals with the whole mechanism of what to pass to the model, when, and in what form, across multiple steps.

Differences in scope and time horizon

A prompt targets a single input, while context targets the entire flow of information that persists while the agent operates.

Comparison itemPrompt engineeringContext engineering
Unit of designA single instructionThe flow of information across multiple turns or steps
Main targetsWording, examples, role settingInstructions, search results, tools, memory, history, output format
Typical use casesOne-off generation, summarization, classificationAI agents that autonomously proceed through multiple steps, business system integration
Means of improvementRewriting the textSelecting, compressing, structuring information, and designing retrieval timing
Practitioner skillsWriting ability, understanding model quirksBusiness understanding, data design, software implementation, evaluation design

Prompt craft is not wasted

How instructions are written remains an important part of context today. It is practical to view it as subsumed under the broader concept.

You sometimes see claims that "prompt engineering is over," but from a practitioner's perspective that is an overstatement. The clarity of system instructions and the choice of concrete examples still greatly affect output quality. What has changed is a growing recognition that polishing instruction text alone has limits, and stability comes only when combined with the surrounding information design.

What are the components of context engineering?

The main components can be organized into six: system instructions, knowledge, tools, memory, conversation history, and output format.

The categorization varies slightly depending on the author. Here, we divide them into six at a granularity that makes it easy for FDEs to divide up and inspect the design in customer environments.

The six components and their roles

Since each component has a different role, deciding "what to include and what to exclude" for each is the starting point of the design.

ComponentRoleConcrete field exampleDesign caution
System instructionsDefine role, policy, and constraints"First-line inquiry responder. Do not make refund decisions."Don't write in overly detailed conditional branching
Knowledge (search/RAG)Pass information that serves as grounds for judgmentInternal regulations, product manuals, past case recordsDon't mix in low-relevance documents
ToolsEnable referencing and operating external systemsInventory API, CRM search, ticket creationDon't line up tools with overlapping functionality
MemoryRetains information across sessionsCustomer-specific assumptions, notes on ongoing tasksDefine rules for updating and discarding outdated information
Conversation historyPreserves the flow of recent interactionsConfirmed conditions, user correctionsSummarize and compress when it gets long
Output formatStandardize into a form downstream processing can handleJSON schemas, report formatsAlign specifications with downstream systems

For tool connectivity, standards for connecting external data and tools to LLMs are also spreading, such as the Model Context Protocol (MCP) released by Anthropic in November 2024. With connection methods standardized, FDEs can spend their time deciding "what to connect" rather than "how to connect."

Why "narrowing down" works better than "adding more"

Context is a finite resource. Cramming in too much information buries important instructions and tends to reduce accuracy.

The 2023 paper "Lost in the Middle" (Liu et al.) reported that LLMs tend to struggle to use information placed in the middle of long contexts. Anthropic's aforementioned blog also touches on the phenomenon that the more tokens there are, the harder it becomes to recall information accurately, standing on the premise that the amount of information that can be attended to is limited.

In the field, people tend to decide "let's include everything just to be safe," but in practice this often backfires. The following three techniques are worth trying first.

  • Selection: Don't pass information that isn't used for the decisions at that step
  • Compression: Summarize long histories and documents, keeping only the key points
  • Deferred retrieval: Don't pass everything upfront; instead, have the system fetch information via tools when needed

How do FDEs implement this at customer sites?

FDEs observe the customer's operations to identify the information needed for decisions, then implement by repeatedly building small versions and validating them on-site.

Rather than completing the design at a desk before bringing it in, working alongside the people on-site and fixing things as it runs often improves accuracy faster. This is also why the FDE role emphasizes "being stationed in the field."

A 5-step design procedure for field use

The basic approach is to identify the decision points in the business, inventory the required information, and proceed in the order of implementation, evaluation, and operation.

StepWhat to doDeliverable
1. Business observationRecord what the staff look at and what decisions they makeList of decision points
2. Information inventoryFor each decision, confirm the location, freshness, and format of the required informationMapping table of information sources
3. Context designDecide the allocation to the six elements and the timing of deliveryDesign notes, first draft of system instructions
4. Evaluation set creationCreate test cases with correct answers from actual casesEvaluation data and scoring criteria
5. Field validation and improvementAnalyze errors together with the staff and fix the contextImprovement history, operational rules

Steps 1 and 2 are especially effective. Even if you ask staff "What do you look at when making decisions?", tacit knowledge is hard to put into words. By looking at actual work screens together and checking each referenced screen or document one by one, candidates for the information to pass to the AI become visible.

The ontology concept seen in Palantir AIP

Palantir structures business data as an ontology and uses it as the foundation of the context passed to AI.

Palantir announced AIP (Artificial Intelligence Platform) in 2023. At its core is the "Ontology." The Ontology represents a customer company's data as business objects such as "equipment," "orders," and "shipments," along with their relationships and executable actions. LLMs reference and manipulate information through structures imbued with business meaning, rather than through scattered raw data.

This concept is also useful even if you don't use Palantir products. When you build context based on "what business information this is" rather than "which table and which column," the AI's decisions align more easily with business language. Perhaps the first thing an FDE should create in the field is not a prompt, but an organization of this business vocabulary and structure.

Common stumbling points in the field

Most failures arise not from insufficient model capability, but from information freshness, permissions, and terminology mismatches.

Stumbling pointWhat happensDirection of countermeasures
Inclusion of outdated documentsAnswers based on pre-revision regulationsGive documents versions and expiration dates, and narrow down via search
Internal terminology mismatchesMixing up words whose meanings differ by departmentInclude a glossary in the context and state definitions explicitly
Overlooked permissionsIncluding information the user isn't authorized to view in answersControl the scope of retrieval according to the user's permissions
Too many toolsChoosing the wrong tool among similar onesLimit the number of tools and clarify usage distinctions in descriptions
Lack of evaluationBelieving things improved while another case got worseCompare with the same evaluation set on every change

How to learn context engineering?

The fastest route is to build a small agent for your own work and gain experience running evaluation and improvement cycles.

Context engineering is a skill that's hard to acquire just by reading about it. By personally accumulating experiences like "accuracy improved when I reduced information" or "an important condition disappeared when I summarized," your judgment instincts develop.

A stage-by-stage learning roadmap

A rough guide is three stages: start with instruction design, move through retrieval and tool integration, and finally expand to evaluation and operational design.

StageGoalExample activities
FoundationStabilize instructions and output formatsPick one of your routine tasks and design system instructions and an output schema
ApplicationConnect external knowledge and toolsTry internal document search (RAG) or tool connections via MCP, and adjust the amount passed
PracticeDesign through evaluation and operationsCreate an evaluation set and compare before and after changes. Define rules for compressing memory and history

As primary sources, Anthropic's technical blog and documentation, as well as each LLM vendor's agent-building guides, are useful. Since terminology and recommended practices in this field update quickly, it's reassuring to develop the habit of consulting original sources, not just secondary information.

Making evaluation (Evals) a habit

Whether things improved is judged with an evaluation set, not by feel. It is important to build the habit of comparing against the same questions every time you make a change.

It is not uncommon for a slight change in context to alter the behavior of another case. A practical evaluation set does not need to be large from the start; even a small set of typical cases and failure-prone cases gathered from real projects is quite useful. Having the client's representatives participate in creating scoring criteria also serves to align everyone's understanding of "what counts as correct."

Perspectives for companies to assess talent they have developed

For companies considering hiring and training FDEs, it becomes easier to evaluate candidates by checking whether they can explain the reasoning behind their information design decisions.

Rather than "which prompts they wrote," the ability to explain "why they passed that information and why they excluded other information" and "how they evaluated and improved" is considered a gauge of whether someone can perform in the field. For decision-makers considering adopting FDEs, simply asking for this explanation in interviews is itself one realistic way to measure a candidate's context engineering skills.
