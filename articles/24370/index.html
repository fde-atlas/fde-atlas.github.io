Companies calling themselves FDE (Forward Deployed Engineer) support providers have suddenly increased over the past year. Hitachi plans to build a domestic FDE organization of 1,000 people by fiscal 2026, Fujitsu has become a Palantir Global FDE Partner, and NTT Data Group has launched FDE-style consulting. Overseas, OpenAI established the OpenAI Deployment Company, and Anthropic launched Ode with Anthropic. Job postings have also grown by more than 800% from January to September 2025.

The options have increased, but there are still no criteria for choosing. If a company says "FDE," will they actually get into the field? Will the person who makes the proposal really implement it? Do they have a track record of things running in production? In my view, what needs to be verified comes down to 7 points. For each, I've included example questions you can use as-is in sales meetings.

The definition of the FDE role itself is summarized in What is an FDE? The meaning and work of a Forward Deployed Engineer, and the differences from SES, SIers, and consulting in Differences between FDE and SES, SIers, and consultants. This article deals only with "how the ordering side should choose."

Why "FDE support companies" are increasing now

Generative AI has arrived. According to the Ministry of Internal Affairs and Communications' 2026 Information and Communications White Paper, 86.4% of Japanese companies use generative AI for some business task. Yet it is not running in production. In July 2024, Gartner predicted that at least 30% of generative AI projects would be abandoned after proof of concept by the end of 2025. The reasons are data quality, risk management, rising costs, and unclear business value.

When "we introduced it but it doesn't run" became the common problem, what's needed is not someone who writes proposals, nor someone who builds to spec, but someone who gets into the field and keeps fixing until it works. That is the FDE, and Nikkei Cross Tech describes this role as "similar to but different from on-site dispatch" and "a small elite of knowledge-intensive workers." Because there is demand, suppliers have started calling themselves "FDE."

Three types of FDE support companies

Even among FDEs, who they enter the field for differs. Before choosing, look at which type the other party is.

Product vendor types are strongest once you have decided to use their product. Major SI types are reliable when paired with overhauling existing systems. Independent firms' job is to fill the "space between business and AI" where nothing has been decided yet. It's not a matter of which is better; suitability is determined at the consultation stage.

Seven criteria and example questions

1. Does the person who made the proposal do the implementation?

The most common failure is when the person who wrote the proposal and the person who comes to the field are different. The proposal is written from a management perspective, and implementation is handled by a separate team given only the specifications. Information gets lost in between.

Example question: "How far into the implementation process will the person who wrote this proposal be involved? How many times a week will they come on-site?"

2. Can they show something that runs in production?

Everyone has PoC experience. What you should look at is production. How many months has it been running, which KPIs moved, and why did stalled projects stall? Even if confidentiality prevents them from naming clients, they can talk about numbers and durations.

Example question: "Excluding PoCs, what is the longest-running production project, in months? What numbers moved there?"

3. Is it a contract that takes responsibility for KPIs?

Even if a company calls itself FDE, if the contract ends at "delivery," the substance is the same as an SIer. Does the contract state who defines the KPIs and what happens if they don't move?

Example question: "Do you offer performance-based contracts? Who defines the KPIs? What happens if nothing moves in three months?"

4. Frequency and form of on-site involvement

"Being stationed" and "entering the field" are different. Being stationed means having a desk; entering the field means being present where business decisions are made. Is there a rhythm of meeting with field managers weekly, observing usage, and making improvements?

Example question: "What does 'entering the field' concretely involve? Who attends which meetings, and how often?"

5. Tools and handover

What you build with directly affects speed. Are they using AI coding tools like Claude Code or Codex in production development, or writing code manually on a man-month basis? And when it's done, who owns the code, design documents, and operational procedures? Is there a plan for in-house development from the start?

Example question: "Which AI tools do you use for development? At contract end, will the code and documentation belong to us? Is there a plan leading up to in-house development?"

6. Pricing structure

There are three forms: monthly accompaniment, fixed-price packages, and performance-based. In our case, FDE implementation support starts at 1 million yen per month (3 months minimum, with KPI-linked success fees), and AI usage security diagnostics starts at a fixed 500,000 yen (2-3 weeks). Major SI types tend to accumulate man-months, making the total cost hard to see. Rather than cheap or expensive, look at whether it's clear what you are paying for.

Example question: "What does the fee cover? Man-months, duration, or results? Under what conditions do additional costs arise?"

7. How the contract ends

Good FDE support ends by creating a state where things run even without them. Bad support creates a state where things don't run without them and extends the contract. Ask about the ending up front, and you'll know which they're aiming for.

Example question: "Even if your company leaves after three months, can we continue operations and improvements on our own? What will you hand over to make that possible?"

Ways of choosing that lead to failure

In my view, failures in choosing come down to three things.

  • Choosing by the thickness of the proposal. A thick proposal is evidence of a company that spends time on proposals, not evidence of a company that implements.
  • Choosing by headcount. A large team feels reassuring, but in the end only 1-2 people come to the field, and it's those 1-2 people who determine the outcome.
  • Choose by product. If you decide on the product first, the problem gets tailored to fit the product. It is cheaper to decide the business process and KPI first, and choose the product afterward.

Conversely, even with a small team, if the proposer comes to the front line, can talk about numbers that actually ran in production, and can state the end point upfront, that company is hard to go wrong with.

Three things to prepare before the consultation

Before consulting an FDE support company, having the following three things makes the conversation go faster.

  • The KPI you want to move. Revenue, man-hours, utilization rate—anything is fine, but narrow it down to one.
  • The stalled PoC itself. The code, evaluation results, and the reason it stalled. If you have these, an assessment of whether it can go to production or needs a rebuild can be given in 30 minutes.
  • The front-line manager. Not an executive, but the person who runs that business process every day. A consultation without this person present ends with nothing but more proposals.

No matter which company you consult, if you prepare these three things and work through the example questions in order, you can tell apart companies that merely call themselves FDE from companies that actually enter the front line at the very first meeting.
