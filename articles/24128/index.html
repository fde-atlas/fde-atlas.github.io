What is Palantir Workshop? Explaining Its Features and How It's Used in FDE Practice

Original title: Palantir Workshopとは？機能とFDE実務での使い方を解説

FDE総研 · 2026-10-27 · 12 min read

Summary

This article explains Palantir Workshop, a low-code tool for building operational business applications on top of the Foundry ontology. Unlike BI dashboards, Workshop apps let frontline users view, decide, and write back within one screen. The author breaks down its five building blocks—widgets, variables, events, Actions, and Functions—and stresses that business rules like permissions and validation should be enforced at the ontology level. Workshop is compared with Slate (custom-coded apps) and Quiver (analytics), with Workshop as the default choice. For FDEs, the recommended workflow is: minimal ontology, read-only screens, field trials, then Actions for writeback. The article closes by arguing that career value lies in decomposing business processes into objects and operations, not tool proficiency.

Key points

  • Workshop builds low-code operational apps on ontology objects, supporting both reading and writing.
  • Widgets, variables, and events drive the UI; Actions handle writeback; Functions add custom logic.
  • Business rules and permissions belong in ontology Action types, not in app-side controls.
  • Choose Workshop by default; Slate only when custom design is essential and maintainable by the client.
  • FDEs should iterate read-only screens quickly before adding writeback, and document for handover.

What is Palantir Workshop?

Palantir Workshop is a tool for assembling business applications with low code on top of Palantir Foundry's ontology. Rather than dashboards for data analysis, it is designed to create "business screens" where frontline staff make daily decisions and perform operations.

Palantir's official documentation also introduces Workshop as the central tool for building operational applications. It is likely the first choice for FDEs (Forward Deployed Engineers) when they go into a customer's site and need to show something working within a short period.

An application-building tool that starts from the ontology

Workshop's distinguishing feature is that it works with "objects" on the ontology rather than tables. Screens are designed around these object units.

Foundry's ontology replaces datasets with business concepts (object types) such as "factory," "equipment," "order," and "customer," along with the links between them. Workshop widgets read and write these objects directly. As a result, app developers do not need to write SQL or API connections every time.

Conversely, if the ontology design is rough, the usability of Workshop apps cannot be much better. More than half of what determines Workshop quality is the ontology-side design.

Who uses it and in what situations

Apps built in Workshop are business applications for frontline staff to "view, decide, and act." The builders are FDEs, customer-side data teams, and power users in business departments.

Typical use cases are as follows.

Example use caseWhat is done on screenMain users
Alerts and exception handlingList objects with anomalies, assign owners, and update response statusOperations staff
Planning and allocationCompare allocation of inventory, personnel, and equipment, and finalize plansPlanners and managers
Case and ticket managementReview history on a detail screen for each case and change statusReview and investigation staff
Executive situational awarenessView the whole picture with metric cards, maps, and charts, then drill down into individual casesDecision makers

Common to all of these use cases is that a single screen completes not just "viewing" but also "writing (recording decisions)." This is the biggest difference from BI tools.

What are Workshop's main features and how does it work?

Workshop is built on a division of roles: composing screens with widgets, variables, and events; writing with Actions; and computing with Functions.

Once you grasp how these five elements relate, you can read almost any Workshop app.

ComponentRolePractical image
WidgetsScreen parts such as tables, charts, maps, filters, and buttons"Equipment list," "map," "counts by status"
VariablesHold state such as object sets, selected values, strings, and numbers"Equipment selected in the list," "filtered order set"
EventsUpdate variables on triggers like clicks, and open screen transitions or overlays"Selecting a row opens the detail panel"
ActionsCreate, edit, and delete ontology objects"Assign an owner," "set status to complete"
FunctionsWrite aggregation, derived values, and complex logic in TypeScript or Python"Calculate priority scores and sort"

The three elements: widgets, variables, and events

Workshop screens work by widgets reading and writing variables, and events switching variables and screen state.

For example, put the results filtered by a filter widget into an object set variable, and pass that variable to both a table and a map; the two widgets will stay in sync. Selecting a row in the table updates the "selected object" variable, and the detail panel on the right switches to that content. Whether you can design this "flow of variables" is the fundamental skill of Workshop development.

Layouts are built by combining sections, tabs, pages, and overlays such as drawers and modals. Since most of the logic can be configured without writing code, you can review screens together with business stakeholders and make corrections on the spot.

Actions and Functions handle writing and computation

Whether something works as a business application depends on write-backs via Actions and embedding logic via Functions.

Actions are defined as Action Types on the ontology. Workshop buttons and forms call them to update objects. Action Types can define submission criteria (conditions for execution) and parameter validation. This means business rules such as "only authorized staff can approve" or "submission is blocked if required fields are empty" are enforced centrally on the ontology side rather than on the screen.

Functions are used to create aggregations and derived metrics that are hard to express with standard widget settings alone. From field experience, relying too heavily on Functions from the start limits who can maintain the app. It is safer to build as much as possible with standard features and use code only where truly necessary.

Integration with AIP

In recent years, Workshops have also been used as a place to embed Palantir AIP functionality into business screens.

Configurations such as calling LLM-based logic built with AIP Logic via Functions or Actions, or placing agent-like assistants on the screen, are introduced in the official documentation and demos. What matters here is not to finalize generative AI output as-is, but to design a flow where a person approves it via an Action. FDEs are expected to design business processes based on "AI proposes, humans decide." Note that AIP-related features are updated frequently, so check the official documentation at the time of implementation to confirm what is available.

What is the difference between Workshop, Slate, and Quiver?

Workshop is for business applications using the ontology, Slate is for highly flexible custom applications, and Quiver is suited for analysis and dashboards.

Since all of these are tools for building "screens" within Foundry, first-time users tend to get confused. The differences in design philosophy are summarized in the table below.

Comparison table of the three tools

The differences can be organized along two dimensions: "who uses the screen and for what purpose" and "how much customization is required."

PerspectiveWorkshopSlateQuiver
Main purposeBusiness applications for operationsHighly customized custom applicationsAnalysis and dashboards of objects and time-series data
Data handlingCentered on ontology objectsFlexibly combines queries and API callsHandles objects and time series from an analytical perspective
Development styleLow-code (configuration-centric, plus Functions as needed)Code-oriented, including HTML/CSS/JavaScriptNo-code-oriented, combining analysis cards
WritebackStandardly supported via ActionsPossible, but the high design flexibility makes it more labor-intensivePrimarily for analysis; writeback is not a main use case
Visual customizationConsistent look within the standard widgetsAdjustable at the pixel levelStandard analytical displays
Ease of maintenanceEasy to hand over to the customer sideTends to depend on the skills of the person who built itAnalysts can easily update it themselves

Criteria for choosing between them

When in doubt, the general rule is to consider Workshop first, and choose Slate only when there is a clear reason Workshop cannot meet the requirements.

In practice, the decision process goes as follows.

  • Is it a screen that field staff operate daily to record decisions? → If so, Workshop
  • Is the main purpose deep-diving into time series or exploring relationships between metrics? → If so, Quiver (also consider embedding it in Workshop)
  • Is a custom design or special interactions absolutely required, as in an external-facing portal? → Consider Slate
  • If you choose Slate, confirm whether there is someone on the handover side who can maintain the frontend

From an FDE's perspective, the fourth point deserves particular emphasis. FDEs will eventually leave the field. Whether the customer can continue making improvements themselves is largely determined at the tool selection stage.

How do FDEs use Workshop in practice?

FDEs use Workshop as a tool to turn the results of business interviews into a working screen within days, and to rebuild it together with the field.

The value of Palantir-style FDEs lies not in accumulating requirements documents, but in narrowing down issues while having the customer's field use "something that works." Workshop's low-code nature fits well with this approach.

Flow from interviews to production operation

The typical approach is, in order: build a minimal ontology configuration, create a screen and try it in the field, then incorporate business processes via Actions.

StepWhat the FDE doesDeliverable
1. Observing the businessWatch which screens and reports staff use to make which decisionsList of decision points
2. Minimal ontology designDefine only the object types and links needed for decisionsMinimal object model
3. Read-only screenBuild a screen with lists, details, and filters that makes "the current situation visible"First Workshop module
4. Trial in the fieldHave staff use it in their actual work and cut screens that go unusedList of improvements
5. Incorporating writebackTurn assignments, approvals, and status updates into ActionsAn application that completes the business process
6. HandoverDocument the variable configuration and naming rules, and entrust future improvements to the customer's staffOperations documentation

It is important to repeat steps 3 and 4 many times at short intervals. If you build all the way to writeback functionality from the start, the scope of rework becomes large when the business flow changes.

Points to watch out for in the field

Because Workshop allows fast development, design debt also tends to accumulate. It is safer to decide variable naming, module division, and permission design upfront.

From the perspective of hands-on practitioners, the following points tend to cause problems later.

  • Too many variables: If you create variables every time you add a screen, it becomes impossible to track which variable is linked to what. Decide on a naming convention that makes roles clear from the start.
  • Cramming too much into a single module: Splitting modules by user role and reusing common parts via embedding makes maintenance easier.
  • Trying to control permissions on the application side: The basic principle is to control which data can be shown and which operations can be written via ontology and Action type permissions.
  • Putting off performance considerations: Streaming large numbers of objects directly into a table slows down display. Check filter defaults and aggregation approaches during the trial phase.

None of these are advanced Workshop-specific topics. However, they are points where FDEs tend to stumble after showing the customer that they can "build quickly."

How do Workshop skills contribute to your career?

What is valued in Workshop skills is not speed of operation. The ability to design—breaking down business processes into objects and operations—leads to recognition as an FDE.

Design ability, not tool operation, is what gets evaluated

You can learn Workshop's operations in a few weeks. What makes the difference is the design: which decisions to put on screen, and which operations to turn into Actions.

In hiring and evaluating FDEs, the qualities that matter include the following.

Skill areaWhat Workshop developsWhy it transfers to other environments
Business decompositionBreaking decision points down into objects and ActionsCommon to the design of any business system
Data modelingDesigning ontology object types and linksDomain modeling in its purest form
Product thinkingCutting unused screens and reducing the number of operationsDirectly applicable to internal tool and SaaS development
Customer dialogueRefining requirements while showing working screensAlso essential in consulting and pre-sales

Even if you move to an environment that doesn't use Palantir products, the mindset of "defining business objects and designing operations and permissions around them" applies as-is. Workshop is also valuable as a teaching material for acquiring this mindset in a short time.

How to learn and demonstrate your experience

The fastest path is to master the basics with the official training materials, and then be able to explain "which business decisions you turned into screens."

An example learning sequence is as follows.

  • Learn the basics of ontologies, Workshop, and Actions in order using Palantir's official learning site (Palantir Learn) and official documentation
  • If an environment for individuals or developers is available, use a familiar business process (e.g., inquiry management) as a subject and build three screens: a list, a detail view, and a status update
  • Write up the app you built, covering "which decisions it supports and which operations it reduced"
  • If you take the official certification, check the current scope and exam requirements on the official website at that time

Developer environments and certification content can change, so always check the official information at the time you start.

In a resume or interview, simply writing "I can use Workshop" won't convey much. If you can explain it in connection with business change, such as "I consolidated exception handling by field staff, from a list view through to assignment, into a single screen," your practical experience as an FDE comes through. For decision-makers at companies considering FDE adoption, this "ability to translate business into screens" is also the key point for evaluating talent, more than tool proficiency.
