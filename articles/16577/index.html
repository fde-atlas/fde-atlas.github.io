FDE Project Kickoff Document Template: Aligning the Client, Your Boss, and Yourself on One Page

Original title: FDE 项目启动文档模板：一页纸对齐客户、老板和自己

FDE中国 · 2026-09-04 · 4 min read

Summary

FDEChina's editorial team shares a one-page project kickoff document template for FDE projects, designed to align three audiences at once: the customer confirming you understood the problem, your manager confirming resources are justified, and yourself months later. The template has an info table plus nine sections. The two most valuable fields are explicit non-goals, which prevent scope creep, and success criteria with concrete measurement methods, which reduce acceptance disputes. The one-page limit forces prioritization; if you can't fill the goals section, the problem is upstream customer research, not the document. After sending it, schedule a 30-minute alignment meeting, and maintain it as the single source of truth via version numbers.

Key points

  • Template has an info table plus nine sections, hard-capped at one page.
  • Non-goals listed with reasons prevent scope creep by week three.
  • Success criteria need metric, tester, and timing to avoid disputes.
  • If goals section is hard to fill, go back and redo customer interviews.
  • Update version numbers on the same doc rather than starting new ones.

Full text

The problem this piece addresses: at project kickoff, the client, your boss, and you each have your own mental picture of "what to build and to what standard," and these only get reconciled at acceptance. The most valuable takeaways are the two fields most often glossed over in the template: "explicitly out of scope" and "success criteria" — the former determines whether scope creep starts in week three, the latter determines whether acceptance turns into an argument. The most common misuse is turning it into an all-encompassing project management document: the more fields you fill in, the less anyone reads it — the "one page" constraint exists precisely to force you to make trade-offs before writing. Whereas the in-site content on interviews and expectation management covers the process, this piece provides the process's landing point — a written artifact you can send out directly to align everyone. In the Chinese context, one extra consideration: this document often needs to help the client's contact relay it internally, so write it so the other side can quote it wholesale. After reading, pick a project that is starting or has started without alignment, fill out the template, send it to the client's key contact, and schedule a thirty-minute alignment meeting. — FDEChina Editorial Team · Practitioner Series

The kickoff document is the highest-ROI artifact in an FDE project: two hours of writing buys you an anchor for the entire delivery cycle to "pull out and check whenever something goes wrong." It has three audiences — the client needs to confirm you understood correctly, your boss needs to confirm the resources were well spent, and you need to remember three months later why you designed it this way. The template consists of one info table plus eight sections, with a hard constraint of one page: if you exceed it, first cut adjectives, then merge sections, and the final warning sign is "scope not yet thought through." It takes the notes from client interviews as input, keeps its boundary items consistent with client expectation management, and its acceptance criteria section maps item by item to the PoC acceptance checklist.

The Template

I. Basic Information

FieldContent
Project name(a name the client can understand; avoid internal codenames)
Client(organization/department, business contact and technical contact)
Our team(project manager, FDE, supporting roles)
Project timeline(kickoff date / PoC end date / final acceptance date)
Document version(v1 with date; bump the version number on every change)

II. Background and Problem

  • Business background: what situation the client is in and why it needs solving now (within three sentences).
  • Problem list: ordered by priority, each item including the scenario where it occurs and its quantified cost, sourced from interview notes.
  • Previous attempts: how the client has dealt with this problem before and why it wasn't enough.

III. Goals and Non-Goals

  • Goals: 2–3 items to achieve this phase, one sentence each, verifiable.
  • Explicitly out of scope: list item by item with reasons (e.g., "handwritten receipts not covered — about 10% of volume and recognition cost too high").
  • Directions after this phase: possible extensions for the next phase, for outlook only, not commitments.

IV. Success Criteria

  • Business criteria: for the core problems on the problem list, to what degree they are solved (e.g., how the reduction in labor for that step will be determined).
  • Technical criteria: core-scenario task success rate reaches the agreed value, with latency and per-item cost within agreed ranges (methodology per the eval baseline document).
  • Verification method: who tests, with what, and when — each item reproducible at acceptance.

V. Milestone Plan

MilestoneCompletion criteria (verifiable)TimeDependencies
M1 Environment and data ready(e.g., client sample data ingested and passed validation)(date)(client provides X)
M2 First usable version(e.g., core scenario meets the bar on N samples)(date)(none)
M3 PoC acceptance(passes the acceptance checklist item by item)(date)(M2)

VI. Roles and Responsibilities

  • Our side: who owns the solution, who owns delivery, who is accountable for commitments to the client (one name per responsibility).
  • Client side: who provides data, who participates in acceptance, who holds veto power over scope changes.
  • Points of contact: who handles day-to-day communication, who receives change requests.

VII. Dependencies and Risks

  • Key dependencies: data, accounts, environments, and staffing the client must provide on time, each with an owner and date.
  • Major risks: each risk with impact and mitigation (e.g., "insufficient sample size; mitigation: adjust success criteria based on reality at the week-two review").
  • Assumptions: premises on which this plan depends, listed item by item (e.g., "the 300 client-provided samples are representative").

VIII. Communication Mechanism

  • Meeting cadence: frequency, duration, attendees, standing agenda.
  • Progress updates: frequency, recipients, contents.
  • Bad-news protocol: for anything affecting schedule or metrics, within how many hours to proactively report and to whom.

IX. Acceptance Criteria

  • Acceptance basis: the success criteria in Section IV of this document, mapped item by item.
  • Acceptance process: timing of the pre-acceptance calibration meeting, format of formal acceptance, how disputed items are classified and handled.
  • Deliverables list: system, documentation, eval sets and scripts, handover materials, each listed explicitly.

Usage Notes

Fill it out hard-first: complete "Goals and Non-Goals" and "Success Criteria" before the rest. If you can fill these two sections, the problem list from the interview phase is solid; if you can't, go back and do more interviews rather than forcing the document. The three most common filling mistakes: goals written as vision ("improve the intelligence of customer service" is unverifiable; "ticket classification accuracy reaches the agreed value" is a goal); leaving non-goals blank or writing "to be discussed later" (blank non-goals hand scope decisions to the client's whims); success criteria lacking a verification method (how much, who tests, when — missing any one means an extra round of arguing at acceptance). The timeline and milestone dates in the info table must be checked against internal scheduling before sending to the client; changing dates after the document goes out erodes trust for next time.

This document's value is half in the writing, half in the alignment. After sending it to the client, don't wait passively for feedback — schedule a thirty-minute alignment meeting, go through it section by section, focusing on non-goals and success criteria. Every client objection to a non-goal in that meeting is a scope risk surfaced early, ten times cheaper than hitting it at acceptance. After finalizing, bump the version number and archive; from then on, every scope change bumps the version on this document rather than starting a new one, keeping it the single source of truth. During delivery it also serves as the reference frame for measurement and retrospectives: when metrics don't match, revisit Section IV; when there's a schedule dispute, revisit Section V; when the project ends, write the retrospective against it. Used together with delivery quality metrics, every commitment on this page gets a corresponding number tracked; teams can include it in the handover items of the project handover checklist, ensuring the next person inherits an original agreement signed off by both sides.
