Party A and Party B: Who Decides Goals, Authority, and Outcomes

Original title: 甲乙双方：谁决定目标、权限与结果

pafa.github.io · 2025-09-01 · 8 min read

Summary

Using a teaching pilot at an equipment maintenance company, this chapter argues that "shared responsibility" between customer and vendor fails when no one can actually make decisions. It proposes assigning five types of work—setting goals, validating real usage, building the system, managing data and permissions, and running operations—to named decision-makers, especially on the customer side. It distinguishes three classes of decisions (reversible engineering choices, commitment-changing choices, and major decisions), advocates short decision records over lengthy status reports, and explains how to handle leadership changes, product teams declining requests, and multi-party boundaries. The core conclusion: someone must explain technical conditions, accept business costs, and provide resources, or even excellent field engineers end up covering for organizational indecision.

Key points

  • Every decision needs a named owner; customer-side accountability is most often missing.
  • Five work types: set goals, validate usage, build systems, manage data, run operations.
  • Separate reversible choices, commitment-changing choices, and major decisions.
  • Technical pass, business acceptance, and next-phase approval are distinct gates.
  • Leadership changes don't void prior authorizations; historical decisions keep their original makers.

When a project collaboration goes smoothly, people tend to treat "joint responsibility" as a sufficient arrangement. Only when the client asks to open up more data, the vendor's product refuses an ad-hoc change, or a manager is suddenly replaced does everyone realize: joint responsibility does not say who can make decisions.

This chapter still uses a teaching pilot at an equipment maintenance company. Repair request materials are scattered across emails, equipment records, and work orders; the system organizes suggestions, and the maintenance supervisor decides how to dispatch jobs; the scope is limited for now to one type of equipment and one service area. The problem is not drawing another reporting chart, but making sure a specific decision can find an owner.

First, Match Each Decision to a Person

If you are assembling a team, you can shift the discussion from "how many FDEs do we need" to five more concrete questions. Who works with users to determine which work should change? Who turns the problem into a working system? Who has the authority to accept the business results after go-live? Who maintains the general-purpose product? Who continues to carry operational responsibility after the project ends? One person can hold several of these roles, but each one needs a person who can truly make the decision.

The client side is especially easy to overlook. The vendor sending excellent engineers does not mean the client already has someone who can approve data access, coordinate schedules, change work processes, or decide to retire the old system. If these decisions still require hunting for an owner layer by layer on the fly, adding engineers will only mean more people waiting together.

The business owner must clarify what "improvement" means. Is it reducing time spent searching for information, lowering the mis-dispatch rate, or detecting equipment likely to fail earlier? If the three goals conflict, he must take part in the trade-offs. For example, more risk alerts may reduce missed inspections but also increase the supervisor's review burden. This choice cannot silently fall on the engineer who tunes the prompts.

The frontline representative needs to provide real materials and actually try out the changed steps. He cannot merely attend the kickoff meeting. If the placement of suggestions interrupts work that used to flow smoothly, or a recommendation lacks a field the supervisor checks every day, these problems need to be spotted while they can still be fixed. The frontline representative's time is a project investment, not a favor after hours.

The FDE carries the cross-system engineering work: confirming how data arrives, translating business rules into verifiable processing steps, locating errors, and turning user feedback into the next change. He does not have to build every connector himself—the software that lets different systems exchange data—but he must be able to trace which steps a repair request passes through and know where a problem gets stuck.

The client's data and systems owner manages access, field definitions, and changes to existing systems. When the meaning of an equipment ID changes, you cannot expect the FDE to absorb it forever through an ever more complex mapping table. Someone must revise the rules at the source and explain whether other applications will be affected.

Finally, there must be an operations owner. What he participates in should not be just handover training; from the start of the pilot he should understand how to detect anomalies, how to roll back to the original process, and how to escalate for help. Otherwise, the day the system goes live may also be the day the organization first realizes that "who is on duty" was never decided.

This table is a tool for discussing responsibility, not a fixed org chart. Working together does not prevent each decision from having a clear owner; someone offering advice does not mean he has been granted decision-making authority.

The UK government's project delivery standard requires that management arrangements specify the activities, deliverables, or outcomes each role is accountable for, and to whom they answer. 1 What is borrowed here is the principle that accountability must be traceable, not a copy of government job titles. Companies can use their own titles, but they cannot leave gaps with no owner among the client side, the platform side, the implementation partner, and field engineers.

The Project Lead Is Not a Proxy for Every Decision

Cross-functional work needs someone who can push things forward. But "pushing" does not mean deciding for others. The technical lead can judge whether an interface design is sound, but cannot decide for the client whether a certain type of data should be used; the business owner can choose the pilot scope, but cannot demand that engineers push a fault known to cause duplicate dispatches into production.

The team is best off separating three types of decisions. The first is reversible, everyday engineering choices, such as how logs are displayed or how internal modules are divided; these are handled directly by the owner. The second changes delivery commitments, such as adding an equipment category, extending retention, or integrating another core system; their impact must be brought back to the business and technical leads for a joint judgment. The third involves major consequences or regulatory requirements, and requires a decision from someone with the corresponding authority.

Separating them is not about adding meetings but about reducing ad-hoc arguments. If every choice goes to a committee, even small changes stall; if everything is left to individuals on the ground, the team accumulates undiscussed commitments in the name of agility. Good boundaries let most daily work proceed autonomously while ensuring the few important choices are seen in time.

A short one-page record can capture these choices: what changed, what options exist, who is affected, who decides, and when to review. It comes closer to what management needs than a long progress report cc'd to everyone daily. The purpose of the record is for the next person taking over to understand why things were done that way at the time, not to prove how many meetings the team held.

Suppose the maintenance supervisor asks the system to dispatch jobs fully automatically. The team can handle this teaching conflict as follows. The FDE first documents which work orders the new action affects, under what conditions duplicate dispatches would occur, and whether it can be rolled back; the client systems owner confirms write permissions and recovery conditions. The two of them are responsible for clarifying the technical conditions, not for accepting, on the supervisor's behalf, the business losses caused by mis-dispatches.

At the start of the project, an agreement can be set: ordinary scope changes are decided by the business owner on the next business day—accept, narrow, or abandon; matters that cross departments and exceed one's authority go to the project sponsor, that is, the client leader with resource allocation authority. This deadline is an example agreement; major incidents still follow the project's emergency procedures. When the rollback conditions for automatic dispatching are not yet met, the technical lead can pause that action while the existing manual-confirmation pilot continues. The business owner can only choose options whose conditions are met; a signature cannot turn a known defect into technical feasibility.

Suppose the core product team again refuses to add a rollback interface on short notice. The project sponsor must ultimately choose among increasing investment, delaying automatic dispatching, or keeping manual confirmation, and adjust delivery commitments accordingly. The FDE does not need to make both sides happy before leaving the meeting; he needs to make sure the decision, the owner, and the unmet conditions all land in the record. When there is no decision, the original scope remains in effect, and the new feature is not automatically approved just because the wait has dragged on.

When the Owner Changes, What to Check First

Now change one more condition in this teaching pilot: the maintenance supervisor is transferred and a new owner takes over. Must the original pilot be re-approved in full, and can automatic dispatching be opened along the way? First check against the existing records: whether the original scope has changed and who inherits it, who confirms the budget and staff time for the next phase, and who has the authority to pause or terminate the pilot.

A change of personnel does not by itself void all prior authorizations. A manual-confirmation pilot whose scope authorization, resources, and emergency shutdown arrangements remain valid can continue; whichever action is affected by a condition that cannot be confirmed is paused first, and the corresponding authority holder decides. Automatic dispatching must still satisfy the original technical and authorization conditions; a handover procedure cannot conjure up the missing rollback interface.

The handover should be communicated to the other party as well. Technical constraints the previous owner accepted, unapproved scope, and pending conditions should all pass to the successor along with the records. Historical decisions keep their original makers, and current responsibility is assigned to the successor; records must not be rewritten for convenience of contact to say the new owner "had already agreed at the time."

Between Product, Partners, and the Field, Decisions Also Need an Outlet

Back to the same automatic dispatching request. The FDE should hand a clearly identified product contact the reproducible duplicate-dispatch problem, the range of affected work orders, and the remedies already tried. The product owner arranges an evaluation and states whether the interface change will enter a maintained version and when a decision can be expected. Day-to-day communication can proceed directly; trade-offs involving original commitments go back to the responsible parties agreed earlier.

Even if a request does not enter the product for now, the project still needs to know who will continue maintaining the existing manual confirmation approach. "The product has not accepted it" must not be treated as a permanent on-site fallback, nor should the customer be promised a capability that has not yet been scheduled. The product owner answers whether the platform will take it on, the project lead answers what the current contract and resources can support, and the customer lead decides whether to accept the corresponding scope.

If industry partners are also involved, the boundaries need to go one step further. A partner may be able to modify connectors, but cannot change the platform's permission mechanisms; a platform engineer can explain the mechanisms, but has no authority to change how the customer's data is used. When a cross-boundary failure occurs, first preserve the request, status, and evidence, and have the technical leads designated by both parties confirm the next handler; do not simply forward the ticket back and forth until frontline staff bypass the system.

Who leads the coordination can be decided based on the contract and actual capabilities. But the coordinator should be able to convene the relevant people, propose options, and hand trade-offs beyond their authority to those empowered to decide. They cannot cover up others' lack of response by taking on all the work themselves.

Someone submits a decidable question, someone gives a clear reply, and someone is accountable for what is temporarily deferred. When Every Customer Is Different, How Does the Product Grow discusses further which capabilities are worth becoming public products; this chapter first ensures that when the product rejects or defers a request, the project still has an executable decision.

Do not write technical approval, business acceptance, and next-phase authorization as one and the same thing

Consider the maintenance pilot again. Engineering checks can provide evidence of whether the system repeats its behavior under the tested conditions; only frontline trial use can show whether supervisors can more easily find supporting grounds; the business owner must also judge whether the review burden and the cost of misassignment are worth it. The three need each other, but none can substitute for the other two.

Likewise, Phase 1 acceptance does not automatically authorize onboarding the next region. Verify the new region's data scope, local users, resources, and operating hours; if it is only routine maintenance within the original scope, there is no need to return to the project-initiation level every time. Only by separating "continuing approved work" from "expanding commitments" can the team balance speed and boundaries.

A usable decision record can be very short. For an automatic dispatch request, record that manual confirmation is still in use; that the automated action is suspended because the rollback conditions are insufficient; that the customer's system owner confirms the write and recovery conditions, the product owner decides the interface approach, and the business owner chooses the next scope among feasible options; also state what evidence is needed to reopen the discussion. It is not an abbreviation of a progress report, but the basis for the next person to act.

What both parties most need to agree on is not that disagreements will never occur, but that when they do, who explains the technical conditions, who accepts the business cost, and who provides the resources. Without these decisions, no matter how excellent the on-site engineers are, they can only keep covering the organization's waiting on its behalf.
