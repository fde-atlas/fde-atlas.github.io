How to Become an FDE? Career Paths, Skills, and Salaries

Original title: 如何成为 FDE？职业路径、技能与薪资

FDE百科 · 2026-07-26 · 7 min read

Summary

This career guide argues that FDE is not a traditional job you apply for—especially in China—but a capability intersection of industry depth, hands-on AI tooling, and end-to-end on-site delivery. It breaks down how FDEs spend their time, the three skill circles, four transition paths (from engineering, consulting, product management, and entrepreneurship), plus salary data and learning resources for 2026.

Key points

  • Entry is proven problem-solving, not degrees or certifications.
  • Prototyping takes ~30% of the work; coding is increasingly AI-assisted via Cursor and Claude Code.
  • Four transition paths exist, each with different gaps to fill.
  • US FDE salaries range $120k-$350k (up to $550k+ with equity); China uses project-based and retainer models.
  • As AI tools get cheaper, scarce skills shift to problem definition, evals, and driving adoption.

Full text

FDE (Forward Deployed Engineer) is not a traditional role you can simply "apply" for—at least not yet in China. It's more like a combination of capabilities: industry depth × hands-on AI tool skills × end-to-end on-site delivery. You don't need a specific degree, major, or certification, but you do need to prove in a real business environment that you can find the problem, build the system, work out the numbers, and get the business running on its own.

The ticket into FDE isn't a degree—it's what real problems you've solved.

Before rushing to figure out how to become an FDE, first look at what an FDE actually does every day—and confirm this is the way you want to work.

PhaseTypical workShare
On-site diagnosisObserving processes with business teams, identifying pain points, mapping the current data landscape, picking the scenarios to tackle first15%
Solution designTechnology selection, architecture design, defining acceptance criteria, estimating ROI, writing a brief proposal15%
Prototype buildingUsing AI tools to build working prototypes, integrating with enterprise systems, designing permissions and evals frameworks30%
Launch follow-throughDriving adoption by business departments, collecting feedback, iterating and improving, tracking data changes20%
Knowledge consolidationWriting SOPs, training internal teams, building knowledge bases, ensuring the business can run on its own after you leave20%

Note two things:

  • Writing code is only part of prototype building, and by 2026 much of the coding can be done with AI programming tools (Cursor, Claude Code). An FDE's core value isn't coding speed—it's knowing what to build, why, and for whom.
  • Communication and coordination take up a lot of time. You need to align requirements with business stakeholders, interfaces with IT, expectations with management, and ROI with finance. If you hate dealing with people, FDE may not be for you.

An FDE's capabilities aren't a single skill tree—they're the intersection of three circles. You need all three, but the proportions vary by person:

  • AI programming: proficiently using AI programming tools like Cursor and Claude Code to build working prototypes
  • Agent design: understanding agent permission models, tool calling, evals frameworks, and release gates
  • System integration: connecting AI safely to existing enterprise systems (CRM, ERP, databases), familiarity with protocols like MCP
  • RAG / knowledge bases: building enterprise-grade knowledge Q&A systems, understanding the key factors in retrieval quality
  • Evals engineering: defining acceptance criteria—tool selection accuracy, continuous success rates, silent failure detection
  • Industry diagnosis: quickly understanding an industry's core objects, processes, and pain points
  • Scenario identification: finding the scenario that should be tackled first among many "we want AI" requests
  • ROI calculation: translating "hours saved" and "output gained" into financially auditable numbers
  • Change management: understanding organizational resistance and knowing how to get employees to actually use new systems
  • Lighthouse project strategy: choosing the right first scenario, delivering measurable results, then replicating and scaling
  • Stakeholder coordination: keeping business stakeholders, IT, management, and finance aligned on progress and expectations
  • Knowledge consolidation: turning each project's experience into SOPs, knowledge bases, and training materials
  • Continuous iteration: tracking usage data after launch and turning complaints and failures into improvements

Counterintuitive judgment: the cheaper AI tools get, the more valuable FDEs become. When the technical barrier to building agents drops to the point anyone can learn it, what's scarce is no longer technical operation, but the ability to understand the enterprise on the ground, define the real problem, design acceptance criteria, and drive organizational adoption—that's exactly the value of the business layer and the delivery layer.

FDE is not a career you start from zero. It's more like a crossroads—people arriving from different directions can all get there by filling in different gaps.

Path A

Transitioning from a technical engineer

Already have: coding skills, system architecture experience, judgment in technology selection

Need to add: business diagnosis skills (how to find the real problem), communication and coordination (how to align with non-technical people), ROI thinking (how to persuade management in financial language)

Advice: find a real enterprise AI project and get involved from the requirements discovery stage (rather than waiting for someone else to write the requirements doc before stepping in). Proactively attend business stakeholders' meetings and learn to describe problems in their language.

Path B

Transitioning from a consultant / implementation consultant

Already have: client communication, requirements analysis, project management, industry knowledge

Need to add: hands-on AI tool skills (able to build prototypes yourself, not just write slides), judgment in technology selection (knowing which technical approaches are viable and which are traps)

Advice: spend 2-4 weeks intensively learning an AI programming tool (Cursor or Claude Code recommended), then use it to solve a real problem you've seen in a consulting project. Your consulting experience gives you business understanding—exactly what people from a purely technical background lack most.

Path C

Transitioning from a product manager

Already have: requirements analysis, user understanding, cross-department coordination, product thinking

Need to add: hands-on technical skills (not just drawing wireframes—being able to actually build it), end-to-end delivery mindset (not stopping at launch—following through until the business sees results)

Advice: the advantage of product managers moving into FDE is that you're used to thinking from the user's perspective; the disadvantage is that you may be used to "specifying requirements and letting others do the work." FDE requires you to do it yourself. Use an AI programming tool to directly build a product requirement you've defined, and experience the full process from definition to delivery.

Path D

Transitioning from an entrepreneur / super individual

Already have: business instinct, resource integration, results orientation, resilience under pressure

Need to add: systematic technical understanding (not just knowing how to use tools—understanding architecture and boundaries), methodological consolidation (turning experience from "intuition" into "reusable frameworks")

Advice: your biggest advantage is that you're naturally accountable for results—this is the most core FDE mindset. Reorganize the "helping businesses solve AI problems" work you're already doing using the FDE's five on-site gates framework, and you may already be an FDE.

The fundamental reason for the high pay: this role requires the intersection of three things—technical depth, business understanding, and end-to-end delivery. Many people possess any one of them; very few possess all three. Scarcity = premium.

But the most effective learning path is always: pick a real enterprise problem and walk through all five on-site stages from start to finish (pain point stage → entry point stage → prototype stage → ROI stage → self-sustaining stage). Certification proves you've learned; delivery proves you can do it.

FDE is not a linear career ladder but a capability platform that can develop in multiple directions:

In July 2026, Microsoft established Frontier Company with $2.5 billion and 6,000 experts—meaning large platform companies are upgrading FDE from "individual heroes" to "organizational infrastructure." In the coming years, FDE demand will shift from a "Silicon Valley niche" to an "enterprise standard."

Honesty is more valuable than encouragement. In the following three situations, we recommend not pursuing FDE for now:

  • People unwilling to go on-site. The F in FDE stands for Forward Deployed—"forward deployment" means going to the customer and facing real-world messiness. If you prefer remote, standardized, predictable work, traditional technical roles suit you better.
  • People who only want to do technology and avoid business. FDEs spend a lot of time listening to requirements, aligning expectations, and handling organizational resistance. If you think "dealing with people is a waste of time," you'll be miserable.
  • People who seek certainty and stability. The norm for FDE projects is vague requirements, messy data, and strong organizational resistance. You need to find direction amid uncertainty. If you need a clear requirements document before you can start working, begin as an implementation consultant instead.

No specific degree required. The barrier to entry for FDE is not credentials but the intersection of three capabilities: deep industry experience, hands-on AI tool skills, and willingness to collaborate on-site. Computer science, engineering, and business backgrounds all work—the key is whether you can solve problems in a real business environment.

Silicon Valley FDEs earn $150K-350K annually (up to $500K+ including stock options), one of the highest-paying roles in AI. The Chinese market is mainly project-based and coaching-based, ranging from RMB 30K-500K per project. The fundamental reason for the high pay is that this role requires the intersection of technical depth, business understanding, and end-to-end delivery—and that intersection is extremely scarce.

Engineering ability is required, but in 2026 engineering ability doesn't mean hand-writing code. If you can build a usable prototype with AI coding tools like Cursor or Claude Code, make sound technical selection judgments, and design an AI agent's permissions and evals system, you meet the bar. The core is being able to turn "business requirements" into "a working system."

As of 2026 there are two official channels: the Shanghai Institute for Advanced Study's FDE Forward Deployed Engineer Advanced Program, and Anthropic's four-role certification system. The OpenAI Partner Network aims to train 300,000 certified consultants by year-end. But the most effective learning path remains: pick a real enterprise problem and work through it from start to finish.

FDEs communicate frequently with business stakeholders, but an extroverted personality isn't required. The key capability is listening and understanding—hearing the other party's real pain points, not performing sociability. Many excellent FDEs come from introverted technical backgrounds; their strength lies in deep thinking and patient, systematic capability.
