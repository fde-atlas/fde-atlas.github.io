Who should you consult about AI adoption? How to choose among 4 types of support companies

Original title: AI導入は誰に相談すべき？支援会社4タイプの選び方

株式会社Sai · 2026-07-05 · 8 min read

Summary

This article by Japan's SAI categorizes AI adoption consulting partners into four types—strategy consulting firms, SIers/custom developers, tool vendors, and hands-on accompaniment support (akin to Palantir's Forward Deployed Engineer model)—and argues the key to choosing is identifying each partner's final deliverable: documents, systems, tool licenses, or working operations plus an organization that can run independently. It describes common failure patterns (consultant-to-SIer handoff gaps, tool-first projects, endless PoC loops), offers five vetting questions, cites Japanese government and IPA survey data, and explains when companies can proceed without external help.

Key points

  • Four partner types are distinguished by deliverables: strategy docs, built systems, tool licenses, or working operations.
  • Hands-on support resembles Palantir's FDE model, embedding engineers on-site from problem identification to adoption.
  • Failure patterns include consultant-SIer handoff gaps, tool-first proposals, and PoCs that never reach production.
  • Five vetting questions: deliverable, who does the work, what remains in-house, failure cases, and starting small.
  • Companies can self-implement only when target workflows and in-house validators are both clearly defined.

"We want to transform our operations with AI, but we don't know who to consult"—this is one of the most common questions we receive. A quick search turns up countless options: consulting firms, SIers, AI startups, tool vendors... Yet surprisingly few people know that what each offers is completely different.

Choosing the wrong consulting partner is a classic failure pattern that costs you both time and budget. In this article, we organize consulting providers into four types and explain how to identify the right partner for your company.

Four Types of Consulting Providers—Distinguish Them by "Deliverables"

AI adoption consulting providers fall into four types: strategy consulting firms, SIers/contract development companies, tool vendors, and hands-on support. The criterion for distinguishing them is what the company ultimately hands over.

Many companies stall before even reaching this point. According to the Ministry of Internal Affairs and Communications' 2026 (Reiwa 8) Information and Communications White Paper, 58.1% of Japanese SMEs said they plan to use generative AI (Ministry of Internal Affairs and Communications, "2026 Information and Communications White Paper," policies on generative AI use, FY2025 corporate survey). Large enterprises stood at 74.0%, leaving a gap by company size.

Type 1: Strategy Consulting Firms

Deliverable: Strategy and plans (documents)

Their strengths are current-state analysis, company-wide AI strategy, and roadmap development. They are powerful for building consensus among management and creating materials for large-scale investment decisions, but they do not implement, as a rule. The typical gap is "we produced an impressive strategy document, but no one can build it."

Type 2: SIers and Contract Development Companies

Deliverable: A system built exactly as ordered

If requirements are clear, they will build the system reliably. However, their basic mode of operation is "build what you're told," and requirements definition is the client's responsibility. In areas like AI adoption, where "what should be built changes as you go," repeated estimates and change procedures tend to become speed bottlenecks.

Type 3: Tool Vendors

Deliverable: Licenses for their own tools and implementation support

Given their proposal structure of "our tool can solve this," you need to be aware that the tool is decided before the problem. If the problem fits the tool, it's the fastest and cheapest option, but forcing an ill-fitting problem onto the tool leaves behind a system the field never uses.

Type 4: Hands-On Support

Deliverable: Working operations and a self-sustaining organization

In this model, engineers and consultants embed in the field and work alongside you from problem identification through implementation and operational adoption. In the US, this is called an FDE (Forward Deployed Engineer). US-based Palantir positions this role as one it pioneered, describing it as a way of working where talented engineers are assigned directly to customers to tackle their most pressing problems head-on (Palantir official job posting, Forward Deployed Software Engineer, verified September 9, 2026). Its strength is that there is no divide between strategy and implementation, and it can extend all the way to in-house capability building, but it is not suited to brute-force efforts that throw large numbers of people at a problem all at once.

Failure-Prone Combination Patterns

Pattern 1: Failed relay from strategy consultant to SIer

When a consultant's vision is ordered from an SIer, the response is "the requirements are vague," along with a substantially higher estimate. Without a translator between vision and implementation, this gap can never be closed.

Pattern 2: Tool implementation becomes an end in itself

"We introduced an AI tool, but no one uses it"—this is the case where a tool was contracted before the problem was even broken down. As explained in Automating operations with generative AI, adoption requires embedding into workflows and developing internal champions. The concern Japanese companies cited most often about generative AI adoption was "we don't know how to use it effectively" (Ministry of Internal Affairs and Communications, "2025 (Reiwa 7) Information and Communications White Paper," current state of AI use in companies, published July 2025 / FY2024 survey). The structure where tools keep accumulating while their purpose remains undecided arises from this.

Pattern 3: The infinite PoC loop

Repeating "let's start with a proof of concept" and never reaching production—a PoC that never ends. The cause is failing to define "conditions for going to production" at the experiment design stage.

Five Questions You Must Ask When Selecting a Partner

In your sales meetings, ask the following five questions. The quality of the answers reveals a great deal about the partner's true capability and integrity.

  • "What is your deliverable?"—A document, a system, or working operations? Confirm it matches what your company actually wants
  • "Who will do the hands-on work?"—It's common for the people proposing and the people executing to be different. Verify the skills of the people who will actually come to your site
  • "What will remain inside our company?"—Just a system, or the skills and structure to use it well?
  • "Tell us about a case that didn't go well"—A partner who can speak to lessons learned from failure is more trustworthy than one who can only talk about success stories
  • "Can we start small?"—Do they demand a large contract from the start, or do they design for building trust with small wins before expanding?

Guidelines for Choosing the Right Consulting Provider for Your Company

  • You need management consensus or decisions on large-scale investment → Strategy consulting firm
  • Requirements are fixed and you want it built reliably → SIer or development company
  • Your problem clearly matches a tool's coverage → Tool vendor
  • You want to consult on where to even start / you want to run things in-house after the build → Hands-on support

Combining multiple types is also effective, but in that case, securing a "translator" who bridges vision and implementation is what determines success or failure.

Companies that can proceed in-house without outside help are those where the tasks to use AI and the people to verify results are already decided

Some companies can proceed on their own without relying on any of the four types. The conditions are two: the tasks where AI will be used are decided, and someone inside the company can verify the results. If both are in place, the remaining work centers on choosing and configuring tools.

Our own service pages draw a similar line. In the FAQ on our AI adoption support, we state that tasks that can be handled by simply using an off-the-shelf SaaS are not a good fit for our support. Our policy is that in such cases, tool selection and configuration alone are enough.

Whether you can proceed in-house can be checked with the following two questions.

  • Can you name the tasks where AI will be used? — Can you say which tasks it will be used for, such as "first-line handling of inquiries" or "drafting documents"? If you are still at the stage of "using generative AI across the company," it is not yet decided.
  • Is there someone assigned to verify the results? — For each task, is there someone who decides whether to use the AI output as is, fix it, or reject it? This is a role separate from the person distributing the tools.

If the answer to both is yes, you can start trialing tools before seeking outside support. Before trialing, decide for each task what information may be entered into external services. How to make those decisions is summarized in How to create internal rules for generative AI use.

In IPA's 2026 survey, "lack of specialized personnel" was the most commonly cited challenge in AI adoption and operation, at 50.1%. Next were "insufficient understanding of the effects and risks of generative AI" (45.8%) and "difficulty creating rules and standards for managing appropriate use" (39.7%) (IPA "DX Trends 2026" (PDF) / accessed October 21, 2026). The third item, rule-making, corresponds to the pre-trial line-drawing mentioned above.

If either of the two is missing, there is value in seeking outside help. If you cannot decide which tasks to use AI for, start with task inventory first. If there is no one to verify results, choose a model that develops verifiers in-house, rather than having an external party verify things indefinitely. How to divide roles in companies without an IT department is covered in AI adoption for companies without an IT department. Even when relying on external help, the proper course is to return to in-house operation once both conditions are met.

SAI's perspective: First, find your "first point of contact for consultation"

We, SAI Inc., belong to the accompanying type (Type 4). As our vision states, "Become the first point of contact for consultation." We believe that being consulted at the stage of "what should we do in the first place," before deciding on a vendor, is actually when we can deliver the most value.

An engineer joins from the sales meeting onward, validates with a working demo, and builds things to completion in the field, as in our case studies — that is our style. For requests to entrust an entire new business launch to us, we sometimes respond with JISEKI.

Please ask us the five questions above directly as well. For consultations before deciding on a vendor, reach out via Contact.
