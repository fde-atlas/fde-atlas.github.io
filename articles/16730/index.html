Understand the role | FDE Academy

Summary

This first chapter of the FDE Academy explains how a forward deployed engineer should define the role before building anything. The core tool is a one-page charter specifying the actual customer (the team whose daily work changes, often not the contract signer), the sponsor who unblocks access, decision rights, and a named handoff owner. Define the customer, the workflow, and your decision rights.

How it works

A forward deployed engineer writes production code inside a customer's problem. The code is the easy part to describe. The hard part is that nobody hands you a ticket. You arrive at a company with a contract, a sponsor who wants results, and a dozen people who each have a different idea of what you are there to do. Before you build anything, you need to know who the customer actually is, which workflow you are changing, and what you are allowed to decide on your own.

Those three answers form a charter. The customer is the group whose daily work should change, which is often different from the person who signed. The sponsor is the person who can unblock access, approve scope, and defend the project when it slips. Decision rights cover what you can change without asking: the schema you write to, the prompts you ship, the users you pilot with. The handoff owner is the person who will run the thing after you leave. If that name is blank on day one, the project will quietly die on the day you move on.

The role pulls in two directions. Ramp describes FDEs who cover the full customer lifecycle , from sales through implementation and long-term support, so context does not get lost between teams. OpenAI's framing of the role starts from a business outcome and then asks how to make the solution repeatable. Cognition's Nick Wong describes the job of a special projects engineer as finding problems and solving them , which is closer to an internal founder than a consultant. All three share one habit: the engineer owns a result, not a task list.

The common failure is starting with motion. A new FDE wants to show value, so they accept every request, build three half-finished tools, and cannot say at the end which one mattered. The fix is boring: write down one outcome you can measure, record today's number for it, and list what is out of scope. An out-of-scope list feels rude to write. It is the document you will point to in week four when a VP asks for a dashboard.

An experienced FDE decides by asking who will notice if this works. If the answer is a specific team doing a specific job, and you can measure that job today, you have a role. If the answer is "leadership, eventually," keep talking until it is concrete.

Worked example

Hypothetical: suppose you join a logistics company's deployment. The contract says "use AI to improve operations." The VP of Operations signed it. In your first week you learn that 12 dispatchers handle carrier exception emails by hand, and each spends about 2 hours a day on them, so the team spends about 24 hours a day in total.

You write a one-page charter. Customer: the dispatch team. Sponsor: the VP, who controls email and TMS access. Outcome: hours spent on exception handling, baseline 24 per day, measured from a two-week time log. Decision rights: you can change routing rules and draft replies, but a dispatcher approves anything sent to a carrier. Handoff owner: the dispatch team lead. Out of scope: billing disputes, the customer portal, and a general analytics dashboard.

The VP pushes back on excluding the dashboard. You agree to revisit it once exception hours drop below 12 per day, which gives the request a clear trigger instead of an open-ended maybe.

Do

  • Write a one-page role charter: customer, sponsor, authority, and handoff owner.
  • Compare an FDE engagement with an SPE project using one concrete workflow.
  • Name a measurable user outcome and record its current baseline.

You are done when: A charter with one outcome, one owner, and an explicit out-of-scope list.
