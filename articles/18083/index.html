How to Run a Miro Workshop: FDE Requirements Research in Practice

Original title: Miro 工作坊怎么做：FDE 需求调研实战

FDE Times · 2026-11-11 · 5 min read

Summary

This hands-on article explains how forward deployed engineers can run a 90-minute Miro workshop to nail down a client's current process and priorities in one session. It walks through a three-frame structure: a user story map (based on Jeff Patton's method) to chart the workflow, anonymous Private Mode input plus Voting to surface pain points, and a 2x2 priority matrix to pick the first iteration's scope. The author stresses that success is decided the day before: build all frames in advance, write questions on the board, set timers, assign a co-facilitator to operate the board, and rehearse for 20 minutes. A key warning: voting results reflect preferences, not feasibility, so they must pass through the matrix. The article closes by suggesting FDE candidates document workshop outcomes as interview evidence.

Key points

  • A three-frame Miro structure maps the process, votes on pain points, and sets priorities in 90 minutes.
  • Private Mode enables anonymous pain-point input when managers are present.
  • Preparation the day before—frames, board questions, timers, co-facilitator, rehearsal—determines success.
  • Voting shows team preference only; results must be re-evaluated in a 2x2 matrix.
  • Documenting workshop outcomes (pain points collected, first deployment) makes strong FDE interview evidence.

Three frames, one rehearsal, and a colleague to help run the whiteboard: a Forward Deployed Engineer can map out the client's process and agree on priorities in 90 minutes.

Key Takeaways

  • Miro has built-in workshop facilitation tools: Timer, Voting, Private Mode, frame hide/show, and Bring Everyone to Me.
  • The work happens in two steps: first map the process with story mapping, then set priorities with voting and a 2x2 matrix.
  • Rehearse in advance, assign a second person to operate the whiteboard, and write the questions on the board so latecomers can catch up.

Every requirements-gathering meeting hits a moment of silence. Five people from the client show up, each describes the process differently, and the final call usually goes to whoever holds the highest title. A carefully built Miro board avoids this: everyone writes, everyone votes, and everyone watches the same timer.

Forward Deployed Engineers (FDEs) run into this in their first week on-site with a client. You need to figure out two things: what the client's current process looks like, and what they most want to solve first. Email back-and-forth can take weeks; a well-structured workshop settles both in one session.

Can Miro handle the workshop?

Miro launched in 2011 under the name RealtimeBoard. Co-founder and CEO Andrey Khusid built it so his design agency could share ideas with remote clients. That's close to the situation FDEs face today.

Miro's facilitation guide lists several built-in tools: Timer, Bring Everyone to Me (which pulls everyone's screen to where you are on the board), and frame hide/show (which keeps part of the board hidden until it's needed).

Miro recommends using the Timer to cap each activity's duration and remembering to schedule breaks. The timer is visible to everyone, so everyone knows how many minutes remain.

The two most critical features for agreeing on priorities are Voting and Private Mode. According to Miro, Voting lets everyone express opinions simultaneously instead of waiting to raise hands in turn. Private Mode is recommended for sensitive or evaluative activities, because when input is anonymous, people offer more diverse views.

Example: 90 minutes with an invoice-processing team

Suppose you're deploying an agent that reads invoices for a distribution company's finance department. You build three frames the day before.

Frame 1 uses the User Story Mapping template, based on the method Jeff Patton popularized in 2005. Patton defines a story map as a way of telling the story of an entire product or feature.

Here, the "story" is the journey of an invoice: from arriving in the inbox to payment approval. In a timed 25 minutes, everyone places sticky notes under each step, noting where manual handling or waiting happens frequently.

Writing down pain points is an evaluative activity, so Private Mode should be on. Input is anonymous, so even with the department head in the room, employees will still write "matching against purchase orders one by one takes an entire afternoon."

Frame 2 is hidden with the hide/show feature until Frame 1 is done. You group the sticky notes—say, into 12 pain points—then start Voting. The 2021 Voting update lets the facilitator choose which objects on the board can be voted on, and results appear seconds after voting ends.

You select only those 12 pain-point sticky notes, so no one accidentally votes for titles or arrows. Everyone votes simultaneously, so no one waits to see which item the boss picks.

Frame 3 is the 2x2 Prio Matrix template. The horizontal axis is importance; the vertical axis is difficulty. The bottom-right quadrant holds high-impact, low-effort items.

The template guides the team to discuss and place items along the horizontal axis first, then the vertical. You put only the top five or so vote-getters into the matrix. The difficulty axis is where you, as the engineer, have the most authority.

Both sides leave seeing the same picture: the current process, the voted pain points, and one or two items in the bottom-right quadrant to tackle in the first iteration.

Whether the meeting goes smoothly depends on the day before

The example above works because everything was prepared in advance. The day before the meeting, complete five tasks in order.

First, build all three frames and hide frames 2 and 3 with hide/show, so the client doesn't see the next step before finishing the previous one. Second, write each activity's question in large text at the top of its frame. The UK-based dxw team found that writing questions on the board helps latecomers catch up.

Third, plan the agenda with the Timer: 25 minutes for process mapping, one segment for voting, one for the matrix, with a break in the middle as Miro recommends.

Fourth, designate a second person to operate the board. dxw found that remote workshops run more smoothly when someone is dedicated to responding quickly in Miro; they also found the Timer extremely useful.

Finally, rehearse. Miro recommends running through the entire workshop, especially with a co-facilitator. Practicing 20 minutes with a colleague is enough to surface the small mistakes that would cost you credibility in front of the client.

Common mistakes and how to avoid them

Most mistakes in Miro workshops stem not from the software itself but from how it's used. The table below summarizes the most common ones.

MistakeConsequenceHow to avoid it
Treating voting as the decisionThe top-voted item involves a system you don't yet have access toAlways run voting results through the 2x2 matrix again
All objects on the board are votableVotes land on titles and arrows, muddying the resultsWhen enabling Voting, select only the pain-point sticky notes
Open input while the boss is presentEmployees won't write down real pain pointsTurn on Private Mode when collecting pain points and evaluations
Voting session designed too longExceeds the limits of a single Voting sessionCheck the limits of the version you're using
Skipping the rehearsalForgot to grant edit access, or accidentally revealed a hidden frameRun a 20-minute practice with your co-facilitator

The first mistake deserves the most attention. Miro describes Voting as a way to help facilitators quickly and easily gauge team sentiment. Sentiment isn't feasibility, so the matrix step must never be skipped.

As for limits, in the 2021 version, each person gets a maximum of 99 votes, and each voting session lasts at most 99 minutes. These numbers may have changed, so verify again before designing longer activities.

What to learn first?

Start with the Timer and Bring Everyone to Me—these two tools set the pace of the entire meeting. Next, learn how to select objects for Voting, then move on to story mapping. The hardest skill isn't in the software at all: designing a focused enough question for each frame.

From a career perspective, if an FDE job posting you're targeting mentions requirements gathering or direct client engagement, workshops like this are worth putting on your resume. Don't just write "proficient in Miro."

Document the workshop's outcomes: how many pain points were collected, which one was chosen first, and which deployment it ultimately led to. A screenshot of the board with client information removed is powerful evidence in interviews.

Clients won't remember which template you used. They'll remember that, for the first time, everyone agreed on what to do first.
