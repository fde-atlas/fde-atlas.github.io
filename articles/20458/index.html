How FDEs Collaborate with System Integrators (SIs)

Original title: FDE 如何与系统集成商（SI）协作

FDE Times · 2026-11-06 · 6 min read

Summary

This guide explains how vendor FDEs should work alongside system integrators (SIs), a collaboration that is increasingly common, with OpenAI partnering with Accenture and Capgemini and Deloitte embedding FDEs in delivery teams. The core tension is that SIs are measured against a signed SOW while FDEs are accountable for business outcomes in production. Using an insurance claims agent example, the author shows how a project can pass all 20 UAT scenarios yet score only 85% on a 200-case eval set against a 95% target. Recommended practices include a responsibility matrix, shared eval sets as acceptance criteria, weekly platform briefings, and rehearsed operational handover, plus three pitfalls to avoid.

Key points

  • SIs deliver per SOW; FDEs are judged on real business outcomes, leaving a gap in who owns 'done'.
  • A claims agent passed 20/20 UAT but hit only 85% on 200 real cases versus a 95% target.
  • Key tools: a responsibility matrix and eval sets run in CI as shared acceptance criteria.
  • FDEs should run weekly platform briefings and rehearse failure handover with SI engineers.
  • Pitfalls: treating SIs as rivals, silently doing their work, and hoarding platform knowledge.

Key Takeaways

  • Vendor FDEs working alongside SI teams is increasingly common: OpenAI brought in Accenture and Capgemini to work with its FDE team, and Deloitte has also embedded FDEs into its delivery teams.
  • The most fragile link is the definition of "done": the SI is measured against project scope, while the FDE isn't done until business outcomes are running in production.
  • The FDE leads through three things: acceptance criteria measured by evals, regular platform walkthroughs, and a handover rehearsed hands-on with the SI engineers.

You're attending your first kickoff meeting with an enterprise customer and realize you're not the only technical person in the room. Across the table sits a team from Accenture or Deloitte, holding a signed statement of work (SOW), a timeline, and their own project manager. The first question that pops into your head is: so what am I here for?

This situation is increasingly common. On February 23, 2026, when OpenAI launched its Frontier platform, consulting firms such as Accenture and Capgemini joined as partners to work alongside OpenAI's Forward Deployed Engineer (FDE) team. Earlier still, in December 2025, Accenture formed a joint business team with Palantir, built on the foundation of more than 2,000 Accenture employees familiar with Palantir.

As a result, working with systems integrators (SIs) has become part of the FDE's craft, not just a matter for the sales side. Done well, you can lead the technical work without doing everything yourself.

Done poorly, one of two outcomes occurs: you become the SI's ticket-answering help desk, or their adversary in the customer's office.

Two teams, two kinds of "done"

At first glance the division of labor is clear: the SI builds and integrates, and you are the one who knows the platform best. But the two sides are measured against different standards.

According to Webfuse, the scope and methodology of a consulting project are fixed in advance by the SOW, consultants are measured on completion within that scope, and the consulting firm tracks staff utilization.

The FDE, by contrast, is measured on real usage in production and measurable impact on workflows. Entrans, a company that sells FDE services, should be taken as one company's own view. It holds that the work is only done when the system actually delivers the intended business outcomes; success is also not measured by traditional service level agreement (SLA) metrics such as uptime or API latency.

The territory between these two milestones often has no one signing up to own it.

Forgepoint Capital explains why this gap is hard to close with a contract: the quality of AI projects is extremely difficult to write into clauses. It asks: if the two parties cannot even agree on what "good" looks like, how can they agree on the deliverables in an SOW?

This is an opinion, not a measurement. But it explains why the hardest parts to define—evals, agent behavior, and model error handling—often land on you, even if the contract says nothing about them. Salesforce's Nick Johnston puts it bluntly: in the agent era, winning or losing is decided by business outcomes, not pilots.

This doesn't mean the SI is the slow party. In August 2026, Deloitte announced it had embedded FDEs directly into the solution delivery team on a Salesforce project. SD Times also argues that FDEs complement rather than replace traditional consultants, filling in where consultants are weaker. Titles don't matter; what matters is who is accountable for what.

Case study: an agent that processes insurance claims

Suppose the customer is an insurance company that wants an agent to read claim files and recommend approval or escalation to human review.

The SI's SOW defines "done" as: the agent is integrated with the claims management system and passes user acceptance testing (UAT) on 20 scenarios. The customer wants simple claims automated, on the condition that the agent's accuracy is at least 95%.

Do the math. The agent passes all 20/20 UAT scenarios, because they are all happy-path use cases. But on an eval set of 200 real, de-identified claim files, it gets only 170 right—85%. By the contract, the project is finished; the outcome the customer needs has not been reached.

No one here did anything wrong. The SI delivered exactly what was signed in the contract; the contract just measured the wrong thing. Your job is to spot this gap in week one, not on launch day.

The first tool is a responsibility matrix, completed before anyone writes a line of code:

Each row has exactly one primary owner. The second row is where you lead: the eval set becomes the shared acceptance document, and the SI team runs it on every change. That way, contractual "done" and business-outcome "done" become the same thing.

Pointing out the gap without overstepping

How you say the number 85% matters as much as the number itself. The conversation at the weekly meeting can go like this.

You: "UAT has fully passed, and your team's integration work is solid. We just ran 200 real claims again, and the result was 85%, against a business target of 95%. The gap is mainly in the agent's behavior, which is our responsibility."

SI lead: "Will that affect our timeline?"

You: "The integration work is unaffected. I'd only suggest adding these 200 claims to your CI, reporting a score on every build. As we fix the agent, you'll see the results immediately."

This conversation does only three things, in order: affirm the SI's good work, claim ownership of the gap, and propose a shared mechanism—running evals in CI—rather than blaming anyone.

Steps starting from week one

First, ask to read the SI's SOW, or at least the scope and acceptance clauses. Write down in one sentence what "done" means under that document, then compare it with the outcome the customer actually needs. The gap between the two sentences is your work plan.

Next, create the responsibility matrix and have the customer's technical lead confirm it. Once the customer nods, the SI won't feel you've appointed yourself to these roles.

Then, build the eval set with real data and propose it as the shared acceptance criterion. An FDE playbook published on dev.to suggests defining "works" with a measurable number before writing production code. If quality is truly as hard to write into a contract as Forgepoint says, then a runnable eval set is the closest thing to a quality clause.

Afterward, hold a brief weekly platform sync with the SI engineers: cover the platform's limitations, failure behavior, and upcoming changes. This knowledge exists only in your head, and sharing it early often saves both sides days of debugging.

Finally, prepare the handover from day one. The same playbook should describe what the FDE leaves behind as a running system, plus the organization's ability to operate it independently.

If operations will be taken over by the SI, write the operations manual together with them and run a failure drill: the SI engineers handle it while you only observe.

Three traps that make the FDE redundant

The most common trap is treating the SI as an adversary. In the client's office, the two teams rise or fall together. What you should bring into the meeting room is the view that "the FDE complements the consultant."

Another trap is quietly doing the SI's work to keep things on schedule. It's easy to get impatient when progress is slow. But the code you write for them will be something nobody knows how to run the day you leave the project.

The hardest trap to spot is hoarding platform knowledge to make yourself seem indispensable. You're paid this salary so that the client and the SI can run the system on their own, not so they can never do without you.

If you're interviewing for an FDE role at a technology vendor, just ask: how often will you need to collaborate with integrators or consulting partners? The answer will tell you which skills you'll use every day.

Be specific on your resume: which eval suite you turned into a shared acceptance standard, and when the SI team started running the system independently.

The next time an SI is sitting next to you at a kickoff meeting, stop asking yourself why you're there. Instead ask: how does the contract define "done"?
