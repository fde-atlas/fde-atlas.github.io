FDE Costs and Scaling: The Economics of the Forward Deployed Engineer

Original title: FDE 成本与规模化：前线部署工程师的经济账

FDE Times · 2026-10-08 · 4 min read

Summary

This article examines the economics of the FDE model. Senior FDEs at leading AI labs earn $350k–$550k, and companies spent roughly $10 billion building such teams within a year, per investor Tom Tunguz. Unless each deployment makes the next one cheaper, headcount grows linearly with customers and margins drift toward services-company levels. The piece compares four scaling paths: Palantir treating FDEs as the product, Valletta Software's formula of feeding reusable work back into the core product, Constellation Research's vision of productizing until FDEs are unnecessary, and Infosys's headcount-based services approach. It closes with advice for Vietnamese developers: document reusable components and choose roles tied to product teams.

Key points

  • Senior FDE salaries reach $350k–$550k; ~$10B invested in one year.
  • Without reuse, FDE headcount scales linearly with customer count.
  • Palantir treats FDEs as core product, roughly 20% of staff.
  • Constellation envisions product maturity eliminating the need for FDEs.
  • Developers should quantify reusable components delivered, not just projects.

AI companies have invested roughly $10 billion in FDE teams within a year; the engineers who retain their value are those who can turn single-client solutions into reusable assets.

Key Takeaways

  • Top AI labs pay senior FDEs $350,000 to $550,000, which raises a question: does the math still work when you scale this model tenfold?
  • Without reuse, the number of FDEs grows linearly with the customer count, and margins slide toward those of a services company.
  • The most valuable FDEs are the ones who can distill the reusable half of their work and fold it into the core product.

According to investor Tom Tunguz, top AI labs pay senior FDEs between $350,000 and $550,000. He also estimates that AI companies spent about $10 billion on building forward deployed engineering teams within just 12 months.

At roughly $400,000 per FDE per year, Tunguz asks directly: does scaling this model tenfold break the economics that make it work? The answer lies not in how many more people to hire, but in whether each deployment makes the next one cheaper.

For developers who want to move into FDE roles, this is not someone else's financial problem. It determines which kind of FDE commands a high salary and gets to stay once the market cools down: the one who solves problems for a single client, or the one who turns solutions into reusable assets for the whole company.

The straight line is a software company's enemy

Valletta Software puts the problem plainly: unless each deployment makes the next one cheaper, headcount grows linearly with the number of customers. In other words, the FDE model is structurally expensive. The reason is not high salaries, but that the cost must be paid again for every new customer.

Imagine a company with 10 enterprise customers, each requiring a dedicated FDE team. When customers grow to 100, if everything is still built manually from scratch, the company needs ten times the staff. At that point, it is no longer a software company but a consulting firm with a product attached.

That is why the Inside Software newsletter stresses that FDEs only make sense while preserving software-company margins, not sliding into services margins. The same source notes that on large projects, FDE teams can grow to around 8 people.

This number reveals a practical ceiling: you cannot pour unlimited people into a single customer, so growth across customers has to come from somewhere else.

One question, four answers

Palantir's choice is to accept the cost and reframe it. As Tunguz describes it, at Palantir the FDE is not a services layer but the core product itself. Data cited by Inside Software puts FDEs at roughly 20% of the company's employees, though other sources give lower figures.

Valletta Software offers a different formula: as long as the reusable half of field work is fed back into the core product, the model can become a path to scale. Here, the FDE acts as the product team's scout.

Every connector, every data pipeline, and every eval suite written for Customer A faces the same question: does Customer B need it too?

Constellation Research pushes this argument to its conclusion. It holds that the enterprise software vendors who ultimately win will embed what FDEs learn into the product, until customers no longer need FDEs at all. In this view, FDEs exist because today's AI products are still immature.

Meanwhile, services companies are joining in. Infosys's CEO has publicly stated that the company is expanding its Forward Deployed Engineer team. For a services company, linear headcount growth is not a flaw — it is the business model itself.

But that is exactly the line Inside Software warns software companies not to cross.

Except for the pure services model, all three other paths bet on the same mechanism: field work must flow back into the product. The only differences are how fast it flows back, and how many FDE roles remain once it does.

Every shortcut in the field has a cost

Valletta's formula and Constellation's vision both imply a demand for the same capability: in the middle of a messy deployment, being able to tell what only this customer needs from what is a pattern the next customer will hit too.

That capability is harder than it looks. In the field, the pressure comes from hitting deadlines, so the fastest path is always to write custom code, hardcode configurations, and fix data by hand. Every shortcut makes sense that week, but added up, they are exactly what drags the company back onto that straight line.

So if a company pays an FDE $400,000, that money is only worth it if what the person leaves behind is greater than one satisfied customer. Even in Constellation's vision, after the product matures and fewer FDEs are needed, the people who did the productization work are the ones who understand the product and the customers best.

Where should Vietnamese developers place their bets?

Many Vietnamese developers work at outsourcing companies, meaning they are inside a services model priced by headcount. That is not a weakness: they are used to sitting with customers, reading unfamiliar systems, and delivering under pressure. What is often missing is the habit of asking which part of a project should become a product.

Write that habit into your resume. Instead of writing "deployed a system for a banking client," spell out which reusable component you distilled — a connector, an eval test suite, or a deployment template — and how much time it saved in later projects.

Writing it this way shows recruiters that you understand the margin problem, instead of leaving them to guess from a list of projects.

When reading job descriptions or interviewing, pay attention to where the FDE sits in the org chart. If the role is tied to the product team and there is a clear process for folding field feedback into the roadmap, that is a model trying to bend the straight line.

If the role is measured only by the number of projects delivered, then what you are applying for is really a services job with a new name.

The question worth watching is not whether the industry still needs FDEs, but how much each FDE leaves behind in the product after leaving the customer. Aim to be the person who can answer that question with numbers.
