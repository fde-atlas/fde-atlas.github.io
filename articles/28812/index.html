How to Calculate Retention Rates: How FDEs Verify Vendor Data

Original title: 留存率怎么算：FDE 如何核查厂商数据

FDE Times · 2026-10-08 · 3 min read

Summary

AI email assistant startup Fyxer claimed in its Series B announcement that 90% of users remain active after three months, but never specified the cohort, the definition of active, or whether users who left after a 14-day trial count in the denominator. Media outlets repeated the figure without independent verification, and reported revenue figures differed slightly. A worked example shows the same data yields 90% retention counting only paying users versus 54% counting all signups. The author advises FDEs to question vendor-reported metrics and to practice cohort retention queries with documented definitions.

Key points

  • Fyxer's 90% retention claim lacks cohort and methodology details.
  • Including or excluding trial users changes retention from 90% to 54%.
  • Media reports cited inconsistent revenue figures ($17M vs $16M+).
  • FDEs should ask about denominators, active definitions, and trial handling.
  • Writing documented cohort retention queries is a resume-worthy FDE skill.

Key Takeaways

  • Fyxer claims 90% of its users are still active after three months, but it doesn't specify the cohort or the measurement method.
  • News outlets republished the figure without independent verification, and the revenue numbers reported by different outlets don't match.
  • Before putting this figure into a customer-facing slide, an FDE should first clarify the denominator, the definition of "active," and how trial users are counted.

AI email assistant startup Fyxer claims that 90% of its users are still active after three months. The figure comes from the company's own Series B funding announcement, used to demonstrate that the product delivers significant value to users.

Unite.AI republished the figure, calling it a rare level of retention for productivity software. Silicon Canals also covered the story, but noted that the figure came from the company itself and made no mention of independent verification.

This matters directly to Forward Deployed Engineers (FDEs). In customer meetings, you often hear claims like "90% of users stick around," sometimes in vendor materials, sometimes on your own team's slides. The people who can distinguish a "defined metric" from "marketing talk" are the ones who win customer trust.

What's missing from this figure?

Fyxer's announcement doesn't define the cohort or explain how the figure was measured. According to Unite.AI, Fyxer serves more than 180,000 users, but no source clarifies whether the 90% covers all users or just one cohort that signed up in a particular month.

Fyxer's article also mentions a 14-day free trial. Do trial users who never convert to paid count in the denominator? If not, the 90% measures how many people stayed among those who had already decided to pay—a group that had already been pre-filtered.

Same data, two retention rates

Suppose a cohort (not Fyxer's data): 1,000 people sign up in the same month. Of them, 400 leave after finishing the 14-day trial, 600 convert to paid, and 540 are still active after three months.

If the denominator counts only paid users, the retention rate is 540 divided by 600—exactly 90%. If the denominator counts all signups, the retention rate is 540 divided by 1,000, or 54%. The data hasn't changed by a single row; only the definition has.

In SQL, this difference often comes down to a single WHERE clause that filters out trial users. So when calculating retention yourself, run both versions and write the definition next to the number.

Even the revenue figures don't match

Small discrepancies between reports show how easily numbers get distorted as they're passed along. Fyxer says its run-rate revenue grew from $1 million in early 2025 to over $17 million by August of the same year, which matches Unite.AI's report; Silicon Canals wrote "over $16 million."

The gap is small but instructive. When two sources disagree, go back to the original text and check how the metric is defined. Run-rate revenue and ARR aren't necessarily the same thing, just as one company's "active" isn't necessarily the same as another's.

Fyxer has about 48 employees. This funding round was led by Madrona, with Lakestar participating. The point here isn't whether Fyxer is right or wrong, but a habit: repeating a startup's self-reported numbers while dropping the definitions that come with them.

How should an FDE handle numbers like these?

If a customer sends you a similar figure to argue that a tool is worth buying, don't dismiss it outright—ask questions. That way, the discussion centers on data rather than impressions.

Ask who measured it, whether the denominator is all users or a specific cohort, and what counts as active: opening the app, sending an email, or using a core feature. Also ask how trial users are counted and whether there's a primary source you can verify.

For developers looking to transition into an FDE role, this skill can be practiced in your current job. Write a cohort retention query on your own product and document the definitions alongside it. If a target role's job description mentions adoption rates or success metrics, you can expand on this skill in the interview.

On a resume, a line like "redefined the active user metric for customer dashboards" carries more weight than a context-free percentage. Fyxer may well have retained 90% of its users. But a good FDE figures out how the number was measured before putting it in front of a customer.
