How to Write a Change Request (RFC) That Gets Approved by the CAB

Original title: 变更请求 RFC 怎么写才能通过 CAB 审批

FDE Times · 2026-11-18 · 5 min read

Summary

This guide targets forward deployed engineers (FDEs) whose deployments must pass a customer's Change Advisory Board (CAB). Citing Accelerate and DORA research, it notes that external approvals correlate with slower delivery but not fewer failed changes, yet FDEs must still follow customer processes. The core advice: answer the five recurring CAB questions upfront—risks, impact, scheduling, implementation steps, and rollback—with rollback described as a concrete, reversible action. Split bundled changes into separate RFCs, learn meeting deadlines early, and avoid citing Accelerate data to argue against the CAB. Long term, propose reclassifying repeatable low-risk changes as standard changes with automated approval, backed by peer review and a deployment pipeline, and learn the customer's ECAB contacts in advance.

Key points

  • External CAB approvals slow delivery without reducing change failure rates (Accelerate).
  • A good RFC pre-answers risks, impact, scheduling, implementation, and rollback.
  • Rollback must be concrete and reversible; if not, fix the deployment design.
  • Split multiple changes into separate RFCs to avoid all-or-nothing rejection.
  • Long term, move repeatable changes to standard changes with peer review and pipelines.

Key takeaways

  • The Accelerate research found that external approvals correlate with slower releases, not with lower change failure rates. But Forward Deployed Engineers (FDEs) still have to follow the customer's process.
  • The CAB asks the same few questions over and over. An RFC that answers risk, impact, scheduling, implementation, and rollback up front gets approved quickly.
  • In the long run, aim to classify repeatable deployments as standard changes, backed by peer review and the deployment pipeline.

The Accelerate research wanted to answer one question: does external change approval make production safer? The conclusion: no such evidence was found.

According to Harness's summary, external approvals correlate with longer lead times, lower deployment frequency, and slower failure recovery, but not with lower change failure rates.

This is correlation, not proof of causation. But it's enough to show that adding another approval doesn't automatically make production safer.

However, Forward Deployed Engineers (FDEs) don't get to decide the customer's process. In many large enterprises, deployments still go through the Change Advisory Board (CAB) no matter what the data says. The common scenario: the code is done, the demo works, and at the Friday meeting the RFC gets sent back for "unclear rollback plan," costing you another meeting cycle.

The skill to practice here: when requesting approval, make it so reviewers can decide after a single read, then gradually move repeatable changes off the meeting agenda. Arguing with the board won't get your deployment out a day earlier. A well-written RFC can; over many meeting cycles, it determines the pace of the entire project.

What does the CAB actually want to know?

DORA, which researches software delivery performance, explicitly notes that centralized committee approvals can cause delays and are often associated with errors; such heavyweight approval processes frequently slow down releases.

According to Octopus, CAB meetings often become a formality, where everyone asks the same one or two questions about every item. That's good news for FDEs: predictable questions can be answered in advance.

AWS's change management documentation sets the standard: a change request must contain enough information for someone with authority to approve or reject it based on it alone. AWS's example RFC template includes identified risks, impact, scheduling, implementation steps, and a rollback plan. Rollback is the bottom line; every request must have one.

Treat these five sections as five questions from someone who knows nothing about your system: What could go wrong? Who is affected if it does? When will it happen? How will it be done? How do you back it out if something goes wrong? If even one is unanswered, that's enough for them to push it to next week.

An RFC rewritten from scratch

Take a hypothetical example. You need to ship an update to the customer's internal customer service agent: a new prompt version, a new document source added to the retrieval index, and a version upgrade of the library that calls the model. The first draft crams all three into one RFC, saying "Agent upgrade, low risk, rollback by redeploying the old version."

That draft will almost certainly get questions back. "Low risk" is an assertion, not evidence. "Redeploy the old version" doesn't say how to remove documents already added to the index. With three changes bundled together, reviewers can't tell what they're actually supposed to evaluate.

The first step is to split it up. Octopus recommends reviewing only a small set of changes at a time, and the same advice applies to the CAB. Split the three changes into three RFCs, or at least three independent sections, each with its own rollback plan. The RFC for the index change could read like this:

RFC：将 HR 政策文档集加入客服 Agent 的检索索引

已识别的风险
- 如果新文档集与现有文档重叠，Agent 可能引用过时文档。
- 建索引耗时增加；建索引期间，查询仍读取旧索引。

影响
- 仅影响内部客服 Agent 的用户，不涉及任何业务数据库。
- 访问权限不变；这些文档所属的用户组已有阅读权限。

排期
- 周二，业务低峰时段。值班：[FDE 姓名] 和 [客户工程师姓名]。

实施步骤
1. 并行构建新索引，不覆盖旧索引。
2. 用固定的评测集测试新索引；达到约定阈值才切换。
3. 将别名指向新索引。

部署后检查
- 重新运行样例问题集，与上一次的结果对比。

回滚
- 将别名指回旧索引（旧索引完整保留至少 7 天）。一条命令，无需重新部署。

同行评审
- 已由 [评审人姓名] 批准的 pull request 链接。

Look at the rollback section. It contains no vague promises, only a concrete, reversible operation. It can be written this way because the deployment design itself left an escape hatch: the old index is kept intact, and only the pointer changes. If your rollback section can't be this short, the problem is in the deployment design; no amount of RFC wording will fix it.

From the meeting table to standard changes

A good RFC only gets you through one meeting. The long-term goal is fewer meetings. AWS defines a standard change as a common, low-risk, well-understood change that therefore doesn't need formal review and approval. AWS also recommends classifying repeatable, low-risk tasks as standard changes and auto-approving them.

Prompt updates and periodic index refreshes are both good candidates. After passing the CAB smoothly a few times with the same RFC template, you have grounds to propose: this type of change has the same steps every time, the same rollback, and has never caused an incident.

This proposal needs to offer something to replace the committee's stamp. DORA recommends peer review, recorded directly on the team's development platform, to satisfy segregation-of-duties requirements.

Harness's article supplies the other half: a lightweight approval process based on peer review, paired with a deployment pipeline that automatically detects and blocks problematic changes. The evals run before the alias switch in the example above are part of that pipeline.

What about emergencies? AWS notes that emergency changes must also follow a documented process, just approved by a smaller approval group, the Emergency Change Advisory Board (ECAB). Ask in advance who is on the customer's ECAB and how to reach them; don't wait until production is down at midnight to find out.

Mistakes that get RFCs rejected in the meeting

Many FDEs don't look into meeting schedules and RFC deadlines until the code is done, and missing one cycle costs a full week. Ask in the first week of the project, just like requesting data access.

The second mistake is bundling. Five changes in one RFC means reviewers can only approve or reject all five together; if one is vague, the whole package gets sent back. The third mistake is a token rollback, such as writing "restore to the previous version" for a change involving data or schema that can't actually be undone.

The fourth mistake is subtler: bringing Accelerate's data into the meeting room to prove the CAB is useless. The data has value, but the people in the room are accountable for their production systems.

Use it as the basis for proposing standard changes, not as a weapon. Finally, don't abuse the emergency channel to bypass meeting schedules. Doing so costs you trust, and future fast approvals depend on exactly that trust.

Turning this skill into a job-hunting advantage

When reading FDE job postings, phrases like "regulated environments," "enterprise customers," and "change management" signal that the role will deal with CABs. Don't just write "deployed to production for customers" on your resume.

Write that you built an RFC template and moved a class of repeatable changes from manual approval to auto-approval. That sentence shows interviewers that you understand not just your own code but the customer's organization.

At every new customer, the first thing to do is ask for a recently approved RFC to read. It tells you faster than any onboarding meeting what the committee cares about; with it, your first RFC can follow the format they're used to reading.
