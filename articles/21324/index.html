Baseten Forward Deployed Engineer Hiring Requirements and Preparation

Original title: Baseten 前线部署工程师招聘要求与准备

FDE Times · 2026-10-24 · 5 min read

Summary

This guide analyzes Baseten's job postings for Forward Deployed Engineers, arguing that preparation should focus on engineering proof rather than sales skills. Baseten's FDEs sit in the engineering organization, not sales, and the team's stated long-term goal is for 70% of FDE work to be integrated into the product. Candidates must show they can turn vague business goals into reliable, observable services with measured quality, latency, and cost, and produce reusable tools. The article recommends building a portfolio project with Baseten's open-source Truss CLI and identifies four common resume mistakes, each with a rewrite example.

Key Takeaways

  • The team's goal is for 70% of FDE work to make it into the product, so your code must be reusable.

You're reading Baseten's Forward Deployed Engineer (FDE) job posting and come across a somewhat odd line: candidates must be an engineer, project manager, and product manager at the same time. The easiest reaction is to stuff your resume with phrases like "strong communication skills" and "customer-facing experience." That's missing the point.

Baseten has been explicit about how it positions this role. A post on the company blog introducing the FDE team states that FDEs belong to the engineering team and frequently contribute code to the product. The job posting for the team's engineering manager role puts it even more bluntly: Baseten's FDE is not a sales function.

So preparing for this role isn't about polishing your presentation skills. You need to prove you're an engineer: given a vague business goal, you can deliver a service that runs in production, is measurable, is general enough, and that the company can turn into a feature.

What is the job posting actually testing?

In Baseten's own words, the team's mission has two halves: helping customers see real value early, and building products and tools that push the Baseten platform further. It's the second half that distinguishes FDEs here from ordinary integration engineers.

The most concrete number appears in the team's long-term goal: 70% of the work FDEs do should be integrated into the product. In day-to-day terms, if you write a latency benchmarking script for customer A, the team expects that script to later work for customers B and C, and eventually become a tool in the product.

The first hiring criterion listed in the blog is extremely solid software engineering fundamentals. The article argues that for technically strong engineers, empathy and communication skills can be developed. The reasonable reading: the technical part is something you have to bring yourself.

The second criterion also comes from the blog: curiosity about every layer of the tech stack, from ML all the way down to infrastructure and networking.

The job listing on Built In adds more detail: ability to write production-grade code in general-purpose programming languages, and familiarity with AI/ML data pipelines and the model development and deployment lifecycle.

The US role requires at least two years of experience in fast-growing environments; the London (EMEA) role requires at least one. Early-career engineers aren't shut out, provided the application materials prove what needs proving.

From "faster, cheaper" to a requirement spec with numbers

If you only read one sentence closely in the job posting, make it this one: FDEs work directly with customers to turn vague business goals into reliable, observable services, with clear results on quality, latency, and cost.

The responsibilities section lays out the full pipeline: from vague goals to clear requirement specs and tightly scoped proofs of concept (PoCs), through to deployment. FDEs also get involved at the earliest stage of the customer journey, which means when you receive a requirement, it's usually at its vaguest.

Imagine a hypothetical scenario. A customer support company uses an LLM to generate ticket summaries and tells you: "The system is too slow, and the bill is too high." That's not yet a technical requirement. Your first task is to break it down along the three dimensions the job posting names.

DimensionRequirement discovery questionsMetrics for the requirement spec (hypothetical example)
Quality"What does a good summary look like? Who judges it?"200 tickets with reference summaries; score no lower than the existing system
Latency"Which interface do users wait on? How long before they give up?"p95 below an agreed threshold with the customer, measured under peak traffic
Cost"Is the budget per request or per month?"Cost per 1,000 requests, compared to the current level

With this table, the PoC becomes clear: swap in a smaller model or a different inference serving configuration, run it on the same 200 tickets, and report three numbers. If quality drops, you have evidence to take back to the customer for discussion, rather than arguing on gut feeling.

This is exactly where the project manager and product manager roles come in. You decide the order of experiments, agree with the customer on what counts as "done," and ask yourself which work should become shared tools. A script that runs evals on 200 tickets, if parameterized from the start, is a prime candidate for that 70%.

How to prepare over a few weeks

Start with Baseten's own tools. Truss is an open-source CLI for deploying and serving ML models on Baseten. Building a small project with it kills two birds with one stone: you learn the model lifecycle the company's way, and you have something concrete to discuss in interviews.

A good portfolio project should repeat the exercise above end to end. Pick a real problem, write a one-page requirement spec covering the three dimensions, package and deploy a model with Truss, measure latency at several load levels, estimate cost, then write a README explaining the trade-offs you made.

The README should end with a note on which parts you would extract into reusable tools if this were work done for a real customer.

Don't skip the fundamentals: Baseten's blog explicitly mentions infrastructure and networking. Picture a very slow endpoint: the problem could be the model, cold starts, infrastructure configuration, or the network path. Train yourself to first ask "where is it slow?" and measure hop by hop, rather than guessing.

Four resume patterns that derail your application

Every bullet on your resume should follow one pattern: the original vague requirement, the metrics you agreed on, the measured results, and which outcomes were shared. The four mistakes below all deviate from this pattern, each with a rewrite (all examples are hypothetical).

The most common mistake is positioning yourself as a Sales Engineer, when Baseten has made clear its FDE is not that role. Weak version: "Demoed products to prospective customers and helped the sales team close deals." Rewrite: "Built a text summarization PoC for a customer, wrote a requirement spec with latency and per-request cost thresholds, and then carried the service into production."

The second is knowing only one layer, when the hiring criterion is curiosity about every layer of the tech stack. Weak version: "Fine-tuned a text classification model with high accuracy." Rewrite: "Fine-tuned a classification model, packaged and deployed it as an endpoint myself, measured latency hop by hop, and pinpointed cold starts as the cause of the slowdown."

The third is harder to spot: describing a "successful" PoC without a single number. Weak version: "Built a successful chatbot PoC; the customer was very happy." Rewrite: "Built a chatbot PoC and reported quality scores, p95 latency, and cost per 1,000 requests on a sample ticket set, compared against the old system."

The last is describing one-off work for a single customer with no follow-up, when the team's goal is for 70% of FDE work to make it into the product.

Weak version: "Wrote a benchmarking script for customer A's project." Rewrite: "Translated the internal team's 'cut costs' need into a requirement spec with latency and per-request cost thresholds, and packaged the measurement tooling as a library used by three other teams."

Put these four rewrites next to the job posting and you'll see they answer exactly the question Baseten is asking: can this engineer turn vague goals into measurable services and leave something behind in the product?
