FDE: Specialist or Generalist? The T-Shaped Forward Deployed Engineer

Original title: FDE 该做专才还是通才：T 型前线部署工程师

FDE Times · 2026-10-08 · 5 min read

Summary

The article uses Hippocratic AI's job posting for a Forward Deployment Engineer in York, PA as its starting point: despite being a healthcare AI company, it lists HL7/FHIR and Epic/Cerner experience only as a plus, while long-term on-site presence is a hard requirement. The author argues the specialist-versus-generalist debate misses the point: industry knowledge doesn't transfer, but the ability to enter an unfamiliar business environment and build what it needs does. It distinguishes pre-chosen industry depth from accumulated customer depth, notes engineers competing on domain knowledge are playing an away game against ops, product, and consulting backgrounds, and recommends betting on the vertical of the T-shape: integration and on-site skills. Company stage determines how broad the role must be.

Key points

  • Hippocratic AI requires long-term on-site work; healthcare integration experience is only a plus.
  • Industry depth and customer depth differ; only the latter accumulates through deployment.
  • Engineers competing on domain knowledge face ops, product, and consulting people on their home turf.
  • In Palantir's model, Deployment Strategists handle upstream problem definition; engineers build.
  • Practical advice: invest in integration and on-site skills rather than picking an industry now.

The specialist-vs-generalist debate misses the point: industry and customer knowledge can't be taken with you, but the ability to enter unfamiliar business contexts can.

Key takeaways

  • Hippocratic AI builds healthcare AI, yet lists HL7/FHIR and Epic/Cerner as nice-to-haves. The hard requirement is willingness to be on-site long term.
  • Paraform, Aced, and Valletta aren't actually contradicting each other. One side opposes generic specialization; the other refers to customer depth accumulated across one to three clients.
  • Engineers should bet on the vertical bar of the T: on-site and integration skills. People from operations, product, or consulting backgrounds already bring industry judgment; in the Palantir model, upstream work belongs to Deployment Strategists.

Hippocratic AI builds healthcare AI. It is hiring a Forward Deployment Engineer in York, Pennsylvania, and the job posting lists healthcare integration experience (HL7/FHIR, Epic/Cerner) as a plus. The hard requirement is willingness to be on-site long term, working at the customer's location.

This detail runs counter to most developers' intuition. When considering a move to a Forward Deployed Engineer (FDE) role, people usually ask: should you go deep in one industry (banking, logistics, healthcare), or be a generalist who can go anywhere? Hippocratic AI is a vertical company with about as clear a positioning as possible, yet its job posting shows this is the wrong question to ask.

If you have two to eight years of experience and are targeting an FDE role, the answer to this question affects what you learn next year, how you write your resume, and how you filter job descriptions. So it's worth examining both sides of the argument closely, then showing that they are actually discussing different things.

Two camps that appear opposed

The generalist camp is louder. Paraform states plainly that most engineering roles allow specialization, but FDE roles don't have that luxury. Aced (formerly Exponent) goes further: for FDEs, breadth beats depth—the opposite of SWE roles.

Practitioner Andrew Stroup adds the reasoning. He argues that on-site capability transfers across industries, because the underlying patterns don't belong to any single industry: walk into a customer's real business context, then build a solution that fits it.

The other camp is quieter, but its argument is solid. Valletta Software believes an FDE's value lies in domain knowledge, which can only be accumulated over time in the same environment. In their view, forward deployment means going deep with one to three clients.

At first glance, the two sides disagree. On closer inspection, the "depth" Valletta describes is not the "depth" Paraform and Aced oppose.

There are two kinds of "depth," and neither can be taken with you as-is

The first is pre-selected industry depth: you decide this year to become "a healthcare person," learn HL7 and hospital workflows, and only then start job hunting. Paraform's warning targets the FDE role as a whole, but read this way, it most obviously applies to this kind of specialization.

This is exactly the kind of knowledge Hippocratic AI's job posting lists as a plus.

The second is gradually accumulated customer depth: you spend enough time inside a customer's business to know which exceptions matter, which systems are actually running, and who calls the shots. This is what Valletta means, and it comes from being on-site, not from pre-selecting an industry.

Viewed this way, Hippocratic AI's requirement for long-term on-site presence can be read as: what the company needs first is people willing to stay at the customer long enough.

Aced describes the FDE as a T-shaped talent: deep in one area, broad across many, plus customer-facing skills. But the vertical bar of the T doesn't have to be an industry.

The vertical bar is the ability to enter an unfamiliar environment and build what that environment actually needs—the patterns Stroup says don't belong to any single industry. The horizontal bar is the industries you've been through; each customer's specifics stay with that customer.

Competing on domain knowledge, engineers are playing an away game

There's an even more practical reason not to bet on industry depth. MindStudio points out that people from operations, product, or consulting backgrounds already bring judgment about the customer's domain: they already know which exceptions matter and why tidy flowcharts break down in reality. What they lack is technical depth.

The conclusion follows naturally. On industry understanding, engineers compete against people who already have that judgment, while your advantage lies precisely in their weakness. If you, as a developer, spend two years grinding on industry knowledge, you're playing a home game on someone else's turf.

Palantir has institutionalized a similar division of labor. According to Paraform, Deployment Strategists there handle all upstream work from requirements to stakeholders, while engineers build. Problem definition and engineering implementation have become two separate roles.

So the question engineers should ask isn't "which industry to pick," but "where is my technical depth." The Pragmatic Engineer offered an uncomfortable take in its May 2026 analysis: the modern FDE role is essentially a consultant or Solutions Architect, leaning more toward consulting and integration than building new software.

If that judgment holds, the FDE's technical vertical bar is likely integration: interfacing with legacy systems, handling dirty data, access permissions, half-finished APIs—not elegant algorithms.

Company stage determines how wide you need to be

There's another variable both camps rarely mention: what stage the company you join is at. Valletta described a fifty-person startup where one person is simultaneously a Sales Engineer, Solutions Architect, and FDE. There, breadth isn't a choice—it's the job itself.

Paraform's advice points the same way: let FDEs cover both functions first, then split them once deployment complexity exceeds what one person can handle. So the same "FDE" title means different work as the organization matures.

ScenarioWho handles "understanding the industry"Should engineers be broad or deep
~50-person startup (Valletta)You yourself, doubling as Sales Engineer and Solutions ArchitectYou need to know almost everything; customer depth accumulates over time
Palantir-style division of labor (Paraform)Deployment Strategists handle upstreamEngineers focus on building; problem definition goes to Strategists
Vertical AI company (Hippocratic AI)The company has already picked the industry for youIndustry knowledge is a plus; long-term on-site presence is a hard requirement

As the table shows, there's no universal answer to "specialist or generalist." What changes with stage is how much industry-level problem definition falls on you.

Where developers should place their bets

For engineers with two to eight years of experience, whether in Vietnam or elsewhere, the practical advice is: don't rush to pick an industry now—pick your vertical bar first.

Practice the unglamorous integration skills: reading legacy systems with no documentation, untangling data mappings for unfamiliar schemas, building a data pipeline that runs in the customer's environment rather than on your own laptop, and talking with frontline operators long enough to learn which exceptions they fear most.

If, like many Vietnamese developers, you've worked across several industries doing outsourcing, that isn't the "lack of specialization" so many worry about. But the prerequisite is being able to articulate, for each project, how quickly you learned that industry and how the real business differed from the initial requirements.

On your resume, replace statements like "X years of experience in industry X" with phrasing like "entered environment Y, discovered Z differed from assumptions, and adjusted what was being built."

When reading a job description, split the requirements into two columns—Hippocratic AI has inadvertently demonstrated this for you: the "must-have" column tells you what the role really is; the "preferred" column tells you what the company hopes for. If industry knowledge is in the second column, don't let it stop you from applying.

As Valletta said, industry knowledge accumulates naturally once you spend enough time in the same environment. What you need to do is become the person sent into that environment: solid integration skills and a willingness to be on-site. Even a healthcare AI company ranks this above healthcare experience.
