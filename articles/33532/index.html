FDE Full Form and Job Titles

BuildAI Careers · 2026-10-03 · 14 min read

Summary

This career guide from BuildAI Careers explains that FDE stands for Forward Deployed Engineer and FDSE for Forward Deployed Software Engineer, both describing engineers working near customer problems and technical delivery. It argues that titles alone reveal little: candidates must read job descriptions for coding, integration, customer communication and production ownership. Citing postings from OpenAI (FDE) and Palantir (FDSE), it shows employers use the terms differently. The guide provides glossaries covering customer-delivery terms (discovery, scope, handover), software and data concepts (API, SQL, tenant, idempotency), and AI vocabulary (RAG, evaluation, agents), then demonstrates decoding a fictional vacancy and reading verbs like assist, implement, own and lead to gauge responsibility levels.

Key points

  • Titles vary by employer; OpenAI uses FDE, Palantir uses FDSE.
  • Job descriptions, not abbreviations, reveal actual coding and delivery duties.

In this guide

FDE stands for Forward Deployed Engineer in this career context. FDSE stands for Forward Deployed Software Engineer. Both titles commonly refer to engineers working close to customer problems and technical delivery. The exact responsibilities vary, so read the job description for coding, integration, customer communication and production ownership. “FDE engineer” is a common search phrase, although the final E already means Engineer.

Knowing the full form is the first step. Understanding the language around the title helps you decide whether a vacancy suits your experience. Job advertisements can include terms such as discovery, production, implementation, RAG and observability. Those words describe different pieces of work, and they can affect what you need to learn or show in an interview.

This guide decodes the title and the terms you are likely to meet while researching it. The examples are fictional reading exercises. For a fuller explanation of the profession, visit what a Forward Deployed Engineer does. For the delivery lifecycle, read the FDE role guide.

What is the full form of FDE?

In software careers, FDE expands to Forward Deployed Engineer. “Forward” describes working close to the customer’s situation. “Deployed” describes being placed where technical work supports delivery. “Engineer” identifies the hands-on technical responsibility. The title does not specify one language, one industry or one working location.

The abbreviation can have other meanings in other fields. Use the context of the document or vacancy to interpret it. A software careers page discussing customer applications and integrations gives a different context from an unrelated technical manual. Expanding an abbreviation correctly requires checking the surrounding words.

Official employer titles provide useful examples. OpenAI’s FDE posting uses Forward Deployed Engineer, while Palantir’s posting uses Forward Deployed Software Engineer. Both titles were checked on 3 October 2026. The responsibilities in each posting explain how that employer uses the role.

What is the difference between FDE and FDSE?

FDSE adds the word Software. It can make the technical emphasis more explicit, but the abbreviation alone cannot prove that an FDSE writes more code than an FDE at another company. Compare the actual tasks. Look for programming requirements, application ownership, integration work, tests and releases.

For example, a vacancy may expect an FDE to build full-stack applications. Another may expect an FDSE to combine data work with customer discovery. A third may focus on platform deployment. Each can involve substantial engineering, but the skills and working pattern differ. The title helps you search; the description helps you decide.

How to read the words around a job title

Words such as junior, senior, lead and manager describe an employer’s expectations, but companies do not all assign them identically. A senior role may require independent technical decisions and customer responsibility. A lead role may coordinate a workstream. A manager role may include people management. Check the duties and reporting structure.

A suffix can describe the customer group or technical domain. “Healthcare”, “enterprise” or “government” can point to different workflows and constraints. “India”, “APAC” or a city can describe hiring location or regional delivery. Ask whether the location refers to where you work, the customers you support or both.

Also read the department. An FDE can sit within Engineering, Customer Success or a specialist deployment organisation. The department offers context, while the work remains the main evidence. A Customer Success department label does not tell you how much coding the role includes.

A glossary of customer-delivery terms

Discovery

Discovery means finding out how the customer currently works and what they need to improve. It includes asking users questions, examining the workflow and identifying constraints. In an interview exercise, you can demonstrate discovery by clarifying the problem before choosing a tool. The output is often a problem statement and a better-defined first step.

Scope

Scope defines the work included in a version or engagement. It also identifies what is outside that boundary. A narrow first scope might retrieve approved guidance for staff review while leaving automatic customer replies for later. Clear scope helps a team make decisions about time, risk and acceptance.

Stakeholder

A stakeholder is a person or group affected by the project or involved in its decisions. Users, managers, administrators and operating teams can all be stakeholders. They may have different concerns. The engineer needs to know who provides requirements, who approves access and who accepts the delivered result.

Acceptance criteria

Acceptance criteria describe what the delivered version must do to be accepted. They should be specific enough to test. “The application works well” is vague. “An authorised staff member can submit a valid request and see its identifier” is easier to check. Add relevant failure and permission cases as well.

Implementation

Implementation is the work of turning an agreed design into a functioning system. It can include coding, configuration, data mapping and integration. When a vacancy uses this word, ask how much custom engineering is involved and which earlier design decisions the person owns.

Integration

An integration connects systems so they can exchange information or actions. A customer portal might send approved orders to an internal service. The engineer needs to handle fields, authentication and failures. A useful portfolio integration includes a repeated request and a dependency outage, not only the successful response.

Migration

A migration moves data or workflows from one system or environment to another. It requires decisions about what moves and how differences are handled. Validation and reconciliation help confirm the result. A migration job may involve substantial engineering even when the destination platform already exists.

Handover

Handover gives the operating team the information and access needed to manage the delivered system. It can include a runbook, configuration notes, known limits and escalation details. A project is easier to hand over when another person has tried following the instructions before release.

A glossary of software and data terms

API

API stands for Application Programming Interface. It defines how software can request information or actions from another component. For a job application, you should be able to explain the inputs, outputs, authentication and failure behaviour of an API you used. Knowing the expansion is less useful than understanding a complete request.

REST

REST is short for Representational State Transfer, an architectural style used by many web APIs. In practice, a vacancy mentioning REST APIs may expect you to work with resources, HTTP requests and structured responses. Check the specific system’s contract rather than assuming that every API behaves identically.

JSON

JSON means JavaScript Object Notation. It is a text format commonly used to exchange structured data. A response can contain objects, arrays and values. In an integration, you should validate the fields you depend on and handle missing or unexpected values deliberately.

SQL

SQL means Structured Query Language. It is used to work with data in relational database systems. Relevant practice includes selecting records, joining related tables and checking a migration result. The PostgreSQL tutorial offers an introduction to SQL and relational concepts.

Schema

A schema describes the structure of information in a particular context. It can define database tables or the expected shape of an API payload. If a source field changes, an integration may fail or quietly produce an incorrect result. The engineer needs to understand and validate the expected structure.

Authentication and authorisation

Authentication establishes identity; authorisation determines permitted access or actions. A person can be signed in and still lack permission to read a particular record. In a portfolio project, demonstrate that distinction through the application’s enforced checks. A user-interface label alone cannot show the control works.

Tenant

A tenant is a customer or organisation context in a system serving multiple groups. The exact model varies. If your project has tenant-specific records, test that one tenant cannot access another’s restricted information. Explain where the boundary is enforced and which test demonstrates it.

Idempotency

Idempotency describes an operation whose repeated execution has the same intended effect as one execution. It is useful when requests may be retried. For a record-creation workflow, an explicit request identifier can help the application recognise a repeat instead of creating a duplicate. The correct design depends on the operation.

A glossary of AI terms in FDE vacancies

LLM

LLM stands for Large Language Model. Such a model can generate or process language, but an application still needs a defined task and checks. In a customer workflow, ask what information the model receives, what it produces and how the result is reviewed. A plausible response can still be unsuitable for the task.

RAG

RAG stands for Retrieval-Augmented Generation. A system retrieves relevant information and supplies it as context for generation. In a fictional policy assistant, that might mean retrieving approved passages before drafting an answer. You need to test retrieval, document versions, access and the answer’s support in the sources.

Embedding

An embedding represents information as numbers that can be used for comparison. In a search application, embeddings can help retrieve material with related meaning. They do not decide whether a user is allowed to read a document. Access controls belong in the application and retrieval design.

Evaluation

Evaluation means checking behaviour against a defined set of tasks and expectations. For an AI answer feature, include ordinary questions, missing information and restricted requests. Record failures and test again after changes. A few successful demonstrations do not describe performance across every possible input.

Tool calling

Tool calling lets an application use a model’s output to request a defined function or action. The application should validate the request and enforce permissions. A lookup tool and a payment-changing tool have different consequences. Decide which actions require a person’s approval before adding them.

Agent

An AI agent is a term used for systems that can take steps toward a task, often using tools. Implementations vary widely. In a vacancy, look for the actual responsibilities behind the word: workflow design, tool permissions, evaluation and operational control. Ask what the system is allowed to do.

A glossary of deployment and operations terms

Production

Production is the environment or service used for real work. A prototype can become a production application only after the relevant delivery checks and operational arrangements are addressed. When describing your own project, state which environment you used and whether real users depended on it.

CI/CD

CI commonly means Continuous Integration: checking changes as they are combined. CD can mean Continuous Delivery or Continuous Deployment, depending on the process. Ask how the team uses the terms. A useful learning project can demonstrate automated checks and a controlled release without a complicated pipeline.

Observability

Observability concerns understanding system behaviour from its outputs, such as logs, metrics and traces. In practical preparation, follow one request and inspect the evidence when it fails. Useful signals should help distinguish the source of a problem and guide the next investigation.

Runbook

A runbook gives an operator instructions for a task or failure. It should say what to check, what action is appropriate and when to escalate. Test it with another person. If a step only makes sense because you remember the setup, improve the instruction.

Rollback

Rollback returns a system to a known working state after a problematic change. The process can involve code, configuration and data considerations. A portfolio deployment should explain what can be reversed and which changes need a separate recovery approach.

Rollout

A rollout introduces a version to its intended users. It can begin with a small group before expanding. The team checks behaviour, collects feedback and decides whether to proceed. For the fictional support tool, a pilot group might use the new search while the existing process remains available. The engineer checks agreed cases, records failures and confirms that users understand the workflow. The rollout plan should identify who makes the expansion decision and what would cause the team to pause. This connects the technical release to practical adoption.

A worked exercise: decode a fictional vacancy

Suppose a fictional posting says: “Own customer discovery and deliver API integrations, RAG workflows and production handover.” Break that sentence into tasks. Discovery asks you to clarify the user and problem. API integration asks you to connect systems and handle requests. RAG asks you to retrieve evidence and generate a supported output. Handover asks you to make the result operable.

Now identify the evidence you would need. A project brief can show discovery. An integration with validation and retry tests can show API work. An evaluation set can show the AI feature. A runbook and deployment record can show handover. This turns the advertisement’s language into a practical preparation list.

Then ask what remains unclear. Does “own” include final customer acceptance? Which data can the system access? How often will you travel? Does the role include ongoing support? A short list of specific questions helps you understand the job before deciding whether it fits.

Pay attention to the verbs in a job description

The verb often tells you more than the abbreviation. “Assist” may describe contributing to a workstream under supervision. “Implement” may describe building an agreed design. “Own” may describe responsibility for a result. “Lead” may include technical decisions and coordination. Ask the hiring team how it uses these words rather than assuming one universal level of independence.

Compare two fictional descriptions. One says, “Assist regional engineers with migration tests.” The other says, “Own migration design, customer acceptance and cutover.” Both concern migrations, but the second explicitly includes decisions and delivery accountability. Your application evidence should match that difference. A successful test contribution is useful; it should not be presented as owning a complete engagement.

Look for the object of the verb as well. Owning a utility is different from owning a customer relationship or a production service. Write the expected responsibility in plain language. Then ask which team members provide review and which decisions need approval. This helps you understand both the challenge and the support around the role.

Use technical terms accurately in your resume

A resume should help a reviewer find evidence. If you write “built a RAG system”, explain the source material, retrieval behaviour and evaluation you completed. If you write “production deployment”, describe the environment and users accurately. If you write “API integration”, be prepared to discuss authentication, payloads and failures.

You can use the full term the first time and the abbreviation afterward. This helps a reader who does not share your exact technical background. Keep the wording tied to the project: “Built an API integration for fictional order records and tested duplicate requests” is more assessable than a row of unexplained acronyms.

Do not add a term only because it appears in the vacancy. Learn the underlying work or identify it as a gap. During an interview, an honest explanation of what you know and what you are learning is easier to build on than an overstated claim. Your project can demonstrate progress without pretending to include every technology in the advertisement.

Distinguish a deployment task from a deployed role

Deploying software means making it available in an environment. Being a Forward Deployed Engineer describes a job’s relationship to customer delivery. These meanings are connected, but they answer different questions. One is about releasing a system; the other is about the engineer’s position and responsibilities around the customer problem.

For example, a developer can deploy a personal website without doing FDE work. An FDE can spend a day on discovery or data validation before any release takes place. When reading a title, use the complete description. When discussing a project, describe the actual task: designed, built, integrated, tested, deployed or handed over.

How to learn the terms without memorising a glossary

Try explaining a term without using its acronym. For RAG, describe how the application finds an approved passage and uses it to prepare an answer. For CI, describe how checks run when changes are combined. For API, describe the request and response. If you can make the explanation concrete, the abbreviation becomes a useful shorthand instead of a substitute for understanding.

Ask someone else to read the explanation and give you one question. Their question may expose a missing step or an assumption. Add the relevant example, test or diagram. This gives you practice explaining technical work to a person who needs the result without sharing all your terminology.

Connect each term to a small task. For API, send and inspect a request. For SQL, query related tables. For migration, move fictional records and reconcile them. For rollback, restore a previous application version. Practical use makes the term easier to understand and gives you evidence for an interview.

Choose the terms in your target vacancies first. If the job emphasises operational troubleshooting, practise logs and recovery. If it emphasises data migration, practise mapping and validation. Use the FDE skills guide to turn those requirements into exercises.

Keep a short project glossary in your own words. Explain what each component does in that project and link to the relevant code or test. Another person should be able to understand the explanation. If a definition remains vague, use it as a signal to practise the underlying task.
