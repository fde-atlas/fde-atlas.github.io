Today's Judgment

Two different supply organization models are emerging as entry points for enterprise AI adoption in China: local governments bundling compute, models, data, and scenarios into public platforms that enterprises can access on demand; and industry leaders packaging private data, expert review, and AI agents into deliverable solutions.

Within one month of launch, Xiamen's AI public service platform aggregated over 2,100 resources; Haizhou's "Wukong—Xirang" platform adopts a "1 portal + 1 foundation + 4 marketplaces" architecture to open up compute and model access; while iKang and Huawei Cloud embed a health management agent into an accountability chain of "AI generation + physician review and sign-off."

Resources are becoming more abundant and cheaper, yet the value of the FDE is growing rather than shrinking—because platforms can lower the upfront cost of "finding models/compute," but they will not complete process selection, data authorization, role adoption, and acceptance testing on behalf of enterprises.

Key Signals

Xiamen's AI public service platform aggregates over 2,100 resources in its first month

  • Source: Fujian Provincial People's Government | Original link
  • The Fujian provincial government disclosed on August 23 that Xiamen's AI public service platform launched on July 23; within one month, it aggregated over 2,100 AI resources including large models, compute, datasets, agents, and expert talent. By the end of 2025, Xiamen had cumulatively built 45 provincial-level representative AI application scenarios.
  • Why it matters: The emergence of public platforms means the cost for enterprises to "find models/find compute/find partners" is falling rapidly. But 2,100 is a resource aggregation count and 45 is a count of existing scenarios—neither equals actual enterprise usage volume, project success rates, or business returns. FDEs should treat platform capabilities as a candidate supply catalog, and still complete entry diagnostics using "business scenario → data conditions → responsible party → acceptance criteria."

Haizhou launches the "Wukong—Xirang" unified portal for enterprise AI services

  • Source: Lianyungang Municipal Government | Original link
  • On August 20, Haizhou District of Lianyungang City launched the "Wukong—Xirang" public service platform, adopting a "1 portal + 1 foundation + 4 marketplaces" architecture covering compute, models, data, and applications. Enterprises can access models such as DeepSeek, Tongyi Qianwen, Kimi, and Zhipu on a per-token basis.
  • Why it matters: Per-token access to public models lowers the barrier to AI adoption for SMEs, but the real difficulty shifts from "buying servers" to task classification, token budgets, data boundaries, and scenario acceptance. FDEs can turn such platforms into client diagnostic tools: which tasks are adequately served by public models, and which require private deployment or human review—this judgment itself is a deliverable.

iKang and Huawei Cloud: health management agent embedded in an "AI generation + physician review and sign-off" accountability chain

  • Source: National Business Daily | Original link
  • On August 21-22, iKang Group and Huawei Cloud showcased a joint health management solution for government and enterprise clients: the core system is deployed on a private cloud certified at China's Level 3 security standard, with AI-generated plans reviewed and signed off by professional physicians. Publicly, iKang claims to hold 80 million compliant health checkup records.
  • Why it matters: In high-risk industries, the deliverable unit is not "letting an agent replace experts," but connecting the data loop, permissions and deployment boundaries, AI initial generation, and expert final sign-off into one accountability chain. This "human-machine accountability chain" structure can be transferred to all expert-judgment scenarios such as tax, legal, and healthcare: compliant data → AI initial generation/drafting → expert review and sign-off → audit trail and retrospective review → exception escalation. AI does not replace the responsible party; it compresses the time they spend on gathering, synthesizing, and initial assessment. Note: the 80 million figure refers to data assets; agent usage rates and service efficiency changes were not disclosed.

Implications for FDE Practitioners

  • Public platforms are supply catalogs, not delivery solutions: Platforms solve "resource availability," but whether a project lands still depends on the business owner, data authorization, baseline metrics, test samples, and exception escalation paths. Writing "registered on the platform" as "AI has been deployed" is self-deception.
  • Build a "task tiering card" for clients using per-token access: business task → risk level → data level → model/deployment method → token budget → human reviewer → exception escalation → acceptance criteria.
  • In high-risk scenarios, first define "who signs off": what AI may generate, what experts must sign off, and how exceptions escalate—map out the accountability chain clearly before discussing the agent interface.
