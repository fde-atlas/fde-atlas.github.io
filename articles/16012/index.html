How to transition to FDE without on-site experience? Replace experience with transferable evidence

Original title: 没有驻场经验怎么转 FDE？用可迁移证据替代经验

FDE中国 · 2026-09-07 · 2 min read

Summary

This Q&A-style piece addresses the most common career-switch question: how to become an FDE without on-site deployment experience. Its core method is replacing experience with transferable evidence—recruiters want proof you can deliver under ambiguous requirements, which can come from internal projects, open-source work, or a complete portfolio. It recommends auditing past projects for customer-facing elements, doing one end-to-end delivery to production standards, and entering through an internal transfer first. A realistic timeline is three to six months, broken into monthly milestones.

Key points

  • On-site experience is not the ticket; transferable delivery evidence is.
  • Restructure past projects into discovery, scope, delivery, and evaluation segments.
  • Complete one real delivery with scope docs, evals, and handoff documentation.
  • Smoothest entry is an internal transfer, treating colleagues as clients.
  • Expect three to six months of sustained effort with monthly milestones.

Full text

This answer targets practitioners who want to switch careers but lack on-site experience—the most frequently asked transition question in this Q&A batch. The most valuable takeaway is the "evidence instead of experience" methodology: what hiring managers really want to confirm is that you can deliver results under ambiguous requirements, which can be demonstrated through internal projects, open-source deliverables, or a complete portfolio—no need to wait for an on-site title. The easiest path to go wrong is mass-submitting resumes and hoping for luck, or only taking courses without producing work—FDE hiring looks for a chain of delivery evidence. Compared with generic transition advice, this article's added value is pointing out that the smoothest entry point is often inside your own company: first serve internal clients, treat your colleagues as customers, and accumulate your first tellable project. Be realistic about the transition timeline too: from starting from scratch to landing an offer generally takes three to six months of sustained effort; breaking this period into one milestone per month is far safer than betting everything on a single interview opportunity, and makes it easier to recalibrate along the way. After reading, do one thing: take your most recent project and rewrite it as a case study across the four stages of discovery, scoping, delivery, and evals, then test whether it can withstand deep probing in an interview. — FDEChina Editorial Team · Practitioner's Perspective

How do I transition to FDE without on-site experience?

First, get your mindset right: on-site experience is not the ticket; transferable delivery evidence is. What hiring managers really want to confirm is one thing—whether you can take a real-world scenario from ambiguous requirements to a working solution. This capability can be built in many places; you don't have to have held an FDE title. So the transition isn't "first find an on-site job," but rather first create transferable evidence in your current position, then use it to make the jump.

Step one: take inventory of the "field factors" in your existing experience. Whether you've done implementation, technical support, pre-sales delivery, vendor-side projects, or even built internal tools for business departments, all of these contain partial elements of FDE work: facing real users, delivering under constraints, handling ad-hoc changes. Reorganize your past projects into the four stages of "discovery—scoping—delivery—evals" to see clearly which stage you're missing; this is far more useful than a vague "I have no experience." For a comparison of specific transition paths by background, see FDE transition paths.

Step two: fill your shortest gap with one complete delivery. Pick a real scenario—it can be an internal department at your company, a nonprofit organization, or even a business simulation you design yourself—and go from requirements interviews all the way to launch evals, holding yourself to production standards throughout: write a scope document, build an eval set, and leave handover documentation. What this process produces is your hardest ammunition in interviews; for how to organize it, see the FDE portfolio guide. By the way, doing this will naturally answer the question of "whether you're a good fit": if you enjoy making things real amid chaos, this role is probably right for you.

Step three: choose the smoothest entry point to make the jump. The common sequence is: first, transfer internally at your current company or take on internal-facing on-site responsibilities, treating colleagues as clients to build your first track record; second, target teams willing to hire "quasi-FDEs," usually platform teams with training systems; only last, aim directly at mature FDE roles, which requires a sufficiently strong portfolio. For how to pass the interview, see what FDE interviews test.

One reminder: the real barrier to transitioning into FDE is the ability to see things through, not a résumé label. Lacking on-site experience isn't scary; what's scary is never having had a "when things went wrong, you were the one who cleaned up" experience. Find a way to be that person who closes things out in your first transition project, and the track record will grow by itself. If you're coming from a consulting background, there's an even more specific path—see how consultants from consulting firms transition to FDE.
