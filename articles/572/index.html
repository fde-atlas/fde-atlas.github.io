Summary

This article explains the Forward Deployed Engineer (FDE) role: engineers who work on-site at customers to make purchased AI systems actually usable, since data formats, workflows and compliance differ per company. It contrasts FDEs with sales engineers, who stop at closing deals. It lists five hiring criteria used by Palantir, OpenAI, Anthropic and Google Cloud, describes a two-role team structure (fast Delta engineers and domain-deep Echo analysts), common tooling (LangGraph, LlamaIndex, Docker), and cites Palantir's Q1 2026 revenue growth of 85% as evidence the model works. Salaries range from about $238,000 at Palantir to $350,000–400,000 for senior roles at OpenAI and Anthropic, with New York overtaking San Francisco as the FDE hub in 2026.

Key points

  • FDEs embed at customer sites to make AI systems actually work.
  • Unlike sales engineers, FDEs start after the contract is signed.
  • Palantir grew Q1 2026 revenue 85% year over year.
  • Teams pair fast Delta engineers with domain-deep Echo analysts.

Have you ever wondered why the AI systems that big companies spend millions or even hundreds of millions to buy often end up unused?

The answer is simple: AI isn't something you can just buy, plug in, and use. Every company has different data formats, different business processes, and different compliance requirements. That's why you need someone who understands both technology and business, someone who goes to the client's company and connects the AI to the client's actual situation.

That person is a Forward Deployed Engineer (FDE).

In 2026, this role suddenly became extremely popular. Google Cloud said it would hire hundreds of them, OpenAI set up a dedicated company to do this, and Anthropic followed suit by pouring billions of dollars into it. Industry leaders have even said this will be one of the most sought-after jobs in the tech industry in the future.

A Forward Deployed Engineer (FDE) is essentially "a super technical consultant who can write code."

A typical software engineer sits in an office, follows the company's plan, and works on tasks assigned by the company. But an FDE is different: they go directly to the client's office and work side by side with the client.

They have only one mission: to make sure the AI technology the client bought actually solves problems, rather than becoming a decoration that nobody touches.

For example, a law firm bought an AI to review contracts, only to find it couldn't tell the difference between US and UK law. That's when an FDE would go directly to the firm, modify the code and tune the system on-site, until it works properly.

Many companies have "Sales Engineers," whose job is to help the company sell products. An FDE's work begins after the contract is signed—they have to make sure the client actually uses it, and uses it well.

So the people selling and the people actually doing the work are two entirely different things.

When companies like Palantir, OpenAI, Anthropic, and Google Cloud hire FDEs, they look for five capabilities:

First, strong technical skills. You need to be able to write code, build systems, and handle complex technical problems.

Second, the ability to work with people. You need to understand what clients are saying and grasp their real needs, not just quietly write code.

Third, deep knowledge of your own product. You need to know what your company's product can and cannot do, and be able to explain it clearly to clients.

Fourth, the ability to execute and deliver. Talking isn't enough; you have to actually build things and solve concrete problems.

Fifth, a long-term perspective. It's not about finishing one deal and leaving, but about maintaining the client relationship so the client keeps using the product over the long term.

Someone summarized what matters most for an FDE like this: if you can only write code but don't understand the client, what you deliver won't be what they actually want; if you can only chat with clients but can't write code, then you're a consultant, not an engineer. You need to be good at both.

Suppose a law firm bought an AI system to help analyze contracts. An FDE is sent over and stays for six weeks. Here's just an ordinary day:

At 8:30 in the morning, the client's IT security department says the system keeps logs too long, which violates regulations. The FDE immediately rewrites the logging code, coordinates with the legal team to confirm everything is fine, and documents the solution for future reference when other clients run into the same issue.

At 10 a.m., the system has a bug—clause matching keeps failing for cross-border contracts. The FDE pulls up the backend data, pinpoints the problem in five minutes, and fixes the code before the client's morning meeting. The client's team doesn't even notice the system was ever down.

At 1 p.m., the FDE compiles the day's findings into a report and sends it to their own product team, telling them which bugs every client will encounter and should be built into the product.

At 4 p.m., a client partner raises a new requirement: they want the system to distinguish between US and UK law. The FDE sketches the architecture on a whiteboard on the spot, confirms it's technically feasible, and promises a prototype within days.

That's the daily life of an FDE—wherever there's a problem, they go solve it.

In the past, one company built an AI product, another company bought and used it, and that was the end of the story. Not anymore.

The new model now has three layers:

At the top are the big companies like OpenAI and Anthropic, which build powerful AI models.

In the middle layer are AI startups, which take the big companies' models to clients across all industries and help them build customized solutions.

At the bottom are the actual clients—banks, hospitals, law firms, factories—they don't want the AI model itself, but something that solves real problems.

The AI companies in the middle layer are essentially doing FDE work. They are users of frontier models on one side, and engineers for specific clients on the other.

Well-run FDE teams are usually split into two roles:

The first is the Delta Engineer, characterized by speed. They charge to the front line, rapidly building prototypes and solving problems quickly, without worrying too much about whether the code is perfect—just get the system running first.

The second is the Echo Analyst, characterized by depth. They know a particular industry inside out, understanding what a client really means when they say "this isn't compliant," and what changes are needed to pass review.

The two must work together. With only fast people who don't understand the industry, what gets built is simply wrong; with only deep people who lack hands-on skills and can talk but not build, it's equally useless.

FDEs can solve problems quickly because they rely on a set of professional tools:

For orchestrating AI agent workflows, they use LangGraph or CrewAI. For enterprise data retrieval and knowledge management, LlamaIndex. For big data and databases, Apache Spark and SQL. For model testing and monitoring, LangSmith. For deploying and running systems, Docker and Kubernetes.

In short, an FDE isn't a worker who only knows how to use a hammer, but someone with a full toolkit at hand.

For company executives, the emergence of the FDE role has changed how they choose vendors.

In the past, executives would ask: which AI model has the highest benchmark scores? Which is the cheapest?

Now they should ask: can this vendor send engineers to work on-site with me long-term and truly understand my business?

Palantir is the best example. It doesn't make consumer products, only enterprise-grade data analytics. In Q1 2026, its revenue grew 85% year over year, and its US commercial revenue surged 133%. How did it do it? Not by winning on model benchmarks, but by sending people to clients and solving problems together with them. It's that simple.

This is not a job just anyone can do.

Former Microsoft executive Keith Ballinger put it well: an ordinary product engineer takes a quarter to feel the impact of their code, but an FDE knows within hours. You write the code and immediately see whether it can withstand real-world testing.

The pace is extremely fast, expectations extremely high, and the intensity extreme. It's not for everyone.

In terms of compensation, Palantir's average annual salary is about $238,000, while mid-to-senior positions at OpenAI and Anthropic range from $350,000 to $400,000. And by 2026, New York has replaced San Francisco as the city with the highest concentration of FDEs, because industries like finance and healthcare are on the East Coast.

The FDE role matters because it solves a fundamental problem: good technology doesn't equal good application.

No matter how powerful AI is, if no one connects it to real business, it's just a lab exhibit. The FDE is that connector—technical depth, independent thinking, fast hands-on execution, and turning real customer feedback into product improvement directions.

If you love writing code but don't want to sit in an office staring at a screen all day, and you're willing to go to the front lines to solve real problems—then FDE might be the career path best suited for you.
