Field feedback that changed a roadmap

FDE Interview Prep · 2026 · 2 min read

Summary

This is a practice prompt for FDE interviews on describing field feedback that changed a product roadmap. The author cites OpenAI's San Francisco FDE posting (September 2026), which requires eval-driven feedback affecting product and model roadmaps, and Ramp's Core FDE posting, which ties FDEs to the core engineering roadmap. A five-part structure is proposed: the observed signal, the cross-customer pattern, the cost of inaction, the smallest viable proposal, and what shipped. Key advice includes stating deployment counts as numbers, asking for a priority swap rather than added scope, and avoiding the single loud-customer story. Common follow-up questions and failure modes are listed, with a two-minute sample answer about a fictional AI support agent deployment.

Key points

  • OpenAI and Ramp job postings both make roadmap-changing feedback an explicit FDE responsibility.
  • Five-part answer structure: signal, pattern, cost of inaction, minimal proposal, outcome.
  • State deployment counts early; numbers turn advocacy into evidence.
  • Ask for a swap in existing priorities, not a new item on top.
  • Common failure: one customer's request with no pattern evidence or stated trade-offs.

How to answer

Some employers write this loop into the role. Plan for the second half: “how did you make the case” asks how you persuaded a team that owed you nothing. Five moves:

  • The signal. What you saw at one customer, as an observation, not a feature request: “They had three people copying approvals into a spreadsheet.”
  • The pattern. How many customers, and how you checked: other deployments’ code, support tickets, a message to the other FDEs. Separate the need from the solution each customer asked for.
  • The cost of doing nothing, in the product team’s terms. Engineering weeks on one-off workarounds, deals stalled, renewals at risk, support load.
  • The proposal. The smallest product change that covers most cases, what you brought to it (a prototype, test data, design-partner customers), and the one decision you asked for.
  • What changed. What they decided, what they gave up for it, when it shipped, and what happened to the one-off versions.

Say the count early, as a number of deployments, not “several customers”. The number moves you from advocate to evidence.

Find out what the product team already committed to, and ask for a swap, not a new priority on top.

The trap is the loud-customer story. If one large customer drove it, say so, and what made it right for the others.

Follow-ups

What the interviewer may ask next, once your first answer is on the table.

  • How did you tell a pattern across customers from one loud customer?
  • What did the product team push back on, and were they right about any of it?
  • What did you keep building for customers as a one-off while you waited, and what happened to it once the product change shipped?

Where answers go wrong

  • Tells the story of one customer’s request that the product team happened to build, with no evidence of a pattern and no case beyond “the customer really wanted it”.
  • Treats filing a ticket or raising it in a channel as making the case, and never says what the product team decided, what they traded away for it, or what shipped.

Answer this in two minutes

Write the answer you would say out loud. The clock starts with your first word.

Illustrative answer about a fictional project

I was a forward deployed engineer at a company that sold an AI support agent to retailers. At my third deployment, a home-goods retailer, the support director wouldn’t let us change the agent’s instructions without a week of manual testing: two of her staff replayed about 80 old conversations by hand and compared answers in a spreadsheet.
