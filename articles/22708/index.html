What's the difference between FDE and SIers or contract development? The gap between "building and delivering" and "keeping things running in the field."

Original title: FDEとSIer・受託開発は何が違うのか。「作って納める」と「現場で動かし続ける」の差。

株式会社Respectify · 2026-08-04 · 16 min read

Summary

This article compares traditional contracted development (SIer/outsourced development) with FDE-style support, focusing specifically on marketing and sales implementation rather than core system projects. It argues that contracted development works well when requirements can be fixed upfront, but marketing and sales work involves frequently shifting requirements, many small deliverables, and speed as a core value, making the estimate-contract-deliver-accept model a poor fit. FDE-style support instead uses weekly demos, continuous responsibility for production systems, and quasi-delegation contracts with fixed monthly fees that absorb changing priorities. The author proposes three decision questions: whether requirements will hold for six months, whether output is one large deliverable or many small ones, and who controls go-ahead decisions. Citing IPA, MIC, and Teikoku Databank data on Japan's DX talent shortage and stalled generative AI adoption, the article concludes that companies can reasonably use both models in parallel, assigning stable projects to vendors and ongoing marketing/sales implementation to FDE-style teams.

Key points

  • Contracted development assumes fixed requirements; it suits core system rebuilds.
  • Marketing/sales implementation changes weekly, with many small deliverables.
  • FDE-style support uses weekly demos and fixed-fee quasi-delegation contracts.
  • Three questions guide the choice: requirement stability, deliverable shape, decision control.
  • Japanese firms face DX talent shortages and AI pilots stalling before production.

The question "How does FDE differ from SIers and contract development?" is one that most business owners ask when they first encounter the term FDE (Forward Deployed Engineer; the overseas usage of this role type is explained in detail in our companion article, "What Is an FDE"). This comparison itself is nothing new, and several Japanese-language explainer articles already exist. However, most of them remain at an abstract level aimed at IT development in general, covering things like "the difference between fixed-price contracts and quasi-mandate contracts" or "on-site versus off-site work." For business owners with hands-on experience commissioning work from SIers or production companies, what they really want to know is something more concrete. What their companies commission is not a core system overhaul, but the day-to-day implementation of marketing and sales that keeps moving: adding LPs to a website, organizing CRM workflows, mass-producing content, and automating sales and marketing operations. When we narrow the focus to this domain, how far does the contract development model work, and where does it start to break down? This article focuses on that single point of comparison.

How the Contract Development Model Works

Contract development with SIers and production companies is a system that treats a series of steps—defining requirements, producing an estimate, signing a contract, developing, delivering, and accepting the deliverable—as a single unit. The "Information System Model Transactions and Contracts" document, which the Information-technology Promotion Agency (IPA) revised and published based on the Ministry of Economy, Trade and Industry's model contract, is a model contract template that breaks this process into stages and clarifies what responsibilities the client company and the IT vendor should bear at each stage (see: IPA "Information System Model Transactions and Contracts (Second Edition)" (2020)). According to an article by a legal specialty media outlet explaining this model, the appropriate contract type differs between stages where "the deliverable cannot be concretely envisioned at the start of the process," such as requirements definition, and stages where the form of the deliverable is already fixed, such as detailed design, implementation, and testing (see: BUSINESS LAWYERS "Should System Development Contracts Use Fixed-Price or Quasi-Mandate?" (2023)). We cover contract types and cost structures in detail in a separate article, "Cost Structure and Contract Types for FDE-Style Support," so we will not go into them here.

What is important to understand here are the conditions under which this system works well. Contract development is strong for work where requirements can be fixed as a single block before work begins, and where those fixed requirements do not change significantly until completion. Examples include core system overhauls, building business systems with clear workflows, and one-off corporate website production with settled specifications. Fixing requirements takes effort, but once they are locked in, work can proceed as planned based on the estimate and contract. The client only needs to prepare specifications and acceptance criteria, then simply wait for delivery. In other words, the strength of this system rests on the premise that "requirements do not move." If requirements change, a new estimate and contract amendment become necessary each time, and the system's inherent strength turns into weakness.

Why Contract Development Struggles in Marketing and Sales

Marketing and sales implementation is a domain where the premise that "requirements do not move" rarely holds from the start. There are three main reasons.

First, requirements change frequently. Ad performance, customer reactions in sales meetings, competitor moves, strategic pivots in management meetings—marketing and sales teams are constantly absorbing changes in the external environment. In a 2026 survey on agile marketing, 89% of marketers practicing agile methods reported reviewing and adjusting their plans at least monthly, a 23-point gap compared to the 66% of those not practicing it (see: AgileSherpas "The 2026 State of Agile Marketing Report" (2026)). The same survey points out that the difference between teams that can respond to change and those that cannot shows up in whether they are perpetually operating a quarter behind. The contract development approach, where development proceeds based on requirements fixed six months earlier, and the on-the-ground reality of marketing and sales, where plans shift weekly if not monthly, are fundamentally misaligned in their time horizons.

Second, deliverables are small and numerous. A core system overhaul justifies treating requirements definition through acceptance as one large project. But marketing and sales implementation is an accumulation of small tasks: adding a single LP, tidying up one CRM input field, replacing the copy of a nurturing email, setting up one automated report. If each of these is commissioned as a separate contract project, the heavy procedures of estimating, contracting, and acceptance must be repeated for each one, making the procedural cost disproportionately heavy relative to the size of the work.

Third, speed itself becomes the value. In marketing and sales implementation, getting something out quickly matters as much as building it accurately. Reflecting a well-performing campaign tactic in an LP the following week, or revising sales materials as soon as a pattern of lost deals emerges—these kinds of moves are a poor fit for the contract development approach of fixing requirements before starting work, and by the time requirements are settled, the opportunity itself has passed.

These structural issues surface, for example, when a Series A to B scale B2B SaaS company commissions a production company to improve LPs on its service website. Even if an estimate and contract are signed based on the requirements at the time of the request, the sales team may request a new messaging angle before work begins, or the team may want to change the page structure after seeing ad performance. Each time, additional estimates and contract amendments go back and forth, work is delayed, and by the time it ships, market conditions themselves have changed. It is not that the vendor is slow to respond; rather, the system's premise of fixing requirements upfront and the nature of the work, where requirements keep moving, are fundamentally mismatched.

How the FDE Model Differs Structurally

The FDE approach does not fix requirements upfront; instead, it assumes continuous building aligned with on-the-ground priorities. Overseas explainer articles describe an FDE's week as starting with a customer discovery session on Monday, interspersed with hands-on implementation time, and moving back and forth within the same week through production operations and incident response. What distinguishes it from traditional project-based development is that involvement does not end when a project is delivered; the engineer remains continuously responsible for something that keeps running in production (see: Exponent, "What Is a Forward Deployed Engineer? Complete 2026 Guide" (2026)).

What supports this approach is the order of operations: showing something working before estimating. Rather than finalizing requirements in writing before starting, the cycle runs weekly: build something small, show it to the field, and decide what to build next based on the reaction. This weekly demo cadence is covered in detail in a separate article, "The Weekly Sprint and Weekly Demo Approach".

The contract structure is also designed to fit this approach. Most FDE-style support uses a quasi-delegation (jun-i-nin) contract, whose purpose is the performance of the work itself rather than a guaranteed deliverable, priced as a fixed monthly fee. This is because even if priorities change from week to week, there is no need to renegotiate the contract each time; changes can be treated as events within the scope of the contract. The detailed mechanics of this contract type and how to think about costs are summarized in the aforementioned "Cost Structure and Contract Types for FDE-Style Support".

Criteria for Choosing Between the Two

The criterion that emerges from this comparison is not which is superior, but determining which better matches the nature of the work you want to delegate. It becomes easier to decide by reframing it as three questions. The first: can you describe the requirements in the same form six months from now? If yes, contracted development fits; if not, or if you know they will certainly change within six months, quasi-delegation / FDE-style fits. The second: is the deliverable one large handoff, or an accumulation of small pieces? Work that ends with acceptance of a single finished product suits contracted development; work where small daily implementations accumulate suits the FDE style. The third: who controls the decision to start? If you want to fix decisions upfront in a specification document, choose contracted development; if you want to keep making decisions on the ground while looking at working software, the FDE style matches reality.

Here is a concrete decision flow using a hypothetical example. Suppose a B2B SaaS company is considering two projects simultaneously: a "full corporate website redesign" and "ongoing production of nurture content linked to CRM." For the former, the content—company profile, history, careers pages—is already fixed, with little prospect of major change in six months, so it can answer "yes" to the first question (can requirements be described the same way in six months?). The deliverable is also a single handoff—the complete site—so the second question also yields an answer favoring contracted development. In this case, it is rational to solidify the specification and acceptance criteria and place a fixed-price order with an SIer or production company. The latter, the nurture content, by nature changes themes and messaging weekly based on metrics like meeting conversion and open rates, so it must answer "we cannot guarantee the same form in six months" to the first question. Its deliverables are also accumulations of small units—a single email, a single landing page—so the second question also tilts toward small, incremental work. In this case, rather than contracted development that fixes requirements upfront, the FDE-style / quasi-delegation approach—building working outputs while aligning priorities each time—matches reality better. The fact that the answer changes even within the same company depending on the nature of the project is the practical advantage of using these three questions. As for the third question (who controls the decision to start), the redesign project can proceed adequately according to a specification fixed in advance, but nurture content requires continuously reflecting the sales team's reactions to delivery results into the following week's production, so it cannot function without a structure for ongoing on-the-ground decision-making. When one company holds both types of projects, assigning one to contracted development and the other to the FDE style is not a contradiction at all; rather, it is a rational division of labor in line with the three questions.

The separate article "Should AI Implementation Be Outsourced or Built In-House?" also covers criteria for sorting out the binary choices of outsourcing versus in-house, or contracted versus FDE-style. In practice, using both in combination works perfectly well. A combination such as ordering a core system overhaul as fixed-price work from an SIer once specs are fixed, while continuously advancing the marketing sites, CRM operations, content mass production, and sales/marketing automation built on top of it in the FDE style, is a choice aligned with the conditions each mechanism handles best.

The Barriers to Building In-House, and the Axis of Retaining Knowledge Within the Company

Before considering contracted development versus the FDE style, you also need to verify whether building it yourself is a realistic option in the first place. Many companies stall at the binary choice of outsourcing implementation or handling it in-house.

The first barrier to in-house development is talent. In IPA's "DX Trends 2025," which surveyed companies in Japan, the US, and Germany, 85.1% of Japanese companies answered that the quantity of personnel driving DX was "somewhat insufficient" or "significantly insufficient"—an outstandingly high figure compared to 23.8% in the US and 44.6% in Germany. This level has remained nearly unchanged from 83.5% in the 2022 survey and 85.7% in the 2023 survey, so it is not a single-year figure. (See: IPA, "DX Trends 2025: Exploring Directions for Creating Results through Japan-US-Germany Comparison" (June 2025)) For many companies, in-house development that begins with hiring becomes a bottleneck before work even starts.

There are also gaps in how widely AI has been adopted and how firmly it has become embedded in operations. According to a survey by the Ministry of Internal Affairs and Communications, 86.4% of Japanese companies use generative AI in some part of their business. On the other hand, 27% of Japanese companies answered that they have "no organizational initiatives" for business transformation through generative AI—a higher level than the US at 1.4%, Germany at 4.9%, and China at 2.6%. (Reference: Ministry of Internal Affairs and Communications, "Outline of the 2026 White Paper on Information and Communications" (July 2026)) Similarly, in a March 2026 survey by Teikoku Databank (10,312 valid responses), only 34.5% of companies reported "actively using" generative AI, and among companies that had not yet adopted it, the challenges cited were "lack of specialized talent and know-how" at 41.3% and "unclear scope of use" at 40.0%. (Reference: Teikoku Databank, "Survey on Corporate Trends Regarding Generative AI (March 2026)" (2026)) In other words, companies tend to stall at the stage of embedding AI into their operations after getting started.

There are also easily overlooked points when outsourcing to external parties. A commonly seen pattern is one where the initial PoC goes smoothly with an external vendor, but the scope of the engagement is then restructured at the stage of integrating it into production operations. PoC deliverables exist to confirm that something works, and their premises differ from an actual implementation that accounts for real business data, exception handling, and integration with existing systems. If this gap goes unnoticed, additional orders arise during production rollout, and costs swell beyond the initial estimate. Furthermore, if the entire implementation is left to an external party, the knowledge leaves the company along with the engagement, making it difficult to drive the next round of improvements in-house.

For this reason, we recommend adding the following two criteria to the decision-making framework discussed earlier. What follows is based on our observations from client engagements.

  • Does implementation know-how need to be retained in-house?: For a one-off tool introduction, outsourcing is perfectly fine. If AI adoption is expected to spread across multiple business functions, you need to accumulate knowledge of how to apply it to your own operations through the initial implementation. In interviews with leading companies and experts featured in the Ministry of Internal Affairs and Communications' white paper, the view was expressed that management should treat AI as an asset whose value grows as know-how and insights accumulate over time. (Reference: Ministry of Internal Affairs and Communications, "Outline of the 2026 White Paper on Information and Communications" (July 2026)) This kind of asset accumulation rarely happens when implementation is entirely outsourced.
  • How deeply should it be embedded into operations?: An analysis published in the Ministry of Finance's PR magazine shows that the more companies exceeded expectations in the effects of generative AI adoption, the more they tended to go as far as formally integrating it as part of their business processes. The analysis notes that effects differ between these companies and those that remain at basic uses such as summarization and document search. (Reference: Ministry of Finance, "Finance" August 2025 issue, column "Generative AI Adoption Is Not the Goal" (August 2025)) The deeper the embedding, the harder it becomes to fit a fixed-specification-first approach.

Considering these two points, the value of the FDE model is not limited to compensating for the weaknesses of contracted development. Its essence lies in advancing implementation in line with frontline priorities while leaving behind systems and processes that enable the company to continue improving on its own. Because the essence is transferring a model of in-house development—not whether someone is stationed on-site under contract—comparing it as equivalent to a staffing contract leads to misunderstanding.

Conclusion

  • Contracted development operates on a framework of fixing requirements upfront and proceeding through milestones of estimation, contracting, delivery, and acceptance, making it well suited to work where requirements do not change.
  • Marketing and sales implementations involve requirements that change frequently, deliverables that are small and numerous, and speed itself as the source of value, making them a poor fit for the contracted development model premised on fixed requirements.
  • The FDE model keeps building in line with frontline priorities, shows working results first through weekly demos, and supports this way of working through quasi-delegation contracts with fixed monthly fees.
  • The criteria for deciding are threefold: whether requirements will remain unchanged six months later, whether the deliverable is one large deliverable or an accumulation of small ones, and whether you want to fix the go-ahead decision upfront or keep deciding at the frontline.
  • A division of labor—contracted development for core systems and the FDE model for ongoing marketing and sales implementations—is entirely viable.

Whichever you choose between an SIer and the FDE model, having a concrete picture of the process after work actually begins will keep your judgment steady. You can review the flow from kickoff to production rollout in "AI Business Implementation Roadmap: A Schedule That Doesn't End at the PoC".
