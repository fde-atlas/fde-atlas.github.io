How to Choose an FDE Role: Look at Outcome Ownership, Not Titles

Key Takeaways

  • Palantir created this role in the early 2010s, when it was called Delta; today the FDE label covers many very different jobs.
  • A reliable test is: who is accountable for a system that actually runs and that customers actually use, not just for an eval that passes once.
  • At Palantir, accountability is written into multi-year, outcome-based contracts; before trusting a title, look at how the company makes money.

In the early 2010s, when Palantir created the role of "software engineers embedded with customers," it didn't call it FDE—it called it "Delta."

This detail is worth remembering, because it shows the name was never the core of the job. More than a decade later, "Forward Deployed Engineer (FDE)" has become a hot recruiting label, and the more widespread it becomes, the harder it is to say what the actual work behind it is.

If you're an engineer with two to eight years of experience considering a transition, the real question is: when the label can no longer be trusted, where should you invest your energy?

From investors to authors who have studied this profession to critics of the trend, the answer is nearly unanimous: the ability to take on a customer's ambiguous problem and stay accountable until it produces results.

The original vision: one customer, many capabilities

Palantir distinguished between two types of engineers. Devs focused on "one capability, many customers," building features that worked across all customers. Delta was the opposite: one customer, many capabilities, integrating everything an organization needed to achieve its goals.

Palantir's description of the job also revolved around the word "own": working in small teams, owning end-to-end execution of high-risk projects. The CIO summarized the original vision in one sentence: FDEs are how Palantir delivers outcomes, not tools, to customers.

The keyword is "outcomes." Tools can be delivered and the team walks away. Outcomes only exist when the customer actually gets what they need.

One title, at least three jobs

As the label spread, its meaning was diluted. The Pragmatic Engineer noted that roles nearly identical to FDE may still go by many different names. The CIO put it more bluntly: "Forward Deployed Engineer" is a vague term whose meaning depends on each company's business model.

FullStack counted three distinct jobs sharing the FDE title. The two easiest to distinguish sit at opposite ends: builder FDEs build production systems in customer environments, while Sales Engineer-type FDEs mainly run demos. Both can list the same title on their profiles, yet their day-to-day work has nothing in common.

The reverse also happens. Chad Horenfeldt argues that renaming customer success teams as FDEs is a trap: once you go deep into solution details, customer success managers (CSMs) have no time to understand the outcomes customers want. By this logic, changing the name itself doesn't add accountability for outcomes.

The evals passed—who's still there?

FullStack offers a concise test: whether you're accountable for a working system and for customers actually using it, not just for a successful eval.

Insight Partners defines the role the same way: FDEs take on ambiguous, undefined problems, build last-mile solutions, and own the outcomes. One investor quoted by Insight put it even more briefly: everything must revolve around outcomes.

Imagine a scenario. You're sent to an insurance company to build an agent that reads claims documents. Three weeks later, the eval set reaches the threshold both sides agreed on, the demo goes smoothly, and the customer signs off on the pilot.

For a demo-type role, the work is done. For an outcome-accountable role, you're only halfway there: is the agent connected to the real document repository? Do claims specialists open it every morning? Who fixes it when the model misreads a certain type of document?

If a month later only two people are using it, the customer hasn't gotten an outcome, even though the evals once passed.

The FDE's real work lies between the evals passing and real users depending on the system every day. This requires technical skills like integration, data, and reliability, as well as people skills: understanding customer workflows, winning user buy-in, and being willing to listen to their complaints.

Accountability can't be faked

a16z explains that Palantir's model works because beneath the customized work for each customer there is a real platform; any company that copies FDE-style deployment without such a platform will end up as a services company.

Insight also lists "FDEs are just professional services with a new name" among common misconceptions. The two views converge on one point: embedded work is only valuable when it's tied to something bigger than a single contract.

a16z also points to a deeper layer: Palantir's revenue comes from multi-year contracts tied to outcomes. Here, accountability isn't a slogan—it's written into the business model. When a company's revenue depends on whether customers achieve outcomes, it's hard for embedded engineers to stop at the demo.

So before accepting an offer, don't just read the job description—look at how the company makes money from customers. If success is measured by the number of pilots signed, whatever the role is called, it will likely steer you toward demos.

Pitfalls that lead you to the wrong role

The most common pitfall is trusting the title. The same FDE may refer to a builder, a Sales Engineer, or a third type of role; inferring the work from the job title alone is no different from guessing.

The second pitfall is subtler: treating a signed pilot or a passed eval as the finish line. In the insurance example, the hardest part starts exactly then. If a candidate describes a project that ended at the signing meeting, they're implicitly signaling they never saw it through.

The third pitfall is assuming being close to the customer is enough. Horenfeldt's warning about CSMs reveals the other side: getting stuck in solution details while forgetting to ask what the customer wants to achieve is also not accountability for outcomes.

Where to invest when labels keep changing

Start by reading the job description. Watch for whether words like "own," "production," "adoption," and "end users" appear alongside the customer environment. If all the metrics are demos, POCs, or wins, be wary: that's a different role—maybe right for you, but know what you're choosing.

In interviews, ask two questions directly: "After the pilot passes the evals, who is accountable until real users use it every day?" and "Why do customers pay the company?" The answers say more than any title.

On your resume, frame things in terms of outcomes, not deliverables. Instead of writing "built a RAG agent PoC," use a template like this: "Integrated the agent into the customer's claims processing workflow; [number] weeks later, [number] daily active users; personally handled failures when the model misread documents."

If you don't have numbers like these yet, the fastest way to practice is to put a side project into the hands of a few real users and personally fix every issue that comes up.

The name Delta gave way to FDE, and that label may change again in the future. What you can take away from every renaming is evidence that you kept a system running until customers actually used it.
