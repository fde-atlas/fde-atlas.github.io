What Is a White Hat Hacker? Legal Requirements, Job Duties, and How Companies Can Use Them

Original title: ホワイトハッカーとは？合法の条件・仕事内容と企業の活用法

FDE総研 · 2026-11-04 · 13 min read

Summary

This Japanese-language article from FDE Sōken explains what a white hacker is, distinguishing them from black and gray hackers by authorization and purpose rather than skill. It outlines three minimum conditions for legal activity (written permission, explicit scope, reporting rules), relevant Japanese laws like the Unauthorized Access Prohibition Act, and typical work: vulnerability diagnosis, penetration testing, red team exercises, and incident response. It compares white hackers with FDEs and advises companies on choosing between outsourcing, bug bounties, and in-house development based on release frequency and data sensitivity.

Key points

  • White, gray, and black hackers differ by owner permission and how vulnerabilities are handled, not by technique.
  • Legal activity requires written permission, explicit scope, and agreed reporting/publication rules.
  • Even well-intentioned unauthorized access can violate Japan's Unauthorized Access Prohibition Act.
  • Core work spans vulnerability diagnosis, penetration testing, red team exercises, and incident response support.
  • Companies should combine outsourcing, bug bounties (HackerOne, Bugcrowd), and in-house hiring based on release frequency and sensitivity.

Both use the same skills, but differ in permission, purpose, and handling of results. Whether permission exists is what separates legal from illegal.

  • Agree in writing with the authorized person: Obtain documented permission from the person who holds administrative rights over the system
  • Define scope and rules: Decide the target hosts, period, permitted techniques, prohibited actions, and emergency contacts
  • Stay within scope: Do not touch systems outside the agreed scope, and keep records of your work
  • Report the results: Communicate discovered vulnerabilities, their severity, and prevention measures only to the client
  • Verify remediation: Re-test after fixes and dispose of acquired data as agreed

If even one of these steps is missing, even a well-intentioned test may violate the Unauthorized Computer Access Law.

The higher tiers can be started sooner, while the lower tiers build more lasting in-house capability. FDE Soken recommends choosing based on three axes: scoping power, continuity, and cost.

What Is a White Hacker? Differences from Black Hackers and Security Engineers

The difference between white hackers and black hackers is not skill, but "permission and purpose." FDE Soken distinguishes security engineers by the scope of their role.

The word "hacker" originally referred to someone with deep expertise in computers. From there, malicious intruders came to be called "black hats" and those who use their skills for defense "white hats." In Japan, the term "white hacker" has become established, but it is not a legal qualification or official job title.

How Do White Hackers Differ from Black Hackers and Gray Hackers?

The three differ in whether they have the owner's permission and how they handle discovered vulnerabilities. The techniques themselves are almost identical.

CategoryOwner's permissionPurposeHandling of discovered vulnerabilities
White hackerObtained in writing in advanceDefense and remediationReports according to contract and rules; disclosure only by agreement
Gray hackerNot obtainedOften claims curiosity or good intentionsOne-sided notification or disclosure (may be illegal)
Black hackerNot obtainedMoney, destruction, information theftExploitation, sale, extortion

Gray hackers deserve particular caution. Even when someone says they "found a hole out of goodwill and reported it," unauthorized intrusion may violate the Unauthorized Computer Access Law. Good intentions do not make unauthorized actions legal.

How Do White Hackers Differ from Security Engineers?

Security engineers design and operate defensive mechanisms, while white hackers test those mechanisms from an attacker's perspective.

A security engineer's work involves designing authentication infrastructure, operating WAFs and EDRs, monitoring logs, handling incidents, and generally building and maintaining defensive systems. White hackers mainly perform penetration testing and vulnerability assessments, checking those systems for holes through an attacker's eyes. In practice, one person often handles both, and job postings may list both titles, such as "Security Engineer (Assessment)."

What Conditions Make White Hacker Activity Legal?

Legality depends on "who" permitted "what" and "within what scope." FDE Soken considers three points the minimum requirements: written permission, explicit scope, and reporting rules.

Which Laws Apply in Japan?

The main relevant laws are the Unauthorized Computer Access Law and computer-related crimes under the Penal Code. In both cases, treatment changes significantly depending on whether permission or legitimate reason exists.

Law / provisionMain covered actsRelation to white hacker activity
Unauthorized Computer Access Law (Act on the Prohibition of Unauthorized Computer Access)Unauthorized use of others' IDs and passwords, intrusion exploiting security holesWith the access administrator's consent, it does not constitute "unauthorized access"
Penal Code Articles 168-2 and 168-3 (crimes concerning unauthorized electromagnetic records)Creation, provision, acquisition, and storage of so-called virusesRequires "without legitimate reason"; assessment and research purposes are taken into account
Penal Code Article 234-2 (crime of damaging computers or obstructing business)Obstructing business, e.g., by shutting down systemsEven permitted assessments can become problematic if out-of-scope load halts business

The statutory penalty for unauthorized access under the Unauthorized Computer Access Law is "imprisonment for up to three years or a fine of up to one million yen" (the June 2025 Penal Code amendment changed "imprisonment" to "detention"). Regarding crimes involving unauthorized electromagnetic records, there is the so-called Coinhive case, which contested the placement of cryptocurrency mining scripts on websites; the Supreme Court acquitted the defendant on January 20, 2022. Since judgments of "legitimate reason" vary case by case, the basic rule is not to venture into gray areas on your own.

What Are the Three Conditions for Operating Legally?

The minimum requirements are three: (1) written permission from an authorized person, (2) explicit target scope and methods, and (3) rules for reporting and disclosure.

  • Written permission from an authorized person: The system owner or access administrator must consent. For cloud environments, also check the provider's assessment policies, such as those of AWS or Google Cloud.
  • Explicit scope: Specify target domains, IPs, periods, and prohibited techniques (DoS, social engineering, etc.) in the contract or rules.
  • Reporting and disclosure rules: Agree in advance on where to report findings, an embargo period before disclosure prior to fixes, and how data is handled.

In bug bounty programs, the program's policy serves the roles of (1) through (3). If you discover a vulnerability on a third party's site that has not requested testing, in Japan you can report it through IPA's "Information Security Early Warning Partnership." This is safer than deepening your own testing or contacting the operator directly.

According to FDE Soken's assessment, the practice most likely to lead to an incident is neither ① nor ③, but ②. When a client's contact person verbally says "please also take a look at this while you're at it," that scope is often outside the contract. In services like VACAN, which integrate with cameras and sensors at client facilities, the client's assets, the company's own assets, and the vendor's assets all get mixed together in a single system. That is why the approach of first establishing "who is the administrator of this device" is effective.

What does a white hat hacker do?

The main work consists of four areas: vulnerability assessment, penetration testing, red team exercises, and incident response support. FDE Soken distinguishes them by their "deliverables."

What are the main tasks and deliverables?

Each type of work differs in the deliverables produced and how the client is involved. Whether planning a career or placing an order, the quickest way to tell them apart is to look at the deliverables first.

TaskPurposeMain deliverablesClient involvement
Vulnerability assessment (Web/platform)Comprehensively identify known vulnerabilitiesAssessment report (with severity ratings based on CVSS, etc.)Scope agreement before starting and a reporting session
Penetration testingVerify whether a specific goal (e.g., reaching confidential data) can be achievedReport on attack scenarios and intrusion pathsConsultation with the client from the goal design stage
Red team exerciseEvaluate the organization's defenses, including detection and response capabilitiesExercise report and improvement recommendationsConducted over a long period with only a limited number of stakeholders aware
Incident response and forensic supportIdentify the extent of damage and the root causeInvestigation report and preventive measuresClose collaboration with the client during emergencies
Bug bounty participation (individual)Find vulnerabilities through public programsVulnerability reports in accordance with program rulesInteraction with the operator via the platform

How does a single engagement proceed?

Assessment engagements generally proceed in the order of "scope agreement → planning → execution → reporting → re-assessment." The consensus-building before and after can take more time than the technical work itself.

  • Scope agreement: Define the targets, duration, prohibited activities, and emergency contact points
  • Preliminary research and planning: Understand the architecture and specifications, and determine test perspectives
  • Assessment and verification: Combine tools with manual work to conduct verification
  • Reporting: Compile reproduction conditions, impact, severity, and remediation proposals into a report
  • Re-assessment: Confirm whether the fixes are effective

From hands-on experience, white hat hackers who are highly rated possess "communication skills" equal to their "discovery skills." Whether they can write remediation proposals at a level of granularity that lets development teams fix issues immediately greatly affects the value of the report. This point also overlaps with the work of FDEs.

How does the path to becoming a white hat hacker change based on years of experience, current job, and English proficiency?

While the entry point varies depending on prerequisites, the common foundation is "fundamentals of networking, OS, and the Web, plus experience in legal practice environments." FDE Soken divides the paths based on whether one's current job experience can be used as-is.

What are the recommended paths by years of experience and current job?

For beginners, starting with study and practice is realistic; for those with infrastructure or development experience, entering through assessments in their area of expertise makes sense.

PrerequisitesLikely entry pathWhat to prioritize learning
Students and beginnersCTFs (e.g., SECCON), IPA's "Security Camp," legal practice environmentsTCP/IP, Linux, how the Web works
1-3 years of web development experienceWeb application assessment roles or assessment firmsOWASP Top 10, authentication and session design
Infrastructure/SRE experiencePlatform assessments, cloud security evaluationCloud IAM, network configuration verification
SIer/IT consultingSecurity consulting, assessment PMRisk evaluation, report writing, contract practices
In-house SE/IT operationsInternal CSIRT, vendor managementLog analysis, incident response procedures

As for certifications, the national certification Information Processing Security Assurance Support Engineer (IPA), OSCP (OffSec) known for its practical exam, and CEH (EC-Council) which tests knowledge frameworks, are frequently mentioned. However, certifications alone do not determine hiring. Many candidates also present practice records and CTF results alongside them.

How do English proficiency and company size change things?

English proficiency determines whether you can directly access the latest vulnerability information and overseas bug bounties. Company size affects how deeply you can specialize.

  • English proficiency: CVE information, tool documentation, and overseas conference materials are primarily in English. Reading comprehension gives you an edge in the speed of information gathering. Overseas bug bounties also require the ability to write reports in English.
  • Large companies and dedicated assessment firms: They have specialized teams for each type of assessment, allowing deep specialization.
  • Startups and operating companies: You often handle a wide range from assessments to defensive design and internal training, taking on more of an FDE-like "jack-of-all-trades" character.

How should companies utilize white hat hackers? What are the differences between outsourcing, bug bounties, and internal development?

Outsourcing excels in comprehensiveness, bug bounties in diverse perspectives, and internal development in continuity. FDE Soken recommends choosing based on two axes: release frequency and confidentiality.

How do the three options compare?

Rather than one being the correct answer, it is common to combine them according to the product's stage.

StrengthsCan comprehensively verify a defined scopeGain diverse perspectives from many researchersCan be integrated into the development process for continuous response
CostsPer engagementRewards and operating costs (performance-based)Personnel and training costs
Best suited forBefore releases, audits or requests from business partnersContinuously verifying public servicesHigh release frequency with in-house development as the focus
CaveatsLimited to the assessment at a point in timeRule design and intake handling require effortHiring difficulty, evaluation tends to be subjective
Typical meansSpecialized assessment providersHackerOne, Bugcrowd, and IssueHunt domesticallyInternal CSIRT, security champion programs

What should be decided at implementation?

The first things to decide are "target scope," "who handles intake and fixes," and "disclosure policy." Starting without these means that even when reports come in, they will not lead to fixes.

  • Scope of diagnosis or disclosure (production vs. staging environment, how to handle external service integrations)
  • The point of contact for receiving reports, and target remediation deadlines by severity
  • Policy on publishing vulnerability information (whether disclosure after the fix is allowed)
  • Agreement with administrators of outsourced or integrated systems

For services like VACAN that integrate with external facilities and equipment, FDE Soken does not recommend jumping straight into a bug bounty. We believe the safe order is to first solidify the scope through outsourced diagnosis, then narrow the public scope and expand into a bug bounty. The more integration partners a service has, the harder it is to fully specify in the terms "how far you may go." For organizational building, also refer to FDE's hiring approach and How to implement FDE.

How do white hackers differ from FDEs? Who is not a good fit?

A white hacker is "someone who verifies whether things can be broken," while an FDE is "someone who builds things that run in the customer's field." Comparing them using FDE Soken's three axes, the scope of permission and the deliverables clearly differ.

How do they differ when viewed across the three comparison axes?

Both go deep into the customer's environment, but comparing them across the three axes of scope of permission, deliverables, and points of contact with the customer makes the difference in roles clear.

Comparison axisWhite hackerFDE (Forward Deployed Engineer)
Scope of permissionPermission for offensive testing (narrow and clearly defined)Authority for data integration and implementation (broad and continuous)
DeliverablesVulnerability reports and improvement recommendationsWorking systems and business improvements
Contact with the customerMainly before the start and at reportingDaily involvement through on-site presence or accompaniment
What is evaluatedDepth of discovery, reproducibility, quality of reportingProblem framing, speed of implementation, adoption in the field
Similar rolesSecurity engineer, auditorSWE, IT consultant, PdM

The FDE role that Palantir made famous is primarily about getting data platforms running in the customer's field. However, because the data handled is highly sensitive, security awareness is indispensable. Experience as a white hacker is useful when an FDE needs to judge "how strict to make the permission design." For FDE itself, see What is an FDE. Lessons learnable from security incidents are covered in FDE lessons from the GMO incident.

Who is not suited to being a white hacker?

People who feel stressed by rules and scope restrictions, or who are not good at steady testing and documentation, tend to be a poor fit.

  • People who are satisfied with "I got in when I tried": Those who cannot respect the permitted scope will not be trusted, no matter their technical skill.
  • People who find writing reports painful: Most deliverables are documents. Findings that do not lead to fixes lose their value.
  • People who find building things the most rewarding: In that case, an FDE or developer role may be a better fit. Also see Who is suited to being an FDE.
  • People who find continuous learning painful: Both attack techniques and defense technologies change rapidly, and knowledge quickly becomes outdated if you stop gathering information.

At FDE Soken, we believe that rather than choosing between white hacker and FDE, a career that accumulates both experiences—such as "an FDE with an attacker's eye" or "a white hacker who understands field operations"—also has value. How to enter from scratch is also explained in How to become an FDE with no prior experience.
