FDE Costs and Pricing Benchmarks: Setting the Right Budget by Role-Based Rates and TCO

Original title: FDEの費用・料金相場｜職位別単価とTCOで見る適正予算

FDX株式会社 · 2026-07-11 · 16 min read

Summary

This article by FDX Inc. lays out pricing benchmarks for Forward Deployed Engineers in Japan and how to judge whether a quote is reasonable. Monthly rates range from ¥1.5–2.5M for junior FDEs to ¥3–5M for senior ones, with 3–5 person teams at ¥10–20M per month. Contracts come in three structures (per-rate, team-based, and performance-linked), all requiring outcome definitions tied to business KPIs. Although FDE rates look 2–4x higher than SES rates, the author argues comparison should use 18–24 month TCO: including rework and failed handover costs, the FDE model (¥80–120M) usually beats a consultant-plus-SIer setup (¥80–135M). Hybrid models with internal talent working alongside external FDEs often offer the best 24-month TCO at ¥55–80M, and the article closes with ROI measurement steps and seven criteria for vetting quotes.

Key points

  • Senior FDEs cost ¥3–5M monthly; 3–5 person teams run ¥10–20M.
  • Contracts must tie outcome definitions to measurable business KPIs.
  • FDE rates are 2–4x SES, but the two cover different scopes of responsibility.
  • Over 18–24 months, FDE TCO usually undercuts consultant-plus-SIer arrangements.
  • Hybrid models (¥55–80M TCO) often balance speed and internal capability best.

What FDE costs are (3-line summary)

  • FDE costs are designed not as "payment for man-hours" but as "investment in business KPI improvement." Contracts generally combine a quasi-delegation agreement with performance-based compensation, and there are three structures: unit-price, team, and performance-based hybrid.
  • Monthly rates by role are roughly 3–5 million yen for senior FDEs, 2–3.5 million yen for mid-level, and 1.5–2.5 million yen for juniors. For a team of 3–5 members, the guideline is 10–20 million yen per month.
  • What should be compared is not the initial unit price but the 18–24 month TCO (total cost of ownership). When additional customization, reinvestment after failed operational handover, and the presence or absence of in-house capability assets are included, the FDE model is the cheapest in most cases.

Three structures of FDE costs | Unit price, team, and performance-based

The cost structure of an FDE (Forward Deployed Engineer) differs from both the "man-month rate" of SES and the "lump-sum contract" of SI. Without first understanding the contract structure, you cannot judge whether a quote is reasonable.

Structure 1: Unit-price (quasi-delegation)

The most basic form, where cost is determined by the monthly rate per FDE multiplied by the engagement period. It uses the same quasi-delegation contract as SES, but differs fundamentally in that deliverables are defined not as "fulfillment of man-hours" but as "improvement of business KPIs." The rate includes all four roles—business redesign, implementation, executive dialogue, and operational handover—so it can look expensive compared with a pure engineer rate.

Structure 2: Team

A setup such as "one senior FDE + two to three mid-level FDEs + spot participation by specialists," with a monthly fee set for the team as a whole. It is adopted for projects spanning multiple business domains or with large data volumes. The typical range is 10–20 million yen per month, with pricing determined by the division of roles across the team rather than a sum of unit prices.

Structure 3: Performance-based hybrid

A form that combines the fixed fee of a quasi-delegation agreement with performance-based compensation tied to business KPI improvement. For example, the contract explicitly states conditions such as "a performance fee of X million yen if inquiry-handling workload is reduced by 50%." Its advantage is that it keeps the fixed portion down while aligning the interests of the FDE side and the client.

Comparison table of the three structures

StructureHow cost is determinedSuitable projectsPoints to note

Common to all three structures is that deliverables being tied to business KPIs is a condition of an FDE contract. A quote without this definition cannot be called an FDE contract, no matter how low the price.

FDE monthly rates are determined by role (years of experience and the responsibilities one can carry). Market rates in Japan are as follows.

Basis of the rates | Relationship to salary ranges

The validity of these rate ranges can be confirmed by back-calculating from the salary ranges of FDE talent. In the Japanese market, FDE salaries are roughly 8–12 million yen for juniors, 12–18 million yen for mid-level, 18–25 million yen for senior, and 25–40 million yen for principals (see Integrating FDEs into your organization for details). Talent combining skills in all four areas—technology, business, domain, and communication—is scarce in the market, and this scarcity is reflected in the rates.

Hybrid setups lower external rates

When adopting a hybrid setup where external FDEs work alongside internal candidates for in-housing rather than leading externally, the external FDE's monthly fee often falls to around 2–4 million yen. This is because the external FDE's involvement decreases gradually as phases transition. The three-phase design of the hybrid model is detailed in "External FDE utilization vs internal development."

Cases where quotes deviate from market rates

There is a reason why quotes are significantly cheaper than the market rate. In most cases, it is one of the following: (1) in reality it is resident SES engineers with no business redesign scope included, (2) operational handover is a separate contract, or (3) the team is junior-heavy with senior FDE involvement of only a few hours per month. You should verify the scope and deliverable definitions, not the low unit price.

Cost comparison with SES, SI, and consulting

Judging that FDEs are "expensive" based on unit price alone is premature. When you line up the correspondence between unit prices and deliverable definitions across models, the meaning of the price difference becomes clear.

In absolute terms, FDE unit prices look 2–4 times higher than SES. However, SES unit prices include neither business redesign nor operational handover, and that responsibility falls on the client's own resources. Strategy consulting unit prices are at or above FDE levels, but implementation is not included. In other words, the four models are "different products," not "price differences for the same product," so side-by-side unit price comparison itself is not valid. Comparing by TCO | Total cost over 18–24 months

The correct yardstick for judging the reasonableness of FDE costs is not unit price but the 18–24 month TCO (Total Cost of Ownership). Taking a generative AI implementation project with business redesign (18 months) as an example, the cost structure breaks down as follows.

TCO of a strategy consultant + SIer configuration

  • Consulting proposal (3 months, 3–6 million yen per month): 10–15 million yen
  • SIer implementation (12 months, fixed-price contract): 50–80 million yen
  • Additional customization due to business fit gaps: 10–20 million yen
  • Reinvestment due to failed operational handover: 10–20 million yen
  • Total: 80–135 million yen, implementation period 18–24 months, low level of internal capability building achieved

TCO of the FDE model

  • One senior FDE + two mid-level FDEs working alongside internal candidates: 80–120 million yen
  • No additional customization, since business fit is built in from the design stage
  • Smooth operational handover, since internal candidates work alongside the FDEs
  • Total: 80–120 million yen, implementation period 12–15 months, high level of internal capability building achieved

Comparing the upper limits, the FDE model is about 15 million yen cheaper than the consultant + SIer configuration, with a period 3–9 months shorter. Even more important is that the internal capability assets that remain reduce costs from the second cycle onward.

24-month TCO by implementation model

Simulating the 24-month TCO across the three models—externally led, hybrid, and internally led—yields the following.

Model12 months24 monthsInternal capability assets
External FDE-led40–60 million yen80–120 million yenLimited
Hybrid35–50 million yen55–80 million yenHigh
Internally led20–30 million yen (with delayed results)45–65 million yenMaximum

The figures vary by project scale, but in terms of the balance between speed of results and internal capability assets, the hybrid model often offers the best cost-effectiveness over a 24-month TCO. The internally led model is cheapest on paper, but since talent development takes 12–18 months, you need to factor in the opportunity cost of delayed business impact.

"Hidden costs" that should be included in TCO

TCO comparison only becomes valid once you include costs that do not appear on quotes.

  • Losses from PoCs that go nowhere: the cost of PoCs that never reach implementation becomes a total sunk cost (see "AI PoC Failure Patterns")
  • Client-side management man-hours: with SES, responsibility for business understanding, design, and management remains with the client, consuming internal resources
  • Re-ordering costs: if operational handover fails, every subsequent modification requires re-ordering from an external vendor
  • Opportunity loss: if implementation is delayed by 6 months, the business improvement benefits during that period are zero

How to measure cost-effectiveness | ROI in practice

Since FDE costs are an investment, you should estimate the payback before submitting for internal approval. In practice, measure it in the following three steps.

Step 1: Baseline measurement of business KPIs

Measure the current values of the target business processes before starting work. Choose metrics that allow improvements to be converted into monetary terms, such as monthly man-hours, number of items processed, lead time, and error rates. Without a baseline, you cannot prove the magnitude of improvement after implementation, and performance-based compensation judgments cannot be made either.

Step 2: Converting expected impact into monetary terms

As a rule of thumb, if a process requiring 1,000 hours per month achieves a 50% reduction in man-hours, and the labor cost rate is set at 5,000 yen per hour, that amounts to an annual reduction of 30 million yen. This is only a hypothetical calculation example, but by accumulating the formula "reduced man-hours × labor cost rate × 12 months" for each target business process, you obtain an annual benefit figure that can be directly compared against FDE costs.

As a concrete example, in a HubSpot CRM integration plus meeting-notes linkage project that FDX supported for a talent-related company (several dozen employees), CRM input workload was reduced by approximately 90% (company's own estimate; FDX itself operates the same mechanism for its internal sales operations). Also, in an AI-based inquiry handling project for an e-commerce business, AI reached a level of answering 83% of 1,200 inquiries per month. Once you can obtain estimated reduction rates and actual processing ratios like these, converting them into monetary value is straightforward.

Step 3: Calculating the payback period and making the continuation decision

Calculate the payback period from the annual benefit amount and FDE costs (TCO). For projects involving business process redesign, review KPI progress quarterly, and decide in advance the course-correction and exit criteria for cases where improvement falls below plan. The main purpose of building outcome definitions into the contract is to prevent a situation where "the contract is simply renewed without knowing whether results are being achieved."

How to set the budget and how to evaluate estimates

5 steps for budget planning

  • Selecting target operations and measuring the baseline: Start with operations whose improvement impact can be converted into monetary value
  • Selecting the implementation model: Decide between external-led, hybrid, or in-house-led based on urgency, availability of internal candidates, and transfer risk
  • Setting the timeline and structure: For hybrid, 18 months across 3 phases is standard. Secure at least 6 months
  • Setting the budget frame based on 24-month TCO: Rather than unit price × duration for a single year, use a TCO table to secure a multi-year frame
  • Incorporating performance-based compensation and exit criteria: Clearly document the conditions when KPIs are not met at the contract stage

Pre-approval checklist

  • KPI baseline for target operations has been measured
  • The three models (external-led, hybrid, in-house-led) have been compared on 24-month TCO
  • Outcome definitions (business KPIs) and measurement methods have been agreed with the vendor
  • Handover completion criteria (graduation criteria) have been documented
  • Conditions and caps for additional costs have been confirmed
  • Quarterly continuation decision gates and exit criteria have been set

Points to note in expense category design

If FDE costs are submitted for approval under the traditional "outsourcing (system development)" category, they tend to conflict with procurement rules aimed at SES and SI vendors. Since this is an investment that includes business process redesign and in-housing training, it is easier to get approval by positioning it as a DX investment or business reform budget and securing management buy-in first. The criteria for selecting an external partner are detailed in "How to Choose an AX Implementation Partner".

Frequently asked questions (FAQ)

Q1. What is the typical cost of an FDE?

A. As a guide to monthly rates: senior FDEs (10+ years of experience plus business process redesign track record) are 3-5 million yen, mid-level FDEs (5-10 years) are 2-3.5 million yen, and junior FDEs (3-5 years, in a support role) are 1.5-2.5 million yen. For team-based engagements, a 3-5 member configuration with a monthly range of 10-20 million yen is typical.

Q2. Is it really worth paying 2-4 times the SES rate?

A. Comparing rates alone, SES (80-1.5 million yen per month for senior engineers) is cheaper, but SES rates do not include business process redesign, executive dialogue, or operational handover, leaving that responsibility and workload with the client. For generative AI implementations involving business process redesign, in most cases the FDE model comes out lower when compared on an 18-24 month TCO that includes re-investment from additional customization or failed handovers. Conversely, for projects where specifications are already fixed and only additional capacity is needed, SES is more rational, and there is no need to choose an FDE.

Q3. Is a performance-based contract possible?

A. Yes. FDE contracts are typically based on a quasi-mandate (jun-i) arrangement, combined with performance-based compensation linked to business KPI improvements in a hybrid contract. The conditions for success are measuring the KPI baseline before starting and clearly documenting the measurement method, judgment timing, and payment terms in the contract. A fully performance-based arrangement (zero fixed cost) is unrealistic because the vendor would bear all the uncertainty of business process redesign.

Q4. What minimum timeline and budget should we plan for?

A. The period should be secured for a minimum of 6 months. The cycle of business redesign, implementation, and operational handover does not turn over in less than 6 months. The budget depends on the team structure, but as a rough starting point, a simple calculation gives 3–5 million yen per month × duration for one senior FDE, or 10–20 million yen per month × duration for a team. For the 18-month operation that is standard for the hybrid model, a guideline is 55–80 million yen in 24-month TCO.

Q5. Under which budget line item should approval be sought?

A. We recommend seeking approval as DX investment / business reform budget, not under the conventional system outsourcing expense category. Since FDE costs include business redesign and in-house talent development, they do not fit well with procurement rules that assume system development unit prices. Securing executive sponsorship first and setting the budget frame on a multi-year TCO basis avoids being reduced to single-year unit price negotiations.

Q6. How much do costs decrease with a hybrid model?

A. While a 24-month TCO led by external FDEs is 80–120 million yen, the hybrid model's guideline is 55–80 million yen, roughly 30% lower. This is because external FDE involvement decreases as phases transition, and in many cases the monthly cost settles at around 2–4 million yen. Furthermore, since in-house capabilities remain as assets, external costs themselves drop significantly in the second project cycle and beyond.

Summary

  • FDE cost market rates: 3–5 million yen per month for senior, 2–3.5 million yen for mid-level, and 1.5–2.5 million yen for junior. For team structures, the guideline is 10–20 million yen per month
  • Contract structures come in three types: unit-price, team, and performance-based hybrid. In all cases, success definitions must be tied to business KPIs
  • Comparing unit prices side by side is meaningless. FDEs are a "different product" with a different scope of responsibility from SES, SI, and consulting; compare on an 18–24 month TCO basis
  • On a 24-month TCO basis, the hybrid model (55–80 million yen) is often the best balance of cost-effectiveness
  • Estimate ROI before starting using the 3 steps of "baseline measurement → monetary conversion → payback period calculation"
  • Evaluate proposals not by unit price, but by 7 criteria: success definitions, handover costs, additional cost conditions, and team composition
