A Must-Read for Engineers Who Felt They "Couldn't Keep Up" in SES | Causes and Solutions, Plus Career Strategies After Escaping | Career Support Media Specializing in FDE, Engineers, and IT Freelancers

Original title: SESで「ついていけない」と感じたエンジニア必読｜原因別の対処法と脱出後のキャリア戦略 | FDE/エンジニア/ITフリーランス特化のキャリア支援メディア

Summary

This Japanese article addresses engineers who feel they cannot keep up in SES (staffing-based) assignments. It argues the problem is often structural to the SES model—no training investment, client-determined tech stacks, narrow skills from long placements, no mentors, and sales-driven assignments that ignore skill fit—rather than personal inadequacy. It classifies causes into five types (skill mismatch, lack of onboarding, adaptation costs, missing domain knowledge, career stagnation) and offers situation-specific remedies: listing unknowns, negotiating reassignment, better communication, rest for burnout, or changing employers. It then outlines three exit paths—in-house development companies, corporate internal SE roles, and freelancing—with 2026 freelance rate benchmarks (AI/ML engineers reaching 1–1.5 million yen monthly) and an independence checklist. The piece ends with recruitment promotion for DeFactory and notes some engineers move toward FDE careers.

Key points

  • Feeling left behind in SES often reflects structural flaws in the model, not personal failure.
  • Causes fall into five types: skill mismatch, missing onboarding, adaptation costs, domain knowledge gaps, and stagnation.
  • Remedies depend on cause; negotiating a project change beats blind studying when the assignment is mismatched.
  • SES experience is valued in freelancing, where income can rise 1.5–2x.
  • Three exit paths: in-house development, internal SE roles, and freelance independence.

Unfamiliar technical terms fly around every day, and you can't keep up with the pace of the engineers around you. Even if you want to consult your boss or senior colleagues, at SES worksites you are often in an isolated situation with "no one you can easily talk to."

There are broadly three patterns in which SES engineers feel they "can't keep up." First, check which pattern is closest to your own situation.

Pattern 1: Can't keep up technically

  • You don't know the frameworks and tools used at the worksite
  • You can't understand the feedback in code reviews
  • You don't know how to read design documents or specifications
  • You can't identify the cause of bugs on your own

Pattern 2: Can't keep up with speed and volume

  • You can't handle the amount of tasks other members complete
  • Fixing issues from reviews and feedback takes too long
  • You don't know when to ask questions, and your work comes to a standstill

Pattern 3: Can't keep up with the environment and culture

  • You don't understand the unwritten rules of the worksite
  • You get exhausted by the communication style specific to on-site client work
  • You're caught between your own company and the client site

If you fit into any of these three patterns, the first step is to identify the cause of feeling "left behind" right away.

To those who are blaming themselves, thinking "It's because I'm no good," we first want to say this: Feeling "left behind" at SES is not solely your problem.

SES (System Engineering Service) is a business model that provides technical expertise by dispatching engineers to companies. This business model has structural disadvantages built in for engineers.

IssueStructural factor of SES
No access to trainingBecause you work on-site at clients, your own company finds it hard to invest in your training
No say in technology choicesYou have to adapt to the technology stack chosen by the client
Skills become narrowLong-term assignment to the same site prevents you from broadening your technical range
No one to consult withYou have a different position from the client's full-time employees, and your own company's supervisors are not on-site
Gap between projects and skillsBecause assignments are sales-driven, your skill level may not be taken into account

Especially for inexperienced and young engineers, there are many cases where they are sent to client sites as "immediate contributors" right after finishing training, making their worksite debut with a large skill gap. This is a structural problem of SES.

If you feel like you "can't keep up," there is a high chance it is a sign that you have run into this structural problem.

To solve the feeling of being "left behind," it is essential to first accurately identify the cause. The solutions differ completely depending on the cause.

This is the most common cause. SES companies' sales staff sometimes propose projects without fully understanding an engineer's skill level. Mismatches are especially likely in the following cases.

  • Judged by years of experience alone: Even with "2 years of Java experience," the complexity of the projects someone has worked on varies greatly from person to person
  • Different versions of technologies used: A case where someone experienced with Java 8 joins a modern development site using Java 17
  • Different layers: A backend specialist assigned to a site that also requires frontend skills
  • Business domain knowledge required: Sites in finance, healthcare, etc., where technical skills alone are not enough without business knowledge

In this pattern, it is difficult to solve through individual learning effort alone, and negotiating a change of project is the fundamental solution.

SES engineers are often treated as "external personnel" and may not receive the same training and support systems as the client's own employees.

Specifically, situations like the following:

  • Almost no onboarding when joining (environment setup, explanation of the codebase)
  • No designated person to ask questions, and you don't know who to ask
  • You're told "ask if there's anything you don't understand," but you don't even know what you don't understand
  • Code review feedback ends with just a single word: "Fix it"

This pattern is a problem with the worksite's system, not an individual problem. However, since it is difficult for SES engineers to change the client's training system, you need a communication strategy to proactively draw out information yourself.

Due to the nature of SES engineers, you need to adapt to a new environment every time your project changes. This brings not just "technical differences" but multifaceted stress, such as the following.

  • Differences in technology stacks: Different languages, frameworks, and infrastructure configurations with every change of worksite
  • Differences in team culture: coding conventions, documentation granularity, how meetings are run
  • Differences in on-site rules: dress code, attendance management, conditions for remote work
  • Differences in business domains: EC, finance, manufacturing, healthcare, etc. — catching up from zero industry knowledge

Especially for less experienced engineers, this adaptation cost is extremely high, and their resources can be exhausted just by adjusting to the environment, before even getting to technical learning.

There are also many cases where engineers have the technical skills but cannot keep up because they don't understand how the business works.

For example, in banking system development, foundational knowledge of financial operations such as "foreign exchange, payments, and core banking" is required before programming. If you can't understand the business terminology used by user departments in requirements definition and design reviews, no amount of technical skill will let you follow the discussion.

IndustryExamples of required domain knowledge
Finance/BankingAccount systems, foreign exchange, payment flows, FISC security standards
EC/RetailInventory management, order processing flows, logistics integration
HealthcareElectronic medical records, medical fees, medical device certification
Manufacturing/FactoriesMES, ERP (SAP, etc.), BOM management
Logistics/TransportationWMS, dispatch logic, 3PL

Acquiring domain knowledge takes time, but conversely, engineers who have deepened their domain knowledge in a specific industry become more valuable in that field, making it a career asset.

There are also many cases where engineers are managing technically but feel they "can't keep up" due to a sense of stagnation, wondering "What will I become if I keep working at an SES company like this?"

Since SES work mainly involves being stationed at client sites, the following kinds of stagnation in growth tend to arise.

  • Skills become too specialized to a particular system, causing anxiety about whether they will transfer to other companies
  • Inability to gain "upstream" experience such as technology selection and architecture design
  • No visible career path toward becoming a PM or tech lead
  • Opaque in-house evaluation systems, with no clear prospects for raises or promotion

This state takes a heavy mental toll, and if left unaddressed, there is a risk of leading to burnout.

Once you have identified the cause, the next step is to choose a solution that fits your situation. "Just changing jobs" is a last resort. First, try methods that can improve your current situation.

Target: Pattern ① (can't keep up technically) × Causes 2-1 and 2-2

To escape the state of "not knowing what you don't know," the first step is to list out "what you don't understand."

Practical steps:

  • Note the terms and technologies that come up on site: Write down every word you didn't understand in conversations, and look them up at the end of the day
  • Create a skill map: List the skills required at your current site and rate your own understanding on a 0–5 scale
  • Prioritize: Pick one item from "the technology that is blocking me most this week" and focus your learning on it
  • Curate your learning resources: Use them in this order: official documentation → Udemy/Zenn → asking ChatGPT

Rather than a vague goal like "study 2 hours every day," a concrete goal such as "understand Docker multi-stage builds and resolve task X by the end of this week" is more effective.

Key point: For problems you get stuck on during work, always document them after solving them to build your own knowledge base. Simply avoiding getting stuck on the same problem twice makes a big difference in productivity.

Target: When Cause 2-1 (assignment mismatch) is clear

If you can objectively judge that "this site doesn't match my level," then consulting your company's manager or sales representative about changing assignments is a legitimate option. In fact, leaving the problem unaddressed and continuing to underperform is a loss for both the engineer and the company.

A framework you can use when consulting:

「現在の現場は〇〇の技術が必要ですが、私の経験は△△までです。
 現状では期待に応えられておらず、クライアントにも迷惑をかけている状況です。
 ●●の技術を活かせる案件や、△△から●●へのステップアップができる案件に変更いただけないでしょうか。」

Rather than an emotional "I want to quit," concretely communicating your current issues and wishes makes it easier for the company to take action.

Target: Pattern ③ (can't keep up with the environment/culture) × Causes 2-2 and 2-3

In SES work environments, communication productivity depends heavily on whether the atmosphere makes it easy to ask questions. Try the following strategies.

  • Identify the "key people" in your first week: Figure out who to ask to resolve issues fastest
  • Show "evidence of research" before asking questions: Simply prefacing with "I looked into it but couldn't solve it" dramatically changes how you're perceived
  • Actively use Slack or chat: If asking questions in person is difficult, organize your question in text before sending it
  • Propose regular one-on-ones: Even 10 minutes once a week as "a time to check status and consult" relieves feelings of isolation

Target: All patterns, especially when mental exhaustion is significant

If the feeling of "not being able to keep up" persists for a long time, it may be caused not by a simple lack of skill but by depletion of mental resources.

If you are showing the following signs, prioritize caring for your physical and mental health before thinking about career issues.

  • Lacking the energy to head to the workplace (or your home desk) in the morning
  • The feeling of "I'm no good" continuing even outside of work hours
  • Unable to sleep, no appetite
  • Losing interest in hobbies and things you used to enjoy

In Japan, options for mental health support include consulting an occupational physician, using sickness benefits, and taking a leave of absence. "Resting = losing" is not true. Recovering before taking action again is clearly a plus for your long-term career.

Target: Cause 2-2 (lack of training systems) and 2-5 (sense of stagnation)

If growth opportunities on site are limited, compensating through external communities is effective.

Target: When there are problems with your current SES company's support system or training policies

If the cause of "not being able to keep up" lies not in the workplace but in the structure of your own SES company itself, simply changing workplaces won't solve the problem fundamentally. In situations like the following, it's worth considering changing your SES company.

  • The sales staff don't understand technology and repeatedly make assignments that ignore your skills
  • There is no support system, and when you're struggling, it ends with a single word: "Just try harder"
  • There is virtually no support for skill development, such as training costs or book expenses
  • There is no opportunity to discuss career paths

Moving to an SES company that genuinely supports engineers' growth can be a major turning point in your career.

At our company, DeFactory, we hire engineers and people with PM/PL experience as full-time employees. About 80% of our projects come from direct transactions with end clients, including contract development, SaaS development, and AI/MVP product development—positions where you can be deeply involved in technology as a "builder." For engineers who want to gain experience not just in "building to instructions" but in "building a business," it serves as a growth environment.

Target: When you feel the limitations of the SES work style and career structure itself

If you feel that "no matter how much the project or company changes, the SES structure remains the same," it may be time to graduate from SES altogether and redesign your career.

The next chapter explains in detail specific career options that use SES as a stepping stone.

Experience in SES is by no means wasted. Experience handling multiple workplaces, technologies, and teams will become a major asset in your next career stage. Here are three representative directions.

This is the career path most commonly pursued by engineers from SES backgrounds. The main options to consider as transfer destinations are as follows.

DestinationCharacteristicsFit from SES
In-house development companies (startups)Modern tech stacks, deep involvement in the businessTechnical skill and self-sufficiency are required. Those with long SES experience need time to adjust
In-house development companies (mega-ventures)Product development in a stable environmentTrack record such as portfolios and OSS contributions is important
SIers (focused on upstream processes)Can experience upstream work such as requirements definition, design, and PMSES engineers with experience on large projects are a strong fit
Web contract development companiesWide range of technologies and client workExperience across many SES workplaces is easy to leverage

Keys to a successful job change:

  • Always build a portfolio: In many cases you can't simply show what you made during your SES days. Build a track record through personal development and OSS contributions
  • Build "depth" in one technology: Since SES tends to involve broad experience, having one specialized area you "know deeply" increases your appeal in the job market
  • Articulate your upstream experience: Carefully document non-coding experience such as design, reviews, and client negotiations in your resume

The route of moving to a corporate information systems department (in-house SE) is also well suited to people from SES backgrounds.

Characteristics of engineers well suited to becoming an in-house SE:

  • Have experience deeply involved in a specific industry or domain (finance, manufacturing, healthcare, etc.)
  • Good at communication and negotiation, better suited as a bridge to the business side than focusing purely on technology
  • Want to prioritize work-life balance

The advantages of being an in-house SE are being able to leverage deep business understanding and being able to deliver long-term results by putting down roots in a specific system, product, or team. However, you need to understand that it's easy to become detached from the cutting edge of technology when choosing this path.

The route of going independent as a freelancer after gaining a certain amount of experience as a full-time SES employee offers significant financial benefits.

While the salary a full-time SES employee receives includes intermediary margins, freelancers contract more directly with clients, so even with the same skills doing the same work, it's not uncommon for take-home pay to be 1.5 to 2 times higher or more.

Specific strategies for transitioning to freelancing are explained in detail in the next chapter.

Many people worry, "Does SES experience actually count as a freelancer?" but in fact, full-time SES experience is highly valued in the freelance market. Here's why.

1. "Flexibility" gained from handling multiple workplaces and technologies

SES engineers have experience adapting to new technologies and teams every time their workplace changes. When holding multiple clients simultaneously as a freelancer or needing to quickly fit into a new workplace, this adaptability is extremely valuable.

2. "Business communication skills" cultivated through on-site client work

Experience interacting directly with end clients' users and staff becomes a major asset when going independent as a freelancer, in proposals, negotiations, and progress reporting.

3. Understanding of diverse technology stacks

Having experienced multiple workplaces, you gain cross-cutting technical understanding, such as "I used this technology at Company A" or "That architecture is similar to Company B's system."

Conditions for securing high rates (over ¥1,000,000 per month):

  • "T-shaped" technical skills: Deep expertise in one technology combined with a broad understanding of related technologies
  • Upstream experience: Having experience in requirements definition, design, and architecture selection significantly boosts your rate

To successfully transition from full-time SES employment to freelancing, it is important to prepare in three areas: income, skills, and paperwork.

【Preparing Skills and Track Record】

  • [ ] Organize your GitHub portfolio (personal development, OSS, etc.)
  • [ ] Rewrite your resume in an "achievement-based" format (not "participated in the development of XX system" but "designed and implemented XX functionality as the lead developer, improving processing speed by 30%")
  • [ ] Check the market rate for your skills (register with agents to get a feel for the market)
  • [ ] Articulate 1–2 of your "signature strengths"

【Preparing Networking and Project Acquisition】

  • [ ] Register with 2–3 freelance agents
  • [ ] Maintain good relationships at your current workplace and company (this becomes the basis for referral projects)
  • [ ] Build visibility by sharing your work on social media (X, LinkedIn, etc.)
  • [ ] Clarify your "availability date" and "desired conditions"

【Preparing Lifestyle and Finances】

  • [ ] Save 3–6 months' worth of living expenses
  • [ ] Check the procedures for National Health Insurance and the National Pension
  • [ ] Learn the basics of tax filing and blue-form tax returns
  • [ ] Confirm the timing for submitting the business startup notification (within one month after going independent)

【Guidelines for Timing Your Independence】

The best time to go independent from SES to freelancing is when the following conditions are met.

  • You have 3+ years of engineering experience
  • You can assess yourself as an "immediate contributor" in a specific technical area
  • You have savings covering 6 months of living expenses
  • You have a clear outlook on projects through agent registration and interviews

There is no need to rush into independence. That said, it is also true that going independent when fully prepared is smoother than you might imagine, and it is by no means rare for engineers to achieve 1.5–2 times their full-time salary.

Feeling like you "can't keep up" at an SES company is nothing to be ashamed of, nor does it indicate the limits of your abilities.

As explained in this article, the state of "not being able to keep up" involves structural factors rooted in the SES business model. What matters is not emotionally denying yourself, but rather identifying the cause and choosing the right course of action for your situation.

Summary of this article:

  • The causes of "not being able to keep up" can be classified into five categories (skill mismatch, lack of training, environmental changes, domain knowledge, and a sense of stagnation)
  • The solutions differ depending on the cause. Choose the option that fits your situation from among "learning, consulting, improving communication, resting, changing jobs, or going independent"
  • After leaving SES, the main career directions are three options: "moving to in-house development, becoming an in-house SE, or going independent as a freelancer"
  • With 3+ years of experience as a full-time SES employee, you have a solid foundation to aim for rates of ¥800,000–1,200,000 per month as a freelancer
