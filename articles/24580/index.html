Summary

This article from FDE总研 chronicles the extortion and data-leak incident at GMO Internet Group's お名前.com Desktop Cloud, from the October 21 ransom demand of roughly 8 billion yen through the October 27 data dump and the October 28 takedown of the attacker's site, alongside a reward of up to 1 million USD. It compares the case with four past extortion-leak incidents (Vastaamo, Medibank, Optus, KADOKAWA), finding a shared pattern of using customers as leverage, while GMO refused payment. Distinctive features include a claimed daily-noon release schedule, reportedly leaked terminal-side data such as browsing history, and tiered consolation payments up to 3 million yen. The author argues a takedown does not end the incident, lists practical steps for IT and field engineers, and ties the case to the FDE (Forward Deployed Engineer) idea of carrying security findings through to implementation.

Key points

  • GMO's incident moved from extortion (10/21) to domain takedown (10/28) in about a week.
  • The ransom was about 8 billion yen, reportedly lowered to 1 million USD; GMO refused to pay.
  • All five cases share attackers using customer data or customers themselves as leverage.
  • GMO's distinctive response: tiered consolation payments and a 1 million USD reward.
  • The author links the case to FDEs bridging security findings and implementation.

How has the GMO incident progressed so far? From the 10/21 extortion to the 10/28 domain suspension

According to FDE Research Institute's analysis, the GMO incident progressed from the 10/21 extortion to the actual publication of information and the suspension of the perpetrator's site domain in about one week.

This article is based on official announcements and media reports as of October 28, 2026, 18:20 (GMO Internet Group's official 14th announcement). As the incident is ongoing, figures and circumstances may change going forward. GMO's official announcements, media reports, and the FDE Research Institute editorial team's analysis and opinions are presented separately.

The subject is the unauthorized access incident involving GMO Internet Group's "onamae.com Desktop Cloud." This article first organizes the events from October 19 to October 28 in chronological order. It then compares the case with past extortion and data leak incidents—Vastaamo, Medibank, Optus, and KADOKAWA—to show similarities and differences. The security background is covered in the previous article, GMO Unauthorized Access and White Hackers' Warnings.

10/19–10/24: From Detection to Disclosure of the Extortion Content

According to GMO's official announcements, the investigation began with a user inquiry, and five days later, on 10/24, the external exfiltration of customer information was confirmed.

DateEvent (GMO official announcements)
10/19Investigation began following a user inquiry
10/20Malicious programs were confirmed
10/21 4:00Service was suspended and the first report was published. On the same day, an extortion email was received demanding payment of approximately 8 billion yen
10/22Consulted with the police
10/23Third report. Servers with a potential for impact: 26,739 (not a confirmed damage count)
10/24Fourth report. External exfiltration of customer information was confirmed. Announced a uniform 5,000 yen condolence payment for all users, compensation for losses from unauthorized transactions, and six months of free alternative service

In the fourth report, the following three points were disclosed as the content of the extortion:

  • Continuing unauthorized manipulation of user trading accounts at more than 70 securities and FX companies in Japan and abroad
  • Publishing information about the service and users' PCs and executing ransomware attacks
  • Publishing information about GMO's systems and operational structure

According to GMO's official announcements, the demanded amount is the "approximately 8 billion yen" in the 10/21 extortion email. On 10/26, Representative Kumagai revealed on his blog that the demanded amount was 50 million dollars (approximately 8 billion yen). Media reports state that the attacker subsequently lowered the demand to 1 million dollars. However, since the reduction has not been confirmed in GMO's official announcements, this article does not assert it as fact.

10/25–10/28: Data Publication, Domain Suspension, and Reward

As the attacker had announced, what they claimed was information was published on 10/27, and GMO responded the next day, 10/28, with the domain suspension and the establishment of a reward.

GMO is urging people not to access the published site, warning that visitors' information could be taken by the attacker. GMO states that the published content is still being verified and may contain inaccurate information.

The domain suspension (takedown) was carried out in cooperation with related service providers. The Asahi Shimbun reported that records of the registrar taking suspension measures could be confirmed via an ICANN lookup. However, what was stopped was only viewing of the published site. The attacker has not been identified or eliminated.

What Happened in Past Extortion and Data Leak Incidents? Vastaamo, Medibank, Optus, and KADOKAWA

In the four incidents—Vastaamo, Medibank, Optus, and KADOKAWA—the damage was prolonged through data publication and threats against individuals, even after payment was refused.

The amounts and numbers of people in the four incidents are based on each company's published figures or media reports. Where reports give differing figures, both are stated.

Vastaamo (Finland) and Medibank (Australia): Threats Against Individuals and Piecemeal Publication

In the Vastaamo case, the attacker began directly threatening patients themselves, and in the Medibank case, data was published in multiple installments.

  • Vastaamo (October 2020): Patient therapy session records were stolen from a psychotherapy center. The attacker demanded 40 bitcoins from the center in exchange for not publishing the data. Reported amounts vary, at roughly 450,000 euros or about 500,000 dollars. When the center refused to pay, the attacker emailed patients directly, demanding about 500 euros each. It is reported that more than 24,000 people filed reports of being threatened. Many victims are said to have refused to pay, and the center closed its business. The perpetrator was sentenced to 6 years and 3 months in prison at the first trial in April 2024. At the appeal, the sentence was increased to just under 7 years, and since the Supreme Court declined to accept the appeal, the sentence has reportedly become final.
  • Medibank (2022): The attacker initially demanded 10 million US dollars, then changed the demand to 1 dollar per customer (about 9.7 million dollars). When Medibank refused to pay, the attacker began publishing the stolen data on the dark web in multiple batches starting November 9, 2022. Approximately 9.7 million customers were affected. According to reports, the data for nearly 500,000 of these customers included health insurance claim data.

Optus (Australia) and KADOKAWA (Japan): Spread after deletion and cases of major domestic companies

At Optus, the spread continued even after the attacker claimed to have deleted the data, and at KADOKAWA, data was published after the ransom claim.

  • Optus (September 2022): The attacker posted part of the data on a forum and demanded 1 million US dollars (about 1.5 million Australian dollars) in cryptocurrency. A few hours after publishing some customer data, the attacker withdrew the demand and apologized, saying the data had been deleted. Optus did not pay. Nevertheless, copies of the data had already fallen into others' hands, and the spread did not stop.
  • KADOKAWA (June 2024): The company suffered a ransomware attack, and services such as Niconico went down. The ransomware group BlackSuit, believed to be Russian-based, claimed responsibility and published the stolen data on the dark web. KADOKAWA announced that personal information had been leaked.

What are the similarities and differences between the GMO incident and past cases?

Looking at FDE Soken's comparison axes, the structure of using customers as leverage is common. What differ are the methods of publication, the types of data, and the design of compensation.

This chapter is an analysis by the FDE Soken editorial department based on public information. The comparison axes were set by FDE Soken to understand "what happens after a company refuses to pay."

Similarity: Using "customers" to make companies pay

In every case, the attackers used customer information or customers themselves as leverage to make the companies pay.

GMO, Medibank, and Optus all refused to pay, and the Vastaamo center also did not pay. GMO declared that it would "never comply" with the attacker's demands. The reasons it cited were the following two points: that payment does not guarantee deletion of the information or prevention of misuse, and that paying would provide funds to a criminal organization.

In the Vastaamo case, when the company refused to pay, the attacker switched to a tactic of directly threatening individuals. For this reason, we believe that in the GMO incident as well, users need to prepare for threats or impersonation targeting them personally. This is the editorial department's outlook, and no such activity has actually been confirmed.

Differences: Methods of publication, types of data, and design of compensation

The GMO incident has three distinctive features: a daily publication schedule, data said to include browsing history, and consolation payments by disclosure category.

  • Method of publication: It may become a "drip-style" release, publishing little by little like Medibank. The attacker is reportedly claiming that publishing everything would "take several years."
  • Types of data: It is reported that even "websites usually browsed" were included, suggesting that device-side information may have been taken. However, the acquisition route is still under investigation by GMO and cannot yet be determined.
  • Design of compensation: The special consolation payments are paid according to the category of the published information. The amounts are 10,000 yen for basic information, 15,000 yen for financial and authentication-related information, and 25,000 yen for sensitive information. If multiple categories apply, the amounts are combined, with a reported cap of 3 million yen. GMO combined this with a reward of up to 1 million US dollars. Such responses were rarely seen in the four past cases.

What is known and still unknown about the GMO incident?

What has been officially confirmed by GMO is the timeline, the fact of data exfiltration, and the compensation details. The cause, the scale of the leak, and the acquisition route are still unknown.

GMO's reports 1 through 14 announced the timeline, the compensation details, and interim results of the device analysis. The table below separates GMO's official information from media reports.

What is still unknown

The cause of the attack, the actual number of leaked items, and the acquisition route of device-side information had not been announced as of October 28, 2026.

  • Cause of the attack: At a press conference on November 2, GMO reportedly acknowledged that the attacker had infiltrated its servers in August, and that although an in-house expert had pointed out the vulnerability about two years earlier, countermeasures had been insufficient. The specific method of intrusion was not disclosed in reports as of November 2.
  • Scale of the leak: 26,739 records is the potential scope of impact. The actual number of leaked records has not been disclosed.
  • Changes in the ransom demand: There are reports that the demand was lowered from the officially announced "approximately 8 billion yen" ($50 million) to $1 million, but GMO has not officially explained this.
  • Authenticity of the published content: GMO states that it may contain content that differs from the facts.
  • Future publications: It is unknown whether the attacker's stated "daily at noon" publication will be carried out or whether a new site will appear. GMO stated in its 13th report that it will move to take down any new site as soon as it is confirmed.

What are the key issues that field engineers and IT departments should focus on?

In FDE Soken's view, the incident does not end when the publication site goes down. The key issue is building a framework that carries identified issues through to implementation and establishment.

This chapter represents the opinion of the FDE Soken editorial team.

Prepare on the premise that "stopping the site" does not mean "the incident is over"

As the Optus case shows, once information has been copied, it should be considered unrecoverable even if posts or domains are taken down.

Taking down a domain is meaningful as a means of limiting spread. However, if mirror sites or new sites appear, it could become a game of whack-a-mole. GMO itself anticipates the possibility of new sites appearing. We have organized the practical issues for IT departments and field engineers, together with the supporting cases.

IssueSpecific responseSupporting cases and announcements
Anticipate secondary harm to usersPrepare warning notices for internal and external audiences on the assumption of threats targeting individuals and impersonation attempts exploiting the incidentThreats against individual patients in the Vastaamo case
Do not access the publication siteDo not view it even for verification purposes. Do not repost or share the URL internally eitherGMO's warning (viewers' information may be captured)
Check endpointsReview your company's endpoint management and log retention practicesReports that endpoint-side information was included in the published data (acquisition route under investigation)

What should you check first, depending on your role?

What you should check first depends on whether you are in an IT department, a development/operations engineer, or a user of GMO's services.

We have rearranged the issues in the table above by role. All of these are editorial summaries derived from GMO's announcements and past cases, and do not represent correct answers for individual environments.

  • For IT departments and security teams: First, inform everyone internally not to view the publication site and not to share the URL. Then prepare warning notices in anticipation of impersonation contacts.
  • For development and operations engineers: Review endpoint management and log retention practices. GMO is investigating how the endpoint-side information was obtained. Be ready to compare the findings with your own environment once the investigation results are published.
  • For users of GMO's services: Check GMO's official announcements as the primary source of information, and follow GMO's guidance for requests such as endpoint inspections. Since contacts exploiting the incident may occur, verify contact details through official announcements.

How to shorten the distance from "identification" to "implementation and establishment"

FDE Soken sees the long distance from a security finding to its implementation and establishment as one of the key issues in this case.

FDE (Forward Deployed Engineer) is a role popularized by Palantir. FDEs work on-site with customers, taking responsibility end to end from problem discovery through implementation to establishment (see What is an FDE? for details, and What is forward deployed engineering? for the approach). At the November 2 press conference, GMO acknowledged that its response to prior findings had been insufficient. However, since the specific method of intrusion has not been disclosed, it cannot be said that "an FDE would have prevented it." Even so, whether security findings go beyond reports and are reflected in actual configurations and operations is a challenge faced by many companies. Engineers embedded in the field are, we believe, strong candidates to serve as that bridge.

The difference between the role that produces findings and the role that carries them through to implementation is explained in The difference between FDEs and consultants, and the steps for building an internal framework in How to introduce FDEs. Our thinking on proactive measures is summarized in What is active cyber defense? Corporate proactive measures and how to leverage FDEs. Future outlook and specific measures will be covered in a separate article.
