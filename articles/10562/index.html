Full text

Exclusive first look

Locking in major clients and large-scale revenue as much as possible is becoming Moonshot AI's new goal amid rumors of its IPO.

On September 10, Moonshot AI launched the Kimi Enterprise Partner "Moonshot Program," partnering with systems integrators such as ChinaSoft International, Kingsoft Cloud, Teamsun, Yakang, and AsiaInfo to jointly build a team of Forward Deployed Engineers (FDE).

FDE is currently one of the hottest to-B business models in the AI industry. It refers to companies dispatching engineers to work on-site at client companies, gaining a deep understanding of business needs, and then deploying products—including large models—into the client's core business processes, making the products easier to use and helping clients use them better.

Moonshot AI has been quite high-profile about the "Moonshot Program," calling itself "the first large model company in China to adopt the FDE model for AI end-stage deployment."

The timing of Moonshot AI's major push into FDE overlaps with rumors of its Hong Kong listing.

Earlier this month, market reports said Moonshot AI plans to IPO in Hong Kong this year, raising up to $5 billion. A few days later, the "Moonshot Program" was unveiled, reflecting Moonshot AI's intent to secure medium- and long-term revenue.

As one of the "AI Six Little Dragons," Moonshot AI's current core business model is built around cloud-based MaaS, essentially no different from the already-listed Zhipu and MiniMax—both sell tokens, sell them well, and will keep selling them.

The problem is that while selling tokens is a good business, the competition is fierce.

At this stage, measured by token consumption and revenue contribution, the major clients of Moonshot AI and similar companies are mainly AI coding, agent, and office platforms, such as ByteDance's Coze/Trae, Alibaba's Qoder, and Tencent's CodeBuddy domestically, and OpenCode, Cursor, and Windsurf abroad.

These platforms all integrate multiple mainstream models. The endless stream of new models with soaring performance at home and abroad, frequent token price adjustments, and even new models' specialized optimizations for certain compute resources or specific types of tasks could all prompt users to recalculate and switch between different models with one click.

During the rapid expansion of AI services, tokens from leading model companies are in short supply. Beyond strong demand from major internet companies and large platforms, model aggregation and distribution platforms like OpenRouter also bring a steady stream of small and medium-sized enterprises and individual users.

But as global AI compute continues to expand, there will come a day when tokens are no longer scarce. If model companies continue selling tokens to internet platforms and small and medium-sized clients as they do today, without any control over whether end users "choose them or choose me," they may well face revenue and performance volatility.

For Moonshot AI, which is racing toward its IPO, this potential volatility has not yet materialized, but it still constitutes a long-term risk.

Against this backdrop, by pushing hard on FDE to capture big-spending, old-money clients who don't casually swap out their IT infrastructure, and even becoming "the Chinese version of Palantir," is the latest solution Moonshot AI has found.

In the month or so before announcing the "Moonshot Program," Moonshot AI was quietly advancing at full speed in the FDE race.

First, on July 20, ChinaSoft International signed an agreement with Moonshot AI to jointly build an FDE innovation lab, collaborating on the definition, R&D, delivery, refinement, testing, and continuous iteration of enterprise-grade Agent products. The initial phase of cooperation will prioritize the energy and power sector.

By the end of August, AsiaInfo announced a partnership with Moonshot AI in the enterprise-grade Agentic AI business area.

Specifically, Moonshot AI provides complete Kimi model weights, technical documentation, and backend R&D support, while AsiaInfo assembles a dedicated FDE delivery team. Together, the two parties will build an integrated delivery system covering model adaptation, parameter tuning, localized deployment, and ongoing operations and maintenance.

Both ChinaSoft International and AsiaInfo appear on the first batch list of the "Moonshot Program." Moonshot AI said the program will remain open to IT service providers, software service providers, and consulting firms.

Moonshot AI's FDE moves are noticeably bigger than those of the other "little dragons," and even exceed those of the tech giants.

Compared with Moonshot AI's grand feast, its rivals are still at the team-building or even wait-and-see stage.

Zhipu has posted a job opening for an FDE lead with a monthly salary of 60,000–80,000 yuan—mid-range in AI circles, and nowhere near the lavish pay at top research institutes. MiniMax, Baichuan, 01.AI, and StepFun have made no public moves.

As for DeepSeek, its ambitions lie elsewhere—"Sage Liang" clearly has no interest in such mundane work as FDE, grubbing in the dirt and working for others on their turf.

The big internet companies have gone deeper than the "little dragons," but only by a few steps.

ByteDance previously recruited FDEs with reported annual packages of up to 1 million yuan. Alibaba Cloud began recruiting FDEs in Beijing, Shanghai, Guangzhou, Shenzhen, and Hangzhou in April this year, but only 10 of them—a clear trial run.

Tencent, on the other hand, shows somewhat greater interest. On the same day as Moonshot AI's "moonshot," Tencent Cloud launched "the industry's first FDE engineer certification system" and opened recruitment for FDE partners.

Overall, Moonshot AI is the AI company most deeply involved in and most vocal about the domestic FDE race. The reason is not complicated: compared with its peers, it has a far stronger desire to make money.

Moonshot AI, in the midst of its IPO process, needs to deliver powerful models like K3 as well as a beautiful financial report.

Recently, according to informed sources, Moonshot AI's annual recurring revenue has surged from $300 million in June to over $1 billion in August. The company plans to raise it to $2 billion by the end of this year.

Moonshot AI is already working toward this, one move being charging licensing fees for open-source models. It has been reported that it is negotiating a 30% revenue share with Microsoft, Amazon, and Google.

But distant water won't put out a nearby fire. While negotiating with foreign giants, Moonshot AI needs to open up more springs. FDE, with its clear business logic and mature business model, has naturally become one of Moonshot AI's key choices.

Moonshot AI wants to make money from FDE, but it also has its own clever ideas: rather than recruiting its own troops, it hopes to adopt a "I provide the weapons, you provide the soldiers" approach, sharing the profits together.

Moonshot AI has long been known for its lean team—to this day it still has only about 300 people, 80% of whom are R&D staff. By comparison, Zhipu has fewer than 1,000 people, MiniMax has 400, and DeepSeek has 500.

In entering FDE, Moonshot AI does not plan to maintain a large corps of on-site engineers. Instead, it hands the Kimi model and Agent foundation to systems integrators like ChinaSoft International and Kingsoft Cloud, with the integrators providing on-site staff while Moonshot AI only supplies technology and products.

The advantage of this approach is that it doesn't need to roll out sales and delivery teams across the country, nor spend effort cultivating and organizing connections. Instead, it can leverage the personnel and networks of established integrators to expand its business, staying lean during the IPO window.

However, this is not overly "clever." In fact, OpenAI and Anthropic, which are seeking to go deep in FDE, have adopted similar strategies.

OpenAI has long coveted the FDE pie. In February this year, it launched the Frontier Alliance program, bringing in global consulting giant Capgemini as a partner. Capgemini provided a large number of certified engineers to work with OpenAI's own FDEs on client projects.

On May 11, OpenAI founded a new company, OpenAI Deployment Company. Controlled by OpenAI, it brought in 19 investment institutions including TPG, Advent, Bain Capital, and Brookfield, with initial funding of $4 billion and a pre-money valuation of $10 billion.

Right after its founding, the new company acquired an AI consulting firm called Tomoro, absorbing all 150 of its senior FDEs as its own foundation.

Anthropic moved even faster than OpenAI in competing for the FDE market.

Just one week before OpenAI's Deployment Company was established, Anthropic teamed up with three Wall Street giants—Blackstone, Hellman & Friedman, and Goldman Sachs—to put up $1.5 billion and form a joint venture. The new company focuses on AI-native enterprise services, embedding engineers into the core operations of client companies to deploy Claude.

Three days later, Anthropic also reached a strategic partnership with PwC, which would provide a large number of certified engineers.

This creates an interesting phenomenon: when AI companies do FDE work, once they go even slightly deep, they have to bring old money along to do business together, even co-founding new companies.

Money is not the only reason. Even if Moonshot AI has limited ammunition and is reluctant to spend too much, OpenAI and Anthropic, which have repeatedly raised massive funding rounds, would never be tied down by a mere few billion dollars, let alone form alliances with others over such sums.

The real reason is that consulting firms and financial institutions that have been around for decades or even a century better understand the needs and thinking of traditional large companies in finance, energy, transportation, and other sectors. Their decades-old relationship networks also give them the clout to make the pitch and unlock budgets when selling FDE solutions.

Moreover, old money enjoys a brand effect that tech companies cannot match, and large companies are highly susceptible to it—which companies you choose to partner with is itself a display of corporate strength. By contrast, a new company, no matter how advanced its technology or cool its products, may not even get a foot in the IT director's door.

On the other hand, established companies have served large clients for years and possess a team of experienced field engineers. They know the know-how of on-site service, which is the very essence and moat of FDE; even if they don't understand large models, that's merely a technical shortcoming that can be fully addressed through internal collaboration.

Old money in traditional sectors is eager to catch the AI express train, and AI companies are happy to bring old money in to make money together from large companies in traditional industries. FDE at home and abroad has followed a similar path.

Through doing FDE, Moonshot AI has the opportunity to answer a question: does the "token economy" actually have user loyalty?

Currently, model companies selling tokens via API is the main form of the so-called "token economy." But the supply side has always been plagued by low user loyalty: no matter how many free credits or top-up discounts are offered upfront, users get lured away the moment they see a new "sweetheart," leaving the old model a helpless "cast-off."

The ultimate solution for model companies, of course, is to produce a model so powerful that no one can match it, making users feel it is their "dream model" and stay faithful to it forever.

But as language model tech stacks increasingly converge, it is the norm for each new model to "have its day for three to five days." Even OpenAI and Anthropic dare not claim they have firmly secured the industry's summit.

On the other hand, model vendors sell tokens through highly uniform methods and channels; apart from adjusting prices, there is almost no room for differentiation, which further reinforces users' habit of "whichever is cheaper wins."

Worse still, as token distribution channels, AI application platforms and LLM routers fundamentally do not encourage "loyalty"—instead, they provide maximum convenience for users to switch models at any time. Model vendors have almost no effective means to retain users.

The FDE model, however, brings an opportunity to "lock in" token demand and bind customers to model vendors.

Integrators like ChinaSoft International and Kingsoft Cloud send staff on-site to integrate the Kimi model into clients' core business processes, such as reconciliation, risk control, customer service, and production scheduling. Once integrated and in use, if a client wants to switch models, it means redoing the entire business process—at extremely high cost.

The token unit price hasn't changed, but the cost of customer churn has been raised by an order of magnitude. This objectively creates "user loyalty."

In addition, FDE can gradually transform model vendors' user and revenue structures, reaching traditional large companies and landing truly long-term, large contracts.

The five companies that signed in the first batch of Moonshot AI's "Moonshot Program" mainly operate in telecommunications, energy, electric power, finance, government affairs, and healthcare, with end customers including State Grid, the three major telecom operators, PetroChina, banks, and other large state-owned enterprises, as well as various listed companies.

If Kimi can enter these enterprises, Moonshot AI's customer structure has the chance to undergo a qualitative change: previously mainly developers, internet companies, and individual C-end users; in the future, mainly central state-owned enterprises, major banks, telecom operators, and energy giants.

The characteristics of such clients: huge contract values, slow but stable payments, and extremely strong stickiness. Once a model company enters a supplier list, it is very hard to be replaced.

For Moonshot AI, an annual contract with a bank or energy company could be worth tens of thousands or even hundreds of thousands of C-end subscriptions. Meanwhile, large clients are highly stable, with contracts typically signed for 1–3 years; unlike C-end subscriptions that can be canceled at any time, they can hedge against revenue fluctuations.

At this point, Moonshot AI's FDE ambitions have gradually unfolded.

But the challenges are also clear: all of this blueprint on paper has yet to be validated.

Although Palantir has proven the viability of the FDE route, AI companies worldwide have yet to make big money in this field. Most domestic AI companies are still stuck at the recruiting stage; OpenAI and Anthropic are making a lot of noise, but have only just embarked on the journey. Moonshot AI's move into FDE has a relatively clear goal; but how exactly to get there cannot be answered by holding a launch event and bringing in a few partners—it requires long-term exploration.

After all, compared with internet companies that are extremely sensitive to technology, old money's money is far harder to earn.
