Summary

This Japanese article by Shohei Tokumitsu, CEO of DeFactory, untangles two often-confused titles in Japan's IT industry. A Tech Lead owns technical vision, architecture and technology choices across projects, coding roughly 30-50% of the time, while a Lead Engineer is the top hands-on implementer within one team, coding 60-80%. The confusion stems from imported titles that companies redefined freely. The author outlines skills separating leads from seniors—translating business requirements into technical ones and raising team-wide productivity—plus common failure modes: taking on too much implementation and micromanaging. He also covers career paths (EM vs. technical track) and freelance prospects: external Tech Leads, fractional CTOs and technical advisors can earn over 1 million yen monthly, with AI-savvy leads sometimes exceeding 2 million yen.

Key points

  • Tech Lead owns architecture and tech selection; Lead Engineer drives implementation within a team.
  • Coding ratios differ: roughly 30-50% for Tech Leads, 60-80% for Lead Engineers.
  • Key promotion skills: translating business needs into technical requirements and boosting team productivity.
  • Common failures are playing too much and micromanaging; delegation and psychological safety fix both.
  • Freelance external Tech Leads can earn 1-2+ million yen per month, especially with AI expertise.

"Tech Lead" and "Lead Engineer" — both are titles often described as positions near the top of an engineer's career path, yet surprisingly few people can accurately explain the difference in their roles. When browsing job postings, you'll find cases where the two are used almost interchangeably, and cases where they are clearly distinguished with well-defined role divisions, which has become a source of confusion for engineers aiming to advance their careers.

Moreover, whether you can land a lead-level position is a major turning point that significantly affects your annual income, market value, and even your rate range after going independent as a freelancer. In this article, drawing on my experience as a technical advisor observing multiple development organizations, I will thoroughly explain the definitional differences between tech leads and lead engineers, the practical skills companies look for, common failure patterns in the field, and even the strategy for going independent and winning high-rate projects exceeding 1 million yen per month as an external tech lead or technical advisor.

About the author of this article Shohei Tokumitsu

Representative Director of DeFactory. Based on his experience leading multiple 0-to-1 new business developments in the software development domain, both in-house and for other companies, and driving business growth, he currently works as an FDE field consultant/delivery lead while also handling product manager (PdM) duties and recruitment (FDE, senior SE, PM, PMO, CPO, CTO, consultants).

Conclusion: While often confused in Japan's IT industry, strictly speaking, a "Tech Lead" tends to refer to the person responsible for cross-project technology selection and architecture design, while a "Lead Engineer" tends to refer to the top player who drives implementation within a specific team (a player-manager). Both are important positions that serve as the technical pillars of a development team.

The biggest reason for this confusion is that when titles born overseas (especially in the US) were imported into Japan, each company added its own definitions, and as a result, the titles spread without a strict industry-wide definition ever being established. In this section, we will organize the essential differences and how they are actually used in practice.

High-rate, direct-contract project information

The Tech Lead is a title established at major tech companies such as Google, Meta, and Amazon, and is a position with final responsibility for the "technical vision" and "architecture design" within a specific project or product area.

Specific responsibilities include the following:

  • Translating product and business requirements into technical requirements and incorporating them into system design
  • Deciding on the programming languages, frameworks, databases, and cloud services to adopt
  • Designing architecture that takes non-functional requirements (performance, availability, security, scalability) into account
  • Managing technical debt and formulating repayment plans
  • Instilling quality standards throughout the organization through design reviews and code reviews
  • Establishing cross-organizational technical guidelines and standards for the engineering organization

The tech lead is the "final decision-maker on technology." While they also write code themselves, the standard practice is to keep their coding ratio at around 30–50% of their overall work, dedicating the rest to design, reviews, coordination, hiring support, and similar activities.

To add a professional perspective, tech leads often do not have line management authority (performance evaluations, 1-on-1s). Management is typically handled separately by an Engineering Manager (EM), and in overseas-style organizations, it is common for the tech lead to focus exclusively on being the "leader of the technical domain."

The Lead Engineer is a position that drives code quality and development speed in the field as the "strongest implementation player" within a specific development team. It is a title widely used at mid-sized companies and startups in Japan, and compared to the overseas-style tech lead, a more "player-oriented" role is expected.

  • Handling difficult implementation tasks and developing core features
  • Actively performing code reviews for other team members
  • Developing junior members through pair programming and mob programming
  • Leading implementation estimation and task breakdown in sprint planning
  • Leading unglamorous but important technical activities such as refactoring and test maintenance
  • Standing on the front lines of bug and incident response

The typical coding ratio for a lead engineer is 60–80%, making the role more implementation-focused than the tech lead. While design decision-making authority rests with the tech lead or higher positions, the lead engineer's main battleground is "implementing the decided design at the highest quality."

When considering a career path from an engineer's perspective, also referring to this article explaining the difference between EM and Tech Lead will help you see the position's place in the organization in a more three-dimensional way.

However, in smaller companies (especially startups with 50 or fewer employees), it is not uncommon for one person to hold both roles. In such cases, even if the title is "Tech Lead," the actual position becomes a dual role of "Lead Engineer + Design Owner."

Conclusion: To take the next step beyond being a "Senior Engineer" with strong coding skills, the ability to translate business requirements into technical requirements and mentoring skills that raise the productivity of the entire team are essential. A perspective that balances resolving technical debt with business growth is required.

When engineers are promoted to lead-level positions, many face the wall of "being evaluated on more than just technical skill." While code quality and speed are certainly important, the decisive factors that separate senior engineers from leads are two additional axes: "the ability to make technical decisions that contribute to the business" and "the ability to raise the level of the entire team."

The most important skill required of a lead is "the ability to translate business requirements into technical requirements." This does not simply mean being able to write requirement specifications; it refers to the ability to understand the value, constraints, and priorities that the business side truly needs, and then work backward to select the optimal technology stack.

Below is a summary of the points an excellent tech lead checks during requirements definition.

  • トラフィック規模と成長予測
    • Current user and request counts, plus projections for 6–12 months ahead
    • Difference between peak and off-peak times (spike patterns)
  • データ規模と特性
    • Expected data volume, data size, and update frequency
    • Whether the data is structured or unstructured, and what the search requirements are
  • 遅延要件（レイテンシ）
    • Acceptable response time range (real-time required, or is batch processing acceptable?)
    • Trade-off between consistency and availability
  • セキュリティ・コンプライアンス要件
    • Handling of personal and confidential information, and encryption requirements
    • Industry-specific regulations (FIEA, PM Act, GDPR, etc.)
  • 運用・保守要件
    • Whether 24/7 monitoring is required, or business-hours-only monitoring is sufficient
    • RTO/RPO in the event of a failure (recovery time objective / recovery point objective)
  • チーム能力と採用市場
    • Whether existing team members can handle the technology, and how easy it is to hire for it
    • Learning costs associated with the technology choice

Making selections such as "managed services vs. building in-house," "monolith vs. microservices," and "RDB vs. NoSQL" after comprehensively evaluating these factors is the job of a professional tech lead.

Common real-world failure examples in technology selection include the following.

  • Choosing a configuration that is difficult to maintain, driven by an engineer's curiosity to "try the latest technology"
  • Adopting excessive microservices suited to large enterprises, based on their case studies, in a small organization
  • Choosing over-provisioned cloud resources that exceed performance requirements, causing costs to balloon
  • Choosing a language with a depleted talent pool in the hiring market (such as an old COBOL-derived language), making it impossible to secure successors

Because technology selection has an extremely large long-term impact on the business, the perspective of "is this sustainable in 3 or 5 years?" rather than "it works, so it's fine" is essential. Decisions become especially complex in data infrastructure and AI utilization areas, so the article on going independent as a freelance MLOps engineer is also a useful reference for technology selection.

Another key responsibility of a lead is "raising the overall development productivity of the team." Unlike senior engineers, who maximize their own development speed, a lead is evaluated on building "an organization that keeps running even without them."

Below are concrete measures for preventing over-reliance on individuals and increasing team velocity.

MeasureSpecific ContentExpected Effect
Establishing coding standardsLanguage-specific style guides, naming conventions, and file structure rulesImproved code consistency and shorter review times
Preparing design templatesTemplates for design documents (RFCs, ADRs)Standardized design discussions and easier follow-up
Documenting code review criteriaExplicitly defining what to review and how far to go with commentsReduced variance in review quality
Setting PR size limitsE.g., 1 PR = within 200–400 changed linesReduced review burden and lower bug introduction rate
Test coverage targetsE.g., 70%+ unit test coverage and coverage of key E2E scenariosHigher quality and easier refactoring
On-call and incident response rotationA system where everyone gains incident response experiencePrevents over-reliance on individuals and improves the whole team's response capability
Regular pair programming and mob programmingPair programming on high-difficulty implementations about once a weekKnowledge sharing and turning tacit knowledge into explicit knowledge
Fostering a documentation cultureMaintaining architecture diagrams and operational runbooksShorter onboarding for new members

The biggest tip for leads introducing these measures is "don't try to do everything perfectly right away." A realistic approach is to assess the team's current state, focus on just 1–2 of the most painful issues, introduce them, and expand while measuring their effect.

Particularly important is "documenting code review criteria." If review granularity varies based on the reviewer's subjective judgment, a sense of unfairness arises—"A's reviews are strict, but B lets things pass"—and the team's trust breaks down. Explicitly documenting coding standards and review criteria to eliminate subjectivity and build an organization where anyone can make consistent judgments is where a lead can truly demonstrate their skill.

Conclusion: The biggest failure in a lead position is the "superplayer trap": taking on all high-difficulty tasks alone out of a sense of responsibility and becoming the bottleneck of development. A professional lead fosters team autonomy through appropriate delegation of authority.

Most engineers promoted to lead positions hit similar walls in their first six months to a year. Simply knowing these failure patterns in advance allows you to prepare countermeasures and makes your start as a lead dramatically smoother.

The most frequent failure is the "playing too much" pattern, where the lead takes on all the difficult tasks themselves because "it's faster to do it myself," leaving no time for their core leadership duties (design, review, mentoring, and coordination).

Once you fall into this pattern, the following vicious cycle occurs:

  • The lead is swamped with implementation tasks, and reviews get delayed
  • Delayed reviews stall the team's PRs, blocking other members' work as well
  • The lead becomes exhausted, and the quality of design decisions declines
  • Junior members feel "I'm never entrusted with important tasks," and their motivation drops
  • As a result, the team becomes a single point of failure (SPOF) that collapses the moment the lead is gone

Here are practical techniques professional leads use to avoid this trap.

TechniqueDetails
Visualize your own time allocationMeasure how your weekly working hours break down into "implementation," "review," "design," "meetings," and "mentoring"
Deliberately hand difficult tasks to othersA mentoring investment: entrust juniors with tasks "you could only teach at a 60-point level" and let them achieve 90 points
Delegation through pair programmingStart by observing alongside them, then gradually let the junior take the lead
Do only the design yourself and delegate implementationBy conducting thorough design reviews, even juniors can maintain quality in implementation
Teach "ways of thinking," not "answers"In code reviews, share decision-making criteria rather than dictating specific rewrites

Particularly important is the mindset of "deliberately handing difficult tasks to others." Many new leads hoard difficult tasks out of anxiety—"I can't afford to have them fail" or "It's more reliable to do it myself." However, depriving juniors of opportunities to learn through failure ultimately weakens the entire team.

The courage to entrust juniors with difficult tasks—after putting in place "designs that limit the risk of failure" and "mechanisms for quick recovery when failure occurs"—is exactly what a professional lead needs.

Alongside "playing too much," another frequent failure is "micromanagement," which is the flip side of a strong sense of responsibility. When a lead gives too many detailed instructions, members feel "my judgment isn't trusted," losing autonomy and motivation.

Typical signs of micromanagement include the following:

  • Checking every commit in detail and issuing granular instructions
  • Checking on progress multiple times a day
  • Overriding members' decisions and replacing them with your own
  • Marking up even the fine wording of meeting minutes and documents
  • Handling all customer interactions and coordination with other departments yourself

Below are practical communication techniques to prevent this and ensure psychological safety.

  • 「なぜ」を共有して「何を」「どうやって」は委譲する
    • Share the purpose of a feature (the Why) with the whole team, and leave the implementation approach (the How) to the members
  • 質問を引き出すフレーズを使う
    • Instead of "you should do it this way," ask "Could you tell me why you chose this design?"
  • 失敗を責めず、失敗から学ぶ場を作る
    • When incidents occur, run Blameless Postmortems (retrospectives that don't blame individuals)
  • 1on1で技術以外のテーマも扱う
    • Regularly check in on career concerns, mental well-being, and interpersonal relationships within the team
  • 「自分が決めること」「相談で決めること」「メンバーが決めること」を明文化する
    • Document the distribution of decision-making authority as a Decision Log to reduce gray areas

In organizations with psychological safety, members can honestly say "I don't understand this" and "I failed," so problems are discovered and resolved early. Conversely, in organizations with low psychological safety, members start hiding information, and problems grow serious without ever surfacing.

In organizations that have adopted Scrum development, clarifying the division of roles with the Scrum Master is also effective. For details, see The Scrum Master's role and going independent as a freelancer.

Conclusion: Lead-level talent combining technical skill with the ability to drive a team is extremely rare in the market. Engineers with these advanced skills, knowledge, and experience have overwhelming market value, and if they go independent as freelancers (external tech leads or technical advisors), they can continuously land high-rate projects well exceeding 1 million yen per month.

After 5 to 10 years building a lead-level career, multiple career paths open up. In addition to stepping up internally to Engineering Manager (EM), VP of Engineering (VPoE), or CTO, a path attracting particular attention in recent years is supporting multiple companies as a freelancer in positions such as "external tech lead," "technical advisor," or "fractional CTO."

When considering career advancement from a lead-level role, you will inevitably face the choice of "pursuing the management track or mastering the technical track." The differences between the two are summarized below.

ItemTech Lead / Lead Engineer (technical track)Engineering Manager (management track)
Primary responsibilitiesTechnical decisions, architecture, code qualityPerformance reviews, hiring, development, organizational management
Coding ratio30–80% (continues to be involved in implementation)0–20% (steps away from implementation)

Compensation ceiling15–25 million yen (including stock options)15–30 million yen (including stock options)

Typical motivations for engineers choosing the technical track include "I love writing code," "I want to make technical decisions myself to the very end," and "I'd rather spend my time on technical exploration than on management work." Meanwhile, engineers choosing the management track often cite motivations such as "I discovered how rewarding it is to build an organization," "I want to leverage my impact through people," and "I want work closer to business management."

Note that the two are not an either-or choice; in recent years, "zigzag careers" that move back and forth between them over the course of a career have also become common. For example, there are career paths such as "Lead Engineer → EM → VPoE → CTO," as well as routes like "Lead Engineer → Tech Lead → Principal Engineer → Technical Advisor." If opportunities to act as a bridge between planning/creative teams increase while keeping technical skills at your core, another option is to expand your career into the role of Technical Director.

  • Understanding of industry domains (business knowledge in FinTech, HealthTech, HR Tech, etc.)

Conclusion: This article answers questions engineers aiming for career advancement or independence have about role boundaries and required experience, based on realistic on-the-ground standards.

A: A senior engineer maximizes "individual productivity," while a lead engineer maximizes "the productivity of the entire team."

A senior engineer is evaluated as an "individual player" who can write large amounts of high-quality code and reliably clear difficult tasks. A lead engineer, on the other hand, is evaluated not only on their own output but on "the total output of all team members."

The specific differences are summarized below.

ItemSenior EngineerLead Engineer
Evaluation criteriaOwn implementation resultsThe team's overall output
Code reviewsOptional (often participates)Mandatory (sets the standards)
Task selectionWorks on assigned tasksDecides who handles which tasks
Mentoring junior engineersOptionalMandatory responsibility
Design decisionsOnly within their own scopeLeads design across the entire team

The hardest part of moving from senior to lead is the mindset shift of "increasing others' output even at the cost of reducing your own." In the short term, your own code commit count decreases, so your evaluation may seem to drop, but over six months to a year, the improvement in overall team productivity is what gets recognized.

A: Because these titles originated overseas and were imported into Japan, each company added its own definitions, and as a result they spread without an industry-wide standard definition ever being established.

In Japan especially, at smaller organizations one person often holds both roles, so some companies define "tech lead = a lead who also implements" and "lead engineer = an implementation-focused lead," while others adopt the opposite definitions.

When job hunting or selecting projects, it is important to judge based on the contents of the "job description (JD)" rather than the title. The specific items to check are as follows.

  • Expected coding ratio (full-time implementation, or around 30%?)
  • Scope of design decision authority (can you choose technologies, or must you use predetermined ones?)
  • Management responsibilities (do you have personnel evaluation authority, or only technical guidance?)
  • Cross-team scope (within a specific team, or across multiple teams?)
  • Scale of the product (a monolith, or a set of microservices?)

For job postings where these points are unclear, there is a high risk of feeling a mismatch after joining, so be sure to confirm them during the interview.

When working as a freelance tech lead, one extremely important practical consideration is "documenting your scope of responsibility." Unlike employment contracts, under service agreements any work not specified in the contract falls outside your scope of responsibility. Clarifying at the contract stage matters such as "how far your technical decision-making authority extends," "how responsibility is shared in the event of an incident," and "intellectual property rights for deliverables" is key to preventing disputes later.

A: You do not need deep knowledge in every area. Rather, a "T-shaped" or "π-shaped" skill profile—deep expertise in one or two areas combined with broad, foundational understanding of the others—is a realistic approach.

Here is an example of an ideal tech lead skill profile.

  • 専門領域（深さ重視）：1〜2領域で「他社の技術選定にもアドバイスできる」レベル

What is especially important is maintaining the state of "knowing what you don't know." You don't need to know everything perfectly, but the broad perspective that allows you to judge "this is outside my expertise, so I should consult a specialist in XX" is the quality most essential to a tech lead.

Conclusion: Tech leads and lead engineers are key figures who determine the success or failure of products in modern software development. By honing skills that drive both technology and teams beyond the role of a mere programmer, you can build a solid career as an engineer. Leveraging that track record to go independent as a freelancer makes it possible to achieve a flexible working style that combines both autonomy and high rates.

In this article, starting from the differences between tech leads and lead engineers, we have thoroughly covered the practical skills companies seek, common failure patterns in the field, and strategies for going independent as a freelancer and landing high-rate projects. Let us recap the key points.

  • Tech leads are "those responsible for technical vision and architecture design," while lead engineers are "player-managers who drive implementation," though definitions vary by company
  • What is required of lead-level engineers is not only technical skill but also "the ability to translate business requirements into technical requirements" and "the ability to raise the productivity of the entire team"
  • Common on-the-ground failures fall into two patterns: "too much hands-on work" and "micromanagement." These can be prevented through deliberate delegation and ensuring psychological safety
  • Talent with lead-level track records is extremely rare in the market, and going independent as a freelancer makes it realistically possible to land high-rate projects exceeding ¥1 million to ¥2 million per month
  • Options for working arrangements are also expanding, including external tech leads, fractional CTOs, and technical advisors, enabling working styles suited to your lifestyle

The world of software development is expected to become increasingly complex as AI technology evolves. Precisely because we are in an era where AI can generate code, the value of tech leads and lead engineers who can make technical judgments about "what to build and how" will only increase relatively.

If you are an engineer with lead-level experience, we encourage you to consider going independent as a freelancer. A career strategy of continuously increasing your market value while serving as a technical pillar for multiple companies' products, maximizing both autonomy and compensation, is one of the working styles best suited to the current era.
