How to Run FDE Projects: Practical Frameworks You Can Use in the Field

Original title: FDEのプロジェクトはどう進める？現場で使える実践の型

FDE総研 · 2026-07-23 · 6 min read

Summary

This article from FDE総研 outlines VACAN Technologies' framework for running FDE projects: enter the customer's site to uncover the real problem, structure and prioritize issues, quickly build small working outputs (often using generative AI), then validate and embed them on-site through iteration. VACAN frames this as digging small wells that are later connected into a channel, arguing small builds surface misalignment early and reduce rework. It contrasts FDE work with contracted development: FDEs own outcomes from problem redefinition to adoption, not just implementation of fixed specs. Palantir's public materials are cited to show FDEs personally handle design, implementation, and testing alongside end users.

Key points

  • FDE projects follow four iterated steps: enter the field, structure problems, build small, validate and embed.
  • VACAN's metaphor: dig small wells to quench immediate needs, then connect them into a channel.
  • Generative AI mainly accelerates the 'build small' step, speeding the iteration loop.
  • Unlike contracted development, FDEs are accountable for outcomes, not just implementing given specs.
  • Palantir's blog confirms FDEs do design, implementation, and testing themselves with end users.

How do you run an FDE project?

Enter the field to capture the real problem, structure it, build something small that works, and validate and embed it in the field. This iteration is the FDE framework. Rather than building a large specification all at once, you iterate quickly toward results.

An FDE (Forward Deployed Engineer) is a role that works alongside the customer's business and takes on problem-solving itself. VACAN Technologies explains this approach using water as a metaphor. The starting point of an FDE project is getting involved before requirements are fixed, at the stage of "problem discovery," rather than moving only once requirements are settled.

What does it mean to enter the field and capture the real problem?

An FDE project begins by going deep into the customer's field and capturing the real problem. The first job is to grasp the issues that are truly stuck underneath, not the surface-level requests. VACAN's CEO, Tamaki Ryu, described the aim of the launch as "going deep into the customer's field, capturing the real problem, organizing and structuring it, and moving forward while also leveraging generative AI" (Wantedly interview). This attitude of "entering the field" determines the quality of every subsequent step.

How do you structure the problem and decide the order?

The problems you capture should not go straight into implementation; instead, organize and structure them and decide the order in which to solve them. This is the step of identifying what the essential bottleneck is and where to start so the field gets relief fastest. If you get the order wrong here, even a working deliverable won't quench the field's thirst. Structuring is the design work that aims the next step, "build small," at the right target.

What is the iteration that runs through validation and adoption?

FDE doesn't end with building; it takes responsibility for getting it used in the field, improving it, and making it stick. You place the small deliverable in the field, learn from how it is actually used, and return to re-capturing the problem. This cycle of "capture → structure → build → validate and embed" is the framework of an FDE project. Its distinguishing feature is that it keeps iterating until results appear, rather than delivering once. With each iteration, understanding of the field deepens, and the real problem that was invisible at first comes into focus. That is why an approach that re-captures the problem while running, rather than fixing all requirements upfront, becomes effective.

Why "build small"?

To quench the field's thirst quickly and improve it while it is being used. Rather than elaborate planning, we prioritize delivering a working output fast.

Instead of building one giant dam, dig a "small well" that quenches the field's thirst, and eventually connect the wells into a waterway. VACAN puts this idea at the core of its message (official website). If you build something large and then release it, the rework when it misses the field's needs is huge. Build small, and you can find and fix the mismatch early.

What is the philosophy of "digging small wells"?

"Dig small wells, and eventually connect them into a waterway" is VACAN's central metaphor for how FDE works. First deliver a small solution that quenches the thirst right in front of you, then use it as a starting point to gradually connect them and grow into a larger system (a waterway). It is the mindset of assembling the whole as a series of working pieces, rather than aiming for the finished form from the start.

What are the advantages of delivering a working output fast?

The biggest advantage of delivering something that works fast is that it can be grown while being used in the field. Problems that only become apparent through use can be incorporated into the next improvement, so you get closer to results than with desk-based planning. Building small means less effort invested, and if the direction is off, the pain of course correction is smaller. Starting from the thirst you quenched, dig the next well, and eventually connect them into a waterway. This is the driving force of the FDE-style project and the reason for choosing to "build small."

What are the four steps of the approach?

Proceed through four steps: enter the field, structure it, build small, and validate and embed. The steps are not one-way; you iterate through them.

What is the aim of each step?

The four steps exist to ensure the quality of the next stage. Organized as a procedure, they are as follows.

  • Enter the field: Go deep into the customer's field and capture the real problem behind the requests.
  • Structure it: Organize and structure the captured problem, and decide the order to solve it.
  • Build small: Quickly shape a working deliverable while leveraging generative AI.
  • Validate and embed: Get it used in the field, and make it stick through repeated improvement.

Palantir's public information also states that FDEs perform design, implementation, and testing themselves and implement solutions in collaboration with end users (Palantir official blog). Not outsourcing the building work but getting hands-on is common to all the steps.

Where does generative AI shine?

Generative AI is most valuable in the third step, "build small." It boosts the speed at which a working deliverable takes shape. Tamaki also described the approach as "organizing and structuring, then moving forward while leveraging generative AI" (Wantedly). The faster you can build, the faster you can run the cycle of validation and improvement.

How is it different from contract development?

Where contract work "builds a fixed specification," FDE takes responsibility "from redefining the problem through to results." The difference is questioning the very thing to be built.

What is the decisive difference from "building to a specification"?

The decisive difference is that the scope of responsibility lies in outcomes, not in implementing specifications. Tamaki describes this distinction as "not merely providing consultation, and not merely building systems. We work hands-on alongside the customer's business" (Wantedly). FDE is neither an advisor nor a development company that simply builds to spec; it runs alongside the customer from redefining the problem through implementation and adoption.
