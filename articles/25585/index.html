After WAIC 2026, the competitive focus of enterprise AI has shifted from "model capability" to "delivery loop". This article breaks down training vs. inference deployment, the AI FDE (Forward Deployed Engineer), and the "pay-for-value-outcome" mechanism, anchoring naming cases and quantifiable gains with reports from QbitAI and 36Kr on Runjian and ChaoQing.

The so-called "delivery loop" means that enterprise AI no longer stops at "can the model answer", but completes the full cycle of "entering the field → uncovering scenarios → doing work with tokens and tools → quantifying business gains → settling payment based on value outcomes". At WAIC 2026, this loop was widely recognized as "the most tangible and weighty standard" for measuring AI capability — as QbitAI put it in Ten Trends I Saw at WAIC 2026: "AI Chat has become commonplace; being able to do work and deliver is the new winning way." If you only want a one-sentence takeaway: the key to preventing enterprise AI implementations from fizzling out has shifted from "picking a stronger model" to "running through a delivery loop that can be quantified and settled".

Below, we break this loop down clearly — why it has replaced "model capability" as the competitive focus, how to weigh training against inference deployment, what an AI FDE is and how it differs from traditional delivery, and why it can support "pay for business outcomes".

What is the "delivery loop"? Why did it replace "model capability" as the competitive focus after WAIC 2026?

In one sentence: because "being able to answer" is no longer scarce, while "being able to deliver" is. As model capabilities converge, the real question for enterprises is no longer "which model scores higher", but "can it get something done in my business and clearly account for the gains".

This shift was clearly corroborated across sources at WAIC 2026. QbitAI observed that this year agents became the default topic jointly showcased and discussed by nearly 70% of exhibitors, with the implicit theme of the venue being "Show me the Agent"; its conclusion was that "whether effective delivery can be achieved is becoming the most tangible and weighty standard for measuring large model capability", and the industry focus has shifted from stacking parameters to supply-demand matching — that is, which scenario is best suited for enterprises to empower with AI and which core business problem to solve.

Practitioners put it even more directly. Tang Chunfeng, CEO of ChaoQing Digital Intelligence, said in an interview with 36Kr that "the development of AI has in fact rapidly shifted from large model training to inference application deployment"; the focus of this year's WAIC has "gradually shifted from model capability competition to application value creation". Runjian distilled this trend into one sentence: "Competition in the AI industry is moving from 'model capability competition' to 'value loop capability competition'". Three sources, three perspectives, all pointing to the same conclusion — the battlefield of competition has moved from "models" to "loops".

Training vs. inference deployment: where should enterprises place their budget bets?

Direct answer: for the vast majority of implementation-focused enterprises, the budget center of gravity should shift from "training" to "inference deployment", and the measurement standard should shift from "accuracy/leaderboards" to "business gains and energy efficiency". Training determines whether a model can be built; inference deployment determines whether it is worth doing under your cost structure.

Behind this is an emerging "token economics". Tang Chunfeng pointed out that the industry is focusing on "AI factories" and token economics, "essentially how to obtain greater output with less input". QbitAI's observations offer two hard thresholds: first, 100,000-GPU-scale compute clusters have become the entry baseline for new-generation AI infrastructure; second, SenseTime proposed using TPW (tokens produced per watt of power) to measure data center value — the measurement dimension has changed from "how much compute" to "how many effective tokens per watt of electricity".

For the economics of inference deployment, Runjian's Wuxiang Yungu Token Factory provides a quantifiable sample: its "Runchan platform" boosted token throughput performance by 700%; a self-built 220 kV dedicated transformer provides 480 MW of capacity, expected to cut compute usage costs by more than 30%; and microchannel liquid cooling combined with 800 V high-voltage DC pushes PUE below 1.1. The same interview also highlights an often-overlooked overseas variable: overseas token costs are currently high, roughly 10 to 30 times those in China — meaning "where to run inference and how many tokens to use" is itself a cost engineering problem, not something the training stage can solve.

It should be noted that this does not mean "training doesn't matter", but rather that for most enterprises that do not build their own foundation models, both value and risk are concentrated on the inference deployment side — see our AI implementation column for more AI implementation practices.

What is an AI FDE (Forward Deployed Engineer)? How does it differ from traditional delivery?

In one sentence: an AI FDE (AI Forward Deployed Engineer) is a delivery role that "embeds at the customer's site, uncovering scenarios with tools and tokens in hand and delivering business value", fundamentally different from the traditional project model of "leave once the system is delivered".

This concept was explicitly systematized by Runjian at this year's WAIC. According to a 36Kr report, Runjian defined an AI FDE (AI Forward Deployed Engineer) service system, further naming it VGE = Value Growth Engineering: "dispatching FDE teams into customer sites to jointly uncover AI application scenarios and value with enterprises... quantifying the economic gains of every token, paying based on value outcomes, and forming a complete value loop." In other words, the FDE is not old wine in a new bottle of "outsourced on-site staffing"; its deliverable is not a system, but a quantifiable, settleable business gain.

DimensionTraditional project deliveryAI FDE / VGE delivery loop
Team positionLeave after deliveryFDE team embeds at the customer's site
Delivery goalDeliver systems / featuresUncover scenarios + quantify the economic gains of every token
Payment methodPer person-day / contract valuePay for value outcomes
Risk ownershipCustomer bears implementation risk aloneVendor and customer share risk and co-create value
VerifiabilityEnds at acceptanceQuantifiable gains (e.g., lower loss rates, higher efficiency)

The key difference lies in the last two rows: in traditional delivery, implementation risk is essentially transferred to the customer after acceptance; under the FDE model, the vendor ties its own revenue to the customer's business outcomes — which is also why it must "enter the field", because without being on-site, the "value" used for settlement cannot be defined or quantified.

Can enterprise AI implementation be paid for based on business outcomes / value?

Direct answer: yes, and there are already cases with proven loops and verified delivery — provided that "value" is quantified in advance into a settleable metric.

Runjian's approach anchors settlement on "the economic benefit generated per token," forming a complete closed loop with pay-for-value outcomes. This is not a PowerPoint concept: according to the same interview, its manufacturing VGE model has landed 7 projects with contracts signed, 2 of which have already been delivered; its "cognitive foundation" product Apollo 11 has also landed seven or eight projects in manufacturing. The figures "contracts signed, some delivered" matter because the hardest part of pay-for-value has never been the concept, but whether both parties can reach an enforceable contract on "what counts and how it is measured."

SuperEngine AI offers another sample from a different industry: in Beijing's Yizhuang district, it partnered with oncology drug developer Yikang Pharmaceutical to deliver a full-stack "AI acceleration platform", aiming to improve new drug R&D efficiency while reducing R&D costs, with multiple projects already landed. R&D efficiency and cost are precisely the kind of business metrics that can be defined upfront and verified afterward—the very soil in which "pay-for-results" can take root.

Why do enterprise AI deployments end up abandoned? How can a delivery closed loop prevent this?

In one sentence: abandonment is usually not because the model isn't strong enough, but because of "comparing only model capability while lacking supply-demand fit and quantifiable delivery"—the closed loop fills these three gaps through "on-site co-creation + benefit quantification + settlement by results."

We previously broke down "why it's hard" in Why Enterprise AI Deployment Is So Difficult; here we offer the follow-up "how the closed loop solves it." The typical path to abandonment: during vendor selection, benchmarks and demos win people over; once in the business, the scenario turns out to be a poor fit, benefits can't be articulated, and no one claims accountability—so the project stalls in the intermediate state of "demoable but not scalable." QbitAI's observation points to exactly the opposite: when the focus shifts to supply-demand fit and effective delivery, the success criterion changes from "does the model answer well" to "did the business metrics move."

One anchor for quantifiable benefits comes from QbitAI's reporting: Lingchu Intelligence, equipped with its proprietary large model Psi-R2, completed tasks such as plug-and-play inspection and quality judgment through multi-robot collaboration, reducing production loss rates by about 10%. "Loss rate down about 10%" is exactly the kind of hard metric that can be written into acceptance criteria and used to back-calculate ROI—the value of the closed loop lies precisely in translating AI outcomes from "it feels better" into a number like this that can be settled. The engineering essentials for avoiding abandonment therefore converge into three steps: go on-site to align with real scenarios, quantify benefits into contract terms, and settle by results rather than by hours.

6AM's AI FDE Delivery Model

6AM (Chenqi Technology) builds the above mechanisms into its own delivery methodology: AI FDEs enter the customer's business site upfront, first identify a scenario where "benefits are quantifiable and metrics are verifiable," then use tools and tokens to run it into a closed loop, with business outcomes as the basis for measurement and settlement—rather than delivering a system no one is accountable for. Its essential difference from traditional project-based "deliver and leave" models is that the deliverable is quantifiable business benefit, not a system.

Can enterprise AI deployment be paid for by business results / value? Yes. Runjian has run a closed loop on "quantify the economic benefit per token and pay for value outcomes"; its manufacturing VGE model has landed 7 projects with contracts signed, 2 of which have been delivered. The key prerequisite is agreeing upfront on "value" as measurable, verifiable contract terms, such as efficiency gains or reduced loss rates.

The way to avoid it is to complete the delivery closed loop: FDEs go on-site to align with real scenarios, quantify benefits into contract terms (such as hard metrics like Lingchu Intelligence's ~10% reduction in production loss rate), and settle by results rather than by hours.
