FDE Role Explained: Column Is Hiring Forward Deployed Engineers

A job posting published on October 6, 2026 shows that Column, a bank that offers its services as APIs, is hiring a Forward Deployed Engineer (FDE) in San Francisco. The engineer will serve as the technical lead for strategic customers, owning everything from onboarding through launch day.

At first glance, this seems contradictory. Column's API is described by an OpenAPI spec and comes with a sandbox—exactly the kind of product that should let customers serve themselves: read the docs, call the endpoints, get things done. So why place an engineer next to the customer?

If you are an FDE, or want to become one, this question is worth thinking through carefully. When the product is an API, the place where customers integrate is the product itself.

The person standing there, if given enough authority, does more than just get customers up and running. They carry the places where customers get stuck back into the product and push for fixes. That is exactly the dividing line between a real FDE and customer support with a new name.

A bank built for engineers, but not built from scratch

Column calls itself the only nationally chartered bank designed specifically to help customers build and fund new financial products. The company says it was founded by engineers to solve the many frictions of working with incumbent banks.

However, "a bank built for engineers" does not mean starting from zero. According to a law firm's wiki, rather than founding another startup, the founders acquired a single-branch bank in Chico, California.

This detail matters more for the FDE role than it might seem. Beneath the API layer is a real bank: Column's Solutions Engineer job posting also lists regulatory and risk-management responsibilities. So the person standing between the customer and the API must understand both code and the constraints that spec documents cannot fully capture.

Why good documentation is not enough

Imagine a startup that wants to embed accounts and transfers into its app. The team reads the docs and quickly builds a flow in the sandbox. Then they get stuck on things the docs don't cover: how to reconcile transfers when they are returned, how to model accounts for end customers, and what must be completed before launch day.

If every question goes through a ticket, the customer team waits days, and the bank sees ten unrelated tickets. But if ten strategic customers get stuck in the same place, that is not ten support cases—it is a flaw in the documentation or API design. Only someone who has followed an integration from start to finish can spot that pattern.

This is why Column's job posting hands both jobs to one person. The FDE designs, builds, and launches products directly alongside partner engineering teams, while working closely with Column's own engineers to shape the API, documentation, and developer experience.

How is an FDE different from a Solutions Engineer?

Column is not short of customer-facing engineers. Its Solutions Engineer role handles the technical engagement with customers across pre-sales, implementation, onboarding, and support, and the posting itself notes that this role is more technical than similar roles commonly seen in the industry.

Since both roles are highly technical, the difference is unlikely to lie in skill level. Reading the two postings closely, the difference is in the phases covered: the Solutions Engineer covers the full journey from pre-sales to post-sales, while the FDE owns a narrower stretch as the technical lead—from onboarding to launch—and gets to contribute to the product itself.

Placing this posting next to independent definitions of the FDE role makes it clear what Column explicitly promises and what you need to ask about yourself. The venture firm CRV considers writing production code in customer environments the defining trait of an FDE, and believes FDEs can cover both pre-sales and post-sales while writing code.

PostHog describes the FDE as someone embedded in customer teams who bridges the gap between the product and real needs. Palantir's Forward Deployed Software Engineer posting says these engineers work with customers, often on site, to understand and solve their problems.

The last column matters most. A job posting gives you promises; the answers in the interview tell you whether those promises are kept.

Read FDE job postings like an engineer

PostHog points out that some companies use the FDE title for work closer to that of a Sales Engineer. So two postings that both say FDE, in any country, are not guaranteed to be the same job.

The clue is in the verbs. If a posting, like Column's, states that the FDE will shape the API, documentation, or platform, the role has a path back into the product. If the posting only talks about support and collaboration, ask directly: who drove the most recent change that pushed a customer need into the product?

One more line in Column's posting is worth noting: candidates need strong project management skills, able to advance multiple customer projects at once while keeping each one moving forward.

Spell out both parts of the role in your resume

If you have ever updated documentation after seeing customers repeatedly ask the same question, or proposed changing an endpoint because it made integrators guess, put those experiences at the top of your resume. For a role described the way Column describes it, this is the most direct evidence that you can turn messy customer problems into concrete changes to the product.

How you write it matters just as much. Don't write "supported customers integrating the API"; write it as a chain: what problem the customer hit, which part you owned through launch, how many customers you handled in parallel, and what you brought back to the product team.

If you don't have that experience yet, the cheapest way to get it is to be the integrator yourself. Pick an API with a public sandbox, build a small flow, note every point where you had to guess, and turn that list into suggestions for improving the docs and API. This is the exercise closest to an FDE's daily work at a company that sells APIs.

A good OpenAPI spec is one answer. This FDE job posting points to a second: put a person between the customer's problems and that spec, so that over time the spec forces customers to guess less and less.
