Summary

This long-form Japanese guide by DeFactory CEO Shohei Tokumitsu targets executives considering adopting Forward Deployed Engineers (FDEs). It defines the FDE as a hybrid engineer-consultant, originating at Palantir, who embeds at customer sites and owns everything from problem discovery through AI implementation to adoption. Growth is driven by the AI "implementation gap"—BCG finds 74% of companies struggle to scale AI value—and Japan's FDE job postings grew 729% year over year (643 to 5,330) as of April 2026. The article contrasts FDEs with SES, IT consulting, and SIer roles, cites compensation ranges (¥10–25M salary in Japan; ¥1.3–3M monthly cost), and argues the model's core is feeding field insights back into products to lower marginal cost. It warns against imitation without a reusable platform and recommends multi-person teams and one-year, outcome-based contracts.

Key points

  • FDEs, pioneered by Palantir, embed on-site and own delivery from problem definition to AI adoption.
  • Japan's FDE postings grew 729% year over year; OpenAI, AWS, Anthropic, and Microsoft made major investments in 2026.
  • Japanese FDE salaries run ¥10–25M; procurement costs roughly ¥1.3–3M per month.
  • The model requires a reusable platform, high enterprise deal sizes, and collaborative customer culture.
  • Common failures: SES-style micromanagement and single-person dependency; small teams and one-year contracts are advised.

An FDE (Forward Deployed Engineer) is an "issue-solving" type of engineering role that embeds into the client company's operations, adapts the company's products and AI technology to the client's business context, and works alongside them until results are achieved. The biggest difference from traditional IT roles is that they sell problem-solving (results) rather than technology (resources).

"Forward Deployed" was originally a military term meaning "forward deployment" or "frontline positioning."

Rather than placing experts at headquarters (i.e., the head office or development base), they are deployed directly at the front line (i.e., the client's site), making decisions and executing in real time according to local conditions. FDE is the concept of bringing this approach into software development.

In other words, an FDE is not an "engineer who develops from behind a desk at headquarters," but rather "an engineer deployed to the front line of the client, writing code on the spot and transforming the business."

The origin of FDE lies with the US data analytics company Palantir.

The company's main clients—government agencies, defense organizations, and financial institutions—could rarely use off-the-shelf software as-is; their operations involved inconsistent data formats, complex business flows, and undocumented tacit knowledge on the ground.

Moreover, due to high confidentiality requirements, there was the unique circumstance that clients themselves could not articulate "what they needed."

To address this challenge, Palantir devised a model of deploying engineers directly to client sites, where they would repeatedly observe, experiment, and build on the spot. This was the beginning of FDE as a role (the company's official title is FDSE: Forward Deployed Software Engineer, with the internal name Delta).

According to Salesforce's official blog, until around 2016, Palantir had more Deltas than regular software engineers, and FDE was the very core of the company's business model. Bob McGrew, former Chief Research Officer at OpenAI, described FDE as "productized consulting."

How Palantir's FDEs actually operate in the field is described in detail in the official blog post "A Day in the Life of a Palantir Forward Deployed Software Engineer".

FDE is a new model positioned in between SaaS (building a product for people to use) and contract development (building from scratch for each client).

  • A regular software engineer: builds one feature for many clients
  • An FDE: implements many features for one client

Taking responsibility not for generalization, but for optimization to a specific client's business context. This is the decisive characteristic that sets FDEs apart from other engineering roles.

An FDE can be defined as a role that is something like "a consultant-engineer who embeds into the client's site, adapts the company's products and technology to the client's business context, and delivers results."

Rather than simply providing development resources or delivering proposals, FDEs are gaining attention as a new model that provides problem-solving.

In the AI era, we have entered an age where what is fundamentally sought is not the drill (resource provision and delivery), but the hole (problem-solving).

The reason FDE is expanding so rapidly lies in a structural problem in AI adoption: the "implementation gap." According to a BCG survey, 74% of companies struggle to scale the value of AI adoption.

While the emergence of generative AI has dramatically expanded technical possibilities, companies on the ground constantly voice concerns like the following:

  • "We want to adopt AI, but we don't know where to start."
  • "We completed a PoC (proof of concept), but hit a wall when moving to production."
  • "We introduced tools, but they never took hold in the field."

Traditional SaaS derived value from being "ready to use right after deployment," but AI products have characteristics such as probabilistic outputs with no guaranteed correct answers, and integration into existing business workflows is essential. Simply deploying generic APIs or tools does not fit into frontline operations.

As talent to fill this "last mile of AI adoption," FDE-related job postings grew 729% year over year, from 643 in April 2025 to 5,330 in April 2026 (as reported by Business Insider).

Unlike traditional specialized engineers (upstream design, development, and support roles), the scope of FDE work is end-to-end, from planning through to adoption. It can be broadly divided into three phases.

Phase 1: Understanding the field and defining problems

FDEs go into customers' offices, factories, logistics sites, and other locations to observe operations, interview stakeholders, and investigate data. Rather than "receiving" requirements via intake sheets, the FDE approach is to directly observe the field and "define" the essential problems that even customers themselves have not noticed.

Phase 2: Design, implementation, and production deployment

FDEs grasp the overall picture of existing SaaS products and data assets, design their integration, and build and implement workflows leveraging their own products and AI (LLMs, RAG, agents, etc.). The basic approach is to build prototypes quickly and iterate on improvements based on field reactions. They do not stop at PoCs; they personally carry projects through to production deployment.

Phase 3: Adoption support and product feedback

FDEs accompany customers through field training and business process redesign until usage takes hold. They also play a role in feeding insights gained in the field back to their own product teams and reflecting them in product improvements. This "feedback loop" is the core of the FDE model (details in Chapter 7).

As of 2026, the standard way of working is becoming hybrid: not only full-time on-site presence, but also one to three site visits per week combined with remote support.

The biggest difference between FDE and SES is "what is provided."

SES provides labor hours (man-hours), while FDE commits to solving business problems. This difference affects everything: costs, contract design, and how results are measured.

On the surface, FDE and SES look similar because both "work on-site at the customer," so they are often confused. However, as Nikkei xTECH clearly distinguishes, stating that "the American-style FDE is similar to but fundamentally different from Japanese on-site dispatch," the two differ in structure.

  • FDE: Defines the customer's problem itself, designs, implements, and carries it through to operations. The evaluation metric is business outcomes
  • SES: Provides development resources (labor) based on the customer's instructions. The evaluation metric is hours worked

The FDE's "on-site presence" is not the kind of presence SES engineers have, of "just being there," but a purpose-driven presence of "joining the field when needed to solve the customer's problem."

FDE includes the functions of a Solution Architect (SA), but goes beyond them.

While SAs focus on design and strategy and often leave implementation to other teams, FDEs take responsibility for building, implementing, deploying, and maintaining the solutions they design themselves.

The difference from Customer Success (CS) is "whether you write code." While CS focuses on driving adoption and supporting usage, FDEs perform the technical integration and custom implementation themselves in the field.

Also, the "Implementation Engineer" role used by foreign IT vendors is an adjacent role to SA and FDE in that it specializes in deploying, configuring, and integration testing specific products. Not simply "building the requested features," but the ability to understand the customer's business structure and workflows, identify "what problem really needs to be solved," and frame it as a question.

Rather than acting once requirements are handed over, the first dividing line in being evaluated as an FDE is whether you can stand on the side that defines the requirements themselves.

Even if you can discover the problem, an FDE's value is only half realized if you cannot shape it into a technical solution.

Specifically, the following technical areas are required.

  • Programming: Python (virtually essential for AI/ML projects), JavaScript / TypeScript (full-stack development, API integration)
  • Full-stack experience: Ability to work across frontend, backend, databases, and infrastructure single-handedly
  • Data infrastructure: Integrating disparate data sources, pipeline design, and cleansing
  • AI implementation: Design through production implementation of LLMs, RAG, and AI agents, plus reliability design covering evaluation (eval), guardrails, and permission management
  • Client engagement and articulation: Ability to explain technical content clearly to non-engineer executives and field staff, and build consensus
  • From software engineering: Strong technical skills, so reinforce client engagement, problem definition, and business understanding
  • From IT consulting: Strong proposal and structuring abilities, so reinforce hands-on implementation skills (Python, LLM implementation, etc.)
  • From PdM: Strong customer understanding and requirements definition, so reinforce technical implementation skills

If you understand FDE as "high-rate on-site dispatch," you will miss its essence. The core of the FDE model is a structure that feeds insights gained in the field back into your own product, continuously lowering marginal costs.

You can find out whether the FDE model fits your company with a free 3-minute assessment.

In traditional consulting and SI models, customized deliverables are handed over per customer and the engagement ends there; the knowledge is completed within that project. Because each project is built from scratch, marginal costs never fall, making it essentially a labor-intensive, man-hour-based business.

In the FDE model, by contrast, the connectors, features, and patterns built for the first customer are generalized and fed back into the product. Deployment costs for subsequent customers then drop, and although FDE effort is incurred each time, the assets created accumulate and push down the cost basis of the next project.

  • Consulting/SIer: Even after working 100 client sites, the results are 100 individual deliverables
  • FDE model: After working 100 client sites, the product evolves 100 times

Palantir's FDEs are valued not because the billable hours of FDEs alone are profitable.

FDEs embed deeply into customer operations to solve problems, and convert those results into expanded contracts for their own platform (Foundry, AIP), effectively functioning as customer acquisition cost.

On the books it's labor cost, but economically it behaves much like customer acquisition investment, and what FDEs generate is recurring product revenue.

From the customer's perspective, once an FDE has redesigned business workflows and implemented data integration, it becomes the "foundation of operations," and canceling would break the core of the business. This is the true nature of Palantir's extremely high customer retention rate.

If you overlook this structure and interpret "FDE = high-priced contract development," then even if you think you're doing the same thing, in reality it's just on-site staffing. For the FDE model to work, the following three conditions are prerequisites.

  • A reusable platform and technical assets exist: There must be a structure or setup where customization is layered on a common foundation rather than built from scratch for each customer. *Note: a product is not strictly required
  • Enterprise-focused with high customer unit prices: The contract size must be large enough to recover FDE labor costs (from ¥1.5 million per month)
  • A culture of collaboration with the front line exists: The customer must accept FDEs as "partners in problem-solving" rather than "targets of instructions"

Marc Andrusko of a16z also warns in "The Palantir-ization of Everything" that prioritizing embedded engineers without a platform foundation leads into the quagmire of custom deployments that cannot be maintained or upgraded.

From here on, I will explain the practical aspects of FDE adoption for corporate personnel responsible for AI implementation and DX promotion.

If your company's problems match the top three, FDE is a strong option.

On the other hand, if the goal is the bottom row, "replenishing headcount," you should consider SES rather than FDE.

If you place an order based solely on the word "FDE" without understanding this difference, you are likely to fall into the failure patterns described later.

Here are additional examples of problems.

Benefit 1: End-to-end from PoC to production to adoption

In conventional AI adoption, consultants draw up strategy, SIers develop, and SES maintains—a division-of-labor setup in which information and speed are lost during handoffs between phases. Because the same person (team) handles everything from problem definition to adoption, FDEs structurally eliminate this handoff loss.

Benefit 2: Tacit knowledge from the front line can be captured in data and systems

Undocumented business rules, exception handling, and frontline know-how cannot be captured through interview sheets. Because FDEs embed into the front line and directly observe operations, they can verbalize tacit knowledge as requirements and reflect it in systems.

Benefit 3: Knowledge transfer toward in-house development

Through the FDE's support period, internal staff can absorb know-how on data utilization and AI implementation. Rather than ending with "having it built externally," it also functions as training investment toward future in-house capability.

Benefit 4: Resilient to changing requirements

In fixed-price contracts, every specification change incurs additional costs and contract amendments, but FDEs operate on a quasi-delegation basis with contract design that assumes dynamic requirement changes—a structure well suited to the AI project style of "requirements solidify while building."

Based on my experience supporting the launch of multiple FDE organizations, there are three typical patterns of FDE adoption failure.

Failure 1: Embedding engineers without a platform foundation, and custom development spiraling into a quagmire

If you merely imitate FDE-style staffing without a reusable foundation, one-off systems proliferate per customer and maintenance becomes impossible (see Section 7-3).

Failure 2: Dependence on a single FDE, with knowledge lost upon their departure

Because FDEs accumulate customer domain knowledge in individuals, when that person leaves, the knowledge is severed. It is unrealistic for one person to cover all three areas—technology, consulting, and implementation—so the countermeasure is to accept a multi-person "tiny team" and distribute knowledge.

Failure 3: SES-style command and control that kills the FDE's problem-discovery ability

If the client issues detailed work instructions, the FDE becomes a "premium SES waiting for instructions." Since the source of an FDE's value lies in "posing questions and discovering problems independently," it is essential to design a relationship where the client hands over issues to be solved rather than tasks, leaving the approach to the FDE.

Reviewing the projects DeFactory has supported by team structure, of the 9 engagements supported by an individual FDE (one person assigned), 2 failed to reach the outcomes the client expected by the deadline.

On the other hand, among the 2 engagements with a tiny team (two or more people) structure, no such shortfalls have occurred to date.

The sample size is still too small to call it a rigorous statistical trend, but analyzing the causes of the 2 shortfalls, what they had in common was "demanding that a single person perform both consulting (problem discovery and verbalization) and engineering (implementation) at a high level."

  • Strong at problem discovery and client engagement, but implementation speed fell short of the required level
  • Strong implementation skills, but misalignment with client expectations arose in problem redefinition and prioritization

The "problem discovery ability" and "technical implementation ability" required for FDEs are multiplicative skills, as described in Chapter 6. It is rare for a single individual to meet both at a high level, which is why DeFactory recommends building a multi-person "tiny team" structure rather than assigning individuals.

As awareness of FDEs spreads, services calling themselves "FDE" while actually operating as SES have emerged. Checking the following before placing an order helps you discern the actual nature of the service.

4 points to check regarding contracts and structure

  • Are performance indicators defined?: Does the proposal include KPIs such as "time saved on operations" or "adoption rate"? If the discussion only covers man-hours (person-months), it is an SES-type service
  • Can you enter from the problem definition phase?: Does the provider propose "defining the requirements together" rather than "acting only after requirements are handed over"?
  • Do they have product and technical assets?: Be cautious if only scratch development from zero is proposed
  • Do they have an organizational structure?: Rather than depending on a single superman, does the organization possess "engineering × consulting" know-how as an institution?

2 points to check regarding fit with your company's needs

  • Does the contract scope include adoption support and business transformation?: Is on-site adoption and training included in the scope, rather than ending with implementation?
  • Is engagement assumed on a yearly basis?: Proposals premised only on 3-month renewals are SES-type designs that do not account for the accumulation of domain knowledge

The appropriate contract period for FDEs is on the order of one year. Accumulating domain knowledge, the source of an FDE's value, requires a ramp-up period of 3–4 months, and short contracts of 3–6 months do not allow onboarding costs to be recovered.

There are three key points in contract design.

  • Contract type: Base it on a deliverable-completion quasi-delegation contract (to accommodate the dynamic requirement changes unique to AI projects)
  • Performance indicators (KPIs): Measure by business outcomes such as "time saved on operations," "tool adoption rate," and "lead time from PoC to production," rather than working hours
  • Handling of intellectual property: Clarify in advance the ownership and scope of reuse of generic components built by the FDE
  • Tip 1: Select people with domain experience at the staffing stage: Choosing personnel with experience close to the target industry from the start can greatly shorten the ramp-up period
  • Tip 2: Accept a "team" rather than an individual: Rather than one person covering all three areas of technology, consulting, and implementation, a structure where a multi-person "tiny team" divides the work is an option that avoids dependence on individuals

In hiring and selecting FDE talent, a useful criterion is whether the candidate can describe "a past experience of redefining the client's requirements themselves," together with concrete numbers, decisions, and trade-offs.

  • In past projects, have you ever questioned requirements given by a client, wondering "Is this really the problem we want to solve?" What did you do at that time?
  • In past projects, can you explain how your deliverables affected the client's numbers (revenue, costs, work hours, etc.)?

A. An FDE (Forward Deployed Engineer) is a role that, while belonging to an AI or SaaS vendor, embeds into customer companies' operations and handles everything end-to-end, from problem discovery and data infrastructure building to AI implementation and business adoption.

This is a model established by US-based Palantir, and as of April 2026, related job postings have surged 729% year-over-year (643 → 5,330 postings).

A. The biggest difference is the value delivered.

While SES operates under a quasi-delegation contract providing labor (man-hours), FDEs commit to outcomes in solving business problems.

Monthly fees also differ: SES is ¥600,000–1.2 million per person, while FDE is ¥1.5–2.5 million per person, but they should be evaluated based on cost-effectiveness, including eliminating stalled PoCs and ensuring business adoption.

A. Depending on the procurement method, the guideline is ¥1.3–3 million per month.

Freelance FDE contracting runs ¥1.3–2 million per month, team-based FDE support services (lab-type) run ¥1.5–3 million per month, and full-time hiring costs ¥10–25 million annually plus recruitment costs.

Contracted work of 3–4 days per week is the option with the lowest barrier to adoption.

A. FDEs are best suited to resolving implementation gaps such as "the PoC succeeded but production deployment isn't progressing" or "we introduced AI tools but the field teams don't use them."

According to a BCG survey, 74% of companies struggle to scale the value of AI adoption, and this group is the primary target for FDE utilization.

Conversely, for simply filling headcount with development resources, SES is more suitable.

Source: Boston Consulting Group, "AI Adoption in 2024: 74% of Companies Struggle to Achieve and Scale Value"

A. It does not work.

Three conditions are prerequisites: ① having a reusable platform foundation, ② being enterprise-focused with high customer unit prices, and ③ having a culture of collaboration with field teams.

Imitating only the embedded style without a platform foundation leads to an unsustainable quagmire of custom development.

A. Annual long-term contracts are recommended.

FDEs only demonstrate value after accumulating business knowledge in the target domain (finance, manufacturing, etc.), so short-term contracts of 3–6 months cannot recover onboarding costs.

In most cases, results only start becoming clear from the 3rd–4th month after engagement begins.

A. The most common failure is "managing FDEs as if they were SES."

If you issue detailed work instructions, FDEs' core value—their problem-discovery and proposal capabilities—cannot be demonstrated.

The next most common issue is dependence on a single FDE, where knowledge is lost the moment they leave. Accepting them as a multi-person tiny team is an effective countermeasure.

The FDE (Forward Deployed Engineer) is a new role for the AI era that combines consulting and engineering skills to resolve structural problems such as "stalled PoCs" and "lack of field adoption." Here is a summary of the key points from this article.

  • FDE is a role originating from Palantir that handles everything end-to-end "on the customer's front lines," from problem discovery to implementation, adoption, and product feedback
  • In 2026, global giants such as OpenAI, AWS, Anthropic, and Salesforce are making major investments in FDE organizations all at once, making it an emerging industry-standard model
  • Annual salaries in Japan range from ¥10–25 million, a level in a different league from SES. The pay gap reflects the gap in delivered value
  • The core of the FDE model is the declining marginal cost achieved by "feeding insights back into the product"; imitation without a foundation leads to a quagmire
  • Expect corporate implementation costs of 1.3 to 3 million yen per month. Contracts should be one-year quasi-delegation agreements with performance metrics, and acceptance by a tiny team prevents failure
