How to Implement Generative AI: 5 Steps to Avoid Failure and the Role of FDEs

Summary

This Japanese-language article from FDE Jobs argues that the hard part of adopting generative AI is not building it but embedding it into daily operations until it delivers business results—why many initiatives stall at the PoC stage. It lays out five sequential steps: pick target work starting from business problems, design PoCs with production in mind, integrate data and existing systems, maintain accuracy through evals and monitoring loops, and drive adoption then expansion to adjacent workflows. Common failure patterns include tool-first thinking, PoCs disconnected from production, and deferred data integration. The article positions FDEs and AI implementation engineers as the role that can drive all five steps end to end, and suggests hiring or internal enablement where such talent is missing.

Key points

  • Adoption fails most often at operationalization, not at building the technology.
  • Five steps: problem-first scoping, production-ready PoC, data/system integration, operational accuracy, adoption and expansion.
  • PoCs should be judged by production readiness, not demo success.
  • Data integration is the biggest workload and must not be postponed.
  • FDEs and AI implementation engineers can own the process end to end.

The Hardest Part of Generative AI Adoption Is "Making It Stick," Not "Building It"

Technically, it is no longer difficult to get generative AI and LLMs running in enterprises. The challenge is embedding them into field operations, keeping them in use, and reaching a state where they deliver business results. This is why many implementations end up stuck at the PoC stage. Here, we organize the implementation steps needed to avoid failure.

The 5 Steps to Avoid Failure

It is important not to skip any of the steps in order.

STEP1. Select Target Operations Based on Issues

Start not with "what AI can do" but with "which business issues to solve." Choosing operations with high impact that are easy to validate as the first target makes it easier to create early success stories.

STEP2. Design the PoC with Production in Mind

From the PoC stage, plan for data integration, permissions, operations, and accuracy evaluation. Use "can it go to production" rather than "the demo works" as the validation criterion.

STEP3. Integrate with Data and Existing Systems

Connect with internal data and existing tools, and embed the AI into field workflows. This is the biggest workload; underestimating it will derail production deployment.

STEP4. Ensure Accuracy Through Operations

Set up a loop of evaluation (Eval), monitoring, and improvement. Since LLMs fluctuate, manage reproducibility through operations rather than one-time accuracy.

STEP5. Drive Adoption and Expansion

Expand usage through training, onboarding, and sharing success stories. Once results appear in one operation, expand to adjacent operations.

Common Stumbling Blocks

The five typical failures are: (1) tool-first with vague issues, (2) building the PoC detached from production, (3) postponing data integration, (4) no operational design for accuracy, and (5) no measures for adoption. All are two sides of the same coin as the reasons PoCs fail in production; the common factor is the lack of "design that can see things through in the field" rather than technology.

Who Drives It: The Role of FDEs / AI Implementation Engineers

The ones who can drive these 5 steps end to end are FDEs (Forward Deployed Engineers) and AI implementation engineers. They handle everything from requirements definition to implementation, integration, evaluation, and adoption in one continuous flow, bridging builders and the field. If you lack such talent in-house, a realistic approach is to establish a model through hiring or with the support of experienced professionals while building internal capability in parallel.
