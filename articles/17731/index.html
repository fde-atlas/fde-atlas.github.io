How to Prepare for the FDE Interview: Problem Decomposition Round and Mock Customer

Original title: FDE 面试怎么准备：问题拆解轮与模拟客户

FDE Times · 2026-10-08 · 5 min read

Summary

Key takeaways at a glance

  • According to an interview guide, Cognition has eliminated coding and system design interviews, replacing them with live customer simulations. Distyl AI still tests code, while Databricks rarely asks classic algorithm questions.
  • The problem decomposition round, pioneered by Palantir, features deliberately vague prompts, and scoring is based on the questions candidates ask, how they break down the problem, and their ability to define scope.
  • The most common mistake is jumping to a solution too early. Engineers transitioning to FDE roles should systematically practice requirements clarification, just as they once drilled LeetCode.

According to an interview guide from Exponent/aced.io, when Cognition hires Forward Deployed Engineers (FDEs), it has dropped the two interview rounds that engineers aiming at big tech companies often spend a year preparing for: coding and system design. The final rounds have been replaced with live customer simulations, such as pitching a solution to executives or analyzing a case over a call.

Many will read this as "FDEs don't need strong coding skills." That interpretation is wrong. Distyl AI and Databricks still have coding rounds; they've simply changed in nature and are no longer the primary screening gate.

The round that carries the most weight is one where the interviewer plays the customer and hands you a deliberately incomplete requirements brief. If you've already invested hundreds of hours on LeetCode to transition into an FDE role, those hours will buy you fewer points than you'd expect.

The good news is that the most heavily scored skill can be trained—it's just that few people deliberately practice it.

Cognition is the exception, not the norm

Distyl AI still keeps a coding round. An article on techinterview.org analyzing the company's FDE hiring process advises candidates to prepare Python and notes that the questions will be very close to day-to-day work. This round tests whether you can handle the job of an engineer embedded at a customer site, not whether you know algorithms.

Databricks is moving in the same direction. According to the Exponent/aced.io guide, the graph, algorithm, and concurrency questions common in Databricks software engineer interviews rarely appear in the FDE interview process. Instead, there is a separate problem decomposition interview.

A Substack article from AI Engineering Insider summarizes this trend as "less LeetCode, more real-world engineering," with coding questions like parsing a messy CSV file or writing a rate limiter.

Educative's FDE course lists the customer simulation as one of four interview types that consistently appear in FDE hiring. The whole process typically has 4 to 6 rounds and takes 3 to 5 weeks.

So Cognition dropping the coding interview is just the most extreme case within a larger trend. Code is still tested, to confirm you clear the bar; but whether you get an offer depends on a different round.

The real screening gate often contains not a single line of code

That Substack article directly calls the problem decomposition round the primary screening gate. Candidates receive a vague business problem with no clear requirements brief and have 45 to 60 minutes to work through it, with no single correct answer.

According to techinterview.org, this round usually involves no code: a hypothetical customer throws a vague problem at you, then watches how you respond.

This format isn't new. According to an aced.io guide on problem decomposition questions, it was pioneered and popularized by Palantir, and today many companies hiring FDEs have their own versions.

What makes this round hard is that the requirements brief is deliberately incomplete by design. The aced.io guide states explicitly that identifying missing scope, constraints, data, and success criteria is itself part of the scoring.

At Distyl, the interviewer playing the customer won't volunteer additional details, so candidates' scores depend on the questions they ask.

With no code, what does the interviewer evaluate?

According to techinterview.org, interviewers assess four abilities: structured problem decomposition, empathy for the end user, data-driven reasoning, and judgment about scope. None of these show up in a function that runs correctly; they all show up in which questions you ask and in what order.

The same source notes that the most common mistake is proposing a solution right after reading the requirements, without first clarifying who the user is and what "done" means. For excellent engineers, this is a natural reflex. Years of being rewarded for solving problems quickly train you to assume the problem itself is correct and complete.

Imagine a hypothetical scenario. The "customer" is a logistics company that says it wants to use AI to reduce late deliveries. An inexperienced candidate will dive straight into delivery-time prediction models, data pipelines, and deployment plans. The answer sounds technically impressive, but it may be solving the wrong problem.

A strong candidate asks questions first. When an order is late, who is affected—the end customer, the drivers, or the customer service team? What counts as late? Where is trip data stored, and how good is its quality? Three months later, which number would prove the project succeeded?

The right answer might not need AI at all—just an alert sent to dispatchers.

Databricks' guidance for its problem decomposition round gives a concrete number: spend 5 to 15 minutes clarifying business goals before moving into technical design. The most common mistake described by techinterview.org is precisely skipping this questioning phase.

Three companies, three weightings

Putting the three companies' processes side by side shows that they haven't abandoned technical ability; they've shifted the weight toward judgment.

All three companies put candidates in front of a customer who hasn't fully figured out their own requirements; the only difference is how much code is tested alongside.

This design makes sense. An FDE who codes slightly slower than their peers can still deliver a project; an FDE who builds an elegant system but solves the wrong problem will fail the project—and fail it in front of the customer. Interviews are increasingly focused on the second type of risk.

What should career-switchers practice?

For many developers—especially those doing outsourcing work (and many in Vietnam do)—requirements arrive via business analysts or project managers in the form of written tickets. You may be excellent at turning requirements into code, yet rarely need to ask the customer whether the requirements are right. The problem decomposition round tests exactly the skill you use the least.

This skill can be trained, the same way you once drilled LeetCode: with problems, a timer, and someone scoring you. Ask a colleague to play the customer, answering only when asked, then grade yourself against the four criteria above. After five or six practice runs, you'll see just how strong your reflex to jump to solutions too early really is.

Don't abandon coding entirely: Distyl still has a Python coding round, and descriptions of the FDE interview suggest that coding questions now lean toward practical engineering rather than LeetCode. What needs to change is how you practice: fewer algorithm puzzles, more tasks like parsing messy data, writing a rate limiter, or calling an API with retries—and write them to the standard of code that would run at a customer site.

When reading job descriptions, watch for terms like "decomposition," "customer simulation," or "case study"—they signal which round carries the most weight. On your resume, instead of writing "developed an X module in Python," describe an experience where the initial requirements were vague: what questions you asked, how the scope was agreed upon, and what the outcome was.

When an FDE recruiter reads a line like that, they see exactly the abilities their interviews are designed to find.

The coding round hasn't disappeared, but it now only tells companies whether you've cleared the bar to move forward. To get an offer, you need to excel in a conversation with a hypothetical customer who doesn't yet know what they need.
