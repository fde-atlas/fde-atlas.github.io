What's the difference between an FDE and an engineer? Comparing scope of responsibility, technical skill level, and career paths

Original title: FDEとエンジニアの違いは？責任範囲・技術水準・移り方を比較

FDE総研 · 2026-07-23 · 9 min read

Summary

This article from FDE Soken (run by VACAN Technologies) compares FDEs with regular software engineers using Palantir's official blog and The Pragmatic Engineer as primary sources. It argues the difference is not technical skill level but the direction of value: SWEs deliver one capability to many customers, while FDEs deliver many capabilities to one customer. FDEs start at problem discovery with the client and are judged by whether their work is actually used on site, not whether code ships. Citing Anthropic's job posting, the article stresses that FDEs still need strong Python implementation skills, with customer discovery layered on top. For SWEs considering the switch, it recommends building experience in defining requirements with customers and owning outcomes, rather than starting over technically.

Key points

  • FDE vs SWE is about value direction, not skill level.
  • FDEs start at problem discovery and end at on-site adoption.
  • Anthropic requires strong Python plus LLM production experience.
  • FDEs resemble startup CTOs in decision-making scope.
  • SWEs can transition by extending, not discarding, their skills.

What's the difference between an FDE and a regular engineer?

The difference between an FDE and a regular SWE is not the level of technical skill, but the direction of value that Palantir's official blog describes as "one customer or many customers."

FDEs (Forward Deployed Engineers) and regular software engineers (SWEs) overlap in the technologies and languages they work with. The FDE Research Institute (operated by VACAN Technologies) compares the two using four common yardsticks: "direction of value," "scope of responsibility," "technical standards," and "career paths." Comparing them by "which has higher technical skill" tends to lead to misunderstanding the FDE role.

How does the way FDEs and SWEs deliver value differ?

SWEs spread a single capability to many customers. FDEs turn the multiple problems a single customer faces into capabilities, one after another.

According to Palantir's primary definition, a regular Dev (SWE) is described as "one capability, many customers," while an FDE (internally called Delta) is "one customer, many capabilities" (Palantir's official blog).

PerspectiveFDERegular SWE
Decision yardstickCan this customer's problem actually be solved?Is this capability generic for many users?
What is prioritizedProducing results in the field at handNot increasing the complexity of the product as a whole
Common perceptionEasily seen from the product side as "lacking generality"Easily seen from the field side as "unusable"

Even the same phrase "good implementation" means different things to an FDE and an SWE. It's not a matter of which is correct—only that the direction they take differs. Sharing this premise makes it easier to understand the friction that arises when FDEs and SWEs work in the same organization.

Where did the FDE role originate?

The FDE role was born at Palantir in the early 2010s, and it has been reported that until around 2016 there were more FDEs than regular SWEs.

This history is reported by The Pragmatic Engineer. Palantir's customers were government agencies and large enterprises, centered on sites where requirements were not yet settled.

Period/EventDetailsWhat can be learned
Early 2010sFDE emerges at PalantirA role created to handle sites where requirements are unsettled
Until around 2016Number of FDEs exceeds regular SWEsSolving problems in the field was the main battleground of the business
Recent yearsAppears in job postings at other companies such as AnthropicThe role is spreading beyond Palantir

What this history shows is that an FDE is not "an engineer who also handles customers," but "a role for producing results in the field where requirements are unsettled." If requirements are clear from the start, there's no need to station engineers at the customer's site.

Where does the scope of responsibility differ?

Measured against the definition in Palantir's official blog, an FDE's responsibility extends to the customer's outcomes, one step broader than an SWE's, which ends with implementing the specifications.

The starting point of the work, the deliverables, and even the definition of failure all differ from an SWE's.

Comparing FDE and SWE responsibilities in a table?

Comparing starting points, deliverables, definitions of failure, and milestones, an FDE starts earlier and ends later than an SWE.

The biggest differences are in the "starting point" and the "definition of failure." An SWE starts work from a state where specifications have been given. An FDE starts by exploring "what should be built in the first place" together with the customer, and considers it a failure if the product works according to spec but isn't used in the field.

Why are FDEs said to be "like a startup CTO"?

An FDE handles everything from problem definition to implementation and adoption with a small team, and makes decisions independently, which is why the role is said to resemble a CTO.

At Palantir, FDEs are positioned as handling difficult projects end-to-end in small teams, a role close to that of a startup CTO (The Pragmatic Engineer). What "CTO-like" refers to is not the seniority of the title, but the scope of decision-making.

  • Deciding what not to build
  • Rearranging priorities
  • Judging how much technical compromise is acceptable

Since no one guarantees whether the specifications are valid, the basis for decisions becomes the facts you have verified yourself in the field. Breadth of responsibility and breadth of authority come as a set.

What technical level is required of an FDE?

Looking at Anthropic's official job posting, a high level of Python implementation skill is a prerequisite for FDEs, with customer-facing skills layered on top—so the technical bar does not drop.

"Even if you're not good at technology, you can be an FDE if you're good with customers" is a common misconception.

What does Anthropic's FDE job posting require?

Anthropic's FDE job posting builds on strong Python implementation skills as a foundation, layering on production LLM experience and high agency as requirements.

The requirements listed in Anthropic's official job posting are the following four.

  • Strong implementation skills in Python
  • Production LLM experience (advanced prompt design and agent development)
  • High agency to move forward amid ambiguity
  • Customer discovery skills

The differences from an ordinary engineer are items 2 through 4. Item 1, implementation skill, is the same foundation as a regular SWE, and even with the customer discovery item, the implementation skill requirement does not go away.

Is FDE a job that involves writing code?

FDE is a job that involves writing code. You design, implement, and test workflows yourself and build production applications on the customer's systems.

Customer engagement and domain understanding are means for correctly deciding "what to build." If the person who "listens" and the person who "builds" are separate, some of the insights gained in the field get lost along the way when compiling the requirements document. An FDE handles both alone, adjusting the implementation on the spot based on the customer's reactions.

Compared to SIers, consultants, and PdMs, who is not a good fit for FDE?

When comparing SIers, consultants, and PdMs using the FDE Research Institute's axis of "starting point and end of deliverables," FDE differs in that one person handles everything from problem discovery through implementation and adoption.

The differences from similar roles become easier to distinguish when viewed not by job title but by "where the work starts and where it ends."

Who is not a good fit for FDE?

People who want to focus on implementation after specifications are finalized, or who want to polish a single feature for many users, are better suited to a regular SWE role.

Since FDE is a "one customer, many capabilities" role, work focused on pursuing highly general-purpose design is not central. FDE is also a poor fit for people who find it burdensome to make decisions while specifications remain ambiguous, or who want to avoid being measured by field usage of their work. This is not about superiority or inferiority, but a difference in orientation.

Do the answers change based on years of experience, current role, English ability, or company size?

The requirements in Anthropic's official job posting center on skill items, and the FDE Research Institute distinguishes prerequisites not by years of experience but by whether you have implementation skills and experience making decisions close to the customer.

Since experience requirements differ by posting, always verify them in the original job posting before applying.

How does preparation differ by years of experience and current role?

For those with limited implementation experience, the shortcut is to prioritize gaining production implementation experience in Python; for those from SIers or outsourced development, prioritize gaining experience building specifications alongside customers.

How do English ability and company size matter?

The primary sources from Palantir and Anthropic referenced in this article are published in English, so reading English job postings is a prerequisite for targeting FDE roles at overseas companies.

Regarding company size, the history of how FDEs emerged at Palantir from "fields where requirements never solidify" at government agencies and large enterprises (The Pragmatic Engineer) is useful reference material. Rather than company size, check whether the assigned customer's requirements are not yet settled. How to read job postings is summarized in How to find FDE jobs and read job postings.

How to move from SWE to FDE?

Anthropic's official job posting is also built on Python implementation skills, so by adding experience building specifications with customers to your SWE experience, you can move into FDE.

There is no need to start over from zero. Thinking separately about what carries over and what is missing makes preparation easier.

How does SWE experience carry over to FDE?

FDE is a role built on SWE implementation skills. Product development experience is not wasted; it carries over directly as a prerequisite.

The full cycle of designing, implementing, and testing features and shipping them to production is something you have already experienced as an SWE. Moving to FDE is not a "switch" that abandons your technical skills, but closer to an "expansion" that broadens your scope on top of your current skills.

What experience is missing in the transition?

What is missing is not technical skill, but the "breadth" of experience: talking with customers before requirements are settled and taking responsibility through to results.

Practice scenarioConcrete actionsConnection to FDE work
Before buildingAttend requirements definition meetings as an implementerDiscovering and redefining problems
Before buildingSit in on interviews with customers and usersCustomer discovery
After buildingPersonally verify whether it is being used in the field after releaseTaking responsibility through to field adoption

All of these are experiences you can accumulate in your current workplace. An experience where a working implementation went unused connects directly to FDE's "definition of failure," making it material you can talk about directly in interviews.
