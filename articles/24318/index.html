AI Pilot Conversion: How FDEs Set Pilot Success Criteria

Original title: AI 试点转化：FDE 如何设定试点成功标准

FDE Times · 2026-11-02 · 7 min read

Summary

This guide argues that whether an AI pilot converts into a contract depends less on charging fees than on settling three things before day one: measurable success criteria, a named buyer with budget authority, and a pre-agreed conversion path with pricing. Citing Jason Lemkin, IDC data (only 4 of 33 AI POCs reach production), and Millennium's Brian Lewis (~25% of pilots convert), the author walks through an invoice-reconciliation example showing how to set baselines, pass thresholds, and fallback rules on a one-page document, so the final meeting is a decision rather than a debate.

Key points

  • Paid pilots convert at 60-90%+, but fees alone don't guarantee a contract.
  • Success criteria must be measurable, time-boxed, and tied to business outcomes.
  • A named budget-holding buyer must attend kickoff and final meetings.
  • One-page terms: baseline, pass threshold, error limit, one extension only.
  • If a pilot must be free, trade it for logs, buyer attendance, and agreed pricing.

Charging alone isn't enough: without readable numbers and a person who can sign on the last day, the pilot still stalls.

Key takeaways

  • Charging creates accountability on both sides, but charging alone doesn't guarantee a pilot converts into a contract.
  • Measurable criteria, a named buyer, and a priced conversion path must all be agreed before day one.
  • Pilots that can't charge a fee need an agreed contract price even more.

Picture the final week of a pilot. Your invoice reconciliation agent is running well, the finance director praises it at the demo, but when you ask about next steps, the answer is: "Let's see if there's budget next quarter."

No one says no, and no one has the authority to say yes. Weeks of work on real data hang in limbo, and you have no grounds to push forward.

It's easy to treat this as a sales problem. Pricing and contracts do belong to sales, but only someone close to the customer's data can write baselines, pass thresholds, and measurement methods accurately. So a capability worth building is putting these terms in writing on one page before the pilot starts.

Does charging a fee solve the problem?

Jason Lemkin of SaaStr believes well-run paid pilots convert to annual contracts at over 70%, with a range of roughly 60% to over 90%. On free pilots he's blunt: "unpaid pilots are a waste of time."

The exception he accepts is free trials that are extremely easy to deploy, and an agent that needs access to customer data and permissions is rarely that easy.

For AI specifically, the numbers are much lower. CIO, citing IDC research, reports that only 4 out of 33 AI proofs of concept (POCs) make it to production. IDC's Nadkarni has observed that many generative AI POCs are "severely underfunded, or not funded at all."

Brian Lewis, an investor at Millennium, estimated at AI Engineer World's Fair that roughly a quarter of pilots end in contracts, and pilot cycles are getting shorter.

Don't put these numbers side by side and conclude that "charging triples the close rate." They measure different things, and customers willing to pay are more serious from the start.

According to Stackmatix's playbook, what a fee brings is accountability on both sides: within the customer's organization, someone must own the outcome.

So a more useful question than "paid or free" is: if the pilot fails, who on the customer's side loses something?

Three things to have ready before day one

First, criteria. Stackmatix requires criteria to be measurable, time-bound, and tied to business outcomes the customer cares about. SoftwareSeni explains why: if criteria are set after results come in, the decision to continue or terminate loses its objective basis.

Second, a named buyer who controls budget and is involved from the start. Without this person, no one can say yes when the pilot ends, just like the opening scene. A team lead praising the agent is a good sign, but praise isn't a signature.

Finally, the conversion path. When a pilot can't charge a fee, Stackmatix recommends designing the conversion route in advance and agreeing on the contract price.

Lemkin notes that many established vendors have made paid pilots default to renewal (opt-out), meaning the contract automatically continues unless the customer stops it, with lower churn than opt-in models that require the customer to actively confirm. Younger companies may not have that leverage, but it's worth knowing so you can raise it when the opportunity arises.

Don't worry that discussing price early seems presumptuous. Redress Compliance, which advises Palantir buyers on negotiations, also requires customers to obtain production pricing before starting new pilots.

Since buyers are already advised to ask about price early, putting your numbers on paper is simply answering a question that will come sooner or later.

A one-pager for an invoice reconciliation pilot

Take a hypothetical example: a logistics company wants to use an agent to reconcile invoices. 400 invoices a day, 6 minutes each, totaling 2,400 minutes, or 40 work hours. You want the agent to handle 60% autonomously, meaning 240 invoices, each requiring just 1 minute of human review.

The math gives you the target: 240 minutes of review is 4 hours, and the remaining 160 handled manually take 16 hours, for a total of 20 work hours per day. The customer isn't buying "an agent that processes 60% of invoices"; they're buying a halving of work hours, so that's the number that should be on paper.

ItemWhat to determine before the pilot
Baseline40 work hours per day, measured from logs from the 2 weeks before the pilot
Pass criteriaNo more than 20 work hours per day, measured over the final 2 weeks
Safety thresholdNo more than 1% missed errors in the sampled audit
Duration4 weeks
Decision-makerCFO, attending the kickoff and closing meetings
If passedSign an annual contract at the price listed on this page
If close to passingAbove 20 hours but no more than 24 hours: a single 2-week extension, with the errors to fix listed
If failedOver 24 hours or beyond the error threshold: terminate, return the data, and document lessons learned

Suppose that at the end of the pilot, the agent can only handle 55% autonomously. That means 220 invoices reviewed in 3 hours 40 minutes, and 180 handled manually in 18 hours, totaling 21 hours 40 minutes.

Without this page, the meeting becomes an argument over whether 55% is good enough. With it, there's nothing to argue: this counts as close to passing, so a 2-week extension with the listed errors to fix. Even if the numbers look bad, both sides know what happens next.

The same math also reveals the remaining boundary: at 48%, 192 reviewed and 208 handled manually, totaling exactly 24 hours; at 45%, the total is 25 hours and the pilot terminates. So you know from the start how much the agent must handle autonomously at minimum to keep the extension option alive.

What to ask in the scoping meeting

These questions sound ordinary, but each one fills in a cell on this page. Here's a sample dialogue between a Forward Deployed Engineer (FDE) and a customer:

FDE: How long does it take to process one invoice today? Where does that number come from? Customer: Just a few minutes. FDE: Could you give us two weeks of logs from your finance system? That way both sides use the same measured number as the baseline. Customer: Sure, I'll have finance export it. FDE: Assuming work hours are cut in half, who decides on the purchase? Where does the budget sit? Customer: The CFO, but she's very busy. FDE: She only needs to attend the first and last meetings. If she approves the criteria at the start, someone will be there to make the decision when the pilot ends.

If the customer won't provide logs or arrange a meeting with the buyer, you've gained valuable information before the pilot even starts. Tell sales, and reconsider the scope before investing in integration work.

Hands-on: from measurement to signature

Start with the baseline; without one, any criterion is just a feeling. Then sit down with frontline users to set thresholds, including safety thresholds, and convert everything into units the buyer cares about: work hours, processing time, missed errors.

Next, hand the draft to sales to fill in pricing and contract structure, then get the buyer's confirmation before kickoff.

Finally, keep the pilot short. Brian Lewis has observed that with short pilot cycles, promises about the future have a harder time making up for missing features. This forces you to pick one narrow process and make it truly work, rather than presenting a roadmap.

Mistakes that make the pilot look good but never get signed

The first mistake to avoid is using technical metrics as the standard. Accuracy or F1 helps with debugging, but the buyer needs to see business results, so keep those as internal metrics.

The second is treating your champion as the buyer. An enthusiastic user can help you get data, but not necessarily budget. The third is allowing endless extensions: a free pilot that drags on forever is the politest way of saying no, so make it clear that only one extension is allowed.

The last is leaving pricing undecided on the grounds of "let's talk once we have results." Writing from the buyer's perspective, Redress notes that a pilot without terms gives the vendor the advantage on price. Customers who think this way will be on guard everywhere, and wary customers sign slowly, so being transparent early works in your favor.

If the pilot must be free, don't give it away for nothing. Trade it for logs, users' time, the buyer's attendance, and an agreed price, because these are what make the customer truly invested.

How to write this skill into your resume

A statement like "designed acceptance criteria based on log-measured baselines and worked with sales to define the conversion path" is more persuasive than "deployed agents for customers," provided you've actually done it.

When reading job postings, watch for the terms scoping, success criteria, and pilot conversion, then ask directly in the interview: does the FDE get involved from the point of writing the criteria, or only take over after the deal is signed? Keywords are just clues; the answers tell you what the job really is.

In interviews, tell the story of a pilot using calculations like the ones above, even if it failed. How you made a decision in the face of a bad number is the story most worth telling.

This week's exercise

Take your most recent pilot, success or failure, and fill in the sheet above with real numbers. Any cell you can't fill in is where that pilot was left to chance.

The next time you hear "let's revisit next quarter," you'll know exactly which week the conversation went off track, and that time, you should have asked earlier.
