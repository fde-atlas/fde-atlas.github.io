After an FDE Delivery Project Goes Live: Monitoring, Iteration, and Handover

Original title: FDE 交付项目上线后：监控、迭代与交接

FDECareer.work · 2026-09-23 · 5 min read

Summary

Using an internal knowledge Q&A system as a running example, this article walks through what forward deployed engineers should do after a delivery goes live: layered monitoring that separates service health from answer quality, actionable incident runbooks, and handover verified by having the client team independently perform updates, troubleshooting, and rollbacks. It stresses judging pilots against business baselines rather than technical metrics, and notes all examples are synthetic engineering guidance, not real client delivery records.

Key points

  • Monitoring spans five layers: service health, answer quality, workflow impact, data maintenance, and cost, each with an assigned owner.
  • Incident runbooks must specify triggers, first steps, and escalation paths; privilege exposure means pausing affected pipelines first.
  • Handover is validated when the client team completes an update, diagnosis, and rollback without the FDE operating.
  • Accounts, alerts, and budgets should belong to long-term owners, not individual developers' temporary access.
  • If technical metrics pass but nobody uses the system, adjust scope or stop the pilot rather than adding features.

For example, after a knowledge Q&A system goes live, you need to monitor both service availability and answer quality. Record error rates, latency, cost, and user feedback, and use authorized samples to continuously evaluate citation correctness and unauthorized-access risk. Metrics should help the team locate problems in data, retrieval, models, or business processes.

Establish Actionable Incident Handling

Define who receives alerts, when to pause automated answers, how to roll back versions, and which issues go directly to humans. If document updates or permission revocations are not synced to the index, there must be a reprocessing and verification workflow. Retrospectives should document root causes, affected scope, and measures to prevent recurrence.

Hand the System Over to Long-Term Owners

The handover package should include architecture diagrams, data owners, access rules, eval sets and thresholds, runbooks, alert contacts, cost boundaries, and known limitations. Rehearse with the client team one document change, one error rollback, and one issue escalation to confirm they can operate independently.

Let Results Decide the Next Round

Compare against the business baseline set at project kickoff to check whether actual usage, manual handling time, and error costs have improved. If targets are not met, identify blockers first and adjust scope; do not claim business value has been delivered just because technical metrics look good. This process is an illustrative method supported by public materials, not a record of real client delivery.

Use a Single Request to Connect Business Feedback and Technical Diagnosis

Continue the internal knowledge Q&A example. When support staff find an answer unreliable, they should be able to trace through the request record which document versions were used, retrieval results, model configuration, and processing output. Business users do not need to see all technical details, but there should be a clear feedback channel so they know whether someone is handling the issue.

Separate operational monitoring from answer quality: a service can return normally while citing wrong or outdated documents; answers can be good quality yet unused because latency or manual verification costs are too high. Google Cloud's deployment and operations guidance supports engineering practices of continuous monitoring and improvement; business outcomes still need to be measured for this project.

Write the Incident Playbook as Steps That Can Be Followed

The incident playbook should specify trigger conditions, impact scope, first actions, and escalation targets. When an error occurs, contain the impact first, then diagnose the cause. For unauthorized access or sensitive data exposure, prioritize pausing the affected pipeline and related access; do not keep automated answers running just to keep a demo alive.

An example drill can start with a document whose permissions have been revoked: after revocation, ask the question again and verify that neither retrieval nor cache returns content; check affected requests, fix the sync process, rerun permission tests, and then let the owner decide on restoration. This drill is a synthetic scenario and does not represent a real incident.

  • Users cannot get reliable answers: show the failure reason and route back to manual retrieval or the support process.
  • Dependency keeps timing out: throttle requests and switch to a verified degraded mode.
  • Quality drops after a version change: stop scaling out, roll back per records, and rerun evals.
  • Permission or data issues: pause the affected scope, preserve necessary evidence, and escalate to data and technical leads.

Handover Must Verify That the Receiver Can Operate Independently

Completing handover documentation does not mean the client team has taken over. Have the receiver complete one document update, one error investigation, and one rollback while you observe without operating on their behalf. If the process depends on your personal account or verbal prompts, add those dependencies into permissions, tooling, or the playbook.

Accounts, alerts, and budgets should all belong to the long-term owner. Do not treat a developer's temporary access as an operations mechanism. OpenAI's FDE examples emphasize distilling field work patterns into tools and reusable materials; the handover package should ensure the next handler does not have to seek help from scratch.

Use the Business Baseline to Decide Whether to Continue, Adjust, or End

After the pilot runs for an agreed period, return to the problem definition from kickoff. Whether handling time has dropped must include manual review; actual usage must distinguish a one-time attempt from sustained adoption; cost must be considered alongside the volume of work resolved. Without a control group or sufficient samples, state the observation scope first and do not infer overall benefits.

If results fall short, first examine document coverage, usage entry points, workflow design, or manual takeover costs. When technical metrics are met but no one uses the system, the next round may require adjusting scope or ending the pilot, not simply adding more model features.

Failure Example: Launch Completed, but All Questions Still Go to the Original Developer

The example team receives a system and a slide deck, but does not know whom to contact when documents become stale, who receives alerts, or how to roll back versions. The project runs, but long-term maintenance responsibility has not been transferred.

To fix the handover, first list the operations that still depend on the original developer, assign a long-term owner for each, fill in permissions and steps, then rehearse again. Finally, record completed items, unresolved risks, support duration, and the date of the next retrospective. The basis for handover acceptance is evidence that the receiving team has completed these actions.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is this a real client project?

No. The three guides use the same internal knowledge Q&A example; the tables and steps are engineering recommendations; where there is no actual measurement data, it remains marked as to-be-measured.

Can acceptance thresholds be copied directly?

No. Samples, baselines, and targets should be agreed based on the project's workflows, data, and operating conditions.

What if launch conditions are not yet met?

Record the missing evidence and owners, keep the manual process in place, and complete the prerequisites before expanding the pilot.
