ML Engineer to FDE: What Changes and How to Make the Switch

FDE Times · 2026-10-14 · 6 min read

Summary

This article explains what changes when an ML engineer becomes a forward deployed engineer (FDE). The core shift is in measurement: an FDE is judged by whether a deployment changes how the client actually operates, not by model scores, and spends roughly 60% of each week with customers. Using an insurance claims classifier as an example, the author shows how a model with a strong F1 score still fails if emailed claims bypass it and predictions never reach the spreadsheet where work is assigned. The piece offers a five-step deployment method, a sample discovery script, common traps for ML people, and CV and interview advice, citing practices at Sierra and Palantir and sources including Perspective AI, Paraform, ZenML and The Pragmatic Engineer.

Key points

  • FDEs are graded on operational outcomes, not F1 scores.
  • About 60% of an FDE's week is spent directly with customers.
  • The hardest transition is moving from insight to integration.
  • Map the real workflow first; find where predictions must be written back.
  • On CVs, lead with the operational change, then integration, then the model.

An FDE is judged by whether a deployment changes how the client actually works, and spends about 60% of each week with customers.

In brief

  • An ML engineer makes the model good. An FDE makes it useful to a real organization.
  • For people coming from ML, the biggest hurdle is the move from insight to integration.
  • An FDE is judged by whether the client works differently, not by the model's score.

Take a hypothetical model that classifies insurance claims, and an engineer who has just lifted its F1 score from 0.81 to 0.93. Two weeks after the demo, the client’s claims team is still assigning work by hand on a spreadsheet. The model is not wrong. It simply has not reached the place where the work actually happens.

A blog post from Perspective AI comparing the two roles sums up the gap neatly: an ML engineer makes the model good, while an FDE makes the model useful to a real organization. The same source says an FDE is judged by whether a deployment actually changes how the client operates.

The skills an ML engineer brings still count, but the way results are measured has to change. The test-set score is no longer what you are graded on. The new yardstick is whether end users work differently.

A new yardstick means a new working day

At Sierra, according to a summary in ZenML’s LLMOps database, FDEs and agent engineers are accountable for “measurable business outcomes” rather than uptime or the number of features shipped. The same summary describes the agent engineer as owning the full life cycle of an agent in production and tracking it through outcome metrics.

Changing the yardstick changes the calendar too. Perspective AI estimates that an FDE spends about 60% of each week working directly with customers, while an ML engineer spends almost none.

Paraform describes the FDE as a hybrid role, sitting between software engineering and hands-on deployment for customers. The core skills, by Paraform’s account, are a backend language, cloud infrastructure, data engineering, applied AI and the ability to communicate with customers.

Applied AI is only one item on that list of five. An article from Scaler argues that the biggest hurdle for data people moving into FDE work is the step from “Insight” to “Integration”, and calls technical discovery with stakeholders the hardest part of the role.

Familiar questions for ML people

  • What does the model score on the test set?
  • Which extra feature would raise recall?
  • Is the training data clean enough?

Questions an FDE asks

  • Who will act on this prediction, and where?
  • Which system is the prediction written to so users can see it?
  • When the model is wrong, who notices and how is it fixed?

Where does a good model get stuck?

Go back to the claims example, this time as an FDE. The first job is not to open the notebook. It is to trace the path of a single claim, from the moment it reaches the company to the moment someone finishes handling it.

Say you discover that claims arrive from two places: email and a web portal. Both feed into a legacy claims-management system, and the team lead then exports the list to a spreadsheet to assign the work.

The dataset you trained on was a CSV file exported from that legacy system. You have never seen the email flow, and the team lead, the person who actually makes the decisions, works only in the spreadsheet.

Seen this way, the bottleneck is obvious. Emailed claims may never pass through the model, and the predictions do not appear where the team lead assigns work. This is precisely the kind of work ZenML mentions when describing how the FDE role at Palantir gradually expanded: integrating data from many scattered sources.

Another point of F1 does nothing here. What is needed is a pipeline that takes in both sources of claims and writes the predicted label into the very column the team lead reads.

Finding all this out means asking. Below is a short script for a 30-minute discovery session with the claims team lead.

FDE: Where do you start when you assign claims in the morning? Could you share your screen? Team lead: I export the list from the system to a spreadsheet, then assign people by claim type. FDE: Are claims sent by email in that list? Team lead: No, someone has to enter them by hand first. FDE: If the “claim type” column were filled in for you, which step could you drop? And when it’s filled in wrong, how would you find out?

The last exchange matters most. It turns a model metric into an operational one, such as the time from a claim’s arrival to its assignment. It also tells you that you need a feedback mechanism so users can report when the model gets it wrong.

Step by step at the client’s site

Step one is to map the process before touching the model. Sit next to real users, capture every screen they open and note every point where they work by hand. Step two is to identify the write-back point: the system and data field the decision-maker looks at every day.

Step three is to agree on one operational metric with the client and measure its baseline before deployment. Step four is to get the whole flow running end to end early, even if at first a simple set of rules stands in for the model.

Step five is to own the full life cycle: once the system is in production, track the operational metric, not just accuracy.

The familiar traps for ML people

The most common trap is to keep tuning the model while the integration still does not work. The sense of progress is real, but the client cannot see it. The second is to treat an exported data file as the whole truth, when part of the process lives outside the system, like the email flow in the example.

The third trap is using a notebook demo to prove value. Users judge a tool on the screen they open every morning, not on your charts. The last is avoiding client conversations because they feel “non-technical”, when discovery is where the real technical problem turns up.

Putting the skill on your CV

The Pragmatic Engineer reports strong demand for FDE hires at Google, OpenAI and Anthropic. Before editing your CV, open a few FDE job postings and count how often words such as integration, customer-facing and data pipeline appear compared with modeling terms. The count tells you which experience to put first.

Then rewrite each CV line in this order: the operational change, how you integrated, and only then the model. “Connected a classification model to the claims system and the assignment spreadsheet, cutting the time to assign a handler” says more than “raised F1 to 0.93”.

In interviews, describe a time you found that the real problem lay outside the model. ML skills remain a big advantage; what needs learning is to follow the problem all the way through, until the model’s predictions genuinely change how the client works.
