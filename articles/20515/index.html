How Forward Deployed Engineers Can Win Support from Customer IT Teams

Original title: 前线部署工程师如何赢得客户 IT 团队支持

FDE Times · 2026-10-30 · 6 min read

Summary

This guide explains how forward deployed engineers can win over wary client IT teams, who often see FDEs as threats taking over their work or leaving behind systems they cannot maintain. The author argues the solution is framing your role as helping the client run the system independently. Concrete tactics include an early meeting about the real system, involving security teams in week one, pair programming, decision logs, and gradual handoff. It also lists five common mistakes and advice for resumes and interviews.

Key points

  • IT teams fear replacement and inheriting systems they don't understand.
  • State upfront that the goal is client independence from the FDE.
  • Involve security early and pair program with client engineers.
  • Avoid bypassing IT, over-requesting access, or deferring security.
  • Resume and interview stories should show measurable handoff outcomes.

The solution sold in the contract rarely matches the real data, and the skeptics in IT know best where things will break. Make them co-owners early.

Key takeaways

  • IT teams are wary of FDEs not because you're capable, but because they fear being replaced, or being left with a system they don't understand.
  • Make it clear from the start: the goal is for them to run and extend the system independently, without depending on you.
  • Involve the security team in the first week, pair program with customer engineers, and hand over gradually throughout the project.

Imagine your first week on-site at the customer. You need read access to the orders database, but the ticket you filed three days ago has gone unanswered. In technical meetings, the head of IT sits with arms crossed, answering every question with "we need to check on that."

They aren't being rude. Put yourself in their shoes: leadership has brought in a stranger to do work that, by rights, should have been done by their own team.

Palantir compares the role of the Forward Deployed Software Engineer to a startup CTO: small teams, end-to-end ownership, and often embedded at the customer site. Given that mandate, you're doing exactly the work that belongs to the IT team.

Even if the customer likes you, they still see you as an outsider. First Round Review tells a story: the general counsel of a Fortune 100 company affectionately called the engineers sent by Ironclad "The Backpacks." The nickname sounds friendly, but the implication is clear: people who arrive with backpacks will also leave with them.

Why can't you succeed without them?

You could bypass the IT team and have your executive sponsor apply pressure. But this backfires quickly. A former Palantir Forward Deployed Engineer (FDE) recruiter told First Round Review that what you see on-site differs greatly from what was sold in the contract.

The head of forward deployed engineering at OpenAI said much the same to The Pragmatic Engineer: what customers describe during requirements gathering often doesn't match the real data and systems.

The people who know exactly where the gaps are—which table is full of dirty data, which job runs at 2 a.m. and locks the tables—are almost all in the IT team.

Squeeze them out, and you lose your most valuable source of information.

So the question isn't how to get around the IT team, but what they're afraid of, and how you should respond to those fears.

Reframe it: you're here to leave

IT teams usually fear two things: being replaced, and being left with a system they don't understand, one they'll have to carry when it breaks at midnight. The answer to both fears is the same.

A Simpliaxis guide on FDE project handoffs sets a clear goal: the customer team must be able to run and extend the system without contacting you.

The article also flips the common assumption: making yourself dispensable doesn't diminish your value—it proves it. A CIO.com article discussing FDEs in the age of agentic AI likewise recommends designing for ownership transfer from the start, so the customer team can operate and extend the system.

Say this in your first meeting and act accordingly, and you're no longer the person coming to take their job, but the person helping them take over a new system.

Example: an inventory agent and a four-person IT team

Suppose you're deploying an agent that answers inventory questions for a retailer. It needs to read data from an ERP system managed by a four-person IT team. There are three things to do, in order.

Step one: hold a 30-minute meeting before writing any code. Don't start by asking for access. First ask about the real state of the system: "Is the inventory table updated in batches or in real time? What caused the last time the numbers went wrong? Is there anything on the system you wouldn't want anyone to touch?" The last question matters most, because it hands them the right to draw the boundaries.

End the meeting with a commitment: "By the end of this project, your team should be able to modify and extend this agent on your own, without calling the vendor. The entire schedule will be designed around that goal."

Step two: involve the security team in the first week. The CIO.com article stresses that governance for agentic AI cannot be a compliance checklist ticked off at the end. ElevenLabs writes that for its FDE team, security, data governance, and regulatory compliance are must-haves from day one.

So bring a one-page data flow description: which three tables the agent has read-only access to, where logs are stored, and whether prompts contain customer data.

Let the security team edit it freely. Changes raised in the first week take a few hours; if dragged to the final week, they can stall the launch entirely.

Step three: design together, code together. The Simpliaxis guide offers three concrete practices: invite customer engineers into design decisions, pair program with them during development, and explain your reasoning as you go. ElevenLabs describes its FDEs working shoulder to shoulder with customer technical teams, inside the customer's own infrastructure.

Applied to the inventory example, that means keeping a decision log in a shared repository, plus two pairing sessions a week, each with a different member of the four-person team. A log entry can be as short as this:

D-03：库存查询结果缓存 15 分钟
原因：避免 Agent 每次回答都直接调用 ERP
否决方案：实时直接读取库存表
共同决定人：[客户工程师姓名]
如需修改：编辑缓存配置文件，IT 团队可自行完成

The "co-decided with" line is the most valuable. It turns your decisions into joint decisions, and tells future readers who to ask once you're gone.

The Simpliaxis guide explains why this works: engineers who develop alongside the customer team hand over continuously throughout the project, so the final handoff is small. By the last week, there's no need for a three-day "handover training."

What phrasing puts the IT team at ease?

Most defensiveness stems from how you open your mouth. You can still ask for everything you need—just say it differently:

Don't saySay instead
"Give me admin access to the ERP so I can move faster.""I only need read-only access to these three tables. Is there one you think should be excluded?"
"I'll handle this part, don't worry about it.""Let's pair on this part—your team will be maintaining it going forward."
"Demo for leadership on Friday.""Dry run with the IT team on Thursday, then present to leadership together on Friday."
"Security can come in a later phase.""Here's the data flow diagram—could the security team review it before development starts?"

Five mistakes that turn allies into obstacles

The most common mistake is going through the project sponsor as a shortcut whenever IT is slow to respond. You win one ticket and lose the whole team. The second mistake is requesting more access than you need, which confirms exactly what the security team fears most.

The third mistake is demoing to leadership without inviting the IT team, letting them hear about a system running on their own infrastructure from someone else.

The fourth mistake is writing code only you can understand: no decision log, no operations manual, everything in your head. The fifth is leaving security until the end—which is exactly what both CIO.com and ElevenLabs warn against.

How do you write this skill into your resume?

When reading FDE job descriptions, watch for phrases like "work side-by-side with technical teams," "handoff," or "enablement." When these appear, it means the company values more than just development—it values handing over to the customer.

In interviews, prepare a story about pulling a wary team into the work. Be specific: what you asked in the first meeting, when you brought in the security team, and who you paired with.

Write handover results on your resume: how many engineers were on the customer team, how long after you left they could run the system independently, and which features they added themselves.

These numbers must be your real data. They map exactly to the goals set by the FDE handover guide: the customer team can run and extend the system without depending on you.

The final measure is simple: after you sling your backpack and leave, the system is still running, and the person maintaining it is the stakeholder who sat with arms crossed at the first meeting.
