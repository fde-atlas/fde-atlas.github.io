Who Handles Requirements Definition? The Decision Maker Is the Ordering Party That Owns the Business

Original title: 要件定義は誰がやるか｜決めるのは業務を持つ発注側

株式会社Sai · 2026-10-30 · 22 min read

Summary

This article by Sai Co. argues that in system development, the decision about what to build belongs to the client side that owns the business, while writing the requirements document and verifying it can be shared with developers. Citing IPA's model contract (2nd edition), its requirements definition guide, and the 17 principles, the author notes the user-side responsible person's top duty is finalizing the requirements document. Companies that struggle with requirements definition usually lack an internally agreed business standard, not writing skill. Comparing contracted development, SES, consulting, and FDE-style accompaniment, the article concludes the decision-making responsibility stays with the client in every model; what differs is who gathers the material and whether decisions come before or during development. When requirements are unsettled, start from observing actual operations and data and decide while showing working software; fully specified large projects or SaaS-solvable problems fit the traditional approach.

Key points

  • Deciding what to build always stays with the client; writing and verifying can be shared.
  • IPA's model contract makes finalizing the requirements document the user-side leader's top responsibility.
  • Failed requirements definition usually reflects unsettled internal business standards, not poor writing.
  • Across contracting, SES, consulting, and FDE models, only who gathers material and when decisions happen differ.
  • For unsettled requirements, observe real operations and decide iteratively with working software.

Key Takeaways

  • In requirements definition, deciding what to build is the responsibility of the ordering party who owns the business, while the tasks of documenting requirements and verifying that the working product meets them are carried out together with the development side.
  • IPA's model contract (second edition) states that users, who know the needs of the business and operations best, must identify requirements under their own responsibility, and places finalizing the requirements definition document at the top of the authority and responsibilities of the user-side manager (accessed October 30, 2026).
  • When requirements definition cannot be done, the underlying issue is not a lack of writing skill but that the correct answer for the business has not been settled internally; IPA's requirements definition guide also notes that the departments using the system are not necessarily unified (accessed October 30, 2026).
  • Whether it is outsourcing, SES, consulting, or an accompaniment model, the responsibility for deciding requirements remains with the ordering party; what changes with the engagement style is who gathers the materials for deciding, and whether decisions are made before building or while building.
  • Before requirements are settled, start by looking at frontline operations and data, and decide while showing working products; for large-scale development with fully finalized requirements or challenges where off-the-shelf SaaS suffices, the traditional approach of solidifying requirements before ordering fits better.

Requirements definition is the process of deciding and articulating what the system to be built will do within the business, and it divides into the roles of deciding, writing, and verifying

Requirements definition is the process of deciding what the system to be built will do within the business and putting it into words that developers can understand. Its content divides into three roles—deciding, writing, and verifying—and the answer to who does what differs by role.

There are no marks in the development-side column because none of the roles can be completed by the development side alone.

Terminology follows IPA's requirements definition guide. The guide states that because "requirement" in JIS X 0166 is commonly translated as either 要求 or 要件, there is no standard definition distinguishing the two. It then defines 要件 (requirements) as what has been documented and specified from 要求 (demands) and agreed upon with stakeholders, and notes that since requirements are the input to the next phase, they must be in a form that can be communicated to third parties. Examples of recipients are from business departments to the IT department, and from the IT department to vendors (IPA Social Infrastructure Center, "Requirements Definition Guide for Users, 2nd Edition: 128 Keys to Successful Requirements Definition" / accessed October 30, 2026).

According to this definition, requirements definition includes three tasks: deciding the demands, documenting them, and verifying that documents or working products meet the requirements; the three rows of the table correspond to these.

IPA's model contract (second edition) also describes the content of each phase in terms of departmental roles. The requirements definition phase is explained as the phase in which, based on the systemization plan, the business department turns operational demands into business requirements, and the information systems department defines the functional and non-functional requirements to be implemented, obtaining executive approval through the execution approval process (IPA, "Information System Model Transactions and Contracts (Second Edition)" / accessed October 30, 2026). Functional requirements are what the system does, while non-functional requirements are conditions demanded outside of functions, such as speed and resistance to downtime. Even in companies without an information systems department, the task of deciding operational demands lies with the business department.

The role of deciding remains with the ordering party who owns the business, while the roles of writing and verifying can be shared with the development side

The role of deciding remains with the ordering party because only the party that owns the business knows which parts of the operations are correct and which may be changed. The roles of writing and verifying, when broken down into tasks such as researching, organizing, proposing, and cross-checking, have many parts that the development side can handle.

According to IPA's model contract (second edition), to build highly reliable and safe information systems, users—who know best the necessity of the business, business systems, and information systems—must accurately identify requirements under their own responsibility and reflect them in system design and development. It also states that in the super-upstream phase before entering development, under the division of roles within the user organization—management, business departments, and the information systems department—users bear the responsibility of clearly defining functional and non-functional requirements (IPA, "Information System Model Transactions and Contracts (Second Edition)" / accessed October 30, 2026).

How the writing role is divided appears in the provisions on requirements definition creation support services in the same model contract. The commentary on Article 14 describes requirements definition as the work of compiling the requirement specifications (functions to be realized in software) of the system the user intends to build, and as work that depends heavily on the user's business content. For this reason, the provisions assume a structure in which the user performs the requirements definition work and the vendor supports it. Article 14, Paragraph 2 stipulates that what the vendor performs is support work such as research, analysis, organization, proposals, and advice based on specialized knowledge and experience in information processing technology, carried out so that the user's work proceeds smoothly and appropriately (IPA, "Information System Model Transactions and Contracts (Second Edition)" / accessed October 30, 2026).

Where the deciding role resides is written in the provisions on managers. Article 9 lists finalizing the requirements definition document at the top of the authority and responsibilities of the user-side manager, and places with the vendor-side manager the authority and responsibility for responding to matters requested by the user in the requirements definition creation support services. Article 17 stipulates that after the user completes the requirements definition document, the user and vendor check whether it conforms to the decisions of the requirements review meeting (meetings held to decide requirements), and both managers sign and seal it. The requirements definition document is finalized upon confirmation by both parties (IPA, "Information System Model Transactions and Contracts (Second Edition)" / accessed October 30, 2026).

Converted into the table, finalizing the requirements definition document is done by the user-side manager. The writing work is performed by the user with support from the vendor through research and proposals, and the verification check is performed by both parties. Even when the development side takes on much of the writing work, the seat where what to build is finalized remains with the ordering party.

The IPA Software Engineering Center's "17 Principles of IT-ization Attacked from the Uppermost Stream" draws the same line. Principle [9] is "requirements definition is the ordering party's responsibility." The ordering party's code of conduct first places spreading within the company the awareness that "we decide the requirements and take responsibility." It then lists advancing requirements definition with the business and IT departments working in tandem, and making good use of the vendor at the requirements definition stage. The vendor's code of conduct is "provide support that stands on the ordering party's side" (IPA Software Engineering Center, "SEC BOOKS: 17 Principles of IT-ization for Practical Use" / accessed October 30, 2026).

The role of verifying becomes clearer when split into two. The requirements definition guide divides confirmation into "verification," checking whether the written requirements satisfy the requirements without omissions or errors, and "validation," checking whether the defined requirements themselves are valid as a means to achieve the purpose (IPA Social Infrastructure Center, "Requirements Definition Guide for Users, 2nd Edition: 128 Keys to Successful Requirements Definition" / accessed October 30, 2026). Verification is matching documents against decisions, so the development side can carry much of it. Validation cannot be done by anyone who does not know the purpose of the business. The same guide states that the business department is responsible for the results of requirements definition, and that at the system testing stage the business department has the responsibility to verify with its own eyes (IPA Social Infrastructure Center, "Requirements Definition Guide for Users, 2nd Edition: 128 Keys to Successful Requirements Definition" / 210 printed pages / accessed October 30, 2026).

When dividing up the work, decide in advance within the company to have a single person approve the requirements. If each department has its own approver, each department's version of correctness lines up as-is in the requirements.

When requirements definition cannot be done or you want to hand it off entirely, in both cases the deciding role is vacant within the company

When requirements definition cannot be done, it is less about writing skill and more that the business's correct answer has not been settled within the company

The trouble of not being able to do requirements definition looks like a writing problem, but it often stalls before that point. There are three places where it stalls: the same business is done differently by department or by person, the handling of exceptions exists only in the heads of the people in charge, and no one has been designated to decide which way of doing things is the correct one.

If departments do things differently, the writer cannot decide which way to write down. If exceptions live only in people's heads, the written requirements represent only the normal flow and stop working in the field the day an exception appears. If there is no decider, even after collecting the differences and exceptions, the choice among them floats in the air. None of the three is filled by writing skill; they all stem from the deciding role being vacant.

The IPA requirements definition guide raises the same problem in the section on selecting the owner (the department responsible for drawing the expected business effects from the system). The heading of the problem to solve is that the business department does not recognize requirements definition as something it should drive with responsibility. Under it, four items line up: requirements are raised regardless of frequency of use, departments differ in their enthusiasm for systematization, people are too busy in their actual work so they "leave it to the IT systems department," and securing system development costs is the IT systems department's job. The text states that the departments using the system are "not necessarily a monolith," and writes that "because their intentions diverge, requirements become skewed" (IPA Social Infrastructure Center, "Requirements Definition Guide for Users, 2nd Edition: 128 Keys to Successful Requirements Definition" / 209 printed pages / accessed October 30, 2026).

The guide's book page states that the need for transformation is growing, away from the conventional approach where IT vendors or systems departments drive requirements definition, toward one where users in the business department participate proactively. In the text's "About this revision," it writes that requirements definition should be led by the users themselves, as the parties who use IT systems in their business. It also acknowledges that even if you say users are responsible for carrying it out, users are not necessarily familiar with requirements definition and it often does not go well. The guide therefore aimed to show not only what to do and what to build, but how to think and how to build (IPA Social Infrastructure Center, "Requirements Definition Guide for Users, 2nd Edition: 128 Keys to Successful Requirements Definition" / accessed October 30, 2026).

Unfamiliarity with writing can be filled by outsourcing the writing work. What cannot be filled is the judgment of which way of doing things is the correct one. Whether you can designate one person in the company to decide, and give that person the time to line up the departmental differences and exceptions and choose among them, is the dividing line between being able to do requirements definition and not.

If you hand it off entirely, the judgment of what to build remains neither inside the company nor with the outsourcing vendor

If you hand requirements definition entirely to the outsourcing vendor, the process advances with the deciding role left vacant. For a receiver who builds from finalized requirements, requirements are something handed over from the ordering side. All a receiver who does not know the business's correct answer can do is fill gaps by guesswork or ask the ordering side back. If there is no decider at the end of those questions, documents get produced while the judgment of what to build remains nowhere.

The requirements definition guide states that the boundary of responsibility between user companies and vendor companies is requirements definition. It then writes that there are not a few cases where a fixed-price contract is signed from requirements definition onward, and that projects can be seen causing problems in a state of total hand-off to the vendor company (IPA Social Infrastructure Center, "Requirements Definition Guide for Users, 2nd Edition: 128 Keys to Successful Requirements Definition" / 23 printed pages / accessed October 30, 2026).

The commentary on the Model Contract (Second Edition) also carries the observation that under a fixed-price type, the user side may develop a stronger sense of "handing it all off" and "leaving everything to the vendor." It also writes that many point out that if you do not use a quasi-mandate type, responsibilities such as the user coordinating with stakeholders in requirements definition tend to become ambiguous, and oversights in requirements definition also become more likely (IPA, "Information System Model Transactions and Contracts (Second Edition)" / accessed October 30, 2026).

Principle [4] of the 17 principles is "do not move to the next process without obtaining agreement among stakeholders (interested parties)." The ordering party's code of conduct places "regard confirming stakeholder agreement as your own work." The vendor's code of conduct places making explicit that while the work of confirming agreement can be supported, the contract (responsibility) cannot be taken on (IPA Software Engineering Center, "SEC BOOKS: 17 Principles of IT-ization for Practical Use" / accessed October 30, 2026).

Development pushed forward by total hand-off does not always stall. However, the state remains in which no one exists to decide the direction of fixes when it does stall. The division of sending work outside while keeping judgment inside applies to outsourcing new businesses too; a table by process is included in 5 Patterns of New Business Outsourcing Failures.

You want to hand it off entirely when the decision-maker's time is unavailable. In that case, what can be outsourced is work that reduces the decision-maker's time: gathering and arranging the differences and exceptions, up to the point of preparing materials for choosing.

Whether it's contracted development, SES, consulting, or an accompaniment model, the responsibility for deciding remains with the client; what differs is who gathers the materials for deciding and when the decision is made.

When choosing a form of outsourcing, compare who gathers the materials for deciding and when the decision is made. On our FDE accompaniment development service page, we describe the differences between the four forms in our FAQ as follows. The right column is what we read from that description about where the responsibility for deciding is placed.

In every form, the responsibility for deciding remains with the client. What differs is who gathers the materials for deciding and how far they go, and whether the decision is made before building or while building. Contracted development decides everything before building, so it fits issues whose requirements are already settled. The accompaniment model decides while building, so it requires continuing to take the decision-maker's time even after work begins. When adding hands via SES, the seat for deciding what to build must also be filled on the client side.

The differences between contract forms (quasi-delegate contract, contracted work, advisory contract) and how to choose are summarized in FDE: hiring or external use, so we won't cover them here.

When requirements aren't settled, start by looking at on-site operations and data, and decide by showing something that works

When outsourcing at a stage where requirements aren't settled, start by looking at on-site operations and data without waiting for a requirements specification. Even before what to build is decided, the client and developer can together put into words the issues to solve based on what's happening on-site. Rather than fixing the specification first, the approach is to decide while showing something that works.

The commentary on IPA's model contract (second edition) states that in the system planning and requirements definition phases, the user's business requirements are not concretely settled, and the users themselves cannot concretely envision the deliverables at the start of the phase. The idea of deciding by showing something that works also appears in the same commentary as a difference in development models. Under the prototype model (a build approach using prototypes), development proceeds without dividing phases by building prototypes from user requests, showing them, and evaluating while developing (IPA "Information Systems Model Transactions and Contracts (Second Edition)" / accessed October 30, 2026).

Showing something that works changes the decision-maker's job. For people who own the operations, it's easier to respond by touching the prototype and saying "this field won't work for our exceptions" than to judge whether a document is correct by reading it. If the developer translates what the decision-maker said into the language of requirements, the writing role and the deciding role each settle on their own side.

The introduction to the Requirements Definition Guide calls fields using relatively new technologies such as IoT, big data, and AI "offensive fields," emphasizing collaboration through connection with customers. In these fields, it says, what matters is not just the line that user companies are responsible for defining requirements, but running a cycle of gathering, accumulating, and analyzing data together with vendor companies to turn it into services. It also states that not all requirements become clear at the development stage, and there's a need to meet genuine requirements that only become apparent through use (IPA Social Infrastructure Center "Requirements Definition Guide for Users, 2nd Edition: 128 Keys to Successful Requirements Definition" / printed page 5, accessed October 30, 2026).

However, even with an approach of showing something that works, if there's no one on the client side to look and decide, prototypes will multiply but requirements won't be settled. The steps from observation to handover are described in the article on FDE's work, and the answer to where you can start outsourcing without a requirements specification is also in that article's FAQ. The evaluation criteria for deciding from the prototype whether to proceed to production are summarized in the article on PoC evaluation criteria.

For large-scale development with fully finalized requirements or issues that off-the-shelf SaaS can handle, the traditional form of finalizing requirements before ordering fits better

The approach described so far doesn't always fit. Even on our FDE accompaniment development service page, we state that it doesn't suit large-scale development with fully finalized requirements or issues that off-the-shelf SaaS can handle, and in those cases we recommend traditional options.

If requirements are fully finalized, the reason to choose a decide-while-building form becomes weak. Since the deciding work is already done, contracted development, which builds from finalized requirements, can draw a clearer line around the scope of work and the endpoint. If off-the-shelf SaaS (ready-made software used over the internet) suffices, requirements definition is done not for what to build but for what to choose.

Even in this case, the deciding role remains with the client. What changes is that the decisions come together before building. How to distinguish fitting issues from non-fitting ones is covered in where to use FDE support, and the background of organizing the FDE model as a type of investment for issues whose requirements aren't yet settled is described in the article on why FDEs are increasing.

FAQ on who does requirements definition

Q. Who writes the requirements specification, the client or the developer?

The writing work can be shared, but approving and finalizing the content is the client's job. IPA's model contract (second edition) also takes the form of the user writing, the vendor supporting, and both parties' responsible persons reviewing and finalizing; the provisions were introduced in the section on the deciding role (IPA "Information Systems Model Transactions and Contracts (Second Edition)" / accessed October 30, 2026). When much of the drafting is delegated to the developer, the client keeps a record of what was decided, by whom, and when. Before approval, check which recorded decision each drafted requirement comes from. If requirements not in the record are mixed in, verify who decided them and when before approving.

Q. What should we do if no one in-house can put together the requirements?

Before choosing who will consolidate things, decide on one person who will make the decisions. The consolidation work can be outsourced, but the decision-maker cannot be brought in from outside. The IPA model contract (second edition) states that even when a company's internal structure is insufficient, as with small and medium-sized business users, the importance of the super-upstream process does not change. It suggests that when a company cannot settle things internally, it is desirable to use consulting as a precondition of the development contract, or to split the planning/requirements definition stage and the development stage into separate contracts (IPA "Information System Model Transactions and Contracts (Second Edition)" / accessed October 30, 2026).

Q. If we use agile development, do we not need requirements definition?

The role of deciding does not disappear; the unit of decision-making becomes smaller and the number of decisions increases. The IPA model contract for agile development treats the product owner (PO), who is responsible for the product's direction and content, as extremely important, and states that the PO is chosen by the user company. The vendor company provides advice on the backlog (a list of features to build, ordered by priority) and explains technical risks, while the scrum master (the role supporting how the team works) is chosen by the vendor company. The same page states that for the user company to realize the benefits, it is not enough to simply choose a vendor company with sufficient skills and know-how; the user company must engage proactively and actively, which entails a corresponding burden (IPA "Information System Model Transactions and Contracts (Agile Development Edition)" / accessed October 30, 2026).

Q. What happens if requirements change in the middle of requirements definition?

In an approach where you decide by looking at something that works, changing requirements is assumed. What is decided in advance is who will decide on changes. The commentary on Article 36 (Handling of Unsettled Matters) of the IPA model contract (second edition) also acknowledges that in actual projects, it is often unavoidable to move to the next phase while leaving some matters unresolved. On that basis, it establishes a practice of confirming in writing the content of unsettled matters and the scheduled time for settling them (IPA "Information System Model Transactions and Contracts (Second Edition)" / accessed October 30, 2026). If the person who makes the decision to change is fixed, you record what changed and move on to the next step.

SAI's perspective: Even when starting before a requirements definition document exists, the counterpart for agreeing on the order of problems to solve is the client's management and frontline staff

Even at the stage of putting into words together what to build, what we lack is a seat for deciding which problem to solve first. We handle identifying problems by examining frontline operations and data, and the order in which to solve them is decided in agreement with management and the frontline. That is because which fix will make operations run smoothly first can only be decided by those who own the operations.

We will help with the consolidation. Please decide what to solve first.

We start with observation in order to identify bottlenecks that do not surface in interviews, from frontline operations and data. Examples of our support are listed by industry on our case studies page.

One point worth checking before placing an order is whether you can write down the name of one person who will choose among the differing practices of each department. Even if you cannot, you can consult us starting from the stage of "we have been asked to produce requirements and are stuck," using our FDE-accompanied development page as the entry point.
