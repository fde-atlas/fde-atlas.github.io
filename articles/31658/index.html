FDEs are getting more and more popular. Do you think this will be the way AI gets deployed in 2026?

Original title: FDE越来越火，你认为这会是2026年AI落地之道吗？

TorchV · 2026-05-26 · 7 min read

Summary

Drawing on over a year of enterprise AI delivery work by his team (clients include banks and state-owned firms), the author argues that Forward Deployed Engineers (FDEs) are becoming critical to AI adoption. AI products are getting simpler, yet enterprise deployment grows harder because real business processes involve approval rules, data definitions, permissions, and tacit knowledge. The article outlines three FDE responsibilities: requirements analysis and solution design, driving implementation, and trial-run evaluation and iteration. It stresses that FDEs differ from traditional outsourcing—they actively push projects forward—and describes organizational changes at the author's company, including API/SDK openings and support teams. The conclusion: FDE may not be the only path, but will likely be a major route for AI deployment in 2026.

Key points

  • Simpler AI products make enterprise deployment harder, not easier.
  • FDEs handle solution design, implementation, and trial-run iteration on-site.
  • FDEs are active project drivers, not passive outsourced labor.
  • Vendors need a closed loop of product, field engineering, and fast iteration.
  • Practitioners should build full-stack, communication, and industry knowledge.

Author: Lu Xiangdong, founder of Hangzhou Mengjia Network Technology Co., Ltd. The team has long focused on enterprise AI implementation and AI knowledge engine practices, and is one of the earliest teams in China to explore RAG enterprise applications. Its product TorchV has successfully served customers such as Inspur Information, WeBank, Wuchan Zhongda, Taizhou Bank, Shitu Automotive, and Zhonghui Tax Agent Firm.

FDE stands for Forward Deployed Engineer, which can be understood as a "frontline deployment engineer." An FDE is the person who stands at the intersection of technology, business, organization, and process.

1. Why FDEs Are Increasingly in Demand ​

The form of software in the AI era is changing. Software generally becomes simpler at the GUI level, and some even have no GUI (graphical user interface) at all, instead adopting a minimalist conversational CUI. The difficulty of getting started for customers will show a polarization: those who like CUI highly praise it, even believing that, like OpenClaw and Hermes, some complex tasks can be completed on a phone simply through conversation.

Figure: The difficulties of implementing AI in enterprises.

But for most enterprise businesses, things are not that simple.

An enterprise's processes cannot automatically run with a single phrase like "handle this for me." Behind them are approval rules, data standards, permission boundaries, and a large amount of tacit knowledge. While CUI lowers the surface barrier to operation, it also brings new problems: user habits need to be migrated, business intent needs to be accurately understood, and AI capabilities need to be embedded into real processes. In other words, AI products themselves may be getting simpler, but AI implementation is actually becoming more complex.

This is the first reason FDEs are in demand: enterprises need someone to translate AI capabilities into executable business solutions.

An even bigger reason is the change in talent structure brought about by AI.

Traditional informatization has been around for more than 30 years. ERP, CMS, CRM, and the CRUD systems programmers often talk about are all familiar to many enterprise customers. Enterprises usually also have internal informatization teams, IT operations teams, and so on. But very few large enterprises already have mature AI departments, AI business architects, and AI project operations teams.

So enterprises need a new role: someone who understands AI systems and also understands the business frontline; who can communicate with business people and collaborate with technical teams; who can design solutions and also drive implementation. This is exactly where the value of the FDE emerges.

Since November 2024, we have been going deep into customer sites, from LC Information to WZ Bank, and later customers as well, all involving a large amount of on-site delivery work. After more than a year of practice, we have increasingly clearly felt that customers do not only care whether your product is so-called "top-tier"; what they really care about is—when they raise various complex questions, can you handle them and solve real business problems.

This is also where AI implementation differs greatly from traditional SaaS sales. AI is not done once accounts are sold and permissions are granted. It needs to be repeatedly debugged, validated, and iterated within the customer's real business environment. In this process, the FDE will increasingly become the most critical connection point between AI service providers and customers.

2. What FDEs Mainly Do ​

Figure: Our understanding of the FDE.

FDE stands for Forward Deployed Engineer, which can be understood as a "frontline deployment engineer." But understanding it merely as an "on-site engineer" is actually inaccurate. An FDE is not simply waiting for requirements and doing development on site, but rather driving AI projects to actually run at the customer's site.

Generally speaking, FDEs mainly do three types of work.

1. Requirements analysis and solution finalization

FDEs need to communicate fully with business and technical staff at the customer's site to identify the business processes truly worth transforming with AI, and to set reasonable goals and implementation plans.

The focus here is not only on product and technical solutions, but also on business architecture design on the customer side. For example: does the customer need to set up a dedicated AI promotion team? Who will lead the project? Which business departments will participate? How should the pilot scope be chosen? How will training be conducted after launch? How will results be evaluated? If these questions are not designed in advance, AI projects can easily stall at the stage of "great demo results, but the business can't actually use it."

At this stage, FDEs can also use existing products and AI tools (we currently generally use Claude Code, CodeX, or v0) to quickly build prototypes, allowing customers to see the results more intuitively. The value of a prototype is not just to demonstrate capability; more importantly, it helps both sides align on goals and reduce errors caused by abstract discussions.

2. Driving implementation progress

FDEs need to know their own product features and technical capabilities very well, and be able to break down the implementation plan together with the internal product and R&D team.

Which features can be quickly launched in the first batch? Which system integrations must be completed first? Which requirements can be solved with existing capabilities? Which need additional customization in the project? These judgments all need to be made by the FDE on site.

At the same time, FDEs need to continuously follow up on implementation progress, push the customer side to cooperate, and organize training and trial runs. Many AI projects are not a one-time "big launch," but start with a pilot in a local scenario and then gradually expand to more business areas. This requires FDEs to have both project management skills and a sense of on-site rhythm.

3. Trial runs, goal checking, and iteration

The real challenge of an AI project often only begins with the trial run.

On site, FDEs need to work with the customer to complete business sorting, knowledge preparation, business testing, result checking, problem analysis, and improvement plan formulation. For example, if knowledge base answers are inaccurate, is it a parsing problem, a chunking problem, a retrieval problem, or is the business process itself not clearly defined? Someone on site needs to make these judgments quickly.

Then, FDEs need to translate on-site problems into clear iteration goals, and drive optimization together with the customer and their own product and R&D team. This process is essentially not "delivering a system," but rather "making AI capabilities steadily generate value in the business."

3. Changes AI Service Providers Need to Make ​

In fact, even before the FDE concept became popular, our requirements for internal staff were already changing.

In 2025, our AIS small team was stationed on site at a bank customer in Shenzhen for as long as 6 months. At the time, we were already thinking: if we keep facing AI implementation needs from high-security industry customers such as banks, energy, and government-enterprise sectors, we must have more engineers who can communicate, develop, and directly solve problems on site. So starting this year, we have also been proactively adjusting our staffing structure in this direction, needing to build faster, more mobile on-site teams.

Although Vibe Coding has multiplied the speed of product upgrades and iterations, some on-site work still cannot be replaced. Especially in enterprise AI projects, customers' data environments, network environments, permission systems, business processes, and organizational structures are all different, and for many high-security industry customers, data cannot leave their domain—many problems can only be truly understood by going on site.

So we have always had a very clear judgment: solving customer problems always comes first.

But one point must also be emphasized here: FDEs are completely different from traditional outsourcing.

In outsourcing, the customer assigns tasks and the vendor completes delivery according to requirements. An FDE, however, is not someone who passively takes orders, but one of the core drivers of the AI project. FDEs need to pull together the client's business and technical teams, as well as their own product and R&D team, to truly land the project.

The foundation of FDE delivery is their own product, with the goal of bridging the gap between product capabilities and the customer's real needs. FDEs need to participate in customer meetings, operate systems hands-on, deploy systems, organize training, record problems, feed back requirements, formulate iteration plans, and even complete some coding or delivery tasks on site.

To reduce the FDE's on-site development workload, we have also made many adjustments to the AIS product. For example, we have opened up APIs/SDKs for knowledge base management, conversation services, application management, organization and permission management, system settings, Skill management, MCP management, and atomic commands, so that on-site teams can do secondary development more lightweightly using APIs. At the same time, we set up a fire support team for each project to provide FDEs with support in technology, documentation, product iteration, and more.

Behind this actually lies an organizational shift for AI service providers: you can no longer rely solely on the traditional division of labor among sales, pre-sales, and delivery; instead, you need to form a closed loop of "product capability + on-site engineering capability + rapid iteration capability."

IV. Impact on Practitioners ​

I don't know how many companies are already changing the way we are, but as AI deployment deepens, I believe the share of roles like FDE in organizations will only keep growing.

This doesn't mean everyone has to become an FDE, but it will indeed change the skill requirements of many roles. For engineers, product managers, and solutions personnel, the future will demand stronger hybrid capabilities.

  • Develop full-stack capabilities. Quickly move away from overly rigid role boundaries like "frontend, backend, product manager," and shift toward full-stack capabilities as much as possible. Projects in the AI era will move faster, and the more someone can solve problems across the entire pipeline, the more valuable they are.
  • Improve communication and listening skills. Often, customers cannot accurately express what they want at the outset. You need to work with the customer to sort out the problem, define goals, and make plans. Perhaps not everyone needs full project-level architecture skills, but some local tasks still require them.
  • Learn industry knowledge. For example, deepen your understanding of the business know-how of two or three industries, and build up your understanding of them through continuous practice. AI deployment is not purely a technical problem; industry understanding will become increasingly important.
  • Adapt to rapid mobility. You may need to get used to short- and medium-term business trips and on-site work, unless you truly believe that online meetings and written communication alone can quickly solve complex problems. Otherwise, keep remembering that saying: Solving the customer's problem always comes first. We need to be on the ground.

Figure: The impact of FDEs on practitioners.

Back to the question in the title: Will the FDE be the way to deploy AI in 2026?

I think it may not be the only answer, but it will most likely become a very important path. Because AI truly entering enterprises is not just a matter of model capability, nor a matter of stacking product features—it requires people who can connect technology, business, organization, and processes.

And the FDE is exactly the person standing at that connection point.
