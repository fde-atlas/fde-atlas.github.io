AI Governance and Implementation Security | Six Control Points

Original title: AIガバナンスと実装セキュリティ｜6つの制御点

FDX株式会社 · 2026-08-28 · 12 min read

Summary

This FDX article argues that AI governance is decided at the implementation layer, not in policy documents, and identifies six control points to design when connecting generative AI to business systems: data boundaries, permission design, input trust boundaries, output verification, audit logs, and model updates. It warns against delegating human account permissions to AI agents, treats prompt injection as unpreventable and thus a matter of limiting blast radius, and cites incidents like Deloitte's flawed Australian government report to show what happens when verification steps are missing. It also notes that declaring compliance with guidelines differs from guaranteeing it through implementation.

Key points

  • Governance effectiveness depends on verifiable implementation, not policy documents.
  • Six control points: data boundaries, permissions, input trust, output verification, audit logs, model updates.
  • Give AI dedicated execution identities with per-operation minimal permissions; exclude irreversible actions.
  • Prompt injection cannot be fully prevented; separate ingestion from execution and limit impact.
  • Output verification must be embedded per business risk, or it gets skipped.

Discussions of AI governance tend to drift toward drafting policy documents. But accidents actually happen outside the documents—in the details of implementation. Which data flows where, what the AI executes with which permissions, and whether erroneous outputs get reflected without anyone's review. If these are not designed, no amount of policy pages will change the outcome.

This article organizes six control points that should be designed when connecting generative AI to business systems. Types of incidents that have actually occurred are covered in "Generative AI Incident Case Studies 2025-2026".

Who this article is for

  • Information systems and development teams responsible for designing the integration of generative AI and AI agents into internal systems
  • Promotion leads who have established AI usage rules but are unsure whether they have been carried down to the implementation level
  • Those who need to evaluate vendor-proposed AI configurations from a security perspective

AI governance is determined by implementation, not documents

There are three typical gaps between policy documents and implementation.

Statement in the documentWhat implementation must answer
"Do not enter confidential information"Who determines what is confidential? Does the determination depend on human attentiveness?
"Always review outputs"Is there a mechanism that blocks progress without review? Are review records kept?
"Operate with appropriate permissions"What exactly are the permissions granted to the AI? Are human permissions handed over as-is?

The left column cannot be verified as being followed. The right column can be checked for whether it exists as a design. The effectiveness of governance is determined by whether it has been reduced to a verifiable form.

Six control points to design at implementation time

1. Data boundaryWhich data may leave to which environmentContractual restrictions and implementation diverge

3. Input trust boundaryHandling of externally sourced textFollowing instructions embedded in ingested documents
4. Output verificationWhere and how humans reviewReview is optional and gets skipped
5. Audit logsWhat is recorded, for how long, and who can see itThe sequence of events cannot be reconstructed afterward
6. Model updatesDecision-making on updates and re-verification proceduresChanges in behavior go unnoticed

1. Data boundary

The first thing to settle is which data may go where. Decisions should be made per business unit. A company-wide uniform rule ends up either too strict to be usable or too loose to be followed.

Three things should be checked:

  • Contractual and regulatory constraints: Do customer contracts restrict provision to third parties? Are there boundaries that industry regulations do not allow crossing?
  • Actual pathways: Where does data physically go, including API destinations, log storage locations, and vendors' subcontractors?
  • Whether training use occurs: Is input data set to be used for training the provider's models?

If constraints cannot be crossed, a configuration that runs entirely within your own environment becomes an option. Decision materials are organized in "What Are Open-Weight Models? Definitions and How to Read Licenses".

2. Permission design

In configurations where AI agents operate internal systems, this becomes the biggest issue. A common implementation is passing the responsible person's account permissions directly to the AI. This should be avoided.

There are two reasons. First, humans constantly make "decisions not to act," but that restraint cannot be expected of AI. Second, sharing permissions makes human and AI operations indistinguishable in audit logs.

The design principles are as follows.

  • Create a dedicated execution principal for the AI: Do not reuse human accounts. Ensure it is always distinguishable in logs
  • Grant operation-level permissions: Cut at granularity such as "read-only" or "append-only to a specific table." Do not apply roles as-is
  • Exclude irreversible operations: Deletion, sending, final approval, payment. Remove these from permissions and limit them to human execution
  • Expand gradually: Start with least privilege and add only the necessary scope based on operational track record

Granting broadly at the start and narrowing later almost never works in practice. Narrowing carries the risk of disrupting business, so it keeps getting postponed.

3. Input trust boundary

Generative AI receives instructions and data as the same text. Because of this property, text contained in externally sourced documents can be interpreted as instructions (prompt injection).

The problem arises in configurations like the following.

  • Having the AI summarize the body of received emails and branching processing based on the result
  • Fetching web pages for the AI to read and having it operate internal systems according to the content
  • Having the AI analyze files submitted by customers and reflecting the output in a ledger

Each of these has a pathway where externally introduced text influences the AI's behavior. Implementation countermeasures are as follows.

  • Always treat externally sourced text as data: Design on the premise that instructions written in ingested content are not executed
  • Separate ingestion from execution: Split the process that reads external data and the process that performs privileged operations into separate execution principals
  • Insert human approval for operations with side effects: Do not auto-execute sending, updating, or final approval in processes triggered by external input
  • Do not pass outputs directly as the next input: In chained configurations, insert verification in between

Complete defense has not been established at this point. A design that "limits the impact scope" rather than "blocks everything" is realistic.

4. Output verification

Verification becomes optional and is skipped. It must be built in as part of the process.

The key point of design is to vary the depth of verification by task. Uniform strict rules either go unobserved or nullify the benefits.

Use of outputLevel of verification
Internal drafts and summariesConfirmation by the user themselves is sufficient
External submissions and client-facing documentsConfirmation by a different person is required
Documents containing citations, figures, laws, or case precedentsVerification against the original sources is required. Do not allow submission without verification
Documents with legal effect, or answers that create financial liabilityDo not use free-form AI generation; limit to templated responses or human authorship

Deloitte's report for the Australian government and the sanctions imposed on US attorneys both resulted from the absence of this verification step. For details of these cases, see "Generative AI Incident Cases 2025-2026".

It is also practically important to include verification effort in the estimate of reduction benefits. If it is excluded, the actual reduction rate will fall below the estimate. The estimation procedure is explained in "What Is an AI Assessment? Designing Task Inventories and ROI".

5. Audit Logs

When an incident occurs, the quality of the response depends on whether the sequence of events can be reconstructed. The items to record are as follows.

  • Executing entity (human or AI, and which AI agent)
  • Input (the prompt and identifiers of the referenced data)
  • Model and version used
  • Output
  • Subsequent operations (what was updated, who approved it)

The design decisions required concern retention periods and access permissions. Because prompts contain business data, the logs themselves become objects of protection. If you start recording without deciding who can view the logs, the logs become a new leakage channel.

6. Model Updates

When a model is updated, behavior changes even with the same prompt. With API services, updates and deprecations occur at the provider's discretion.

Three things are required:

  • Version recording: manage which tasks have been verified with which model version
  • Re-verification criteria: define in advance, for each task, what must be checked upon an update to judge that operations can continue
  • Rollback means: keep a path to return to the previous operations if problems emerge after an update

There is also the option of fixing the version in your own environment, but in that case the responsibility for incorporating security fixes shifts to your company. Fixing a version is not the same as leaving it unattended.

Relationship with Guidelines

In Japan, the Ministry of Economy, Trade and Industry and the Ministry of Internal Affairs and Communications have published the "AI Business Guidelines," providing guidance according to the respective roles of AI developers, providers, and users. Professional bodies are also making progress: on March 27, 2025, the Tokyo Bar Association enacted the "Guidelines for the Appropriate Use of Generative AI Services in Legal Practice," recommending verification of primary sources when using outputs in professional work.

These documents indicate "what should be protected," but "how to implement it" is left to each company's design. The six control points in this article correspond to those implementation-side design items. Declaring compliance with guidelines and ensuring compliance through implementation are separate tasks.

Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ)

Q1. Does governance hold up once a document of AI usage rules is in place?

A. Not with documents alone. Statements such as "do not input confidential information" and "always check outputs" cannot be verified for compliance. To make them effective, you need mechanisms that do not rely on human attentiveness for judgment, processes that block progress without confirmation, and designs that leave records. Documents are a necessary condition, not a sufficient one.

Q2. Is it safe to give AI agents permission to operate internal systems?

A. It depends on how permissions are scoped. Avoid implementations that simply hand over the responsible person's account permissions. Create a dedicated AI execution entity, grant minimal permissions per operation, and exclude irreversible operations such as deletion, sending, approvals, and payments from those permissions, limiting them to human execution. Granting broad permissions at first and narrowing them later is effectively non-functional in practice, because the narrowing work carries business-interruption risk and gets postponed.

Q3. Can prompt injection be fully prevented technically?

A. No complete defense has been established at this point. The premise is that it "can happen," and the design policy should be to limit the scope of impact. Always treat externally sourced text as data, separate ingestion processing from operations involving permissions into different execution entities, and insert human approval for operations with side effects. With these three points, even if an attack succeeds, the damage is contained.

Q4. How much should audit logs record?

A. The standard is the level at which the sequence of events can be reconstructed in case of an incident. The minimum items are the executing entity, the input prompt and identifiers of referenced data, the model and version, the output, and subsequent operations and approvers. At the same time, because prompts contain business data, the logs themselves become objects of protection. If you start recording without deciding retention periods and viewing permissions, the logs become a new leakage channel.

Q5. When using cloud APIs, how can you confirm that input data is not used for training?

A. Check the original terms of use and the settings for each account and contract plan. Corporate plans often default to not using data for training, but this varies by plan and contract type, and the terms are subject to revision. Do not stop at checking at the time of introduction; establish a process to reconfirm at contract renewal or plan changes. At the same time, understand the data path, including where logs are stored and the vendor's subcontractors.

Q6. What is required to declare compliance with the guidelines?

A. Declaring compliance and ensuring compliance through implementation are separate tasks. The guidelines show what should be observed, but the implementation method is left to each company's design. To support the declaration, you need to document, for each of the six control points in this article, how your company's implementation works, in a verifiable form. When questioned by a third party, the criterion is whether you can demonstrate that the design exists.

Summary

  • The effectiveness of AI governance is determined not by policy documents but by whether the implementation is verifiable
  • There are six control points to design: data boundaries, permission design, input trust boundaries, output validation, audit logs, and model updates
  • In permission design, do not reuse human accounts; create AI-dedicated execution principals and grant minimum permissions per operation. Exclude deletion, sending, final approval, and payments from permissions
  • Assume that prompt injection cannot be fully prevented, and limit the impact by separating ingestion from execution and requiring human approval for operations with side effects
  • Vary the rigor of output validation by business function. Uniform strict rules will either be ignored or negate the benefits
  • The criterion for audit logs is reproducibility in the event of an incident. At the same time, the logs themselves are assets to protect, so decide viewing permissions first
  • The guidelines show "what to protect," but "how to implement it" is left to each company's design. Declaration and implementation-level assurance are separate tasks
