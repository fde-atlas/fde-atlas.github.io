What Is an FDE (Forward Deployed Engineer)? 7 Tips for Drawing Out Insights

Original title: FDE（Forward Deployed Engineer）とは何か｜インサイトを引き出す7つの秘訣

TIMEWELL · 2026-09-12 · 17 min read

Summary

TIMEWELL's Hamamoto explains what a Forward Deployed Engineer (FDE) is: an engineer who sits beside the customer, builds working systems on real, messy data, and feeds lessons back into the vendor's product. Tracing the role from Palantir to a16z's 2025 essay, OpenAI's Deployment Company with roughly 150 FDEs, Anthropic's work with FIS, and Accenture's Japan organization, he argues the boom stems from MIT findings that 95% of organizations see no measurable return on generative AI investment. The article details seven techniques for extracting real requirements on-site and warns that FDE without product feedback loops is just expensive contracting.

Key points

  • FDEs originated at Palantir: build at the customer's site, return learnings to the product.
  • MIT NANDA found 95% of organizations get no measurable return on GenAI investment.
  • a16z, OpenAI, Anthropic, and Accenture all invested in FDE models in 2025–2026.
  • Seven field techniques include touching real data on day one and collecting exceptions.
  • Without product feedback, FDE work degrades into high-priced custom development.

Hello, I'm Hamamoto from TIMEWELL Inc. I spent over 10 years building new businesses at large enterprises, served as the operations lead for "CHANGE by ONE JAPAN," a challenger support program with more than 100 large companies participating, and now run "WARP ENTRE," an AI-driven development program for entrepreneurs and intrapreneurs. Our company openly declares that "we run our business in the FDE style." Whenever we say that, we always get asked: what is an FDE? This article answers that question in a way that newcomers can understand, and then concretely describes the "techniques for drawing out insights" that FDEs actually use in the field.

Summary: An FDE (Forward Deployed Engineer) is an engineer who sits next to the customer to handle development and implementation in order to get their company's product running in production at the customer's site. It was started by Palantir; in June 2025 a16z called it "the hottest job in startups"; in 2026 OpenAI established an implementation company with roughly 150 FDEs, and Accenture launched an FDE organization of several thousand people in Japan. The difference from on-site development or consulting lies in what is being sold: FDEs sell the product and feed what they learn in the field back into it. There are seven secrets to drawing out insights: sit next to them, touch real data on day one, make working prototypes into question-asking devices, collect exceptions, write out nouns and verbs, ask about the three layers of requirements separately, and bring findings back weekly.

What is an FDE? The job explained in 3 minutes

FDE stands for Forward Deployed Engineer. In Japanese it would be "forward-deployed engineer," and this "forward deployment" is a military term. The idea is not to station troops at headquarters but at the front line. Translated into the world of software, it means a way of working where, instead of waiting for specifications at the head office, you sit at the customer's site and build there.

Let me explain the job for newcomers through the flow of a single day. In the morning, you show up at the customer's factory or office—not your own company's meeting room. Sitting next to the person in charge, you have them show you the system they actually use and the actual data. Dirty data: inconsistencies in manual input, blank fields, different field names for each department. Within that same day, you build an incomplete prototype that runs on that data and put it in front of them. They say, "This case is different," or "This needs the manager's approval." You fix it and put it in front of them again. Within a few weeks you put it into production, fix what breaks in production, and bring what you learned in the process back to your company's product, turning it into features usable from day one with the next customer. That is an FDE's day, and one project.

The origin lies with Palantir. Later accounts consistently agree on this point: in the company's early days, customer data was dirtier than expected, business processes were more complex than expected, and there was often no one who could even articulate the requirements. So they had engineers sit next to customers and learn the business itself. Since we have not looked inside Palantir, we will not assert this as the founding story, but the archetype of the role is there. In a January 2026 essay, a16z summarized this archetype as "dropping a small team into a chaotic environment and standing up a customized, working foundation within months" 1.

Here is a table of how this role differs from similar jobs. This is the point I most want to convey to newcomers: if you only look at "entering the field and building together," they all look the same, but what they sell is different.

On-site development generates revenue the longer it lasts. FDE is the opposite: the faster the customer becomes self-sufficient and the more it can be rolled out horizontally to the next customer, the better. That is why FDE man-hours are an investment, not revenue. I previously wrote about this difference in detail in What is the difference between FDEs and AI accompaniment support?, covering three points: what is sold, ownership of the deliverable, and responsibility for writing.

Why did it suddenly spread from 2025 to 2026?

The term FDE started appearing even in Japanese news only after the start of 2026. The reason is one thing: generative AI adoption has not reached production. A report published by MIT NANDA in July 2025 stated that despite an estimated 30 to 40 billion dollars of corporate investment in generative AI, 95% of organizations had not obtained measurable returns. The cause, it said, was not model performance but the lack of mechanisms to accumulate field feedback and improve on it 2. Even if something works in a meeting room, it stalls on the dirty data of the actual workplace. Once it stalls, the person in charge never touches it again. The demand for someone to bridge this gap is the backdrop behind the resurgence of the FDE.

Here is a timeline of milestones in the United States. On June 4, 2025, a16z's Joe Schmidt, in an essay titled "Trading Margin for Moat," called the FDE "the hottest job in startups." The gist was a business argument: enterprise AI cannot penetrate core business operations through self-service adoption, so companies should thicken implementation and temporarily lower gross margin in order to buy a moat that controls the entry points to data and business processes. The same piece noted that of OpenAI's 311 public job postings, 22 were FDE or solutions-related roles 3. On January 16, 2026, a16z's Marc Andrusko, in "The Palantirization of everything," noted that FDE job postings had "grown several hundred percent this year," while also issuing a warning: Palantir works because it has a real platform underneath, and imitating only the embedded-engineer part will produce "an unmaintainable mountain of bespoke implementations" 1.

In the spring of 2026, the model makers made their move. On May 4, financial infrastructure giant FIS announced that Anthropic's Applied AI team and FDEs would be embedded within FIS to jointly design AI agents for financial crime prevention. It was explicitly stated that the goal was not to stay permanently, but to hand over knowledge so FIS could run on its own 4. On May 11, OpenAI announced the establishment of the "OpenAI Deployment Company." It acquired applied AI consultancy Tomoro, starting with roughly 150 FDEs and implementation specialists from day one, with initial investment exceeding $4 billion 5. In the summer, a16z launched an 8-week "FDE Fellowship," with OpenAI FDEs, Cursor's head of AI adoption, and a former Palantir FDE lead lined up as founding fellows 6.

Japan also moved in the same year. On April 16, 2026, Accenture announced that, with Microsoft's cooperation, it would establish a specialized organization for "forward deployed engineering" in Japan. The core of the statement is this sentence: "The barrier to AI adoption in many companies lies not in a lack of technology, but in the fact that engineering expertise is not being fully exercised in the field." It states that they will assemble several thousand engineers with AI skills and build a structure to work directly with customers 7.

Here, let me add one sober note. FDEs are not a panacea. As a16z warns, sending engineers to customers without a product is simply high-rate contracting. The condition for the FDE model to work is that what is learned in the field flows back into the product, lowering deployment costs for the next customer. I believe you should be wary of companies calling themselves "FDE" without this feedback loop. If you want to check where your company stands, you can find out in a few minutes with the AI Literacy Diagnostic.

Why Our Company Openly Declares the FDE Style

Our company, TIMEWELL, builds "ZEROCK," which holds internal knowledge in a form agents can traverse, and "TRAFEED," an AI agent for export control, and runs "WARP," an AI-driven development program. And we openly declare that we run our business in the FDE style. I will write three reasons, explained so that first-time readers can understand.

First, our products are the kind that only start working once they enter the customer's field. To have AI traverse a company's internal knowledge, the nouns and verbs—what "customer" means at that company, at what point "shipment complete" occurs, and who experiences what when "delay" exceeds a threshold—must be put into a machine-readable form. You cannot figure this out in a conference room at headquarters. The same goes for export control: whether a part falls under regulations can only be judged by actually looking at the drawings, specifications, and the real items from trading partners. Because the structure does not allow us to simply sell the product and be done, the honest truth is that there is no way other than having engineers go into the field.

Second, we want to be on the 95% side. In projects I have been involved in, I have repeatedly seen agreements made on a polished demo, only to fall apart when faced with real field data. A demo in a conference room reveals only half the requirements. The other half only emerges when you put something working in front of the people in charge. That is why we produce code on day one. It is fine if it is incomplete. Once people see something working, they begin to talk about things that could never have been written in a blank specification sheet.

Third, we are at a scale where what is learned in the field can flow back into the product. Large companies' FDE organizations number in the thousands, but we are a small company. The advantage of being small is that the engineer in the field and the engineer building the product are the same person, or sit next to each other. An "exception unique to this company" found in the field on Friday is judged the following week as "this is a pattern other companies have too" and becomes a standard feature. I believe this speed of feedback is the rationale for a small company adopting the FDE style.

To be honest, the training and accompaniment offerings within our WARP are work closer to the "implementation consultant" in the table above. However, what we sell is not a report but a state in which participants themselves can build "something that works," and we operate that with the same philosophy as our products. The "building while listening" approach I wrote about in the first installment, The Technique of Building Mocks During Customer Interviews, is the field technique of FDE transplanted to new business development.

Seven Tips for Drawing Insights from the Field

Now for the main topic. The value of an FDE lies not in the speed of writing code, but in the technique of drawing out the "real requirements" from the field. I have organized this into seven points in a form usable even by non-engineers.

Tip 1: Sit at the field workstation, not in the conference room. In a conference room, people talk about the "ideal workflow." At the field workstation, you can see the "actual workflow." The first thing I ask is a single sentence: "Please let me watch you do your usual work, just as you always do." As you watch, undocumented steps always appear: exception rules written on sticky notes, hidden Excel columns, a separate screen opened to check with a supervisor. Most insights are not in the explanations, but in these "undocumented steps."

Tip 2: Touch real data on day one. Sample data lies. Something that works on a neatly arranged 100-row sample will always break on 100,000 rows of real data. That is why on day one, I ask to see the real thing, even if anonymized: the proportion of blank fields, inconsistencies in item names, mixed date formats, operations that differ by department. The mess itself is the requirement. You must not say here, "The data is messy, so let's clean it up first." If you wait for the cleanup work, the field will have moved three months ahead. Building something that works with the mess as-is is the FDE's job.

Tip 3: Show the working thing as a "questioning device." People cannot verbalize their own work when faced with a blank specification sheet. When they see something working, they can. "This case is different." "This screen wouldn't work in our field." That is why you put an incomplete working thing in front of them on day one and simply ask, "What's different?" If you ask, "What do you think?" you will be complimented and that will be the end. This technique can be used directly in new business interviews. In the first article, I wrote out the procedure and prompts as a "live mock."

Tip 4: Collect "when things go wrong," not "the everyday." Everyday operations are usually already running on existing systems. The room for an FDE to enter lies in the exceptions. "What was the most troubling issue last month?" "How was that issue ultimately handled?" "Who knows about that handling?" Exceptions are rules that exist only in that person's head, written in neither the system nor the manual. I create an "exception notebook" in the field. Once ten exceptions accumulate, you find that three of them are actually an "everyday" occurrence happening weekly. That is the first feature you should build.

Tip 5. Write down the company's nouns and verbs in the language of the front line. Customers, invoices, parts, workers. Issue a refund, place an order, rearrange shifts. Write down the nouns and verbs used at the company in front-line language, without translating them into system terminology. A single remark like "For us, 'reserving' and 'securing' mean different things" can change an entire design. This glossary becomes the prototype of the "structure of meaning" you will later teach the AI. In fancy terms, it's an ontology, but what you're actually doing is turning the company's own language into a dictionary.

Tip 6. Ask about the requirements of the three layers—management, division heads, and the front line—separately. The three layers want different things from the same project. Management says they want to change industry conventions, the division head says they want to cut this quarter's costs, and the front line doesn't want their current procedures changed. If you gather all three in the same meeting room, only management's voice survives. So ask them separately. Then look for a single point that satisfies all three at once: a point where "front-line procedures stay unchanged, this quarter's costs are cut, and next quarter you have an industry story to tell." Sometimes you won't find it. Knowing that you can't find it is also an insight.

Tip 7. Bring findings back weekly and return them to the product. Don't just fix what you found in the field on the spot and be done with it; once a week, bring it back to your own company and classify it. Is this a branch specific to this customer, or a pattern shared with other companies? If it's a pattern, make it a standard feature. If it's a branch, record it as a branch and keep watch so it doesn't multiply too much. If you don't make this judgment every week, six months later you'll have ten different things per customer that no one can maintain. What a16z called the "services trap" is the result of neglecting this weekly classification. The record of what you bring back is exactly the progress document I described in the third installment on spec-driven development.

What all seven have in common is an emphasis on "watching" over "asking," and on "having them operate something and capturing the reaction" over "having them talk." And what has changed in the AI era is the speed of building the "working thing" in Tip 3. Before, only a skilled engineer could produce code on day one. Now, with coding agents, a new-business owner can produce a mock screen in minutes. The field craft of the FDE is no longer a skill for engineers alone.

Preparation on the receiving company's side: don't lock the FDE in a meeting room

Finally, I write for the large enterprises on the "receiving side" who take in FDEs. Projects where an FDE arrives but produces no results share a common pattern: the pattern of locking them in a meeting room.

First, the seat. Provide a seat next to the front-line owner and a screen where they can see the actual system. If the FDE spends the day in a guest meeting room, all they can see is slides. Next, the data. Decide the anonymization procedure in advance so they can touch real data on day one. If you say "after the security review is done," three months disappear. You need a setup where the review proceeds in parallel and they work with anonymized data while it's underway.

Decide permissions in advance, too. Read-only, or write-back? If write-back, which actions, with whose approval, and how far can be automated? If you go live with this left ambiguous, erroneous orders and shipments will happen. Writes require four things: permissions, prior simulation, human involvement for critical decisions, and auditable records. This is not a technical matter; it's a decision-making matter for the receiving side.

Also secure the owner's time. An FDE learns by sitting next to the owner. If the owner keeps saying "I'm busy, later," the FDE has no choice but to build on guesswork, and you're back to meeting-room demos. A few hours a week is enough—secure the owner's time as a formal part of the project. And measure outcomes with business metrics. Not "95% accuracy," but "did a 3-hour task become 20 minutes?" and "how many rework items decreased?" If you measure with technical metrics, the evaluation ends before it ever reaches production.

Finally, don't demand requirements on paper. The moment you say "first, give us a requirements document," the FDE pattern breaks down. Requirements emerge after a working thing is placed in front of you. The paper is written afterward, as a record. Agree on this order in advance with your procurement and IT departments. When our company enters the field, we decide this preparation together at the first meeting. The structure of the program is described on the WARP page.

Summary

An FDE is an engineer who sits next to the customer and builds in order to get their own company's product running in production at the customer's site. What is sold is the product, the hours are an investment, and the learning flows back into the product. From 2025 to 2026, a16z, OpenAI, Anthropic, and even Accenture in Japan put people and money into this pattern. The reason is that 95% of generative AI never reaches production.

There are seven tips for drawing out insights: sit in the front-line seat, touch real data on day one, make the working thing a questioning device, collect exceptions, write down nouns and verbs in front-line language, ask the three layers' requirements separately, and bring findings back weekly to return them to the product. We publicly declare our FDE style because our product is the kind that doesn't work unless it enters the field, because we don't want to be in the 95%, and because we are at a scale where learning can flow back into the product.

If you want to act tomorrow, start by asking one person inside your company: "Please let me watch you do your usual work, just as you normally do." You will always find at least one procedure that was never explained. That is your first insight. If you'd like to design together, starting from entering the field, using one of your own business processes, let's talk via individual consultation.
