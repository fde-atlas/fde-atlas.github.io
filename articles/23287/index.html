Product Manager is one of the fastest-growing roles in demand in the Japanese IT industry in recent years. Both the "DX Report" published by METI (Ministry of Economy, Trade and Industry) and various surveys by IPA (Information-technology Promotion Agency) point to a serious shortage of digital talent, especially a lack of professionals who understand both business and technology and can determine product direction.

PdM is exactly that role, and they are in high demand everywhere—from SaaS companies and DX divisions of enterprises to startups. First, let's clarify the essence of the PdM role and how it differs from related roles that are often confused with it.

Limited to engineers with 5+ years of practical experience

High-rate, direct-contract project information

A PdM (Product Manager) is a professional whose ultimate goal is to maximize both "business value" and "user value" through the product. As the term "mini-CEO" suggests, they make all decisions related to the product (What, Why, When) and take full responsibility for its success while engaging the development team, sales, marketing, and management.

On the other hand, their scope of responsibility and KPIs differ significantly from the often-confused roles of "Project Manager (PjM)" and "Product Marketing Manager (PMM)."

RoleMain scope of responsibilityKGI/KPI examplesPerspective
PdM (Product Manager)Product planning, growth strategy, roadmap developmentMAU, retention rate, ARR, NPSMedium- to long-term business and user value
PjM (Project Manager)QCD (quality, cost, delivery) management of individual projectsOn-time delivery rate, budget consumption rate, progress rateShort-term execution and delivery
PMM (Product Marketing Manager)Product go-to-market strategy and positioningOrder rate, number of leads acquired, market shareMarket and customer touchpoints
PO (Product Owner)Backlog management and prioritization in ScrumVelocity, sprint goal achievement rateClose collaboration with the development team

To add a professional perspective, many Japanese companies operate with vague definitions like "PdM = PjM + development lead," and there is a constant stream of professionals who feel a mismatch after joining. The background behind the emergence of "PdMs in name only" is explained in detail in this article. When reading a job description (JD), it is extremely important for your career strategy to check "What are the KPIs?" and "Do they decide down to the How (implementation), or only the What (specifications)?"

Also, among those aiming to become PMs or PdMs, many compare and consider career paths such as Tech Lead or Engineering Manager (EM). To understand the differences in these roles, please also refer to this article explaining the differences between EMs and Tech Leads.

In the Japanese talent market, the number of PdM job openings has expanded significantly over the past five years, and salary ranges have risen as well. Looking at public job listings on major job-change services such as doda, Bizreach, and Levtech, the typical salary range for PdMs is 8 to 15 million yen, and offers exceeding 20 million yen are not uncommon at mega-ventures and foreign SaaS companies.

Behind companies' willingness to pay high rates for PdMs are the following structural factors.

  • Scaling of SaaS businesses: The domestic Japanese SaaS market is growing at a pace of around 20% per year (according to market research by Fuji Keizai Chimeras Research Institute, etc.), and professionals capable of continuously improving and expanding products have become the lifeline of business growth
  • Full-scale DX: When traditional companies launch digital businesses, there are very few professionals who can design and operate their own products from scratch
  • Scarcity of CTO/CPO talent: Product leadership-level talent (CPO, VPoP) is difficult to develop through internal promotion and training, forcing companies to rely on external hiring or external advisory contracts
  • Maturation of engineering organizations: As development productivity has improved, the importance of roles that decide "what to build" has relatively increased

Particularly noteworthy is that the market value of PdMs is "resistant to economic fluctuations." Even during periods when the engineering job market as a whole cools down, demand for professionals who can determine product direction remains solid; in fact, structural demand continues in the form of "we need excellent PdMs to achieve results with a small, elite team."

Conclusion: The expertise required of a PdM varies greatly depending on the product phase (0→1, 1→10, 10→100). By building a track record aligned with the phase, you can not only increase your salary as a full-time employee but also secure high-rate freelance engagements in the range of 800,000 to 1.5 million yen per month.

The most important perspective in a PdM career strategy is clarifying "which phase of product can I contribute to the most." Even with the same "PdM" title, the required behaviors, ways of thinking, and KPIs are completely different between the startup phase and the maturity phase.

Product growth phases are generally divided into three stages: "0→1 (pre-PMF)," "1→10 (rapid growth after PMF)," and "10→100 (scaling and maturity)." Below is an overview of the skills and KPIs required in each phase.

0→1 (Pre-PMF)Achieving product-market fit (PMF)Discovering customer problems, MVP design, hypothesis validation, fast decision-makingActivation rate, retention rate, number of customer interviews8–12 million yen/year / 800,000–1.2 million yen/month
1→10 (Growth phase)Rapid growth, feature expansion, and scaling the engineering teamProduct roadmap planning, data analysis, organizational management, hiringMAU/DAU, MRR growth rate, churn rate10–15 million yen/year / 1–1.4 million yen/month
10→100 (Scaling phase)Scalability and product portfolio expansionStrategy formulation, managing multiple products, partnerships, international expansionARR, LTV, CAC, market share12–25 million yen/year / 1.2–2 million yen/month

From a professional standpoint, one common misconception among aspiring PdMs is that "the 0→1 phase is the most difficult." While uncertainty is indeed high, it is actually the "1→10" phase that involves the greatest organizational and interpersonal complexity, and it is the area where excellent PdMs are in shortest supply.

This is because the 1→10 phase presents multifaceted challenges—"sustaining growth while simultaneously expanding the engineering, sales, and customer success organizations"—requiring decision-making grounded in an understanding of business, technology, and organization alike. For grasping the essence of running Scrum, the article on the Scrum Master role and going freelance is also a useful reference.

As many practitioners point out, the PdM skill set consists of three pillars: "Business (Biz)," "Technology (Tech)," and "User Experience (UX)." This is widely known as the "PM triangle (Venn diagram)" model proposed by Martin Eriksson of Productized.

Representative skills in each area are summarized below.

  • Biz（ビジネス）領域
    • Business strategy and business model design
    • Understanding financial metrics (ARR, MRR, LTV, CAC, unit economics)
    • Understanding marketing and sales processes
    • Partner alliances and contract negotiation
  • Tech（テクノロジー）領域
    • Basic understanding of software architecture
    • Ability to read API design and data modeling
    • Basic knowledge of cloud platforms (AWS, GCP, Azure)
    • Enough technical literacy to discuss technical matters with engineers
  • UX（ユーザーエクスペリエンス）領域
    • User interviews and customer journey design
    • Prototyping (Figma, etc.)
    • UI design fundamentals and understanding of design systems
    • Usability testing and behavioral analysis

You don't need to be perfect in every area. Rather, a realistic career strategy is to aim for a "T-shaped" or "π-shaped" skill profile: deep expertise in one or two areas, with a solid foundation in the rest.

As concrete steps for creating a skill map, we recommend the following five steps.

  • Self-assess each skill item in the three areas above on a 5-point scale (1: no experience to 5: can teach others)
  • Mark with a "★" the skills you actually used to produce output in past projects
  • Compare yourself with the public profiles (e.g., LinkedIn) of excellent PdMs in the same industry
  • Plan your learning for the next six months with a policy of "leveraging strengths" rather than "filling weaknesses"
  • Update it every three months and use it for project selection and career discussions

Conclusion: The most common failure in a PdM career is becoming an "order-taker (feature factory manager)" who simply implements stakeholder requests as-is. A track record of discovering users' true problems and driving business growth through data-driven decision-making is what determines your market value.

From the early to mid stages of a PdM career, many people face similar walls. Simply "knowing about these walls in advance" allows you to prepare countermeasures and dramatically changes how your market value grows. In this section, we explain the three walls that frequently occur in the field and the professional techniques for overcoming them.

The biggest challenge PdMs face is "stakeholder management," that is, coordinating multiple parties with differing interests. Specifically, being caught in the middle of conflicts like the following becomes an everyday occurrence.

  • Sales vs. Engineering: "I need this feature by tomorrow for a sales meeting" (sales) vs. "Considering quality and maintainability, we need two weeks" (engineering)
  • Management vs. Users: "Add more paid features to hit next quarter's ARR target" (management) vs. "We want better free features" (users)
  • Designers vs. Engineers: "This animation is essential" (designers) vs. "The implementation cost isn't worth it" (engineers)

If a PdM takes all of these head-on, burnout is inevitable. The fundamental principles of stakeholder management practiced by professional PdMs are as follows.

PrincipleSpecific Practices
Document your decision-making criteriaDocument the product vision, OKRs, and prioritization frameworks (RICE, ICE, etc.) and share them with everyone
Have grounds for saying NOAlways keep data, user research results, and competitive analysis at hand, and argue with logic rather than emotion
Adjust expectations proactivelyBefore being told "I want this done," proactively present plans like "In the next sprint we'll do A; B will come next month or later"
Present Win-Win optionsInstead of "A or B," present "the trade-offs of A, B, and C respectively" and let stakeholders choose
Keep recordsRecord all decisions in a Decision Log to prevent later "he said, she said" disputes

Particularly important is "documenting your decision-making criteria." The biggest reason PdMs burn out is being asked to argue from scratch every single time. If the product vision, prioritization criteria, and KPIs are documented upfront, discussions shift from "which criteria should we use" to "how does this fit the established criteria," greatly reducing emotional conflict.

For PdMs from business backgrounds, the biggest hurdle is how to earn "technical credibility with the development team." A PdM perceived as technically weak gets labeled by engineers as "someone who makes unreasonable demands" or "someone who doesn't understand reality," effectively losing decision-making authority over the product.

Practical approaches for earning technical credibility are summarized below.

  • Solidly master fundamental terminology: Understand the meaning and background of terms engineers use daily, such as API, repository, branch, CI/CD, database normalization, latency, and throughput
  • Ability to read architecture diagrams: Interpret system architecture diagrams, ER diagrams, and sequence diagrams, and understand "what impact a change in one place will have elsewhere"
  • Ability to ask about "implementation difficulty": When submitting requirements, be able to honestly ask "How difficult is this?" or "Is there a better way to do this?"
  • Understanding technical debt: Understand the trade-off between "code that works in the short term" and "maintainable code," and respect sprints dedicated to refactoring and resolving technical debt
  • Understanding code review discussions: Read pull request and code review discussions to grasp the context of technical decisions

A common failure among PdMs from business backgrounds is "trying to act like an engineer." Jumping into technical discussions with half-baked knowledge actually erodes trust. Rather, clearly taking the stance of "I don't understand the technical details, but I take responsibility for judging business value and user value" earns more respect from professional engineers.

When building technical literacy, foundational knowledge of AI and machine learning will become increasingly important going forward. For the cutting edge of LLM and MLOps implementation, referring to the article on LLM engineer skills and careers will sharpen the resolution of your discussions with development teams.

The most important capability that determines a PdM's market value is "data-driven decision-making." A PdM who can make decisions based on data and hypotheses—rather than on intuition like "this just seems good"—contributes to business growth at a completely different level.

The five steps for practicing data-driven hypothesis testing and decision-making are as follows.

  • 課題の特定
    • Combine quantitative data (product metrics and funnel analysis) with qualitative data (user interviews and customer support inquiries) to identify bottlenecks and growth inhibitors
  • 仮説の設定
    • Set a testable hypothesis using the format: "If we do X, the metric Y will improve by Z%, because W"
  • 検証方法の設計
    • Choose the method that verifies the hypothesis at minimal cost, such as A/B testing, limited releases to beta users, or prototype testing
    • Calculate in advance the sample size and duration needed to achieve statistical significance
  • 実行とモニタリング
    • Decide in advance the "criteria for stopping early" during the testing period (e.g., stop if negative impact appears for three consecutive days)
    • Monitor not only the primary KPIs but also guardrail metrics (churn rate and error rate) at the same time
  • 学習と次への反映
    • Dig deeper especially when results differ from expectations: articulating "why the hypothesis missed" improves the accuracy of the next hypothesis
    • Document the results as a Decision Log or RFC and share them within the organization

Diligently repeating these steps is the surest way to continuously increase your market value as a PdM. Running 20-30 hypothesis-testing cycles in one year accumulates 60-90 testing results over three years. This becomes a powerful, differentiating track record that other PdMs cannot easily match.

Conclusion: In addition to the path of aiming for CPO (Chief Product Officer) or VPoP within a company, the option of becoming a "freelance PdM (external CPO or product advisor)" who offers their expertise to multiple companies is rapidly increasing. People who can craft advanced product strategies are extremely rare, and going independent allows you to achieve both exceptionally high rates and a flexible working style.

After building 5-10 years of career as a PdM, two paths come into view: stepping up within an organization (VPoP or CPO), or going independent and working as a freelancer. Neither is inherently the right answer, but in recent years more senior professionals are choosing the latter, and it is attracting particular attention as a career strategy for those in their 40s and beyond.

Let me organize the differences between the two based on real-world conditions.

The biggest advantage of being an in-house CPO/VPoP is the potential for "capital gains on the scale of hundreds of millions of yen at IPO or exit" through stock options. On the other hand, this is inversely related to risk (a major opportunity loss if the company falters).

The advantages of being a freelance PdM are being able to diversify income across multiple projects, dramatically broadening the range of experience, and being able to keep working at high rates into one's 40s and beyond while balancing health, family, and one's main work. The drawback is that you are responsible for acquiring projects yourself (sales, contracts, and taxes), but these can be greatly mitigated by using a reliable agent.

In particular, in recent years, more high-level PdMs are practicing parallel careers, such as "serving as CPO at an operating company 3-4 days a week and as a product advisor for another company the remaining 1-2 days." In Japan, where job mobility is low, this is an extremely flexible and aggressive career strategy.

To land high-rate projects as a freelance PdM, carefully crafting your resume (rirekisho) and portfolio is extremely important. I will explain the PdM-specific writing points that differ from a typical engineer's resume.

Five elements that must be included in your resume

  • 担当プロダクトの概要と事業規模
    • Always include numbers that demonstrate business impact, such as product overview, user count, ARR scale, and competitive position
  • 担当フェーズの明示
    • Clearly state whether your work was 0-to-1, 1-to-10, or 10-to-100. This makes it easier for clients to match your experience with their product phase
  • 定量実績
    • Always show the KPIs of your results as numbers, such as "improved MAU by 40% in six months," "grew ARR 2.5x in one year," or "reduced churn rate from 15% to 8%"
  • 意思決定プロセスの記述
    • Briefly describe "why you made that decision" and "what hypothesis you held and how you tested it." PdMs who can articulate their process are rare and tend to be highly valued
  • チーム構成と役割
    • State the scale of your management, such as "leading a team of 5 engineers, 2 designers, and 1 QA"

Content to include in your portfolio

  • Past product roadmaps (to the extent publicly shareable)
  • Research reports (excerpts of user interview results and competitive analysis)
  • Summaries of A/B test results
  • Conference presentations and published articles
  • Product-related essays on note or Medium

What agents value most is not "a mere list of achievements" but whether you demonstrate "a reproducible thought process." Writing that makes clients feel "if placed in the same situation, this person could deliver the same results again" is the key to securing high rates.

In this article, we have explained product manager (PdM) career strategies based on real-world standards, covering the essence of the role, skills by phase, common failure patterns in the field, and going independent as a freelancer. Here are the key points to remember once again.

  • A PdM is a "mini CEO" responsible for a product's success, with clearly different responsibilities and KPIs from PjMs, PMMs, and POs
  • The required skills differ greatly depending on the product phase (0→1, 1→10, 10→100), so it is important to clarify which phase you can contribute to
  • Combining the three pillars of business, technology, and UX with expertise in a specific business domain dramatically increases your market value
  • Three things determine a PdM's market value: stakeholder management, earning technical trust, and data-driven decision-making
  • In addition to stepping up to an in-house CPO/VPoP, options to go independent as a freelance PdM (external CPO or product advisor) are rapidly increasing
  • Monthly rates for freelance PdMs range from ¥1 million to ¥2 million, and domain PdMs in fields such as finance, healthcare, HR, and legal in particular tend to command high rates

The PdM role is a rare profession whose demand will actually expand in the AI era. Even in an age when AI can write code, the skills to decide "what to build" and "why to build it" and to drive execution across an organization are extremely difficult to replace, and their value will only continue to grow.

Going independent as a freelancer is a powerful career strategy for making the most of such rare expertise. Its greatest appeal is that you can achieve high rates and a flexible working style while gaining diverse experience across multiple projects. If you are a PdM who feels "I want to go independent someday" or "I see no further growth opportunities in my current organization," why not start by objectively taking stock of your skills and experience to accurately understand your market value?
