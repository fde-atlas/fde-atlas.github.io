What an FDE Owns From Presales to Renewal

FDE Times · 2026-10-08 · 7 min read

Summary

This article maps what a forward deployed engineer owns across a contract lifecycle. Whether FDEs join presales varies by company: PostHog's handbook excludes it, while a Pluto job post includes it. After signature, the FDE owns the technical outcome within a scoped engagement, turning symptom-level requests into measurable briefs with explicit out-of-scope lines. Success is measured by production usage and product feedback, not demos. Customer Success owns renewal, but renewal evidence depends on what the FDE built and handed over. The author lists four common mistakes and recommends rewriting CVs around engagement outcomes with real numbers rather than tech stacks.

Key points

  • Presales involvement differs by company; check the job description.
  • Post-signature, the FDE owns the technical outcome within a scoped brief.
  • Done means daily production usage, not a clean demo.
  • CS owns renewal, but relies on the FDE's adoption evidence and handover.
  • CVs should show owned outcomes with real numbers, not tools used.

Whether FDEs join presales varies by company, so read the JD closely; from signing to renewal, every stage has its own deliverable.

In brief

  • The presales boundary varies by company: PostHog does not put FDEs on presales, while a Pluto job post does.
  • After signing, the FDE owns the technical outcome: a system running in production and feedback carried back to the product team.
  • Customer Success owns the relationship and the renewal, but the evidence that persuades a customer to renew comes from their use of what the FDE built.

Picture a Monday morning. An account executive messages you: “Deal’s signed, you’re on-site with the customer next week.” The customer is a logistics company that wants an agent to read import documents. You open the contract and find a single line on scope: “automate document processing”.

The first question is not which model to use. You need to know who owns what between now and the customer’s renewal decision: what is yours, what belongs to sales, and what belongs to Customer Success. Get it wrong and there are two likely outcomes: either you take over the whole customer relationship, or you build the thing and disappear.

The skill here is knowing what you must deliver at each stage of the contract. With that clear, you work more smoothly with sales and CS, pick the right roles when reading job descriptions, and write the kind of CV FDE recruiters want to read.

Presales: some companies give it to FDEs, some do not

The first thing to check when reading a job description is whether the FDE steps in before the signature, because that varies by company. PostHog’s handbook states that an FDE engagement begins where pre-sales ends, and its FDEs currently do not do presales; Kunal Ganglani likewise places the FDE in the post-signature phase, separate from the solutions engineer.

A Pluto FDE job post, however, asks for direct work with customers across both pre-sales and post-sales engagements. That role follows the customer from proof of concept, through pilot, to getting the system into production.

So assume nothing when reading a job description. Look for the phrases “pre-sales”, “proof of concept”, “pilot” and “post-sale implementation”, then ask directly in the interview: do FDEs take part before the deal is signed, and if so, what is the deliverable at that stage?

If the role does include presales, what you own is an honest POC. It should prove exactly what the customer wants to see, and record the assumptions that have not yet been tested, so that whoever handles implementation (sometimes you) does not have to guess again from scratch.

After the signature, you own the technical outcome

Once the deal is signed, you own the technical outcome end to end, as Palantir’s job post describes the FDSE role, but within a clearly bounded scope. PostHog calls these scoped engagements, aimed at unblocking adoption or expansion.

“Scoped” is the crux. Underdog.io observes that customers rarely describe the root cause; they describe symptoms. “Automate document processing” is a symptom. Your first job is to turn it into a measurable requirement.

Back to the logistics customer. Suppose that after two days sitting with the data-entry staff, you find most of their time goes on typing invoices and packing lists from two large suppliers into the ERP by hand. The brief you send back to the customer and to sales can be as short as this:

ENGAGEMENT BRIEF — Document-reading agent
Problem: manual entry of invoices into ERP
Scope: invoices + packing lists, 2 large suppliers
Out of scope: customs declarations
Output: agent writes drafts to ERP, a person approves
Measure: documents/day through agent, manual-correction rate
After handover: CS + customer's IT team

Before finalising the brief, ask three questions in the same meeting. Does the ERP have an API for writing data, or does it have to go through a file import? Who grants access, and how long does that take? On the customer’s side, who will approve the drafts each day?

The “Out of scope” line is the most valuable line in the brief. It locks the scope of the engagement, and it gives sales a ready answer when the customer asks about customs declarations: that is a follow-on engagement, not extra work to squeeze in.

The measure is production, not the demo

Stackmatix draws a clear line between two kinds of metric. FDEs are measured by a system running in production and the feedback they bring back to the product team. Customer Success is measured by retention, expansion and NRR.

Recruiting from Scratch describes FDEs as typically embedded with customers for anywhere from a few weeks to a few months, handling deployment and customisation and keeping the system running smoothly after the sale. In other words, a demo that runs cleanly in a meeting room does not count as done. Done is when the data-entry staff use the agent every morning without you sitting beside them.

Product feedback is the other half of the measure. Suppose both suppliers send skewed scanned PDFs and the agent keeps misreading the quantity column. You patch it for this customer, but you must also write it up as a clear ticket for the product team, because the next customer is quite likely to hit the same problem.

Renewal is not your job, but it depends on you

At many companies, CS and TAMs own the long-term relationship, track account health and handle renewal. Even so, adoption, satisfaction and renewal often still appear on the FDE’s scorecard.

There is no contradiction here. An FDE’s success directly affects renewal rates and expansion opportunities: CS sits at the renewal table, but what they bring with them is evidence that the customer is using the system you built.

That makes the handover a deliverable, not a formality. When the work is done, the FDE hands the relationship back along with a record of what was built, what the customer should do next and the follow-on work.

For the logistics customer, the handover note should include the number of documents a day going through the agent, the skewed-PDF ticket, and customs declarations flagged as a candidate for the next engagement.

Four mistakes that let an engagement slip away

The first is hoarding the relationship. If, after go-live, the customer still calls you about everything, CS loses sight of the account’s health and you have no time for the next customer.

The second is a brief with no “Out of scope” line. Scope creeps a little with every meeting, and by week five you are halfway through three things, none of them in production.

The third is measuring by demo rather than by usage. A smooth demo reassures everyone, but it tells CS nothing about whether the customer actually uses the system every day.

The fourth is handing over nothing but a repo, with no next steps for the customer. CS then walks into the renewal meeting empty-handed, and nobody proposes the follow-on engagements.

Rewrite your CV around the lifecycle, not the tech stack

FDE recruiters read a CV looking for signs that you have owned an outcome. A line such as “Built an LLM-based document-processing agent for a logistics customer” only says you can use the tools.

For the hypothetical example above, a rewrite might read: “Led a 6-week engagement at a logistics customer: scoped to 2 document types, put the agent into production in week 4, 70% of invoices from the 2 main suppliers now go through the agent, handed over to CS with 3 follow-on items.” The numbers must be your real ones, but the shape of the sentence stays the same.

If you have never worked on-site with a customer, you can still apply this frame to an internal project: who the users are, how long it took to reach production, how many people actually use it, and who took over the handover. The last two are where developers’ CVs are usually blank.

This week’s exercise: take the project you are working on and write a seven-line engagement brief like the one above, with mandatory “Out of scope” and “After handover” lines. If you cannot fill in the last line, you have just discovered who will get the call when the system breaks.
