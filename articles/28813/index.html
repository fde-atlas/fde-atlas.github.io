Approval Gates for AI Agents: When to Keep Humans in the Loop

Original title: Agent 审批关卡：何时保留人工审批

FDE Times · 2026-10-08 · 3 min read

Key Takeaways

  • The arXiv paper "The Last Human Gate" (September 24, 2026) proposes replacing the tasks at governance gates with agents, rule engines, and evidence services, connected by an FDE.
  • Data from 300 synthetic projects, 899 runs, and 135 repeated runs separates "decision correctness" from "execution reliability"; one counterexample shows that some gates cannot be decided on with the information available.
  • Frontline practitioners use a simpler rule: remove any gate with a 95% same-day approval rate and a 0% modification rate; keep any gate for tasks you wouldn't let a new hire do without checking first.

On September 24, Jeremy Canale published a paper on arXiv with a thought-provoking title: "The Last Human Gate." It is quite technical: it proposes a framework that replaces every task at a governance gate with agents, rule engines, and evidence services, and the person responsible for connecting it all is the Forward Deployed Engineer (FDE).

The question the paper raises is one customers will throw at you in the first working meeting: which steps still need a human signature, and which can be handed to machines? Lean one way, and the agent gets stuck in the approval queue; lean the other way, and high-risk operations execute without anyone noticing.

So the paper's value lies less in the framework itself than in the fact that it separates two things that are often conflated.

The evals use DGF-Bench, which contains 300 synthetic projects and 899 scorable runs, where each run is one model processing one project. The paper also adds evidence audits and 135 repeated runs to distinguish "decision correctness" from "execution reliability."

Getting it right once isn't enough for an agent—it has to get it right on run 135 too.

Some Gates Can't Be Removed Yet, and That's Good News

The most notable detail is a limitation the paper itself acknowledges: a document counterexample shows that some gates cannot be decided on with the information available. At such gates, the problem lies neither in the agent nor in the rule engine, but in the input data itself being insufficient to automate that step.

These gates can't be removed yet, and the FDE's job is to identify them before anyone promises a customer "full automation."

Frontline practitioners look at the same problem more simply. Practitioner Alejandro Rioja writes on his personal blog that he reviews his approval queue once a month and removes any gate with a 95% same-day approval rate and a 0% modification rate.

If no one approves within the SLA, Rioja removes the gate as well and switches to audit logs. A gate where reviewers just click "approve" protects no one; it only slows the agent down.

His criterion for whether to keep a gate is equally simple: if you wouldn't want a new employee to do this task without checking first, the agent needs a gate. Airia, a vendor in this space, has turned the same idea into policy: approval authority should match the risk level of the operation. Still, vendor advice should be taken with caution.

The FDE's Job Is to Turn Criteria into Code

The point Airia gets most right is also the starting point of the FDE's work: the conditions that trigger a gate must be machine-readable and enforced at the execution layer, not just written in a prompt. A line in a system prompt saying "ask a human before deleting data" is not a gate.

A gate is a piece of code: it blocks the operation from executing until the sign-off is obtained, and records who signed and when.

What Canale's paper hands to the FDE is precisely the work of connecting agents, rule engines, and evidence services.

From an implementer's perspective, this connection work comes down to two questions: what evidence is sufficient for each gate, and whether the agent can reliably repeat the same decision. These are exactly the two things the paper's evals measure.

A ZenML case study about Factory describes the same shift. The FDEs there don't do professional services; their role has moved from directly operating software to maintaining and managing the systems that produce software.

For developers who want this role, whether in Vietnam or elsewhere, repeatedly practicing prompts is practicing the wrong thing. Instead, find an internal workflow with an approval step, track its same-day approval rate and modification rate for a month, then write the trigger conditions as policy code rather than as instructions.

On a resume, a line like "removed three rubber-stamp gates based on data and kept two high-risk gates" is more convincing than any agent certification.

The last gate won't disappear; only the gatekeeper changes: from the person who clicks "approve" to the person who decides which gates are worth someone clicking.
