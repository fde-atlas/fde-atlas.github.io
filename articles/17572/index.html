The Rise of the AI Engineer: The Birth of a New Engineering Role

Original title: AI Engineer 的崛起：一个新工程角色的诞生

FDE中国 · 2023-06-30 · 12 min read

Summary

This is the origin text of the term "AI Engineer" (Latent Space, mid-2023). Author swyx argues that API-based foundation models have split application-layer engineering from research, creating a role that will soon outnumber ML engineers. Key claims: application engineers should own product-specific data and evals rather than act as half-researchers; prompt-based prototyping cuts product validation costs by 1000-10000x; and Software 3.0 re-establishes human-written code for orchestration and verification. The piece cites a supply gap of roughly 5,000 LLM researchers versus 50 million software engineers, lists challenges across models, tooling, and research, and announces the first AI Engineer Summit. Note for readers: 2023 salary and funding figures need recalibration, and the app/delivery layer split is blurrier in China.

Key points

  • Origin document of the "AI Engineer" title, traceable to today's hiring and community usage.
  • Application-layer engineers should treat product data and evals as core work, not research.
  • Prompt-first prototyping makes AI product validation 1000-10000x cheaper.
  • Software 3.0 restores human-written code's role in orchestration and verification.
  • Suggested action: audit skills against models, tools, and research; invest in evals next.

The problem this piece addresses: as the barrier to building AI applications plummets, how should software engineers understand the boundaries and opportunities of the new identity called "AI Engineer"? The three most valuable takeaways are three sets of judgments. First, the divide between the application layer and the research layer—engineers on the other side of the API should treat product-specific data and evals as their core job, not see themselves as half researchers. Second, "shoot first, aim later"—prompt-based prototyping lowers the cost of product validation by several orders of magnitude; this is agility for the AI era. Third, the return of code—Software 3.0 does not eliminate human-written code, but re-establishes the importance of orchestration and verification. The easiest misreading is to equate AI Engineer with prompt tricks; the article makes clear that evals, data, and toolchains are the real professional moat. Chinese readers should read this because it is the founding document of the term "AI Engineer"—the job title used today in domestic hiring and community discussions can all be traced back to it. Two things must be layered onto the domestic context: the job market's division between the "application layer" and the "delivery layer" is blurrier than in Silicon Valley, and the salary and funding figures cited in this 2023 article now need to be recalibrated to current conditions. One recommended action after reading: go through the article's checklist of challenges (models, tooling, research) to audit your own skill gaps, and list evals capability as your next investment. — FDEChina Editorial Team · Practitioners

We are witnessing a once-in-a-generation "shift right" in applied AI, fueled by the emergent capabilities of Foundation Models and their availability via open source/APIs.

The kinds of AI tasks that in 2013 took five years and an entire research team can now be done in 2023 with an API doc and a free afternoon.

Important caveat: the API line is permeable—AI Engineers can move left into fine-tuning and hosting models, and research engineers can move right to build on top of APIs! This diagram has also been criticized for the placement of evals and data; we fully agree that evals are an important part of the job! Machine Learning Researchers/Machine Learning Engineers (MLR/MLE) handle things at the foundation model level—that is, pretraining-scale data and general benchmark evals; but AI Engineers should of course treat product-specific data and evals as their own core responsibility.

"In terms of numbers, there will likely be significantly more AI Engineers than machine learning engineers / LLM engineers. One can be very successful in this role without ever training anything from scratch." — Andrej Karpathy

However, the devil is in the details—on the road to successfully evaluating, applying, and productizing AI, the challenges keep coming:

  • Models: from evals of the largest GPT-4 and Claude to the smallest open source Huggingface, LLaMA, and other models
  • Tooling: from the most popular chaining, retrieval, and vector search tools like LangChain, LlamaIndex, and Pinecone, to the emerging field of autonomous agents like Auto-GPT and BabyAGI (Lilian Weng wrote a must-read survey)
  • Research/Progress: on top of all this, the number of papers, models, and techniques published each day is growing exponentially as attention and funding pour in, to the point that keeping up with it all is nearly a full-time job.

I take that statement seriously and literally. I believe it is a full-time job. I believe software engineering will give rise to a new subdiscipline dedicated to applying AI and effectively navigating this emerging tech stack, just as "Site Reliability Engineer (SRE)," "DevOps Engineer," "Data Engineer," and "Analytics Engineer" emerged in succession.

Of the various names taking shape for this role, the least awkward one seems to be: AI Engineer.

Every startup I know has some kind of #discuss-ai Slack channel. These channels evolve from informal groups into formal teams—Amplitude, Replit, and Notion have already done so. The thousands of software engineers pushing AI APIs and open source models into production—whether on company time or nights and weekends, whether in a company Slack or an indie hacker's Discord—will professionalize and converge on one title: AI Engineer. This is likely to be the most in-demand engineering job of the decade.

From the largest companies like Microsoft and Google, to frontier startups like Figma (via the acquisition of Diagram), Vercel (e.g., Hassan El Mghari's viral RoomGPT), and Notion (e.g., Notion AI by Ivan Zhao and Simon Last), to indie hackers like Simon Willison, Pieter Levels (creator of Photo/InteriorAI), and Riley Goodside (now at Scale AI), AI Engineers are everywhere. Someone does prompt engineering at Anthropic for $300K a year; someone does software at OpenAI for $900K. They spend weekends heads-down building ideas at AGI House and swap notes on /r/LocalLLaMA. What they have in common: shaping AI progress into real products used by millions of people, almost overnight.

Not a single PhD in sight. To build AI products, you want engineers, not researchers.

The "changing of the guard" between AI Engineer and ML Engineer

I am naming a trend that has already happened, not starting one. On Indeed, there are 10 times as many machine learning engineer jobs as AI Engineer jobs, but the faster growth of "AI" leads me to predict that this ratio will flip within 5 years.

All job titles are flawed, but some are useful. We are wary of and weary of the endless semantic squabbles between AI and ML, and we know that an ordinary "software engineer" role is fully capable of building AI software. But a question on Hacker News titled "How to transition into AI engineering" shows that a deeply rooted perception still exists in the market:

Most people still view AI engineering as some form of machine learning or data engineering, and thus recommend the same prerequisite paths. But I guarantee that few of the highly effective AI Engineers named above have done coursework equivalent to Andrew Ng's Coursera course, know PyTorch, or can explain the difference between a Data Lake and a Data Warehouse.

In the near future, no one will suggest you start learning AI engineering by reading "Attention is All You Need," just as you wouldn't learn to drive by studying the blueprints of the Ford Model T. Of course, understanding fundamentals and history always helps, and it can indeed help you discover ideas and efficiency/capability gains that haven't yet entered mainstream awareness. But sometimes you can simply start using the products and understand their nature through experience.

I don't expect this "changing of the guard" in curricula to happen overnight. Padding résumés, filling in boxes on market maps, and invoking more esoteric topics to signal authority is human nature. In other words, for a long time, prompt engineering and AI engineering will seem inferior in the eyes of people with solid data science/ML backgrounds. But I believe the pure economics of supply and demand will ultimately prevail.

Why the AI Engineer is emerging right now

If this role was destined to emerge, why now? The answer is not a single cause but multiple conditions maturing within the same time window: model capabilities spilling over beyond the research community, compute and talent being packaged into rentable services, developer workflows inverting from "collect data first, then model" to "prototype first, backfill data later," the tooling ecosystem breaking out of the Python-only community, and application forms diverging completely from the previous generation of machine learning. The following five reasons each independently push up the demand for AI Engineers.

Foundation models are "few-shot learners"

Foundation models are "few-shot learners," exhibiting in-context learning and even zero-shot transfer capabilities, with a generalization range that exceeds the original intent of the people who trained them. In other words, the people who created the models don't fully know what they can do. People who are not LLM researchers can discover and exploit these capabilities simply by spending more time with the models and applying them to domains underestimated by the research community (e.g., Jasper for copywriting).

Research talent is "rented out via the cloud"

Microsoft, Google, Meta, and the major foundation model labs have pooled scarce research talent, essentially delivering "AI research as a service" via APIs. You can't hire them, but you can rent them—provided you have software engineers on the other end of the API who know how to work with them. There are roughly 5,000 LLM researchers in the world, but about 50 million software engineers. This supply constraint dictates that an intermediary class of "AI Engineers" must rise to meet demand.

GPU hoarding

Of course, OpenAI/Microsoft was the first to do this, but Stability AI's emphasis on owning a 4,000-GPU cluster ignited the startup GPU arms race. Remember October 2022?

Since then, it has become the norm for new startups like Inflection ($1.3 billion), Mistral ($113 million), Reka ($58 million), Poolside ($26 million), and Contextual ($20 million) to raise huge seed rounds to own their own hardware. Dan Gross and Nat Friedman even announced Andromeda—a $100 million, 10-exaflop GPU cluster dedicated to startups in their investment portfolio. The global chip shortage is reflexively creating more shortages. On the other side of the API line, AI Engineers will have vastly more compute to use models rather than train them.

Fire first, aim later

The traditional approach: data scientists/machine learning engineers first run a laborious data collection campaign, then train a domain-specific model, then put it into production. Now, product managers/software engineers can first prompt an LLM to build out and validate a product idea, and only later gather specific data for fine-tuning.

If the latter group is 100-1000x larger than the former, and the "fire first, aim later" prompt-based LLM prototyping workflow makes you 10-100x faster than traditional ML, then an AI Engineer can validate an AI product 1000-10000x more cheaply. This is the waterfall vs. agile story playing out again. AI is agile.

Python + JavaScript

The data/AI field has traditionally been extremely Python-centric, and the earliest AI engineering tools—LangChain, LlamaIndex, and Guardrails—came out of that same community. However, the number of JavaScript developers is at least on par with Python, so tooling is now catering to this vastly expanded audience: from LangChain.js and Transformers.js to Vercel's newly launched AI SDK. The addressable market (TAM) and opportunity have expanded by at least 100%.

Generative AI vs. classifier ML

The term "generative AI" is already a bit stale, giving way to analogies like "reasoning engine," but it still succinctly captures the distinction between the existing MLOps tooling and ML practitioner population, and the emerging, distinctly different profile of people best at wielding LLMs and text-to-image models. The previous generation of ML cared about fraud risk, recommender systems, anomaly detection, and feature stores, while AI Engineers are building writing apps, personalized learning tools, natural language spreadsheets, and Factorio-like visual programming languages.

Whenever a subgroup has a completely different background, speaks a different language, produces a completely different set of products, and uses a completely different set of tools, they will eventually split off into their own tribe.

Putting these five reasons together, you can see why the emergence of this role is almost irreversible: the spillover of model capabilities provides the possibility, compute and APIs turn scarce research into broadly accessible supply, the workflow inversion lowers the barrier to entry, the language ecosystem expands the candidate talent pool, and the difference in application forms gives this group an identity of its own. With supply, demand, and organizational identity all in place, it's only a matter of time.

1+2=3: The role of code—from Software 2.0 to Software 3.0

Six years ago, Andrej Karpathy wrote a hugely influential long-form piece describing Software 2.0: on one side, the "classic stack" of hand-written programming languages that model logic precisely; on the other, the new "machine learning" stack of neural networks that approximate logic, enabling software to solve problems far beyond what humans can model by hand. By 2023, he pointed out that the hottest new programming language is English, finally filling in the gray area left blank in his original diagram—moving from Software 2.0 toward something... considerably broader.

Update: Andrej responded! With a bit of disagreement, too!

Last year, when people started putting GPT-3 and Stable Diffusion to work, prompt engineering was the popular answer to "how will work change." People mocked AI startups as "OpenAI wrappers" and worried that LLM applications could easily be compromised by prompt injection and reverse prompt engineering. Where's the moat?

But one of the biggest themes of 2023 has been precisely re-establishing the role of human-written code in orchestrating and substituting for LLM capabilities: from the $200-million-plus-valued behemoth LangChain, to the enormous importance of code generation and reuse demonstrated by Voyager, backed by NVIDIA (I recently attended an online seminar with Harrison on Chains vs. Agents, expanding on this "Code Core vs. LLM Core" thesis for applications).

The most fundamental architectural divide is: "software on top of intelligence" vs. "software made intelligent."

Prompt engineering is both overhyped and here to stay, and the return of the Software 1.0 paradigm within Software 3.0 applications is both a huge source of opportunity/chaos and a blank space left for a whole wave of startups—there's no VC who doesn't draw market maps.

Of course, it won't only be human-written code. My recent tinkering with smol-developer, the more ambitious gpt-engineer, and code-generating agents like Codium AI, Codegen.ai, and Morph/Rift will increasingly enter the AI Engineer's toolbox. As human engineers learn to wield AI, AI will increasingly do the engineering, until some distant day when we suddenly find we can no longer tell them apart.

We are builders

Sharp readers will notice that we have gradually been steering the Latent Space podcast and newsletter toward serving the AI Engineer persona. What excites me most about serving this audience is the combination of technological optimism and pragmatism. Marc Andreessen recently wrote that the vast majority of public AI discourse is "hysterical fear and paranoia," pointing out that there is an entire class of people paid to be doomers with job titles like "AI safety expert," "AI ethicist," and "AI risk researcher," yet no corresponding class of builders and accelerators. At the other end of the spectrum, there are many unserious accelerationists and insufferable "singularity bros" who spend all day on Twitter talking about distant utopian futures without being able to articulate what they are doing to make them a reality.

The AI Engineer will tame and ride the Shoggoth (the Cthulhu-like metaphor for large models).

Let's make this happen.

Summary and next steps

Looking back at this article written in mid-2023, its predictions for the following three years largely came true: AI Engineer became a real and continuously expanding job title in the hiring market, evals and data were recognized as core work of the application layer, and "the role of code in AI applications" became a central architectural topic. For Chinese readers, the three most actionable takeaways are: first, the supply-demand gap at the application layer is far larger than at the research layer, so getting started doesn't have to begin with reading papers; second, the "fire, then aim" prompt-based prototyping workflow lowers validation costs by several orders of magnitude and is worth adopting immediately by every team; third, evals and data pipelines are the professional moat that distinguishes AI Engineers from "people who tweak prompts."
