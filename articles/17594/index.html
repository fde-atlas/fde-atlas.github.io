The Difference Between FDEs and Product Managers and Where Their Responsibilities End

Original title: FDE 和产品经理的区别与职责边界

FDE Times · 2026-10-21 · 6 min read

Summary

This guide clarifies the boundary between FDEs and product managers, using OpenAI's job posting for a Deployed Product Manager for Codex as its anchor. FDEs own the technical "how" of making deployments work; PMs—especially forward deployed product managers embedded at customer accounts—own the "what" and "why" and are accountable for account outcomes. The author proposes a practical workflow: split customer issues into two columns, log tagged work items to detect whether problems recur across accounts, and send structured feedback (blocker, impact, workaround, recurrence count, product suggestion) back to product teams. It also warns against turning workarounds into unmaintained customer-specific products and against vague feedback lacking data.

Key points

  • FDEs own implementation; PMs decide what to build and why.
  • OpenAI is hiring a Deployed Product Manager for Codex.
  • Tagged work items reveal whether issues recur across accounts.
  • Structured feedback should include blocker, impact, workaround, and suggestion.
  • Avoid workarounds becoming unmaintained single-customer products.

Key Takeaways

  • A traditional PM owns a product; a Forward Deployed PM owns outcomes for a single customer account, while the technical "how" belongs to the FDE
  • OpenAI is hiring a Deployed Product Manager for Codex to work closely with deployment engineers, turning customer blockers into clear product feedback
  • Whether or not there's a deployed product manager, FDEs need to shorten the feedback loop from customer to product — a skill worth putting on your resume

Imagine you're in week three of a deployment. The integration is working and data is flowing. Then the customer's tech lead asks: "Why can't your product do this?" You have two options: write a few hundred lines of code yourself to close the gap, or say "I'll pass this along to the product team."

Either answer could be right — or wrong. To know which, you need to understand where the Forward Deployed Engineer's (FDE) responsibilities end and the Product Manager's (PM) begin. OpenAI is a great example: it's hiring for a role that sits exactly on this boundary — a Deployed Product Manager for Codex.

Who answers the "why"?

Scaler distinguishes the two roles by how success is measured: FDEs are judged on whether the deployment works, PMs on whether the team built the right thing. Under this division of labor, the PM decides what to build and why, while the FDE makes it run in the customer's real environment.

Mind the Product extends this division into the customer's organization. Forward Deployed Product Managers (FDPMs) are hired by the vendor but work inside the customer's organization: employed by one company, embedded in another. They aren't responsible for a product — they're responsible for an account's outcomes.

When the two work side by side, the FDPM owns the "what" and "why" for that account, and the FDE owns the technical implementation. The FDPM also brings what they've learned back to the core product team so other customers can benefit.

The tech lead's question above actually contains both parts. "How do we make it work" is your job; "should we do it, and why" is a product decision.

What OpenAI's job posting reveals about the gap

OpenAI describes its forward deployed engineering team as working with customers to translate research breakthroughs into production systems. The job posting for that team's manager also requires distilling proven practices into tools, playbooks, and roadmap inputs. In other words, FDEs there are already expected to bring customer experience back to the product.

The Deployed Product Manager posting doesn't explain why the role was created. But read against the FDE team's posting, a reasonable inference is: bringing experience back to the product takes real effort, and there aren't many engineers who can both debug a data pipeline at midnight and write a solid product analysis. This is an inference, not OpenAI's statement.

What the posting does say explicitly: this person serves as the product lead for strategic customer accounts, helping customers adopt and expand their use of OpenAI's products. They work closely with Deployment Engineers and go-to-market (GTM) teams to translate customer needs and technical blockers into clear product feedback, and to give internal teams direction on execution.

One blocker, two questions

Consider a concrete case. You're deploying a coding agent at a company with hundreds of internal repositories. The pull requests (PRs) the agent submits keep getting blocked by continuous integration (CI), because the company has its own lint rules that the agent doesn't know about.

The "how" question is yours. You can load the lint configuration into the agent's context, add an auto-formatting step before submitting PRs, and compare CI pass rates for PRs before and after the fix. By the end of the week the deployment works — and that's exactly how an FDE is measured.

The "what and why" question remains open. Is this specific to one customer, or will every enterprise customer hit it? If you fix it and move on, the product team will never know the problem exists.

PostHog points out why this gap arises naturally: FDEs explore and build for one customer at a time, and generality comes second. Spreading that experience to other customers is the core product team's job — but only if the experience reaches them.

How do you know the problem will recur?

Agile Lab's FDE playbook describes a habit worth borrowing: every time an engineer logs a customer issue, they create a traceable work item stating the problem, its value to the customer, and the expected outcome. With these work items, "will this recur" turns from a gut feeling into a single search.

In practice, if your team uses Jira, Linear, or another task-tracking tool, tag each work item with an issue type such as

ci-policy

or

repo-conventions

, along with the account name. When you hit a lint blocker, filter by that tag and count how many distinct accounts already have similar work items.

If there's nothing in the tracking tool, just ask in the FDEs' shared chat channel: "Has anyone seen an agent blocked by CI because of a customer's own repo conventions?" As soon as two or three people answer "yes," your write-up goes from one customer's complaint to a hypothesis worth investigating.

Once you have a count, the write-up you send back should be short and structured:

阻塞：Agent 的 PR 因内部 lint 规则被 CI 拦下
影响：[被拦下的 PR 数 / 1 周内 PR 总数——真实测量]
临时方案：把 lint 配置加载进上下文 + 提交 PR 前增加格式化步骤
重复出现：[带 ci-policy 标签的账户数]
产品建议：允许在组织层面声明仓库规范

If the company has a deployed product manager, this write-up is exactly the material they need to get started. If not, this write-up is the product work you take on yourself.

Do it yourself: separate "how" from "what" in every meeting

Start with the habit of two-column notes. Every issue a customer raises goes into one of two columns: one for what's needed to make the system work, the other for whether the product should be different. Most issues have content in both columns.

The "how" column should be resolved and measured as quickly as possible, because that's the yardstick for you. For the "what and why" column, create tagged work items first, search for work items with the same tag before writing a recommendation, then send the write-up to the deployed product manager (if there is one) or the PM who owns that area, and agree on a review date.

Agile Lab's playbook sums up the purpose of the whole process: shortening the feedback loop between customer evidence and the product engineering team.

Common mistakes

The most common mistake is turning a temporary workaround into a standalone product. A well-coding FDE can easily build an entire layer of functionality that only works for one customer — a layer that then becomes an unmaintained maintenance burden nobody owns. The opposite mistake is pushing everything to the product team, even issues you could fix yourself in an afternoon, and losing the customer's trust as a result.

The third mistake is harder to spot: sending feedback like "the customer wants feature X." That sentence has no impact, no workaround, and no count of accounts hitting the same problem. The product team reads it and still can't decide anything.

Which role is right for you?

When reading job postings, watch for phrases like "partner closely with Deployment Engineers" or "product feedback." They tell you whether you'll have a product counterpart or have to carry both columns alone. In FDE postings, phrases like "roadmap inputs" indicate that the feedback loop to the product is part of what you'll be evaluated on.

Whichever path you choose, being able to write the kind of write-up above is valuable. Put a line on your resume describing how you turned a customer blocker into a product change, with before-and-after measurements and the number of customers who benefited.

"Why can't the product do this?" gets asked again at every customer. What earns trust is knowing when to fix things yourself and when to bring the issue back to the product team for a decision.
