Winning back a customer’s trust · FDE behavioral question

FDE Interview Prep · 2026 · 2 min read

Summary

This is a practice article for FDE behavioral interviews on the question of regaining a customer's trust. It cites Sarah Khalid, a Director at Salesforce, who said in March 2026 that such engagements test judgment, trust, and communication more than coding. The recommended structure: own your share of the breakdown, acknowledge it without a "but", make small verifiable commitments on a fixed cadence, fix the root cause, and point to a customer action as evidence trust returned. It warns against rescuer stories and winning trust back with bigger promises, and lists likely follow-up questions.

How to answer

Sarah Khalid, an Director at Salesforce, said in Salesforce’s newsroom in March 2026 that an insurance engagement she recalled was more “judgment, trust, and communication skills” than coding. Trust comes back through a run of small promises kept, not one big fix. Structure the answer to show that:

  • The break, as behavior. What went wrong, and the moment you knew trust was gone: they copied your manager, asked for daily status, or started checking your numbers against their own. Say what share of it was yours.
  • The acknowledgment. What you said, how soon, with no “but”. Name the failure and what it cost them. Quote it.
  • Small promises, kept where they could see. Shrink commitments to ones you were certain to keep, on a fixed cadence, in a form they could verify without you. Include the time you reported your own bad news before they found it; that is when the pattern becomes believable.
  • The fix to the cause. One or two sentences on what stopped it recurring.
  • The sign it came back. Something they did: dropped a check they had imposed, handed you harder work, or vouched for you to someone else.

Say the acknowledgment out loud, word for word. The interviewer is judging whether you can say “that was us” to an angry customer without hedging.

Hold back the technical cause; name it in a clause.

Two traps. The rescuer story, where someone else broke it, gives you nothing to own. Winning trust back with a bigger promise is the fastest way to lose it again, so if you made one, say how you made sure to keep it.

Follow-ups

What the interviewer may ask next, once your first answer is on the table.

  • They asked for someone else. Why were you still the one in the room, and who decided that?
  • Which of your promises was hardest to keep, and what did you give up to keep it?
  • If that customer had a problem today, would they call you first? How do you know?

Where answers go wrong

  • Picks a loss of trust someone else caused, such as sales or a previous team, and casts themselves only as the rescuer, so there is nothing of theirs to acknowledge.
  • Wins the customer back with one heroic fix or a big new promise, and offers “the relationship is great now” as the evidence instead of something the customer did differently.

Answer this in two minutes

Write the answer you would say out loud. The clock starts with your first word.
