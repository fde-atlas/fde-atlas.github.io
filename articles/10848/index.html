What Exactly Is an FDE, and Why Are AI Companies Sending Engineers into the Field?

Original title: FDE 到底是什么，为什么 AI 公司开始把工程师送进现场

53AI · 2026-08-05 · 6 min read

Summary

This overview article defines the Forward Deployed Engineer (FDE) role by synthesizing official job descriptions and engineering docs from OpenAI, Scale AI, and Palantir. It argues that what defines an FDE is not working on-site but owning a closed loop: customer context, production-grade delivery, and feedback flowing back into products. The author proposes a five-step workflow, minimal criteria for judging whether a role is a complete FDE loop, and cites 2026 Shanghai and Beijing policies as evidence the model has entered Chinese industrial policy, while cautioning this does not establish a unified professional standard.

Key points

  • FDE is defined by responsibility scope, not location.
  • Stronger models make local context the bottleneck.
  • The role follows a five-step loop ending in product feedback.
  • OpenAI, Scale AI, and Palantir job descriptions share a stable structure.
  • Shanghai and Beijing 2026 policies adopt FDE as a deployment mechanism.

An FDE, simply put, is an engineer who follows customers' real problems into the field, turns AI prototypes into production systems that run reliably, and brings what is learned on-site back into the product.

The stronger models become, the more important this role gets. That's because what customers buy is general capability, but what actually determines a project's success is local context: where the business rules are hidden, whether the data is usable, how to integrate with legacy systems, how to handle permissions and compliance, and how to ensure cost, latency, and reliability after launch. Model upgrades won't automatically fill these gaps.

The Promise of This Article: After reading it, you will have a judgment framework that doesn't depend on job titles, understand the complete working loop of an FDE, and be able to see why "residing on-site but never writing production code" and "only doing delivery without feeding back into the product" are both incomplete.

Forward Deployed Engineer is usually translated as "前沿部署工程师" in Chinese. "Forward" tends to make people focus only on "frontline" or "on-site," but location is not what defines this role—the scope of responsibility is.

OpenAI's official FDE job posting places the team "at the intersection of customer delivery and core platform development." The role covers requirements discovery, technical scoping, system design, building, and production launches; success metrics include production adoption, measurable workflow impact, and eval feedback that can change product and model roadmaps. The posting also explicitly requires writing and reviewing production-grade frontend and backend code, and distilling effective practices into tools, playbooks, or reusable components.

Scale AI's official job description provides another set of evidence: FDEs collaborate with technical customers daily, translate customer problems into engineering solutions, and design, build, and deploy full-stack features from frontend and backend to infrastructure, while directly influencing the product roadmap.

Scale AI's FDE Lead role makes production responsibility even more explicit: delivered systems must meet standards for reliability, safety, observability, and evals, and on-site experience must be converted into reusable platform capabilities so the next deployment doesn't start from scratch.

Palantir's description of Forward Deployed Engineering is closer to a product development methodology: engineering teams stay as close to the problem as possible, collaborate with core engineering teams, continuously synthesize field feedback, and ship new features. Palantir likens this process to "backpropagation for humans."

This is a synthesis of official responsibilities from multiple companies and does not imply that all enterprises use exactly the same job title.

This is why AI implementation cannot rely solely on "model experts providing solutions remotely." Many constraints only surface once you enter the actual workflow: users may bypass the system, historical fields may have multiple conflicting definitions, APIs may be undocumented, and even correct model output may not fit the required approval sequence.

Here we need to distinguish facts from judgment. The official materials from OpenAI, Scale AI, and Palantir prove that these companies require FDEs to handle customer collaboration, full-stack building, production deployment, and feedback loops; "the stronger the model, the more on-site context becomes the relative bottleneck" is an engineering judgment based on these shared responsibilities, not a unified conclusion published by the three companies.

Compressing the job descriptions of different companies into an executable process, an FDE's work can be summarized in five steps.

Step 1: Discover the problem. Enter the business site and find the processes, roles, and constraints that truly affect outcomes. When a customer says "we want to build an intelligent customer service bot," the FDE needs to keep asking: Is the goal to reduce first response time or human handoff rate? Which questions are allowed to be answered automatically? Who is accountable for wrong answers?

Step 2: Define the solution. Turn vague requirements into technical scope, acceptance metrics, and risk boundaries. Here you decide whether to use models, rules, or traditional code, which data can enter the context, and which actions must retain human confirmation.

Step 3: Build and integrate. Write production code and connect the model to data sources, business systems, permission systems, and user interfaces. An FDE doesn't hand off requirements to another engineering team and walk away, but directly participates in key engineering implementation.

Step 4: Run in production. Get the prototype past the launch threshold: evals, observability, cost, latency, safety, failure degradation, and rollback all need owners. OpenAI's job description runs from "the first prototype" all the way to "stable production"—this is exactly the dividing line between FDE work and demo projects.

Step 5: Feed back. Abstract recurring on-site problems into tools, components, eval sets, product features, or inputs to the development roadmap. Otherwise every customer gets a from-scratch customization, and the engineering team keeps accumulating one-off burdens.

These five steps are not a waterfall process. Production feedback brings the team back to problem definition, and product improvements feed into the next round of deployment. A complete FDE engagement is a loop, not a one-time delivery.

Domestic (Chinese) policy is not the source of the FDE definition, but it demonstrates that this organizational model has moved from corporate job titles into the language of industry execution.

Shanghai's "Three-Year Action Plan for Supporting Advanced Manufacturing Transformation and Upgrading (2026–2028)" explicitly proposes cultivating an FDE workforce under "deepening digital-intelligent transformation," to support enterprises in applying large models, building industry models, and industrial AI agents. The emphasis here is on manufacturing digital-intelligent scenarios.

Beijing's 2026 AI agent industry measures read even closer to the closed loop in this article: supporting model providers and industry enterprises in accelerating AI agent adoption through models like FDE, with "on-site co-creation and continuous iteration" feeding back into AI agent capability improvements.

The policies of the two cities support a cautious conclusion: FDE has been adopted in formal domestic policy as an implementation mechanism connecting model capabilities with industry scenarios. They do not prove that FDE has formed a unified professional standard, nor can they infer that the number of positions, salaries, or individual subsidies will necessarily grow. Shanghai's training, competition, and professional title mechanisms, as well as regional maturity differences, will be covered separately in the next article of the series.

The value of FDE for developers is not learning another job title acronym, but gaining a new standard of engineering completeness. In the past, when building an AI demo, the focus might have been whether the model could answer; in the FDE context, the questions extend further: Can it enter the business process? Can it run reliably? Can results be measured? Can one project improve the next delivery?
