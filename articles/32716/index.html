Theory of Constraints and *The Goal*: FDEs Find the Bottleneck Before Automating

Original title: 约束理论与《目标》：FDE 先找瓶颈再自动化

FDE Times · 2026-11-12 · 4 min read

Summary

This article uses Goldratt's 1984 novel The Goal to argue that FDEs should locate the customer's bottleneck before automating anything. Since the constraint's output caps the whole system, speeding up non-bottleneck steps only grows queues. A logistics example shows data-entry automation leaving throughput at 120 orders per day. The recommended sequence: identify the constraint, exploit it, subordinate everything else, and automate last.

Key points

  • The Goal, by Eliyahu M. Goldratt and Jeff Cox, launched the Theory of Constraints movement.
  • A system's constraint determines total output; strengthening other steps wastes effort.
  • In the logistics case, automating data entry left throughput unchanged at 120 orders daily.
  • FDEs should identify and fully exploit the constraint before automating it.
  • Bottlenecks should be managed, not eliminated; resumes should state constraint found and throughput gained.

Goldratt's *The Goal* shows that accelerating anything other than the bottleneck does not increase customer output—it only makes queues longer.

The Goal: A Process of Ongoing Improvement · Eliyahu M. Goldratt ·

Cover image: Open Library

Key Takeaways

  • *The Goal*, co-authored by Eliyahu M. Goldratt and Jeff Cox, is a novel centered on factory manager Alex Rogo and the founding work of the Theory of Constraints movement.
  • The bottleneck's output determines the output of the entire organization, so strengthening anything else is wasted effort.
  • For FDEs, the key is to find where work queues up, fully exploit that step first, and leave automation for last.

A factory novel that has never gone out of print since 1984, and one closer to an FDE's actual work than many books about AI. On-site with customers, many requests you receive start with "help us automate this step"—and *The Goal* explains why doing so is often completely ineffective.

The book was co-authored by Eliyahu M. Goldratt and Jeff Cox and published by North River Press. The protagonist, Alex Rogo, is a factory manager under enormous pressure, increasingly struggling to find ways to improve performance.

Through his story, the novel lays out the core ideas of the Theory of Constraints (TOC). The TOC Institute calls it the book that launched the entire Theory of Constraints movement.

This book trains the rigorous customer-investigation skills of a Forward Deployed Engineer (FDE): before writing the first line of code, you need to know exactly which step is limiting the customer's output. Skip this step, and you may deliver a perfectly functioning AI agent while the customer processes not a single additional order.

The demo is impressive—so why don't the numbers move?

The TOC Institute offers a short proposition: every system has a constraint that limits its output, whether we acknowledge it or not. From this follows a second proposition: the constraint's output determines the output of the entire organization.

In the story, the constraint takes the form of Herbie, a character who plays the bottleneck. The name is unforgettable, making it a perfect question to carry with you: where is the customer's Herbie?

Imagine a logistics customer who wants an AI agent to read emails and create orders. They receive 500 orders per day by email, data-entry staff can enter 300, the credit review team manually approves 120, and the warehouse can ship 400.

The system's actual output is 120 orders per day, with 180 orders piling up in front of the credit review team each day.

Now you automate data entry, raising capacity to 500. The demo is dazzling. But output is still 120, and the queue in front of credit review grows from 180 per day to 380. From an operations standpoint, you have just made things worse.

Three ideas worth bringing to every project

The first idea is about measurement. Goldratt's team believes the book's core lesson is: the primary goal must be increasing throughput, not cutting costs or improving local efficiency.

In the example above, data-entry speed nearly doubled—that's local efficiency—yet throughput didn't change at all. When writing value reports for customers, measure by the number of orders completed through the entire process.

The second idea follows from the first. The TOC Institute writes that strengthening any link other than the weakest one in the chain is a waste of time and energy.

For an FDE, this should become a reflex: when a customer points at a step, your first question should be whether it is the weakest link, not which model to use.

The third idea often surprises readers most: bottlenecks need to be managed, not eliminated. Since every system has a constraint, the sensible goal is not to destroy it entirely. Your job is to know precisely where the constraint is and to consciously control it.

Why should automation come last?

TOC turns these three ideas into five focusing steps. What you need to master in projects is the first four: the first step is to identify the constraint. The next two are to fully exploit the constraint, then make everything else follow its rhythm.

The TOC Institute emphasizes that you should only elevate the constraint—the fourth step—after the first two steps are fully done; sometimes elevation isn't needed at all.

Back to the logistics customer. Fully exploiting the constraint means first looking at where the credit review team spends its time. Suppose they spend hours gathering customer data from multiple systems; then a script that pre-assembles the information could increase the number of approved orders—no AI agent required at all.

Following the bottleneck's rhythm means data entry should not push orders faster than the credit team can process them, and the queue should be sorted by order value.

Only when the credit team is already running at full capacity and capacity is still insufficient does automating the approval step make sense. This sequencing also helps build customer trust: small, cheap, measurable changes come before big projects.

Who should read it, and how?

Look for the 30th anniversary edition. According to North River Press, this is the first substantial update to the story.

As a novel, the book is a quick read, well suited to engineers with 2-8 years of experience who have never done operations work or who have just moved into an FDE role.

After finishing, open the TOC Institute's page on the five focusing steps to see a more distilled version of the method.

The book says nothing about LLMs or data pipelines—don't use it as a substitute for those. It teaches the questions you should ask before using those skills.

On your resume, state clearly which constraint you found and how throughput changed, rather than listing what you built.

The next time a customer points at a step and says "help us automate this," go find their Herbie first.
