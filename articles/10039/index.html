Afterword: The Professional Ethics of the FDE

Original title: 后记 FDE 的职业道德

FDE4.AI · 2026 · 5 min read

Summary

This afterword to a book on FDE methodology lays out six ethical bottom lines for forward deployed engineers: client data sovereignty, honest reporting including bad news, avoiding vendor lock-in and fear-mongering, taking displaced workers seriously, refusing improper client requests, and recognizing that FDEs represent the AI industry itself. The author cites a Gartner forecast that 70% of enterprises will abandon frontline-deployment-led approaches by 2028, and Anthropic's finding that users approve about 93% of approval prompts as fatigue sets in. It also discusses Nabil Kureshi's three-way classification of projects by moral character, argues deployers should stay in the room rather than exit, and warns job seekers to distinguish real FDE roles from outsourced field work.

Key points

  • Six ethical bottom lines, starting with client data sovereignty and honest reporting
  • Gartner: 70% of enterprises may abandon frontline-deployment-led solutions by 2028
  • Anthropic: users approve ~93% of prompts as human oversight fatigues
  • Nabil Kureshi classifies projects as neutral, clearly good, or gray-zone; stay at the table
  • Job seekers should verify FDE roles involve production code, not disguised outsourcing

Full text

Every book on methodology must end by addressing one question: once you have mastered this method, where are its boundaries. And the topic of this book is heavier than most methodologies. Because what the FDE holds is no ordinary technology—it is the deepest secrets of a client's organization, and a growing power to make decisions on behalf of human beings.

The nature of the FDE's work determines what it will see. To deliver well, you must look at the client's most authentic operating data—including the ugly parts; you must map the organization's power structure—including who is incompetent and who is losing influence; you must touch core business processes—including the workarounds that skirt the gray areas. The client opens all of this to you on the basis of a simple covenant: you are here to help. This trust is the foundation of the FDE model, and it takes only one transgression to destroy it.

I believe the professional ethics of the FDE have at least six bottom lines, which I write down here for the mutual encouragement of all peers.

First, data sovereignty belongs to the client. Data seen at the client's site should not appear, not a single byte, in any place it does not belong—not into AI training data (unless the contract explicitly authorizes it), not into case-study material (unless the client agrees in writing), not into talking points for your next job. The principle of least privilege is not just a technical specification but professional conduct: don't look at what you don't need to, and anonymize whatever can be anonymized.

Second, report results honestly, including bad news. In a pay-for-results model, the greatest moral hazard is whitewashing results—packaging "the system went live" as "value was realized," packaging correlation as causation. The value measurement system in Chapter 6 can be either the most honest tool or the most sophisticated lying machine; the difference lies only in the human heart. The FDE's foundation is "daring to be tested," so when testing reveals a bad result, it should be presented with the same mindset.

Third, do not manufacture dependency, do not sell fear. There are two kinds of covert practices in this industry: one is deliberately building the system as a black box so the client can never leave you; the other is exaggerating the panic that "you'll die without AI" to close deals. Gartner predicts that by 2028, 70% of enterprises will be forced to abandon Forward Deployed Engineer–led solutions due to cost and skill hollowing-out—that prediction is a wake-up call for the entire industry.

A healthy FDE model, by contrast, marks the end of delivery by the client being able to run on its own: knowledge is transferred to the client, capability is deposited into the client's team.

Fourth, take "the people being replaced" seriously. The systems FDEs deliver will, in many scenarios, genuinely replace part of some people's work. This book covers a great deal of "change management" technique, but beyond technique lies ethics: do not celebrate efficiency in front of those being replaced, do not write people off as "cost items" in your proposals without offering them a way out, and do not play dumb about "whose fate you are changing." Don't use "technology is neutral" as an excuse—the choices deployers make every day are themselves a stance.

Fifth, say no to "what the client asks for but shouldn't have." You will encounter requests that skirt the edge of compliance: "help us make our employee behavior monitoring even more granular," "these data are a bit murky compliance-wise, but let's take them on first." The metaphor of the "French waiter" has its deepest meaning here—true professionalism is not satisfying every client request, but daring to steer the client toward what is genuinely good for them and harmless to the world. The confidence to say no comes from having other clients on your books; so ethics has always been tied to the business model.

Sixth, remember that you represent "technology" itself. For many clients, you are the first face they encounter of AI. Every exaggeration of yours draws down the entire industry's credit in their hearts; every promise you keep makes a deposit for the entire industry.

The guardrails you design are also making decisions for ordinary people—Anthropic's engineering team publicly reviewed one number: when the system pops up a request for approval on everything, users approve about 93% of requests, and the more they see, the less carefully they look; "human-in-the-loop" (a human as the gatekeeper) thus becomes a mere formality through fatigue. So those who design guardrails cannot assume the gatekeepers are always alert. As AI penetrates ever deeper into the workings of society, the deployer is the last translator between technology and humanity's daily life—and the cost of a distorted translation is paid by everyone.

These six bottom lines are, in reverse, also a litmus test for choosing your employer. In the 2026 job market, jobs bearing the FDE title have surged within a year, yet only about one in ten engineers is willing to do this work—and the gap between supply and demand is crowded with bandwagon jumpers. Under the same title, positions at frontier labs are "software engineering plus the client's site," while positions at bandwagon companies may just be on-site outsourcing under a new name: one engineer, after joining, found the company only wanted him to do project coordination and never planned to let him write a single line of code, and quit after four weeks. (Source in Appendix C)

Before entering the field, asking one more question—does this role write production code, does field experience feed back into the product—matters far more than salary negotiation.

Palantir as a company is itself deeply controversial: its history of serving intelligence and military agencies makes many people wary of everything about it—including the FDE model. My extensive citation of its methodology in this book does not mean I endorse all of its client choices. Quite the opposite: precisely because the domains it serves are so sensitive, the disciplines in its engineering culture around permissions, auditing, and need-to-know are especially worth learning from.

Nabil Kureishi, who spent nearly eight years as an FDE at Palantir, divided the projects he handled into three categories by moral character: morally neutral, clearly for good (pandemic response, fighting child exploitation), and gray areas (military, immigration, policing). Facing the gray areas, his answer was neither to pursue moral purity nor to walk away, but to "stay in the room"—to keep his own vote at the decision table. I cite this taxonomy because it is closer to the deployer's real situation than either "boycott" or "obey": most moral dilemmas occur precisely in the gray areas, and leaving the room to hand it to less scrupulous people is not necessarily the more moral answer.

The FDE is a young profession, and no one has yet written its code of conduct. I hope this book will be its first boundary marker.
