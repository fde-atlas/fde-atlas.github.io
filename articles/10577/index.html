Good products also lose deals: old Palantir interview goes viral — winning enterprise AI deals takes more than just models

Original title: 好产品也会丢单，Palantir 旧访谈翻红：企业级 AI 订单，拼的不只是模型

A podcast interview with Alexandr Wang from December 2024 has recently been recirculating in Chinese tech communities.

The interview was organized by the venture capital community South Park Commons, when he was still the founder and CEO of Scale AI—half a year before he joined Meta as Chief AI Officer. The host asked him how to balance building products with doing sales. One line from his answer was repeatedly shared: In large organizations and government clients, people's perception of something can sometimes influence decisions more than the objective facts.

He then gave Palantir as an example. A company whose clients are the US military, government, and large enterprises, he described it as a hybrid of a theater troupe and a software company, one that even long gave new employees a book on performance. Over the past two years, Palantir's market cap went from breaking $100 billion in October 2024 to a peak of over $490 billion in November 2025, now falling back to around $450 billion. His remarks came to be circulated as a key to the company's growth story.

An improv handbook

The resurfaced retellings put the emphasis on "perception shaping," with some versions going so far as to frame it as scripts teaching engineers to "take over the client's brain from the first second they walk into the meeting room." Palantir's own publicly documented delivery process says: engineers live on-site at the client, the system runs on the client's own data, and then the contract is signed.

The claim about the performance book has a firsthand account. Nabeel Qureshi, who worked at Palantir for eight years, published a long essay, "Reflections on Palantir," in October 2024, writing that during his tenure new hires received several books, one of which was Keith Johnstone's *Impro*, a work on improvisational theater.

As he understood it, the book was used on client sites: by observing status signals in human interactions and learning to speak the client's language, engineers could earn trust and push forward data integration and software deployment. Qureshi said he wasn't sure whether the book is still given out today.

Wang used the theater troupe metaphor to describe Palantir's posture toward clients. He said that with large organizations and government clients, how a company appears in the client's eyes can sometimes influence decisions more than the product itself, and Palantir is an example of this. He called this practice of shaping client perception a "dark art", with the premise that the product must be built well first and deliver real value—that is the bottom line, and without it, nothing else holds.

What's truly hard to replicate is embedded engineering

Attributing Palantir's growth to acting classes amounts to ignoring the hardest part of this playbook to replicate.

Palantir has been doing data analysis for US intelligence agencies since 2003. Clients couldn't articulate what they wanted, data was scattered across multiple systems, and the standard software product model failed here. Engineers had no choice but to embed on-site, building the system within the client's data and workflows. This role later got a formal name: Forward Deployed Engineer. Industry retrospectives show that as late as 2016, Palantir's Forward Deployed Engineers at one point outnumbered its product engineers.

This playbook now has a standardized entry point: Palantir officially set the goal of its AIP Bootcamp at five days—clients bring their own data and use cases, and within five days build an initial use case in the software.

Among the customer testimonials on its website, Walgreens used Foundry and AIP to scale a pilot from 10 stores to about 4,000 in eight months; AT&T runs 660 applications on Foundry, with one application, S.C.O.U.T., backed by more than 100 AT&T engineers. The financial numbers are a product of the same model: Palantir's total revenue in Q1 2026 grew 85% year over year, with US commercial revenue up 133%.

Trust here is broken down into steps that can be verified one by one: clients don't need to believe a salesperson's pitch—in the five-day workshop they see how their own data is read and how their own workflows are moved into the software. How permissions are set, who approves errors, where audit logs are kept—Palantir's governance documentation has corresponding tools for all of it. Before the contract is signed, the client has already verified it with their own hands.

Model companies start learning from Palantir

On May 11 this year, OpenAI announced the creation of the OpenAI Deployment Company, an independent deployment company majority-owned by OpenAI, with over $4 billion in startup funding from 19 institutions led by TPG, and simultaneously announced the acquisition of Edinburgh-headquartered Tomoro, bringing in about 150 Forward Deployed Engineers at once.

On OpenAI's careers page, FDE positions are listed separately for industries such as finance and healthcare, with job descriptions stating that engineers must embed within client organizations and turn model capabilities into auditable, deliverable production systems.

Anthropic's approach is two-pronged: on one hand, it announced on its website a multi-year partnership with IT services provider DXC, under which DXC will train tens of thousands of Claude Forward Deployed Engineers through Anthropic's certification system and place them into the existing IT systems of banks, airlines, insurers, and government agencies; on the other hand, it is recruiting on-site FDEs on its own careers page, with salary ranges of $280,000 to $320,000. According to earlier reports, Anthropic is also preparing a similar deployment joint venture entity.

A 2025 report from MIT's NANDA project estimated that 95% of enterprise generative AI pilots produced no quantifiable business value, with the problems mostly occurring at the deployment stage. The gap in model capabilities is narrowing, while organizations capable of installing models into client workflows are becoming scarcer.

The domestic To B market is stuck at the same stage. Before signing, buyers must answer: who is responsible when something goes wrong, can it be validated on their own data, and how much surgery is required to integrate the system into existing workflows. None of these questions can be answered with a performance comparison table.

The problem Wang pointed out in that interview still holds two years later: how clients understand your capabilities. Palantir answered it with over two decades of embedded engineering, and OpenAI and Anthropic are copying it with real money. Enterprise AI contracts will increasingly go to the companies that station engineers on-site at their clients.
