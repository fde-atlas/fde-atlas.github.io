What Is a Forward Deployed Infrastructure Engineer: The Palantir FDE Role

Original title: 前线部署基础设施工程师是什么：Palantir FDE 岗位

FDE Times · 2026-11-04 · 5 min read

Summary

The article examines Palantir's Forward Deployed Infrastructure Engineer role, a variant of the FDE track aimed at infrastructure engineers, including new graduates. Because some customers run software in air-gapped or on-premises environments with no telemetry returning to the vendor, the on-site engineer effectively becomes the operations team. Drawing on CoreWeave and TrueFoundry documentation and a worked example of a missed container image causing ImagePullBackOff, the author argues this is not rebranded DevOps: the job requires strong coding (Java, Go, Python, JavaScript), Kubernetes, CNI familiarity (Calico, Cilium, Multus), GitOps with ArgoCD or Flux, and experience delivering software to isolated environments. New graduates can qualify by building reproducible projects, such as an internet-less cluster deployment.

Key points

  • Palantir has a dedicated Forward Deployed Infrastructure Engineer role, with a new-graduate version.
  • Air-gapped customers send no telemetry, so the on-site engineer is the operations team.
  • Requirements include strong coding, Kubernetes, CNI plugins, GitOps, and air-gapped delivery experience.
  • Unlike DevOps, the role means bringing one product into many customer-owned, differently configured environments.
  • Graduates should prove themselves with reproducible projects, like deploying into an internet-less cluster.

Customers with isolated networks and on-premises deployments need someone to write code and maintain stability in places vendors can't reach, and new graduates have opportunities too.

Key Takeaways

  • Palantir has a dedicated "Forward Deployed Infrastructure Engineer" role that also hires new graduates, with responsibilities including on-site deployment, migration, and on-call duty at customer sites.
  • In air-gapped environments, clusters cannot pull images from public registries or send telemetry back to the vendor, so the on-site engineer is the operations team.
  • This is not DevOps under a different name: Palantir requires strong coding skills, and Ultimate Knowledge's FDE job posting asks for Kubernetes, CNI, GitOps, and experience delivering software to air-gapped environments.

Palantir does not put all its Forward Deployed Engineers (FDEs) under a single job title. Its careers page lists a role called "Forward Deployed Infrastructure Engineer," and the job description is blunt: you will develop software while also being responsible for the reliability and operations of software systems. The role even has a new-graduate version.

This detail matters. The prevailing image of the FDE today is someone who builds agents, connects data, and sits alongside customers. If your strengths are Kubernetes, Terraform, or networking, you may never have considered the FDE path as an option for you.

The job postings say otherwise. There is an FDE track where infrastructure skills are the core capability customers need, not an ancillary skill. This track exists for a clear reason: some customers need software to run in places the vendor cannot reach.

The Customer's Environment Shapes the Role

The documentation for Palantir's deployment platform, Apollo, states that Apollo supports all major cloud environments, on-premises environments, and air-gapped (physically isolated network) environments. A community-maintained FDE roadmap on GitHub describes the infrastructure FDEs typically face as "hostile, legacy, air-gapped or hybrid."

In an air-gapped cluster, even the most ordinary things are missing. CoreWeave's air-gapped deployment documentation warns that clusters cannot pull images from public registries, so before deployment, every required container image must already be placed in the customer's internal registry.

The second issue is what truly changes the nature of the job. TrueFoundry, in explaining how to bring large models into heavily regulated industries, notes that the common approach is to install via Helm into the customer's own Kubernetes cluster, disable automatic updates, and send no telemetry back to the vendor's control plane.

Without telemetry, the SRE team at headquarters sees nothing: no dashboards, no alerts, no logs. When a pod goes down at 2 a.m., the only people who can look at the system are those on the customer's side with access. This is why the role must exist: the on-site engineer is also the operations team.

An Installation Without Internet

Imagine you bring a Helm chart to a financial services customer's cluster. Suppose the chart references nine images: seven belong to the application itself, one is a log-collection sidecar, and one is an init container that runs migrations. You have synced the seven main images to the internal registry but missed the other two because they were hidden in subcharts.

On a cluster with internet access, this mistake would barely be noticed, because nodes pull images themselves. In an air-gapped environment, the pods get stuck in ImagePullBackOff. To fix it, you have to find a way to get those two images into the internal registry, and the cluster has no route to the internet at all.

The lesson here is not about Kubernetes but about work habits. Before going on site, render the entire chart, extract a manifest of all referenced images, and write a script that checks this manifest against the target registry one by one.

This is exactly the kind of "runbook automation" Palantir lists in its job description, alongside deploying new products across many production environments.

This Is Not DevOps Under a Different Name

Ultimate Knowledge's FDE job posting on Workable makes it clear: you will work in both cloud and on-premises environments, and this is not a traditional DevOps or IT role. The same posting lists Kubernetes as a mandatory requirement.

These two statements sound contradictory, but they are not. DevOps engineers typically maintain their own company's platform, choosing their own tools with full control. A forward deployed infrastructure engineer, by contrast, has to bring a product into many environments that do not belong to them, each with its own configuration and rules.

That is why Palantir places coding requirements on par with US government security clearance requirements: you must have strong coding skills and be proficient in languages such as Java, Go, Python, or JavaScript. Manually executing runbooks across ten different environments guarantees mistakes. Turning them into tools is the software engineer part of this job.

Putting several job postings side by side makes the contours of the role clear, and what each requirement means on a customer site becomes obvious.

Networking Skills Make the Difference

In the table above, the CNI row deserves the closest reading. Ultimate Knowledge's posting does not pick just one plugin; it lists all three names: Calico, Cilium, and Multus. The reasonable interpretation is that you have no choice: the cluster belongs to the customer, and the network layer was decided before you arrived.

So when one service cannot call another, you need to be able to determine whether the problem lies in the application, the network policies, or the way the customer's CNI handles traffic. Someone who only knows the defaults will get stuck here; someone who understands networking can pinpoint the failure before the end of the day.

GitOps works similarly. Imagine you leave the project and a colleague takes over: since there's no telemetry coming back, keeping the system's desired state in a repository and letting ArgoCD or Flux sync it means the next person can understand the environment without guessing.

New grads have a path too, but you need to show evidence

Palantir's new-grad version doesn't shrink the scope of the work. The responsibilities still include deploying new products in customer deployment environments, executing migrations to the latest infrastructure, and participating in on-call duty. In other words, even fresh graduates work directly with real production systems.

Proving you're up to the task doesn't come from yet another certificate, but from a reproducible experiment project others can replicate. Set up a cluster, cut off its route to the internet, sync images to an internal registry, install a real chart with Helm, manage it with ArgoCD, then swap the CNI for Cilium and observe how network policy behavior differs.

When writing your resume, describe outcomes, not just a list of tools. A sentence like "installed a system of nine images into an internet-isolated cluster and wrote pre-deployment check scripts" carries far more weight than simply writing "Kubernetes, Helm, ArgoCD".

When reading job postings, watch for the words air-gapped, on-call, and migrations: they signal you're looking at the infrastructure track, not the demo track.

For many infrastructure engineers, FDE sounds like a job requiring a whole new set of skills. But in places without internet access, infrastructure expertise is exactly what customers need most.
