RACI Matrix and Stakeholder Mapping: Hands-On Guide for FDE Customer Projects

Original title: RACI 矩阵与干系人地图：FDE 客户项目实操

FDE Times · 2026-11-15 · 6 min read

Summary

This hands-on guide shows FDEs how to build a RACI matrix and a power-interest stakeholder map in about 45 minutes right after a project kickoff. Using a fictional insurance claims AI assistant project, it walks through listing tasks before people, assigning exactly one Accountable per task, validating the matrix with a short Python script, scoring stakeholders honestly, and turning each quadrant into a concrete action sentence. It stresses separating the budget-approving sponsor from the line-of-business owner who decides actual adoption, flagging potentially obstructive stakeholders, and securing an internal champion. It closes with resume and interview framing tips.

Key points

  • One A per task; multiple Accountables slow decisions and spark disputes.
  • The sponsor signs the budget, but the LoB owner decides whether the product gets used.
  • A simple Python script catches RACI errors like missing or duplicate A's.
  • Mark threatened stakeholders with (!) and invite them into eval design.
  • Name an internal champion in week one; without one, consider exiting.

Right after the kickoff meeting, draw a RACI matrix and a power-interest map to see clearly who approves budget, who decides adoption, and who might stall the project.

Key Takeaways

  • Assign only one A per task. Too many As slow down decisions and invite disputes.
  • The sponsor provides the budget, while the business owner decides whether the product is actually used. Both must appear on your map.
  • The map only becomes a plan when every quadrant has a concrete action sentence and all parties have signed off before the project kickoff.

In client AI projects, two people are easily mistaken for one. One signs the budget; the other decides whether what you build will actually be used.

As Truong Phung puts it in a Forward Deployed Engineer (FDE) field manual published on DEV Community: the executive sponsor signs the check, but the line-of-business (LoB) owner decides whether your work actually gets used.

Conflating the two usually exacts its cost around week six: the prototype works, but nobody opens it. Two small documents can prevent that outcome: a RACI matrix and a stakeholder map. Together they take about 45 minutes, ideally done right after the kickoff meeting ends.

The walkthrough below uses a fictional project. Open your laptop and follow along, swapping in the people and tasks from your own project.

What will you build, and what do you need?

Suppose an FDE is assigned to the claims department of an insurance company, tasked with building an AI assistant that summarizes claim files for employees.

On the client side there is a Director of Operations (the sponsor), a Claims Manager (the business owner), a data engineer, the security team, and a team lead who manages a group of claims specialists.

When you finish, you will have a CSV file holding the RACI matrix, a short Python script for catching errors, and a power-interest map with an action sentence for every quadrant. You need Python 3, an editor, and a list of everyone you saw or heard mentioned at the kickoff meeting.

Step 1 (10 minutes): List the tasks first, then think about people

Lucid recommends putting project deliverables in the leftmost column in order of completion, then filling in the roles across the top row. Order matters. If you start from a list of people, you will invent work for each person instead of seeing the work the project actually needs.

task,FDE,Ops_director,Claims_manager,Client_data_eng,Security,Team_lead
获取样本数据访问权限,,,,,,
商定评测标准,,,,,,
构建原型,,,,,,
数据安全审查,,,,,,
在一个小组试点,,,,,,
移交运营,,,,,,

Check: Read the left column top to bottom. If every task depends on the previous one finishing before it can start, the order is right. If no row mentions evals or handover, the list is incomplete.

Step 2 (10 minutes): One A per row

TeamGantt defines Accountable as the person who assigns the work and gives final approval before the deliverable is considered done. The core rule is that each task has exactly one such person, so decisions stay clear. A CIO analysis puts it more bluntly: too many As make decisions slow and contentious.

task,FDE,Ops_director,Claims_manager,Client_data_eng,Security,Team_lead
获取样本数据访问权限,C,I,A,R,C,
商定评测标准,R,I,A,C,,C
构建原型,R,,A,C,,I
数据安全审查,C,I,,C,A,
在一个小组试点,R,I,A,,,C
移交运营,C,I,A,R,,C

Look at the "agree on eval criteria" row. The A is the Claims Manager, not the FDE, because his team grapples daily with the definition of an "acceptable summary." The FDE is the R: you do the work, he nods.

Step 3 (5 minutes): Let a script do the checking

Once the matrix gets long, the eye starts missing errors. The script below is deliberately simple: it counts the letters in each row and checks them against R and C thresholds you set yourself.

import csv, sys

MAX_R, MAX_C = 2, 3  # 阈值自行设定，可按项目调整

with open(sys.argv[1], newline="") as f:
    for row in csv.DictReader(f):
        cells = [v.strip() for k, v in row.items() if k != "task"]
        a, r, c = cells.count("A"), cells.count("R"), cells.count("C")
        if a != 1:
            print(f"[ERROR] {row['task']}：{a} 人标记为 A")
        if r == 0 or r > MAX_R:
            print(f"[WARNING] {row['task']}：{r} 人标记为 R")
        if c > MAX_C:
            print(f"[WARNING] {row['task']}：{c} 人标记为 C")
python3 check_raci.py raci.csv

Check: The run passes if there are no

[ERROR]

rows. The R and C warnings are also worth a second look. Lucid notes that too many Responsible entries slow down the process; the fix is to split tasks or reduce headcount. CIO warns that too many participating Cs drag down the whole project. Keep both the C and I lists as short as possible.

Step 4 (10 minutes): Score stakeholders honestly

Now start the second sheet. Give each person two scores from 1 to 5: their power over the project and their interest in it. Using 3 as the dividing line yields the familiar four quadrants: Manage Closely (high power, high interest), Keep Satisfied (high power, low interest), Keep Informed, and Monitor.

人员               权力      利益      象限
运营总监               5         2      保持满意
理赔经理               4         5      重点管理
安全团队               4         3      重点管理
组长                   2         5      随时告知  (!)
客户方数据工程师       2         3      随时告知

Rock offers a simple test: if everyone lands in "Manage Closely," your scoring is not honest enough and the map is useless.

In this example, the Director of Operations has the most power but pays the least attention to details. Recording this is not a slight; it tells you how to communicate with him.

The

(!)

next to the team lead is the most important part of this step. An AI assistant that summarizes claim files strikes at the core of his team's work. Truong Phung warns that people who feel threatened may quietly obstruct the project until it dies, while you never notice. Mark such people directly on the map.

Step 5 (7 minutes): Turn each quadrant into an action sentence

Rock argues that specificity is what turns a map from a poster into a plan. So each quadrant needs a sentence stating frequency, channel, and owner. For the "Keep Satisfied" quadrant, Rock's advice is brief: brief updates, no surprises, and not too much contact.

保持满意 | 运营总监 | 每周五发一封 5 行的邮件 | FDE 发送
重点管理 | 理赔经理 | 每周两次 30 分钟会议 | FDE 主持
随时告知 | 组长 | 每周与理赔团队坐一次班，
           邀请其为评测集提供意见 | FDE + 理赔经理

The sentence for the team lead is the pivot of the whole plan. Inviting him to help build the eval set turns someone who might feel threatened into someone who helps define quality.

Step 6 (3 minutes): Designate an internal champion and get sign-off

Truong Phung recommends identifying an internal owner in week 1, involving them in the work from week 2, and ensuring that by the end of the project they can run things on their own.

If you cannot find such a person, you should consider whether to walk away from the project. In this example, the natural champion is the Claims Manager, who holds the A on five of the six rows.

The final step is to send both sheets to the client. Lucid stresses that the matrix should be approved before the project starts so everyone is aligned. The power-interest map, however, stays within your own team: nobody likes seeing themselves filed under "Monitor."

What are the most common mistakes?

A common mistake is the FDE taking the A on tasks that belong to the client's business. It looks proactive, but in reality you are taking on a decision you have no authority to make.

Another mistake is treating the sponsor and the business owner as one person just because both attended the kickoff. The last mistake is producing the documents and shelving them: the matrix is only useful if it is reopened at every phase transition, such as moving from prototype to pilot.

How do you put this skill on your resume and use it in interviews?

Stick with the insurance project as the example. The typical "before" resume line reads: "Coordinated with multiple client stakeholders." That sentence applies to every engineer and proves nothing.

The "after" version states what you actually did: "Built a RACI matrix and stakeholder map for an AI assistant project spanning six client-side parties; identified an internal owner in week 1 and completed the handover so they could run independently." At a glance, the reader sees that you know who decides, who uses, and who needs to be brought in.

In an interview, if asked about a project that nearly failed, tell the story of the person you marked

(!)

and the action sentence you wrote for them.

The code runs on your machine, but at the client, the project only survives once the right people nod. Spending 45 minutes figuring out who those people are is the cheapest investment in the entire project.
