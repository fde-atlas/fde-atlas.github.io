How to Write a Mutual Action Plan (MAP): FDE Post-Demo Follow-Up

Original title: 共同行动计划（MAP）怎么写：FDE 演示后跟进

FDE Times · 2026-11-08 · 6 min read

Summary

This guide addresses a common FDE problem: a smooth product demo followed by customer silence. It argues the outcome is decided in the demo's final minutes, and recommends a Mutual Action Plan (MAP) with three requirements from Rework: calendar dates for every milestone, named owners on both buyer and vendor sides, and explicit confirmation from the decision-maker that milestones are feasible. The FDE should write the technical portions, since they know what data, access, and approvals a pilot needs. Timing matters: present the MAP only after the customer confirms the problem matters, the budget path, and intent to proceed. A worked logistics example shows closing scripts and a milestone table with a 30-day pilot ending in a go/no-go meeting with the economic buyer, drawing on Sybill, Stackmatix, Exec, and Echai Ventures. It also covers risks like champions who won't advocate internally, and five common mistakes.

Key points

  • Post-demo silence usually means no next step with dates and owners was set in the meeting.
  • A MAP needs calendar dates, named owners on both sides, and decision-maker sign-off on feasibility.
  • Present the MAP as a technical plan only after the customer confirms intent, budget path, and desire to proceed.
  • Keep pilots to 30 days with written success criteria, ending in a go/no-go meeting with the economic buyer.
  • The most enthusiastic attendee is often not the decision-maker; verify they will advocate internally.

Key Takeaways

  • Clients go silent after demos usually because the meeting ended without next steps tied to dates and owners.
  • Every milestone needs a calendar date and a designated owner on both sides, and the decision-maker must confirm the milestones are actually feasible.
  • Keep the pilot within 30 days, put success criteria in writing, and end with a go/no-go meeting with the economic buyer.

The demo went smoothly. The agent accurately read a sample invoice from a logistics company, the head of operations kept nodding, and then someone said: "Nice. Let's discuss internally and get back to you." Two weeks later, the inbox is still empty.

Anyone who has been a Forward Deployed Engineer (FDE) for long has run into this. The common reaction is to send a "just following up" email, then another one.

But the outcome is usually decided in the last five minutes of the demo: either you leave with next steps that have dates, owners, and a decision-maker, or you leave with a promise that binds no one.

Many people think this is sales' job. In reality, the FDE is the person the client trusts on the technical side, and the one who knows best what data and permissions the pilot needs and who in IT must sign off. That's why the next-step plan is only realistic when the FDE writes the technical portion.

Silence usually means there's no plan yet

The tool for solving this is the Mutual Action Plan (MAP). Rework's MAP guide sets out three requirements. First, every milestone must have a specific calendar date, not "within two weeks" or "as soon as possible."

Second, every milestone must have a designated owner on both the buyer's and the seller's side. Third, the decision-maker must explicitly confirm the milestones are achievable.

Calendar dates matter because milestones without dates keep slipping, as Sybill, a company that builds tools for sales teams, points out. For the logistics company, "IT will take a look next week" won't appear on anyone's calendar; "November 20: IT engineer approves the data flow diagram" is a task whose owner knows they'll be asked about it.

The third requirement is the most often overlooked. Buy-in from the head of operations alone isn't enough; the person with the authority to say "yes" must personally review the milestones and confirm they're feasible.

When to present the plan without making the client feel pressured

Rework cautions that pulling out a MAP the moment the demo ends can easily come across as a closing tactic. So timing and framing matter as much as content. Sybill suggests proposing the plan only after the client has confirmed three things in the meeting: the problem is worth solving; they know which budget path it would go through; and they want to move forward.

Applied to the invoice demo, those three confirmations might be: the head of operations says manual invoice entry is consuming headcount, explains that this expense goes through the CFO, and proactively asks what the next step is. If any one of the three is missing, don't rush to pull out the milestone table—keep asking questions until you find out.

These three confirmations also resolve the apparent tension between the caution above and "settling next steps in the meeting": once the client has just said they want to move forward, the milestone table is answering their question, not a closing tactic.

Once all three are in place, present the plan as a technical plan. Don't ask "when will you sign"; instead say "to get the pilot running, we need these things; please tell me who on your side can own each one."

The script for the last five minutes

Once the logistics company has confirmed all three, the FDE can say:

"Before we wrap up, let's settle three things. First: which metric will prove the pilot succeeded, so I can write it down. Second: I need one month of anonymized invoices, and your IT team needs to approve our access method. Who on your side can own these two items, and by what date? Third: at the end of the pilot, who decides whether to move forward? Can we schedule a meeting with that person right now?"

If the head of operations answers "I'd have to ask my boss," you've just learned something important: the person in front of you is not the decision-maker. The next step is to request a short meeting with that leader. Draft the milestone table below in that meeting and send it out within an hour:

In a real table, the owner column contains names, not job titles. Once a milestone slips, you need to know who to chase, and that person needs to know the task is theirs.

A pilot needs an end date

The last row of the table is the most important. Go-to-market consulting firm Echai Ventures believes a pilot needs four things to end in a signed deal rather than fizzling out: written success criteria, a designated decision-maker, an end date, and a price.

The table above has only the first three. The price needs to be agreed with the client owner and written into the plan before November 23, so the go/no-go meeting doesn't open a new round of negotiation.

The pilot here is exactly 30 days, from November 23 to December 22, also following the consulting firm's advice: a 90-day pilot has long lost its urgency, and deals often "die" there.

For the FDE, this deadline also helps technically: it forces you to pick a narrow, measurable workflow, such as one month of invoices, rather than promising to integrate every system.

The November 16 milestone is not arbitrary. According to Stackmatix's guide on converting pilots into contracts, the designated economic buyer with budget authority should be involved from the very start of the pilot, and the pilot should end with a formal go/no-go meeting with that same person.

If the CFO only appears in the last row, the person signing will hear about the project for the first time on the very day they must decide.

The person nodding isn't necessarily the decision-maker

In the MEDDPICC framework, the decision process is the series of steps, approvals, and dates the client goes through to complete a purchase, while the economic buyer is the person with final say over the budget. The most enthusiastic person in the demo is often not this person.

Exec's MEDDPICC guide offers a test to distinguish a true internal champion from someone who's just giving you feedback: would this person sell for you when you're not in the room? If you suspect the head of operations won't even mention the pilot in internal meetings, your plan is built on someone who won't push it.

There's another risk: the internal champion leaves or changes roles midway. Stackmatix recommends finding a replacement or escalating to a higher level, rather than waiting for the newcomer to reach out to you.

Keeping momentum after the meeting

A plan is only useful if it stays current: update it after every call and attach the latest version to every follow-up email. That turns the "just following up" email into a concrete question: "Is IT still on track for November 20?"

If the client keeps dodging—refusing to commit to dates or name owners—take that as a signal. As Rework puts it, a client unwilling to commit to a plan is usually not ready to commit to the deal either.

When you see this signal, don't increase the pressure—instead, ask directly what's making them hesitate. Finding out early that the deal won't close is far better than dragging out a pilot with no end in sight.

Common mistakes

The first mistake is ending a meeting with "I'll send over some materials," which puts all the work on you while the customer does nothing. The second is accepting milestones like "early next month," because such milestones inevitably slip.

The third is making the plan only with the end users, only to discover at the end of the pilot that the person who signs the contract never heard about it.

The fourth is pulling out the milestone table too early—before the customer has confirmed the problem is worth solving, the whole demo feels like a sales pitch. The last is letting the pilot drag on indefinitely with no go/no-go meeting.

From a career perspective, this is a skill worth spelling out clearly on your resume. Rather than writing "supported demos," describe how you turned demos into pilots with written criteria and go/no-go meetings.

When interviewing for an FDE role, ask who on the team is responsible for agreeing on next steps after a demo; the answer will tell you what the company expects of you.

The next time a customer says "we'll discuss it internally," you can respond: "Of course. So let's decide right now who will discuss it, and which day I should follow up with you."
