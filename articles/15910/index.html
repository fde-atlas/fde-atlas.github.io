OpenAI and Anthropic are both racing to build their own Forward Deployed Engineer (FDE) teams, a job title whose openings have surged sevenfold in a year. What 91APP wants to say is that this way of working is nothing new to retail brands: embedding systems on-site with customers, handling data integration, and feeding the pitfalls back into the product—91APP's consulting team has been working this way for years.

Over the past year, a job title once circulated only within a handful of tech companies saw its openings grow 729% year over year, from 643 to 5,330 (LeadDev citing Indeed and Business Insider talent market analysis, 2026). The title is Forward Deployed Engineer (FDE). In May 2026, OpenAI established a dedicated deployment company and agreed to acquire its first applied AI company, then announced a second acquisition agreement in July; Anthropic brought in three heavyweight institutions—Blackstone, Hellman & Friedman, and Goldman Sachs—to jointly establish an AI services company focused on serving mid-sized enterprises. A survey by recruiting firm Christian & Timbers showed that among surveyed companies, only 5% to 10% planned to hire FDEs in early 2026, but by the end of Q2 that figure had surged to 70% (TechCrunch, 2026).

This boom may look like a Silicon Valley novelty, but it is hardly unfamiliar to the retail and e-commerce industries.

When our team saw this news, what came to mind wasn't something novel—it was what we do every day

When our team read the FDE job-opening figures, our first reaction was a sense of déjà vu, mixed with a wry smile. What flashed to mind was the daily routine of data consultants over the past few years, sitting in brands' marketing meeting rooms with IT departments, checking data integration cell by cell in Excel: clearing data-integration pitfalls one by one, stitching old and new systems together, and organizing the problems encountered on-site into product requirements to feed back to R&D for version planning. These tasks overlap heavily with the core working style described for FDEs. The only difference is that Silicon Valley has now come up with a catchy new name for it, while data consultants in the retail industry have never had a unified external title.

Consultants give advice, SaaS gives tools, and FDEs fill the gap in between

To understand what problem FDEs actually solve, you first need to see how they typically differ from two older roles.

Strategy-oriented traditional management consultants usually work in an analyze-and-recommend mode: conduct interviews, produce slide decks, and leave—rarely writing code that goes into client systems, and rarely being accountable for how systems perform after launch. Highly standardized, self-service SaaS works in a deliver-the-tool mode: sell the product to the customer, run the training, and leave most of the operation and integration to the customer. Of course, some consulting firms and SaaS vendors' professional services teams do participate deeply in implementation; what's discussed here are two typical archetypes, not absolute categories.

FDEs fill exactly the gap between these two typical models.

  • The gap between general-purpose models and enterprise reality: no matter how powerful a large language model is, when it meets a customer's own data formats, internal systems, and business processes, someone still has to connect it and make it run.
  • The gap between consulting advice and production code: FDEs are expected not just to give advice, but to write directly into the systems customers are using and be accountable for post-launch results.
  • The gap between one-off implementation and continuous feedback: FDEs organize the pitfalls encountered on-site into features the product should add and feed them back to their own company's R&D team, so the next customer doesn't hit the same problems again.

The table below summarizes the typical differences among the three roles:

RoleTypical way of being accountable for results
Strategy-oriented traditional management consultantGives advice and produces reports, with little involvement in execution after leaving
Highly standardized SaaS self-service implementationDelivers tools and training, leaving most integration to the customer
Forward Deployed Engineer (FDE)Writes into client systems, adjusts based on launch results, and feeds on-site experience back into the product

This model was not invented in Silicon Valley. FDE borrows the military concept of "forward deployment," analogizing the engineer's role to troops stationed on the front line rather than people drawing maps in a rear command post. Around 2011, Palantir brought this concept into the tech industry, merging the previously separate Solutions Engineer and systems integration engineer roles into a single function and institutionalizing the position (Business Next, 2026; Wikipedia entry).

From Palantir to OpenAI and Anthropic: how this talent war broke out

In 2026, the FDE went from Palantir's signature playbook to standard equipment across the entire AI industry.

OpenAI's approach is to buy teams outright. In May 2026, OpenAI agreed to acquire applied AI consulting firm Tomoro, expected to bring in roughly 150 experienced Forward Deployed Engineers once the deal closes; at the same time, it established the dedicated OpenAI Deployment Company, led by TPG with 19 investment institutions and consulting firms participating, with initial funding of over $4 billion (OpenAI official announcement, 2026). On July 8, the deployment company announced an agreement to acquire Northslope, a deal still pending standard regulatory review, after which the entire deployment team is expected to grow to several hundred people. Northslope's founding team itself came from Palantir (Axios, 2026). This talent war has come full circle, back to the company that originally invented the playbook.

Anthropic's approach is to partner with financial capital. In May 2026, Anthropic announced a joint venture with private equity giants Blackstone, Hellman & Friedman, and Goldman Sachs to establish a $1.5 billion enterprise AI services company (Anthropic official announcement, 2026), targeting mid-sized enterprises with limited resources that still need AI adoption—such as community banks, mid-sized manufacturers, and regional healthcare systems—rather than Fortune 500 companies. The company was officially named "Ode with Anthropic" on July 15, 2026, built around the applied AI team Fractional AI acquired earlier, with Chris Taylor as CEO. Interestingly, a Blackstone executive involved in the partnership described the team they assembled in an interview as "special forces, not an FDE army," directly highlighting the reality that this role is in short supply (TechCrunch, 2026).

Even cloud giants have joined this talent grab. In a single 2026 hiring push, Google Cloud opened 59 FDE-related positions across four locations: the US, London, Paris, and Hong Kong (Channel Dive, 2026). Google Cloud CEO Thomas Kurian publicly stated this was to meet explosive customer demand for AI transformation and agent development. This approach of "embedding engineers directly into customer sites" actually has an older counterpart in the hardware industry: the semiconductor industry's Field Application Engineer (FAE). For white-label phone customers, MediaTek has long shipped integrated chips, reference designs, and software tools as packaged solutions, with FAEs embedded deep in customers' production lines as the core of the model, accompanying customers all the way to success (Business Next, 2026). FDEs and FAEs do very similar work—both embed engineers directly into customer sites to solve integration problems—except FDEs more often involve the formal deployment of custom software, so their scope of responsibility differs somewhat from FAEs.

This boom has also drawn plenty of skepticism. A survey by Christian & Timbers estimates that the US market currently has about 17,000 FDEs, of whom only around 2,000 are top talent truly capable of consistently delivering meaningful business results (TechCrunch, 2026). These figures may seem to contradict each other, but they actually measure different things: the 729% measures the growth rate of job postings, the 17,000 is an estimate of the headcount in the market carrying this title, and the 2,000 is the top talent the headhunting firm filtered out by its own threshold for business impact. Fast-growing job postings do not mean the supply of genuinely top talent has kept pace—that is the gap that truly deserves attention. Some tech community members have also directly questioned whether this is just "consulting with a new name," repackaging technical consulting work that has existed for decades into a cooler-sounding title; an industry commentary from GTM Engineering has also warned that once a playbook becomes so popular it turns into a "standard script," it tends to fade, just like product-led growth (PLG) did in recent years. A blog post from the Illinois Institute of Technology offers another angle: compared with "prompt engineers," who faded quickly over the past two years, FDEs require a solid foundation in software engineering and applied AI, which is why this role is more likely to endure (IIT, 2026).

As for how long this role will last, the industry has yet to reach a consensus. Stay too short, and client relationships and industry know-how never fully accumulate; stay too long, and you risk being labeled with a single industry or a single client, making it hard to switch tracks. Meanwhile, against the backdrop of the software industry as a whole continuing to cut headcount, FDE job postings are surging against the trend—a contradiction that has left many engineers on the fence about whether this boom reflects genuine demand or is just another round of capital-market hype.

Understanding the client's business matters more than PoC speed

After reviewing these cases, one easily overlooked point stands out: doing a PoC quickly and having a system that actually runs long-term in the client's hands are two different things.

A fast proof of concept (PoC) only demonstrates strong engineering capability. What truly determines success or failure is the depth of understanding of the client's business, and whether that understanding can be continuously fed back into the product, becoming an asset that prevents stepping on the same landmines in the next deployment. No matter how polished a PoC is, if no one truly understands the client's product structure, member behavior, and the legacy baggage of existing systems, the launch will most likely get stuck at the "demo runs smoothly, production keeps jamming" stage. This is exactly the point repeatedly emphasized in our four questions to ask before choosing a CDP AI agent: a vendor's demo environment and the client's real production environment are often two different worlds.

An even more easily overlooked point is that a truly excellent FDE does not simply ask clients "what do you need?" The reason is straightforward: most clients cannot clearly articulate their real problem themselves, and when asked about their needs, they often give only surface-level answers like "we'd like things to be a bit more automated." This echoes a long-circulated business parable—whose true origin no one can verify—that if you had asked consumers back then what they wanted, the answer would most likely have been "a faster horse," not a car. The value of an FDE lies precisely in uncovering the problem that clients themselves cannot articulate yet is truly holding back their business growth.

AI adoption often gets stuck, and where it gets stuck is usually not that the model isn't smart enough—it's that no one truly understands the client's data structure and business logic. AI provides the compute, but it is ultimately humans who make the judgment. This may sound like a cliché, but re-examined in the context of the FDE boom, it is actually the statement closest to the truth.

91APP's consulting team has always worked much like this

Turning the lens back to Taiwan's retail industry, this way of working is actually nothing new.

91APP's data consulting service team has long been doing something very similar: landing systems like CDMP and personalized marketing into brands' real operating environments, handling cross-system data integration, bridging the gaps between new and old platforms, and consolidating problems encountered on-site at clients into product requirements that are fed back to the R&D team. In recent years, the role of the customer success manager has evolved from a pure product consultant into one that uses data analysis to help clients identify growth opportunities—overlapping considerably with what FDEs are expected to do, just in a different industry with a different set of clients.

This way of working is not something 91APP pulled out on the spot because of this boom; 91APP's consulting service team has operated this way for years. The data retail brands have to handle is far more fragmented than pure software companies imagine: online orders, in-store POS, membership systems, advertising platforms, and customer service tickets are often scattered across different vendors and different generations of systems, and even the most powerful AI model alone cannot connect them—it would just spin its wheels. Solving exactly this problem is what 91APP CDMP is for, relying not only on the tools themselves but also on a group of people willing to keep investing and accompany brands in truly putting their systems to use.

When choosing a data and AI adoption partner, ask these questions first

The most practical takeaway of the FDE boom for Taiwan's retail brands is really a new angle for evaluating adoption partners. The following questions can be used directly the next time you evaluate an AI or data system adoption.

  • Ask about staffing stability before asking about the feature list: a beautiful feature list cannot solve the real problems of data integration. What you should really ask is whether, after adoption, the vendor assigns dedicated points of contact and a rapid-response mechanism to keep watching for pitfalls that only emerge after go-live, and whether there is a formal handover process when staff change (we recommend directly asking the vendor to explain their support cadence and response times for the first three months after adoption before signing the contract).
  • Treat "the system is still in use three months later" as one observation metric, not the only one: usage rate reflects whether the system has been genuinely adopted, but business impact metrics such as revenue growth, data quality, and error rates need separately defined KPIs and cannot be judged by usage rate alone. When evaluating vendors, you can directly ask about their past projects—what actual usage and business metrics looked like six months after go-live (recommended cadence: conduct a review at the third and sixth months after each adoption).
  • Ask how they handle requirements that clients themselves cannot articulate: if a vendor simply accepts whatever requirements list you hand over, it likely means they are still at the tool-selling stage. A truly mature adoption partner will show records of actual requirements interviews, process audits, and hypothesis validation from past projects, rather than just verbally claiming they will challenge surface-level requirements.
  • Check whether on-site feedback actually makes it back into the product: ask the vendor what concrete product changes were made in the past year because of problems encountered at client sites. If they cannot give specific examples, it may mean the vendor's consulting services and product development are two disconnected lines; it could also mean custom requirements were deliberately kept out of the core product for security or multi-tenant architecture reasons—in that case, you can follow up by asking how feedback is triaged and tracked.
  • Clarify who ultimately owns the customizations: custom features, integration logic, and even business rules developed on-site, if kept only within the adoption partner's own systems, will make it very difficult for the brand to later switch to another maintainer or take management back in-house. Confirming the ownership and portability of custom deliverables before adoption is basic due diligence for protecting your data sovereignty.

Systems don't walk to the customer on their own—people do

Silicon Valley has given an old concept a new name this year, and job postings have grown sevenfold, but the core of this has never been whether the name is trendy enough—it's whether there are people willing to keep investing and accompany clients in truly putting their systems to use. 91APP's data consulting team has spent many years on this path, starting long before FDE became a buzzword, and they will not stop just because the term fades.

For Taiwan's retail brands, this is actually good news: there is no need to fly to Silicon Valley to compete for those 2,000 top FDEs, because data consultants who work in much the same way have always been right beside you.

The most frequently asked Forward Deployed Engineer questions from brands

Q1: What exactly is a Forward Deployed Engineer (FDE)? A1: An FDE is an engineering role that works deep in the field at customer sites. The job involves understanding customers' real needs, quickly building usable prototypes, deploying systems into customers' existing environments, and feeding problems encountered in the field back to their own company's product team. The concept comes from the military term "forward deployment," and around 2011 Palantir institutionalized this role, defining it as an official position.

Q2: How is an FDE different from a traditional technical consultant? A2: Strategy-oriented traditional consultants typically only handle analysis and recommendations, leaving once the report is delivered. They rarely write code that goes into the customer's systems and rarely take responsibility for post-launch outcomes. An FDE is expected to write code directly into the systems the customer is actually using, keep iterating based on real post-launch performance, and feed lessons learned in the field back into their own company's product development.

Q3: Can small and mid-sized brands with limited budgets also find FDE-like support? A3: Yes. Ongoing on-site technical support is not exclusive to large systems. The key is that when evaluating any data or AI vendor, clearly ask whether they provide a dedicated point of contact and a continuous response mechanism after deployment, rather than looking only at one-off training or feature lists.

Q4: After deploying a system, how long until you see real results? A4: It depends on the system's complexity and the difficulty of data integration; there is no single answer. Rather than pressing for a fixed timeline, it is more worthwhile to check whether the vendor has set ongoing post-launch usage and the corresponding business metrics as success criteria, instead of treating a smooth demo as completed deployment.

Q5: Does this approach require a brand to have a high technical bar? A5: No. What a brand needs to prepare is openness in systems and data, plus organizational willingness to cooperate with on-site assessments. The technical bar can be borne by the deployment partner.

Q6: What are the common misconceptions or risks around this FDE wave? A6: A common misconception is equating FDEs with fast PoC teams, assuming that a smooth demo means successful deployment. PoC speed and deployment success rate are two different things. What determines success is the depth of understanding of the customer's business, and whether that understanding is continuously fed back into the product.
