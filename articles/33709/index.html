Healthcare FDE Job Requirements: HL7, FHIR, and Epic Integration

Original title: 医疗 FDE 岗位要求：HL7、FHIR 与 Epic 集成

FDE Times · 2026-11-19 · 5 min read

Summary

This article compares three healthcare FDE job postings and finds sharply different requirements. Optum demands 8+ years of engineering and 7+ years of consulting experience, while Qualified Health says Epic-focused candidates need only HL7 v2 and FHIR R4 conceptual knowledge and need not be developers. The author argues healthcare has two customer types—payers and providers—whose needs shape the roles. Provider-side work centers on Epic integration specs and on-site presence, like Commure engineers sitting with doctors at NEMS. Medplum says industry experience isn't required, but data-standard knowledge is. Advice: learn HL7/FHIR beforehand, build a sample integration spec, and check customer type, EHR named, and travel ratio when reading postings.

Key Takeaways

  • For the same healthcare FDE role, Optum requires 8+ years of engineering experience and 7+ years of consulting experience, while Qualified Health says you don't have to be a developer
  • Qualified Health's core work is writing integration specifications on Epic based on HL7 v2 and FHIR R4, with up to 30% travel; Commure's FDE sits right next to physicians
  • Medplum believes healthcare industry experience isn't needed, only a willingness to learn industry standards, regulations, and clinical realities

Qualified Health is hiring a Forward Deployed Engineer (FDE) focused on Epic, and the posting contains a rare statement: familiarity with the concepts of HL7 v2 and FHIR R4 is enough, "you don't need to be a developer." Meanwhile, Optum, a subsidiary of UnitedHealth Group, requires FDE candidates to have at least eight years of software engineering experience, plus at least seven years of consulting experience.

The two postings share the same job title and the same industry, yet describe completely different jobs. The most effective way to understand them is to look at which type of customer each role serves: healthcare has two types of customers, and two types of needs.

For engineers with a few years of experience looking to move into FDE work, the healthcare industry is worth a close look. On the hospital side, the barriers listed in job postings are mostly knowledge of data standards and clinical realities, both of which can be learned.

Payers and providers have different needs

In healthcare, payers refers to insurance companies; providers refers to hospitals, clinics, and health systems. Cedar, which handles patient billing, explains why so many problems have persisted for so long: insurers lack the incentive to improve processes, while providers lack the technology to reach patients at scale.

Cedar believes an FDE must get things done amid this complexity. For engineers, the role arises precisely from the gap between the two sides: neither can fill it alone, so someone needs to stand between the product and the customer's real workflows.

Optum's posting positions the FDE at the intersection of consulting, engineering, and customer delivery, serving both payers and providers. The domains named in the posting include claims, revenue cycle, clinical workflows, and member experience. Claims and member experience are the language of insurers; revenue cycle and clinical workflows are the language of hospitals.

Beyond years of consulting experience, Optum also requires hands-on experience deploying distributed systems and cloud-native solutions. Requirements like these make it hard for this to be a starting point for engineers with only a few years of experience.

At hospitals, the work all happens in Epic

The provider side is narrower, but deeper. Qualified Health's FDE is the primary technical point of contact for one or two health systems, either on-site or remote, but working closely with the customer's team. The core task is designing end-to-end integration specifications for each use case, including configuring Epic Bridges interfaces and FHIR R4 endpoints.

In a mid-to-senior role posted on October 1, 2026, Hippocratic AI listed EHR integration (Epic, Cerner, Athena), FHIR, HL7, and healthcare data standards as preferred qualifications rather than required skills. That leaves the door open for engineers who have never touched an EHR.

Consider a hypothetical case to see what such a specification requires. Suppose a hospital wants an AI tool to read a patient's information before a visit and write a summary back into the medical record.

For this use case, the specification must answer: which data goes through HL7 v2-style interfaces, which data is pulled through FHIR R4 endpoints, where in the EHR the results are written, and which parts the hospital side configures and who configures them.

None of these questions require you to write algorithms. They require you to understand how healthcare data flows through existing systems, which can be learned before you apply.

The work happens in the exam room

If integration is the technical layer, then being on-site is the craft. The head of Commure's FDE team calls the job "a physical contact sport": you have to be right there to understand problems quickly.

The story of NEMS shows how literal this is. NEMS's CIO recalls that Commure's FDEs would show up after the 7 p.m. clinic, sitting next to physicians, even next to patients.

For FDEs working with hospitals, customer research may mean sitting in the exam room, watching how a physician works while a patient tells their story. The travel percentages in job postings can tell you how much of this kind of work is involved: Qualified Health is up to 30%, Hippocratic AI is 25% to 40%.

Three postings are too small a sample to establish a pattern, but they suggest a way of looking at the market. Qualified Health's health-system-facing role relies more on data standards and on-site presence, consistent with Commure's description at NEMS. Optum weighs years of experience more heavily, perhaps because its scope spans many workflows and two types of customers.

Why engineers should consider this field

Medplum, which builds a healthcare data platform, believes buyers in this industry have always expected engineers to be on-site working alongside them, almost since the industry began. If Medplum is right, demand for FDEs in healthcare predates the current wave of FDE hiring.

Medplum also says engineers don't need healthcare industry experience. What they need is a willingness to learn the industry's standards, regulations, and clinical realities.

But this shouldn't be understood as "get hired first, then learn the standards from scratch." Qualified Health's "you don't need to be a developer" refers to coding ability; the posting still requires domain knowledge, namely familiarity with the concepts of HL7 v2 and FHIR R4. By Medplum's account, not needing healthcare experience doesn't mean knowing nothing about data standards is fine.

So if you plan to target this field, invest in the knowledge the job postings name before you apply. Learn the concepts of HL7 v2 and FHIR R4 well enough to explain the difference between them to someone else.

Then write your own integration specification for a fictional use case and put it in your portfolio, because that is exactly the deliverable Qualified Health describes.

On your resume, don't just write "did integrations." Write clearly that you specified the data flow between two systems, worked directly with end users, and pushed things to production. Integration experience from other industries is still transferable, as long as you describe it in terms of specifications.

When reading a job posting, three details reveal the real role: whether the client is a payer or a provider, which EHR is named, and what the travel percentage is. A posting that says "primary point of contact for one or two health systems" is telling you that you'll be working closely with the client every day. Make sure that's what you want first.

On the hospital side, the door to FDE roles is open to anyone willing to learn data standards. The rest is being willing to stay after the 7 PM clinic ends, long enough to understand what physicians actually need from the data.
