UK: How public accountability enters co-development

Original title: 英国：公共问责怎样进入共同开发

pafa.github.io · 2025-03-20 · 6 min read

Summary

This chapter examines how public accountability enters co-development, centered on Consult, a consultation-analysis tool built by the UK government's i.AI team within the Humphrey suite. In a Scottish Government trial on non-surgical cosmetic procedure regulation, Consult helped process over 2,000 responses; its evaluation found 60% of answer classifications went unmodified by reviewers, but themes could not be edited after sign-off and expert-engineer discussion time was insufficient. The author argues for joint review before theme confirmation and the ability to revisit themes based on sample results. Contrasting cases include Redbox, discontinued once commercial tools like Copilot covered its capabilities, and NatWest's OpenAI partnership built on its existing Cora+ and AskArchie+ assistants. The chapter concludes that build-versus-buy decisions should be revisited as products mature, and FDE value lies in specific gaps rather than co-development as an end in itself.

Key points

  • Consult, built by i.AI in the Humphrey suite, analyzed 2,000+ Scottish consultation responses.
  • 60% of classifications went unmodified, but locked themes limited expert revision.
  • The author recommends joint expert-engineer review before theme confirmation.
  • Redbox development stopped once commercial tools offered equivalent capabilities.
  • NatWest's OpenAI collaboration builds on existing assistants and internal AI governance.

A government consultation received a large volume of responses, and an engineering team can help organize them, but the responsibility for deciding what those responses mean still rests with the business agency. The most valuable part of the UK material is that it preserves the specific difficulties of expert review, so that the next build can change accordingly.

Consult: Engineering Must Leave a Path for Expert Judgment to Return

The UK's Consult offers another point of observation. It was developed by the government's AI incubator team, i.AI, and belongs to the Humphrey tool suite. A UK government explanation dated May 14, 2025 described a real consultation trial: the Scottish Government solicited opinions on the regulation of non-surgical cosmetic procedures, and Consult helped analyze more than two thousand responses; at the time the tool was still in a trial phase. 1

Public responses are not a column of pre-filled numbers. People may support stronger oversight in one sentence and worry about rising costs in the next. Staff need to identify discussion themes and then determine which themes each response touches. This involves both repetitive labor and room for interpretation.

Consult's evaluation report is dated March 20, 2025. The process was split into two steps: theme generation and response classification. Experts first reviewed the themes, then double-checked the themes the system assigned to each response. In the evaluation, 60% of response classifications were not modified by reviewers; the report also noted that confirming themes was labor-intensive and that the tool was poor at identifying missing themes. 2

The report also recorded how this strain occurred: reviewers felt there was insufficient time to discuss with i.AI engineers; once themes were signed off they could not be edited, creating anxiety that everything had to be perfect in one pass. Experts were already involved, yet lacked adequate communication and opportunities for revision after confirmation. A single confirmation thus bore too much pressure, and this cannot be solved merely by adding a step of "human review."

Only by putting these results together can the engineering problem be seen. If one reports only "most classifications needed no changes," it is easy to conclude that reviewers can simply be removed. But a classification tool can only choose among the categories it possesses. If the initial theme list omits a certain kind of opinion, the subsequent classification may look smooth while never truly hearing that voice.

This book therefore recommends arranging the joint verification between experts and engineers before theme confirmation, and preserving the opportunity to re-examine themes based on sample classification results. Experts are responsible for explaining what revisions are needed; engineers are responsible for making the changes flow into subsequent processing, keeping the before-and-after versions and the affected responses. This is a way of working yet to be built, not a feature the report proves already exists; it lets professionals point out "an important category of questions is missing here," rather than merely ticking existing classifications as approved.

What most deserves changing here is the timing of "confirmation." If themes are locked first, experts must make judgments before they have adequately seen the classification results; allowing themes to be revised based on samples adds one more processing step, but may reduce large-scale rework later. The obstacles recorded in the report are not that the model cannot classify at all, but that there are too few opportunities for professional judgment to enter the engineering process.

This is a case where the client already has an internal engineering team. If an external vendor joins, it should first look at which product capability is still missing, rather than repackaging internal development as vendor delivery. Work already carried by someone does not disappear because the team changes its name.

When Commercial Tools Catch Up, Internal Building Can Also Stop

In a separate explanation on October 16, 2025, the UK government stated that the experience from Redbox helping staff process documents would carry into future work, but that the tool would no longer be developed: commercial solutions could already provide the relevant capabilities within existing systems; the announcement cited Copilot and noted that the existing engineering knowledge would be applied to tools such as Humphrey and GOV.UK Chat. 3

This is not an announcement that all services were shut down that day, and even less can it be called a confirmed FDE failure. What it demonstrates is a genuine substitution decision: the value of continuing to build in-house changes as external products evolve. Nor does engineering knowledge already invested have to be entirely discarded when a particular tool stops being developed.

Placed alongside Consult, the UK material is no longer just a story of "government should build its own AI." Where repeated adjustment around professional judgment is needed, internal co-building has a role; where mature products already cover the need, narrowing in-house scope is also justified. Procurement and engineering are not two gates that, once passed in sequence, become irrelevant to each other; procurement choices re-enter the project after products mature.

When a Bank Already Has Systems, Which Layer Does Cooperation Plug Into

Public-sector arrangements cannot represent all UK enterprises. In its 2025 partnership announcement, NatWest, building on its existing Cora+ customer assistant and AskArchie+ employee assistant, stated it would continue developing assistant capabilities with OpenAI while adhering to the bank's own AI and data ethics code of conduct. Customer service, anti-fraud, and complaint handling were the directions listed in the announcement; the plans must not all be recorded as completed deployments. 4

This contrast keeps the UK chapter from being merely one model of government building. The bank was not waiting for a model vendor to build a system from scratch; it already had customer touchpoints, employee tools, and internal governance. An external engineering team must first understand which responsibilities remain with the bank, which touchpoint the new capability operates in, and which complex requests are routed back to human staff.

From Consult to bank assistants, what can be jointly observed is that professional organizations want to retain the interpretation and handling of outcomes. This is an organizational need exposed by the chosen cases, not a national-character judgment that "the British are more cautious." What truly affects the build is when classifications can be changed, who may alter customer records, and which consequences require a business owner to judge again.

Letting a Second Deployment Accept Different Answers

Imagine another public agency preparing to adopt a consultation analysis tool; this is a pedagogical judgment. The existing material offers it two reasonable options: buy a general-purpose text tool and have experts organize themes by hand; or build a dedicated process capable of repeatedly revising themes and re-running affected responses.

In a smaller consultation with stable topics, the former approach may save more resources. If themes must be adjusted along with expert review, and it is necessary to trace back which responses changed classification because of the adjustments, then the dedicated process has a clearer justification. After making the latter choice, project planning should prioritize leaving time for joint verification between experts and engineers, and validate "whether processing can continue after themes change," rather than only checking the speed of the initial classification.

This decision is inspired by Consult's real obstacles, but is not its later completed revamp. If the second agency can only schedule one expert discussion per week, its iteration speed cannot simply copy the first; if a commercial product already has the needed revision and trace-back features, the build-versus-buy comparison should be redone. The Redbox record reminds us that such changes can occur after a project has already been invested in.

A delivery statement suited to the next client should list separately: what the agency can already do, what professionals still need to judge, what capabilities the software must supply, and the conditions under which in-house building can stop once commercial products change. If professionals only appear at the final acceptance stage, it becomes easy to repeat the difficulty of themes already locked down that no one can confidently confirm.

The working philosophy this chapter demonstrates is modifiable professional judgment and a re-selectable way of building. It requires engineers who can hear review feedback, and an organization willing to accept that a certain internal product is no longer worth continued investment. The value of an FDE team should land on these concrete gaps, rather than treating "co-development" itself as an eternally correct goal.
