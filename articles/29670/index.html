Palantir Founding Engineer Shares In-Depth Insights: The FDE Model Is the PMF Paradigm for the Agent Era

Original title: Palantir 创始工程师深度分享：FDE 模式是 Agent 时代的 PMF 范式

FDE中国社区 · 2026 · 27 min read

Summary

This article compiles a deep-dive interview with Bob McGrew, former Palantir executive and former OpenAI chief research officer, on the Forward Deployed Engineer (FDE) model. As Palantir's second engineer, he traces how Shyam Sankar devised the model: embedding engineers at customer sites to close gaps between product capabilities and needs, delivering outcomes rather than software. He describes the Echo (embedded analysts) and Delta (deployment engineers) team structure, their hiring profiles, and why FDE differs from consulting—product teams must abstract field learnings into a general platform, as with Palantir Ontology. His core argument: AI agents have no off-the-shelf products, and product discovery must happen inside enterprises, making FDE the likely organizational and PMF paradigm for the agent era. Over 100 YC startups now hire FDEs, up from zero three years ago, and contract sizes should grow over time.

Key points

  • FDE, devised by Shyam Sankar, embeds engineers on-site to deliver outcomes, not software.
  • Echo teams handle embedded analysis and accounts; Delta teams build and deploy prototypes.
  • FDE is not consulting: product teams must generalize field work, as Ontology shows.
  • AI agents lack existing products, so discovery must happen inside enterprises—fitting FDE.
  • Over 100 YC startups hire FDEs; contract size and product leverage should grow.

Full text

The FDE (Forward Deployed Engineer) model is Palantir's unique go-to-market and product deployment model, but today it has become one of the hottest topics in Silicon Valley. According to one YC survey, more than 100 YC startups are currently hiring for FDE-related roles, while three years ago that number was zero.

This article is a compilation of the deep reflections on the FDE model by Bob McGrew, former Palantir executive and former Chief Research Officer at OpenAI. As Palantir's second engineer, Bob McGrew personally experienced the birth of the FDE model, the product building, talent selection, and team building processes. In this piece, Bob also explains in detail what Palantir's FDE model actually is, and why in today's AI agent era it represents a new organizational paradigm and PMF paradigm for startups.

• The role of the FDE team is to fill the gap between product capabilities and customer needs, with the goal of delivering valuable outcomes for customers rather than software or services;

• FDE is essentially a process of product discovery conducted from inside the enterprise;

• Although it looks like consulting or on-prem work, it is actually doing non-scalable things in a scalable way, so a general-purpose underlying product remains equally important — this is the core value of Palantir's Ontology platform;

• The absence of ready-made products for AI agents is the most important reason for the rise of FDE. AI agents cannot yet achieve scaled expansion based on standardized products the way SaaS did in the past, and agent products require a lot of product discovery work that can only start from real business practices inside enterprises — which fits the FDE model perfectly;

• Unlike standardized SaaS tools, under the FDE model a company's contract size will inevitably grow larger and larger;

• In the agent era, the FDE model is very likely to become the new organizational paradigm and PMF model for agent startups.

01.

What exactly is an FDE?

Host: A few months ago, Bob attended an AI conference organized by YC. An interesting phenomenon was that he originally expected all the founders to come talk to him about ChatGPT, but instead all of them wanted to talk about Palantir's FDE. What exactly is an FDE, and why has it become so important today?

Bob: It wasn't just that one conference. Over the past year, when I advised AI startups, many of them cared almost exclusively about how the FDE strategy works.

An FDE is typically a technical person who is embedded at the customer's site, filling the gap between product capabilities and customer needs.

As a startup, you first have a product, then you approach a new customer and try to work with them, and the problem they want you to solve is one you have never solved before.

In this process, you believe that through hard work you can solve this specific problem for this specific customer, and you will deliver a very valuable outcome for them.

So the FDE takes the existing product and, with help from the product team, figures out how to deliver that outcome, building a use case that is valuable to the customer, delivering the software you have already built in a way that truly works for the customer.

Host: Let's look back at how Palantir invented the FDE strategy model?

Bob: When Palantir was first founded, the company's focus was building software systems for the intelligence community. The challenge was that we didn't know anyone in intelligence — almost no one in the real world does — and even if we found such a person, if you asked them, "What exactly does your job involve?" they couldn't possibly tell you. This forced us to adopt an approach that was very unusual at the time.

We started this task by building a demo, first trying to show that demo to potential customers in the intelligence community and gather feedback.

Stefan Cohen, one of Palantir's founders, did exactly this. He showed the demo to potential customers and asked them, "What do you think?" They said, "This product is terrible, it has nothing to do with what we do." Cohen would then ask, "What would you want it to do differently?" And they would say, "Could you make this change and that change?" So Cohen wrote all of it down.

Ideally, in the early days of a startup you have to do a lot of things that don't scale — going out to visit customers and study users. Once you find PMF, your strategy changes: you distance yourself from users, focus on scaling, and treat all customers the same.

If a founder is in an industry where scaling can happen this smoothly, then they don't need FDEs at all. But for Palantir at the time, that wasn't possible.

The FDE approach was actually proposed by Shyam Sankar (an early Palantir member, currently President and CTO).

We found that every customer wanted something different. Rather than building a separate version for each customer, or patching on a bunch of features that only fit one specific customer, it was better to make the product a highly customizable platform. This meant we had to send employees on-site to deeply understand users and do localized adaptation.

By conventional thinking, all of this counts as services, which in the PMF paradigm framework is best avoided.

But Shyam flipped this logic: make FDE serve as the process of product discovery.

The FDE comes in with the existing product, fills the gap between product capabilities and actual needs, and paves the route first into a "gravel road." The product and engineering teams at headquarters then abstract and generalize these on-site practices, turning them into a "paved superhighway" that can serve the next five to ten customers.

Host: At Palantir, engineers face enterprise customers directly rather than professional salespeople — how did this come about? Especially when the customers are government and defense agencies, most people would tend to hire a group of experienced salespeople who have long dealt with government officials, but Palantir didn't do that?

Bob: There were two reasons:

On one hand, early on we interacted with many such people, and their reaction was: "Why would I work with a Silicon Valley company instead of one of the five big defense contractors?"

On the other hand, while these people's backgrounds seemed qualified for the job, we actually knew they couldn't fit into our culture and couldn't truly succeed. When we tried that approach, it almost never worked.

So we discovered a very different way. I think the difference between sales-led product discovery and FDE-led product discovery is that sales-led product discovery means talking to people from the outside. FDE-led product discovery is more effective because FDEs solve these problems from the inside.

You need to focus on solving the critical problems identified by leadership. If what you're solving isn't one of the CEO's top five priorities, it probably won't succeed — they won't have the energy to persist through the more challenging route of truly landing a brand-new product capability in their specific context.

Once you've solved the first critical problem, the FDE can then discover other critical problems within the enterprise—sometimes problems that may be even more valuable than your original goal. Perhaps it isn't obvious whether Palantir or your company can solve these problems, but once you're there, you can see through product insight that you actually can. Then you go solve those problems too.

So this shifts from "how do I sell the same thing to every customer" to "land and expand."

Host: Can you elaborate on how Palantir's FDE model works?

Bob: The first question to think about is how the FDE team is structured. There are two key roles, which we call the Echo team and the Delta team.

The Echo team, specifically, consists of embedded analysts. They go on-site to customers, talk with users, and try to figure out what kind of demo or use case is truly valuable to users in that scenario, and what the key problems to solve are. At the same time, they also serve as account managers, so they are the ones managing the customer relationship.

The Delta team, as deployment engineers, are essentially software engineers. These people are usually very good at writing code quickly—they're the ones who bring those ideas into the real world. They build solutions and prototypes, then turn them into products that actually run, and ultimately deploy them for the customer.

All of this happens within a very short time frame. The FDE team goes in with an idea for a project to do, and sets a date a few months out to give leadership a demo showing their progress. If that demo goes well, it gets truly deployed and rolled out across the customer's entire organization.

02.

How to Build an FDE Team

Host: What's interesting about these two roles is that they are completely different types of people with different backgrounds. How do you find the right people for these roles? Because not just any ordinary engineer can be an FDE—they need stronger abilities to talk with users, or the Echo team also needs a stronger technical background rather than just being account managers. How does Palantir build its FDE teams?

Bob: The ideal profile for the Echo team is a domain expert. For example, it might be a former Army officer, or someone with deep work experience in healthcare. They have deep domain knowledge. Beyond that, one more thing is very important: Echo team members need to have a "rebel" quality—Sham even calls them "heretics." These are people who not only understand the existing way of doing things, but can also recognize where that way falls short. Because if you can't see the problem, you'll never come up with new software or the kind of step-change it can bring.

For the Delta team, what we need are people who are extremely good at prototyping.

The wrong hire for Delta is the perfectionist craftsman type—someone who is great at writing code, loves being absolutely correct at the level of abstractions, and hopes the software they build will be maintainable for the next decade or more.

What we really want is the kind of person who can figure out how to get a product implemented, even if the code they write is rough. Sometimes, if you find the right person, their code is also beautiful, but most of the time it isn't, and that's not the core of the Delta team anyway. The Delta team consists of people who can actually deliver results in the form of software within a set timeline. Even if the first version they write isn't high quality and has to be thrown out and redone, they're excellent at prototyping—that's the key skill for this team.

Host: The talent profiles for Echo and Delta sound a lot like a startup team. Does Palantir recruit former startup founders to fill these roles? Or is it mostly the other way around?

Bob: Palantir has incubated a surprising number of startups, and that's no coincidence, because FDE training is training to become a startup founder—you're actually learning all the skills of a startup founder. Back when we first started doing this, there weren't a lot of founders available for us to choose from.

The Palantir Mafia: Palantir alumni, early collaborators, and investors have formed a tight-knit alumni network. They share recruiting and fundraising information in private groups (such as Palantir Pals), and also organize offline events (such as camping on the Russian River in Sonoma), forming a powerful network of connections and capital. Luba Lesiva, Palantir's former head of investor relations, also launched Palumni VC, which invests exclusively in alumni companies.

Palantir alumni have founded or run 350+ tech companies, at least a dozen or more of which have reached unicorn status. Representative companies and people include:

• Defense tech company Anduril (founders Trae Stephens, Matt Grimm, and Brian Schimpf are all Palantir alumni), valued at roughly $30.5 billion as of June 2025

• Data analysis and collaboration platform Hex (founders Barry McCardel, Caitlin Colgrove, and Glen Takahashi are all Palantir alumni)

• Public safety data platform Peregrine (founder Nick Noone previously led Palantir's special operations business; valued at roughly $2.5 billion after its March 2025 funding round)

• Medicare navigation and brokerage platform Chapter (founder Cobi Blumenfeld-Gantz is a Palantir alumnus)

• AI voice company ElevenLabs (co-founder Mati/Mateusz Staniszewski is a former Palantir FDE)

• Digital contracting platform Ironclad (co-founder Cai GoGwilt is a former Palantir engineer)

• Digital health insurance company Angle Health (founder Ty Wang and CTO Anirban Gangopadhyay both came from Palantir engineering)

• Fintech company Blend (founder Nima Ghamsari worked at Palantir early in his career)

• Developer tools company Sourcegraph (CTO Beyang Liu previously worked as an engineer at Palantir)

• Sales intelligence company RelateIQ (co-founder Adam Evans previously built Palantir's health business)

You're absolutely right—what FDE teams do at each customer site is indeed a bit like being a founder. But the difference for FDE members is that they're founders with access to some very powerful product leverage, which makes your job easier. I think it's great training, and that may be why we're now seeing so many founders coming out of Palantir.

03.

FDE Is Not Consulting—Building General Products Matters Just as Much

Host: People who don't know much about FDE often say: "This is just consulting wrapped up in fancy marketing language." Why is that claim wrong?

Bob: Objectively speaking, FDE does carry the risk of becoming that kind of situation. If you had talked to people about Palantir in 2015, you would probably have heard two main things:

• First, Palantir is evil (note: in its early days, Palantir worked with intelligence agencies on espionage-related business),

• Second, it was a consulting business that could never scale—it wasn't a software company, but a consulting firm.

In the past, we actually spent a lot of time trying to understand whether this assessment was correct.

In terms of business model, a key trend we would see is: when we go to a new customer for a new project deployment, at the beginning we may actually be losing money, but as we spend more time with the customer, our product becomes better suited to the customer's needs through product exploration, so we no longer need a large team on-site at the customer to figure out what they need. At the same time, as Sham said, we gradually earn the right to work on the more important problems at the customer's site.

So the end result is that we see the cost per value of our deliverables declining. In other words, our profit margins start out negative, but eventually, after a period of time—maybe a year, maybe several years—they turn positive.

If you look at it from this angle, you'll see that we are actually delivering genuinely repeatable value. The product team is key to making this model work and driving down costs.

Host: How does the product team work with the FDE team?

Bob: Palantir has two core teams: one is the FDE team, and the other is the product management team.

The products we build are not highly verticalized; they are not standard workflows like Airbnb's that can simultaneously support the needs of millions of people. The product team's role is to truly own the product vision. So they have to think: when they see a new problem at a customer site, what would its generalized form look like, and what version could be applied to the next 10 customers?

A typical failure example is when an FDE builds something for one customer and then directly introduces it into the product for everyone. The result is that what the FDE built is too specialized for other customers. The key here is having product people who are capable of building more generalized products.

Host: So you need some know-how to judge whether something only applies to this vertical or can be generalized. Can you give us a concrete example?

Bob: Probably the best example is the invention of Palantir Ontology.

When we first considered working with the US government, especially intelligence agencies, the instinctive approach was: create a table for "people," create another table for "funds"...

But we quickly discovered that going down this path, once you needed to deploy across different agencies and customers, you would run into the problem of not being able to generalize. So we raised the level of abstraction, no longer presupposing specific object types, and instead let FDEs define things according to each customer's context. The underlying layer only provides generic objects, properties, and so on. Palantir Ontology was born out of this line of thinking.

Host: How does Ontology work now? Is there a base database schema containing generic reusable objects like people and money, which is then customized for each site?

Bob: The database schema itself is extremely generic, keeping only: objects, properties, media, and links between objects.

I'm referring here to Palantir's government product (which was also our first product). As for the specific semantics in each customer scenario, those are encoded by the Ontology—for example, labeling a certain entity as a "person," labeling a certain entity as a "ship," or labeling a set of relationships as a "flow of funds."

If we only built features for a single customer, we would be held hostage by that customer's way of expressing things—for example, we would start thinking "for people, we'll do it this way." The right approach is to raise the level of abstraction further: think "do we have a generic operation that applies to all objects with a certain property?" For example, "people" and "ships" may both have a certain property, but payment records do not, so the processing logic cannot be one-size-fits-all. This requires you to always think at a higher level of abstraction.

We didn't hire product managers for a long time. When we finally needed to hire product managers, I interviewed quite a few people who were excellent at other companies, but getting them to think at this level of abstraction was not easy. Many would immediately fall into "designing a specific workflow for this customer," but in Palantir's model, that's the wrong thing to do. They need to elevate their perspective to the ontology layer and think about how to adjust the modeling within the ontology framework so that a specialized feature can be reused across customers.

04.

Why FDE Is the PMF of the Agent Era

Host: Today almost all of these AI companies are hiring FDEs at scale. What do you think is the reason behind this? Why didn't the previous generation of SaaS companies do this? Even after Palantir succeeded and the FDE model became better known, FDE was still seen as a special case, because people believed Palantir was a unique company, one selling to the government.

Bob: I'm also surprised by this trend. But my advice to those considering trying the FDE strategy is: unless you absolutely need FDE, don't actually try it yourself, because you may end up doing services. If, after a series of attempts, you find that you will definitely fail without FDE, then FDE may be the only viable way in your market, and it may well be a moat for you.

As for why the FDE trend has emerged in today's AI agent market, perhaps we can return to this question: why did Palantir have to adopt this model?

Palantir's market is not a single coherent market. We work with national intelligence agencies, national law enforcement agencies, and the military. Although all of these organizations have some similar projects, even between a counterproliferation workflow and a counterterrorism workflow there are huge differences—one is figuring out who is building nuclear bombs, the other is figuring out who is building IEDs—and in how they operate, they are actually very different.

So there is enormous heterogeneity here. We should think of the market as different segments, and within each segment, you can build something, and the Crossing the Chasm story applies to some extent.

At first it seems like nothing works, then suddenly you find PMF in a certain segment, and you can deploy to the people doing that kind of workflow. Then at the next customer, you find the same kind of people doing similar workflows, and you can deploy with very little customization.

So now you want to go after a different segment, and you have to develop a new technology, which can then be referenced in other segments. A segment doesn't necessarily equal a customer, especially in enterprises or very large organizations like governments, where one customer may mean tens of thousands of users. In that case, the FDE strategy becomes important, because you're doing things that don't scale, but you're doing them scalably, over and over again, for each segment you enter.

I think another thing that's unique about Palantir is that we were building a completely new type of product.

Users were used to doing analysis and tracking people with a tool that looked like PowerPoint, and they collaborated by sending these files back and forth to each other. But the product we built essentially said: "When you're drawing that link chart, you're not just editing a file, you're actually changing a database, and everyone has the same database." While to the user, this seemed like a small change on top of the work they were already doing, for the enterprises and organizations we sold to, it was a completely different market category.

I think that's what's happening with AI agents—it's a completely new market category.

If you're implementing a standard SaaS product, you're replacing one way of paying bills with another way of paying bills, and everyone understands what that market is. There aren't that many fine-grained segments in such a market, and there isn't that much exploration of comparable products—you try to build a product better than the existing one and scale by displacing it.

But with AI agents, there's no existing product. Building an AI agent could actually mean many different things, and we don't yet know what those are—we have to figure it out. It's even possible that five years from now we'll look back and realize AI agents weren't one thing at all, and that we were actually doing all these different things.

So that's why I think you're seeing the FDE model rise: there's so much product exploration work to be done, and you can only do it from inside enterprises.

Host: What does this have to do with the classic YC advice to "do things that don't scale"?

Bob: That's advice YC gives to early-stage founders, and the FDE model is actually doing those things that don't scale at scale.

Host: A lot of people are now trying to adopt the FDE model, including many who never worked at Palantir. Have you seen where people are getting it wrong?

Bob: The most successful startups doing the FDE model that I've seen so far all have people from Palantir running their FDE operations.

As I said, engineering teams tend to be quite similar, but the actual FDE operating mechanics—how to establish these customers, how to define outcomes, and so on—are actually very different from a standard software company.

One key difference, and I think one that people find hard to grasp, is: how to choose a problem, and then how to price that problem. Fundamentally, with the FDE model you're not selling the installation of a specific piece of software—you're selling an outcome. As Sham would say, you're selling the fact that you solved a problem.

Host: In the SaaS era, you'd price based on usage, subscriptions, or seats. FDE is completely different—it's outcome-based. How should all these AI founders price their solutions?

Bob: Yes. I think one very important difference between the FDE model and the standard SaaS model is that with the SaaS model and PMF, people tend toward very simple, repeatable contracts and very simple, repeatable pricing that makes sense across all customers, and SaaS teams are usually quite happy with small-dollar contracts because the marginal cost of deployment is very low.

With the FDE model, on the other hand, business interactions get pushed toward larger and larger contracts. As we discussed earlier, each customer's contract value grows over time. Because the product requirements are complex, the contracts are complex too, and both the product and the contracts need to be more flexible and elastic to accommodate that complexity.

Host: Some YC companies are starting to do this too. For example, Kastle is an AI voice agent serving mortgage servicing for bank customers; they measure outcomes by the number of calls successfully handling all these mortgage requests, and their contract sizes are gradually increasing. Happy Robot mainly builds AI voice agents for the logistics space, working with large companies like DHL—a similar situation.

Bob: There's an asymmetry here between the startup and the party it's selling to, which is usually a large enterprise.

When startups sell to large enterprises, first, enterprise customers often don't believe they can actually get anything done themselves—that's because they've often had many failed large projects—and they also don't believe the startup team can get anything done, because they assume your startup is just like them.

But the startup knows it can actually execute and deliver. So early on, it makes sense for the startup to take on all the risk and say, "We believe in our own execution. We'll take the risk—you pay us if we succeed, or pay us when we're able to expand."

Host: One place this can go wrong is if what the startup builds needs to be deployed inside the enterprise—on-premise, or even if just any part of it needs to run locally—then there's friction and coordination needed with the enterprise's internal IT team.

Bob: We've seen this at some companies too. The question may be: whose support inside the organization does an FDE project's success require winning? Because those people don't think like startups, and their interests aren't aligned with the end users. So you have to find ways to work around them.

That's why it's important for startups doing FDE to focus on solving one of the CEO's top five problems—because when you hit internal organizational resistance, you need to be able to bring in someone from the top who says, "Yes, give them access to operate, they need to use this particular type of database..."

Host: How did Palantir get executive buy-in from the companies it worked with?

Bob: The core is that you need truly excellent leadership on the FDE team to have that discipline, and to share what works, applying that discipline and those methods at one customer.

I don't think it's anything particularly magical or hard to do. The best companies I've seen doing this learned it from people who had done it before. It can be learned—we learned it.

Host: YC has a concept called the Collison install. We basically boil it down to: don't wait for people to come to your website—go find them, get them to install the software, go to them in person, go to their offices, sit next to them. I've always thought that's a great starting strategy.

But most startups can't land big contracts at the start. So in practice, the reason they eventually have to stop this manual, high-touch process is that without a product that can scale, you simply can't sustain your growth rate. It's a bit like what we discussed earlier: at some point, you want the product you've built to be good enough that people can handle it themselves, and then all your problems become scaling problems.

With AI, though, the difference is that because these contracts are now so large, you can actually go quite far doing this high-touch stuff. A question I get asked a lot is: "How far can I push this?" My advice is broadly: "It's fine to do custom work for each customer—you just want the degree of customization per customer to keep decreasing."

What's your take on this? How do you know whether it's okay to keep adding new customers in this high-touch way, or whether I actually need to start abstracting and building a real product—when do I need to change strategy?

Bob: Yes. I think this actually captures the key difference between a PMF strategy and an FDE strategy.

In a PMF strategy, teams want to do less and less work per customer, reduce costs, and keep contract size constant.

But in an FDE strategy, the expectation becomes increasing contract size, and increasing contract size means you're doing more and more valuable work for that customer and for future customers. Because you're doing more valuable work, it's fine to keep the level of customization per customer constant.

Host: So the KPI is contract size, not necessarily how much customization they do per customer?

Bob: There are two useful things here.

First, the thing you can measure is contract size. I'd even put it more generally: the value of the outcomes you deliver, because that's the real thing. If you can deliver increasingly valuable outcomes for customers, then you're doing it right.

The second part is the value of the product. So the other thing to measure is whether you're gaining more and more product leverage to amplify that outcome.

When you're in the middle of it, all of this is very counterintuitive.

If you're doing the FDE model, or leading an FDE team, there are many things you must do that look very counterintuitive, and it's very hard, because you have to build for customers things they didn't ask for but actually want.

And on the product side, you need to think about how to make a product that's very easy for every customer to use. Because if you care about user experience, you have to do that, but at the same time you must remember that your other key customer is the FDE. Your product should ultimately deliver good things to users after the FDE customizes it; the product should provide leverage for the FDE delivering that outcome at the customer site, and the amount of product leverage should increase over time.

Host: As in, the FDE should be able to deliver more value to customers with your product, without having to pull in three more engineers to do it.

Bob: Yes. If the first customer you deploy for takes a lot of work, then if you want to sell the same outcome to another customer, it should be much easier. As you go from one customer to the next, it should get easier and easier.

But remember, the FDE should be building a platform, so you're not just doing a stack of vertical use cases. You need to properly abstract out the core concepts you're really building, which makes things easier for you and gives you more product leverage, even when you're not doing that exact use case but something similar. Or you may find that if your product is really good, your FDEs can come up with new ways to use the technology you've built to solve completely different problems.

Host: It's almost like an internal market dynamic at work. If you build it well enough, the FDEs should choose to use it, and you should see demand from FDEs for your kind of abstracted product, rather than them building their own one-off solutions.

Bob: Yes. But I want to point out that this is a very painful process for everyone involved. In the FDE world, I can't use the word "pain" enough.

There were many times when I built something I thought was great and beautiful—not quite there yet, but I thought it would really help the FDEs, if only they had the ability to see it. I'd say: "Please use my product." And they'd say: "No, it's too much work, it's useless."

Honestly, most of the time I was probably wrong—I built the wrong product for them, and I should have seen that. But sometimes I was on the right track and just hadn't done enough to make it easier for the FDEs to use. So I would send developers on-site to deploy those early solutions and help them get over the hump.

Host: Is the FDE model continuously effective? Or should founders sometimes decide top-down and say, "I just want you to do it this way"?

Bob: Both approaches can be right in different situations. The right answer here is largely a matter of judgment.

Going back to this question of product vision—from one customer to the next three, to the next ten—what is the right general-purpose product? When you look at the first customer, you really don't have the information needed to answer that question. So it purely becomes a matter of judgment.

Host: In the SaaS world, engineers resist demo-driven product development, but in the FDE context, demo-driven is important?

Bob: Actually, I think if you have the right type of product, demo-driven development works very well.

In Palantir's early days, we really only had one demo, a workflow where you were stopping a terrorist plot. We started with just one feature, and every time we integrated a new feature, we had to think: how do I showcase this new feature in a way that's genuinely helpful to the analyst going through this demo, stopping this plot? When we integrated histograms, we had to ask: how would we actually use this, and how does it work with the features we already had? Same question when we integrated maps.

If you look at the world through the lens of "what am I building," you're thinking about your capabilities, and you might consider each feature in isolation and how to build the best version of each. But when you're building a demo, you're thinking from the customer's perspective.

A good demo is one you show to the customer where you create desire in their mind for what you've done. They have to see what you've built and just want to reach out and grab it and bring it into their lives. If you see that, then you know you've found a real pain point for the customer. And doing this also forces you to build a better product, because you're not just thinking about whether each feature makes sense on its own, but also about how they work together.

If I'm going to show this demo over and over, even something as simple as moving from one feature to another has to be very smooth. These are product pain points you often see, but usually only after you've actually deployed to customers. So the role of the demo is that it shifts your focus from thinking "what can I build" to "what does the customer want," and "am I solving that pain point for the customer?"

Host: It sounds a bit like you have to continuously do gradient ascent in this very, very high-dimensional, multi-dimensional space, constantly changing the variables (a math metaphor).

Bob: Yes. Maybe this is a really crucial point: the type of company you have to build is a learning company, and I think everyone wants to build a learning company.

But if you're a company like Google or Meta, it's easy not to learn, because what you're doing now is working. If you just keep doing it, the market is growing, everyone wants to do what you're doing, and you can just coast on the same strategy, and it keeps paying off for you.

My advice to people considering which company to join is: I tell them to join a young company—not necessarily a small one, but a young one. Because a young company is still figuring things out, still learning; it hasn't succeeded yet. If you've just graduated from college, you should join a young company that's growing very fast, and then you'll see what success looks like, which positions you to be a founder of your own company later.

That's why Palantir has spawned so many other startups—because even as a very large company, it remains a company where everyone is constantly learning and focused on learning, and always works as hard as a startup.

Host: You recently joined another very large organization, the U.S. Army Reserve. What experiences from Palantir can be applied to this new role?

Bob: The Army leadership feels very different from when we worked with them in Palantir's early days. Back then (around 2005 or 2010), the Army leadership had already laid out their plan for transforming the Army. They clearly understood that the military needed to shift from the warfare of Iraq and Afghanistan to the kind of warfare we see in Ukraine today, or what it would look like if we faced large-scale combat operations in the Pacific.

When they brought in Palantir, they pointed us toward the Army's priorities and assigned each of us a domain where we should operate. But they also gave us the freedom to go out and find problems, work directly with officers on the ground to solve them, or, if needed, escalate them to leadership and get them resolved.

So I think one really interesting thing is that, in many ways, this is very much like running the FDE strategy within the Army. We get to see: what are the leadership's top five priorities? Can we make progress on them? But at the same time, in a world where there's a disconnect between what leadership wants and how things have been implemented for 20 years, change takes a long time. So we're helping them make that change. I'm really eager for the opportunity to contribute there.

Host: What do you think are the best opportunities right now for startup founders?

Bob: I think it really comes back to the question of why AI agents are adopting the FDE strategy.

The trend we're seeing today is that AI capabilities are actually improving very quickly. Some argue that people feel AI has plateaued somewhat after GPT-5, but if you look at the period between the best model in April 2024, the release of GPT-4o, and the release of o3 in April 2025, that was actually a very rapid pace of progress. I believe this trend will continue, and we'll see AI model capabilities keep advancing rapidly.

But what's truly striking is that AI adoption is nowhere near as fast as the progress in capabilities. It's very likely that over the next 5 years, the world's AI capabilities will keep advancing at breakneck speed, yet our experience in the real world will feel increasingly mediocre. It's like how we sit in a Waymo now without thinking "Oh my god, self-driving has arrived!"—instead we think "Traffic is so congested, so slow."

The FDE opportunity lies in the fact that right now is a great time to bridge the gap between what AI capabilities can actually do and what customers can adopt. Large-scale adoption of AI requires a methodology—it doesn't just happen on its own. It demands human creativity, exploration, and dealing with a huge number of problems to achieve. So I think there's a massive opportunity in looking at what capabilities exist today, and figuring out how to make them genuinely useful to people.

Host: An analogy comes to mind—it might be a bit of a stretch—but it's as if OpenAI is the headquarters product team, while startups are the FDEs, out in the field figuring out how to get the research OpenAI develops at its headquarters office adopted.

Bob: I think that analogy isn't bad at all. In fact, I think that may be exactly why the whole FDE strategy is becoming hot again.
