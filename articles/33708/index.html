New FDE Direction: An Overview of RL Environments and Evals Roles

Original title: FDE 新方向：RL 环境与评测岗位解析

Prime Intellect and Labelbox are hiring FDEs to turn customers' real workflows into RL environments and automated verifiers.

Key takeaways

  • Both Prime Intellect and Labelbox are hiring FDEs whose main job is building RL environments, eval frameworks, and verifiers.
  • Epoch AI sees enterprise workflows as a major growth area for RL environments; Wing VC notes that enterprises typically access these services through forward deployed engineering.
  • Wing VC also cautions that environment companies lacking research depth may end up as on-site outsourcing teams.

Labelbox is hiring for a role called "Forward Deployed Engineer, RL Environments." According to the job posting, the main work is building isolated, reproducible execution environments for AI agents to use during training.

On its own, that's just one more job title. But put it alongside a Prime Intellect posting and recent market analysis, and the trend becomes much clearer.

The core skill of a Forward Deployed Engineer (FDE) is sitting next to the customer, understanding their real work, and turning it into something measurable. That skill is now being packaged as a product and sold to AI labs.

For engineers already doing FDE work or wanting to, two questions follow: if the market is buying the ability to write evals for customers, how deep should this craft be learned? And is deployment work turning into data outsourcing?

The job postings describe a different kind of product

Prime Intellect calls the role a forward-deployed research engineer. One of the core responsibilities is designing and building reinforcement learning (RL) environments, eval harnesses, and verifiers tailored to each customer. The method is still classic FDE practice: embed with strategic customers to understand their agent architectures, failure modes, and product goals.

The most revealing line is in the skills section. Prime Intellect wants candidates with strong intuition for eval design—the ability to look at a customer's agent and quickly judge what needs to be measured. The company lists evals as a core competency of the role, not an afterthought once deployment is done.

Labelbox is moving in the same direction from the data-vendor side. Its Alignerr division works directly with leading AI labs to produce RL environments and eval benchmarks, with Fortune 500 companies among its customers.

Within a single company, the line between data vendor and FDE is blurring.

Why labs need people close to real work

Labelbox gives its reasoning in the job posting: the quality of RL environments is one of the biggest bottlenecks in training agentic AI today. Bad environments teach agents bad habits. Good environments require someone who understands what agents will actually do in the real world.

In a Q&A on RL environments, Epoch AI named enterprise workflows as an important growth area, citing examples like Salesforce, reporting, and spreadsheets.

Epoch also argues that product companies are natural partners for building environments around their own software. The partner provides the workflow, the FDE builds the environment, and the customer's real work flows directly into the training loop.

Lab demand is substantial. According to TechCrunch, Anthropic leadership has discussed spending over $1 billion on RL environments within a year. According to Epoch, such contracts typically run six to seven figures per quarter.

TechCrunch also reported that established data-labeling company Surge has set up a dedicated RL environments division internally. Data vendors are flooding into this niche.

How a CRM workflow becomes an environment

A hypothetical example shows what this work involves. A CRM customer wants an agent to read notes after sales calls, update the stage of each deal, and create follow-up tasks. An FDE working the traditional way would wire up the API, write the prompts, test with a few dozen records, and ship.

Turning that workflow into an RL environment takes three more steps. First, build an isolated copy of the CRM preloaded with fixed data, so the thousandth run is identical to the first.

Second, write the tasks as concrete test cases. For example, if a note says "the customer agreed to sign a trial contract," the deal stage must change to negotiation, and there must be exactly one follow-up task assigned to the account owner.

The third step is the hardest: writing verifiers. The verifier scores only the final state of the CRM, not the agent's replies. The agent can smoothly report "all updated," but if the record is still stuck at the old stage, or two duplicate follow-up tasks were created, the score is 0.

Failure modes observed while sitting with the customer—duplicate records, wrong owners—become test cases.

This example shows why Prime Intellect puts eval intuition first. Docker and RL algorithms are the easy part. The hard part is defining what "doing a job right" means, and only people inside the customer's industry fully understand that job.

Enterprises buy the people first, then the environments

In its analysis of the RL environment market, Wing VC observed that most enterprises don't buy environments directly; this work typically reaches enterprises through forward deployed engineering. In other words, FDEs are the distribution channel. Without someone sitting next to the customer, real workflows never make it into environments.

Wing also offers a counterwarning: environment startups lacking research depth, or that haven't earned labs' trust, may stall out as mere forward deployed teams.

That path can dead-end: people on site, but without the ability to design high-quality training signals, the company ends up selling hours, not a product.

Putting Wing's two observations together, the concern raised at the start gets a fairly clear answer. Deployment work isn't turning into selling data; it's growing a new branch whose value lies in converting an understanding of customer work into trustworthy measurement. Good measurement is valuable; hours alone are not.

What to prepare if you want this path

When reading job postings, watch for phrases like RL environments, evaluation harness, verifier, reproducible sandbox, or failure modes. Roles mentioning these are hiring people to design measurement, even if the title still says FDE or applied research. If a posting only mentions integration and delivery, it's the traditional deployment track.

The skill to practice is the chain from the CRM example: break a vague workflow into concrete tasks, build a reproducible environment, then write scoring programs based on the final state. Don't just write "did evals" on your resume—spell out what you measured, which failure modes you caught, and what decisions the evals drove.

One habit worth developing from the start: before turning a customer's workflow into an environment, first ask exactly what the contract permits it to be used for.

When a company's real work might become training material for someone else's model, the FDE should be the first to ask who owns that workflow, rather than waiting for legal to ask on their behalf.

The benchmark for the previous generation of FDEs was whether the system could go into production. The next generation may also have to answer a harder question: can you prove the AI agent did the work correctly?
