FDE vs SPE: forward deployed vs special projects engineer

open-fde.ai · 2026-10-31 · 2 min read

Summary

This article compares the Forward Deployed Engineer (FDE) with the Special Projects Engineer (SPE). An FDE owns a single customer's deployment, making the product work within that customer's data, workflows, and constraints. An SPE takes on an unclear, consequential problem inside the company that no team owns and carries it across boundaries to a result. The piece walks through each stage of both jobs—discovering, framing, building, deploying, measuring, and scaling—to show where the roles align and diverge. It stresses that titles vary by company, neither role indicates seniority, and the comparison is editorial. It links to further reading, including OpenAI's forward deployed engineering team and an account by Nick Wong.

Key points

  • FDEs own customer deployments; SPEs own unowned internal problems.
  • The craft is shared; the problem and location differ.
  • Comparison covers discover, frame, build, deploy, measure, scale stages.
  • Neither role is a seniority level; titles vary by company.
  • Further reading includes OpenAI FDE and Nick Wong's SPE account.

Full text

Updated

A Forward Deployed Engineer (FDE) owns a deployment for one customer and makes the product work inside that customer’s data and workflows. A Special Projects Engineer (SPE) owns an unclear, consequential problem inside the company and carries it across team boundaries to a result. The craft is shared; the problem is different.

Aspect Forward Deployed Engineer Special Projects Engineer
The job Own the deployment. Make a product work inside one customer’s data, workflows, and constraints. Own the unclear problem. Take a consequential problem nobody owns and carry it to a result.
Where you work With the customer: their systems, their users, their stakeholders. Inside the company, across team boundaries: infrastructure, operations, new products.
Discover Observe the customer’s workflow and earn enough context to challenge the initial request. Find a consequential problem between teams. Establish who can authorize a change and who will operate it.
Frame Negotiate scope with the customer and align the pilot with the product’s actual capabilities. Compare candidate projects and explain why this opportunity deserves attention now.
Build Make the integration work within the customer’s real permissions and operating constraints. Create a useful first version, then test the assumptions that justified the project.
Deploy Train customer operators and make the handoff part of delivery. Resolve cross-team ownership and ensure the project survives beyond its original builder.
Measure Review customer outcomes and use the evidence to decide whether to expand. Revisit the original business case and decide whether to continue, change direction, or stop.
Scale Bring repeated field problems to product and reduce the next deployment’s effort. Decide whether the solution should become a product, shared platform, or well-maintained local tool.
