How to Measure and Contract FDE Outcomes | Designing to Treat Man-Hours as Investment, Not Revenue

Original title: FDEの成果をどう測り、どう契約するか｜工数を売上ではなく投資にする設計

TIMEWELL · 2026-09-15 · 12 min read

Summary

TIMEWELL's Hamamoto argues that FDE hours should be treated as investment, not revenue. Results are measured by changes in a business user's daily work—such as approval wait time dropping from 47 to 12 minutes—rather than technical metrics. Contracts use fixed 45–90 day periods, three-tier outcome metrics, and six clauses, with knowledge ownership split three ways: reusable patterns go to the product, company-specific branches and dictionary contents stay with the customer. The piece cites a16z, Palantir's path to 80% gross margin by 2023, and AWS, Anthropic/FIS, and OpenAI practices. The key distinction from traditional contracting is that renewal incentives are excluded from the contract. The author honestly notes limits: engagements lacking measurable metrics, feasible timelines, or reusable patterns are declined.

Key points

  • Measure outcomes by the user's daily metrics, not technical ones.
  • On-site engineer hours should decrease per customer; learning flows back into the product.
  • Contracts use 45–90 day periods and six clauses; fees are never based on accumulated hours.
  • Knowledge split: patterns to product, branches and dictionary contents to the customer.
  • The core line from traditional contracting: no renewal incentive built into the contract.

Hello, this is Hamamoto from TIMEWELL Inc.

This is the ninth installment of the FDE practice series. So far, I have written about on-site techniques, how to structure a week, and market figures. This time, I will write about the premise that makes all of that possible: how to measure results and how to structure contracts.

Our company keeps saying that FDE man-hours are an investment, not revenue. This phrasing sounds nice, but unless the way results are measured and the contract structure differ from conventional development contracts, it is just a turn of phrase. If you sell man-hours by time, end at acceptance, and take no learning home with you, that is just conventional development under a different name. In this article, I will write openly about what we measure, what we put in contracts, what we hand over to customers, and what we return to the product. If you want to check first which type your own contracts resemble, please use the AI Literacy Diagnostic.

What to measure: not technical metrics, but the metrics of the person in charge's day

First, what to measure. We do not measure results with technical metrics. 95% accuracy, 2-second response time, 99.9% uptime. These are product quality metrics, not results metrics. Results metrics lie in the day of the person in charge. Did approval wait time drop from 47 minutes to 12 minutes? How many rework requests per week decreased? How many hours of month-end closing overtime were reduced?

The measurement method is as I wrote in the video analysis article. Before deploying anything, count the same tasks. The week after deployment, count again. The difference is the result. If there is no consent for filming, count using the five-column observation records. As long as the counting method does not change, the differences can be compared.

Why not measure with technical metrics? There are two reasons. One is that if you measure with technical metrics, the evaluation ends before reaching production. 95% accuracy can be achieved in a conference room demo. A 12-minute approval wait can only be achieved when the person in charge uses it every day. The other is that, as the person who led Palantir's commercial division wrote, the FDE acts as a "zero-authority CEO" of the customer's business, a role responsible for business outcomes rather than deliverables 1. Business outcomes cannot be expressed in technical metrics.

We divide metrics into three layers. For management, industry metrics; for division heads, this quarter's numbers; for the front line, daily time. As I wrote in the interview article, the three layers want different things about the same project. So we set three metrics and, in the first week, look for a single point where all three point in the same direction. If we can draw a line such as "shortening front-line approval wait time shortens the division's shipping lead time, which leads to the delivery competitiveness management talks about," we measure all three along that line. If we cannot draw that line, we do not take on the project.

Man-hours are an investment, not revenue: the three ratios we watch

Next, how we view man-hours. In June 2025, a16z's Joe Schmidt wrote about giving up short-term gross margin to buy depth. It is a management decision to thicken implementation and temporarily lower margins in order to buy a moat that controls the entry point of business operations 2. Also at a16z, Marc Andrusko organized in January 2026 that the genuine model explicitly holds time-boxed deployments and an engineer-to-ARR ratio, and that engineering man-hours decrease with mature customers 3.

There are three ratios we watch. The first is the trend of on-site man-hours per customer. From week 1 to week 8, is the time our engineers spend on-site decreasing? If not, either the person in charge has not become able to touch the dictionary, or things are stuck at the writing stage. The second is the number of patterns returned to the product. How many patterns from that project became standard product features? If zero, the project consists only of branches and will not benefit the next customer. The third is the reuse rate. For the next customer, how many patterns returned from the previous customer were used? The higher this ratio, the faster we can produce code on day one for the next customer.

These three run opposite to the metrics of conventional development contracts. In conventional development, on-site man-hours are revenue, so more is better, and learning is the customer's asset, so it does not flow back. In FDE, on-site man-hours should decrease, and learning should return to the product. That is why man-hours are an investment. Being an investment, we cannot take on projects with no prospect of return. Projects unlikely to produce even one pattern—that is, projects with only branches unique to that company—are better suited to conventional development than to us. We say this honestly.

Palantir's numbers show what happens to these ratios over the long term. According to the recollections of a former FDE, the company was seen as a services company around 2016, but by continuously returning learning to the product, it reached an 80% gross margin in 2023 4. When man-hours are recovered as an investment, margins approach software levels.

The contract structure: not fixed-price or time-and-materials, but time-boxing, results metrics, and exit conditions

I will write about the contract structure. Contracts for system development in Japan broadly divide into fixed-price (ukeoi) and quasi-mandate (jun'inin). Under the Civil Code, fixed-price contracts, which promise completion of work in exchange for compensation, and quasi-mandate contracts, which entrust the handling of affairs that are not legal acts, are placed in separate categories 5. Which category applies is determined by the substance, not the name of the contract document.

FDE work fits poorly into either. A fixed-price contract promises completion, but an FDE updates working software every week, and the definition of completion changes week by week. A quasi-mandate sells man-hours by time, but a structure where staying longer increases revenue runs opposite to the FDE's purpose, where leaving sooner creates more value. So we take the approach of writing the following six items into the contract and then confirming the legal category with legal counsel.

ClauseWhat to write
DurationBound the on-site period at 45 to 90 days. Extensions require rewriting the purpose into a separate contract
Results metricsThe three-layer metrics and the counting methods before and after deployment. Technical metrics are placed separately as quality conditions
Ownership of learningPatterns go to our product, branches go to the customer's extensions, and the dictionary contents go to the customer
Four conditions for writingAuthority, prior simulation, human involvement in critical decisions, and audit records. Do not write back to production until all four conditions are met
Handling of additional requestsPatterns go into the product roadmap at no additional cost; branches are either absorbed through configuration or declined with justification
Exit conditionsThe person in charge can update the dictionary themselves, the results metrics are visible to the person in charge themselves, and patterns have been returned to the product

The cost structure is also divided into two: product usage fees and the cost of time-boxed deployments. Deployment costs are tied to the duration and results metrics, not determined by accumulated man-hours. If determined by man-hours, the motivation to stay longer enters the contract. If determined by duration, the motivation to leave sooner enters the contract.

This model is not our invention. The FDE organization AWS launched in June 2026 centers its design on leaving customers able to run on their own at the end—that is, leaving behind a working thing, documentation, and trained internal staff 6. The Anthropic and FIS partnership explicitly states that the goal of embedding FDEs is not to remain permanently, but to hand over knowledge so FIS can run on its own 7. OpenAI's implementation-focused company has published its typical approach: start with a diagnosis of value and select a small number of priority workflows 8. Decide the end first, measure outcomes in the business, and hand over the learning. We have translated the model common to all three companies' announcements into contract clauses.

Attribution of learning. Patterns go to the product, branches go to the customer, dictionary contents go to the customer

Within the contract, the most contentious and most important point is the attribution of learning. FDEs learn the business at the customer's site. If we say we will return what we learn to the product, the customer feels, "Are you taking our business knowledge away?" Naturally. So we split it into three and write it into the contract.

Patterns return to our product and are ours. These are structures like "approvals can have multiple stages," "exceptions branch by category," and "urgent routes need a separate path." These are what a16z's Andrusko called reusable primitives—data model and workflow components 3. This is not any particular company's business; it is the general structure of business.

Branches are held as customer-side extensions and belong to the customer. These are procedures unique to that company, like "Mr. Tanaka in accounting holds that document" or "this form is used only at month-end." We do not put them into the product; we place them in the customer's environment as configuration or extensions.

Dictionary contents belong to the customer. These are the nouns, verbs, relations, states, and exceptions of that company, as I wrote in the ontology article. What returns to the product is the structure "orders have states," not the contents "this company's order states are received, pending approval, approved, and shipped." The contents belong to the customer, and we do not carry them to the next customer.

When this three-way classification is written explicitly into the contract, customers feel safe showing us their actual operations. Without it, FDEs look like people who take away business knowledge, and the people on site will only show us the "ideal" business. Making attribution explicit is, before it is a legal matter, a condition for being shown the real business on site.

Differences from contract development

From a contract perspective, here are the differences from contract development.

PerspectiveContract developmentFDE
Nature of hoursRevenue. The longer you stay, the betterInvestment. The fewer hours per customer, the better
How outcomes are measuredAcceptance (does it match the spec?)The person in charge's daily metric (the difference before and after deployment)
Handling of changesAdditional cost and re-estimationPatterns: no additional cost; branches: configuration or decline
Attribution of learningCustomer's assetPatterns to the product, branches to the customer, dictionary contents to the customer
How it endsAcceptance and withdrawalThe person in charge runs on their own, and we become unnecessary
Motivation for extensionInherent in the contractNot built into the contract

The biggest difference is not building the motivation for extension into the contract. In contract development, hours are revenue, so extension is a natural motivation. In FDE, cost is fixed by period and end conditions are written in terms of outcomes, so extension becomes a separate contract involving rewriting the purpose. This design is the line that separates FDE from contract development.

Another difference is the handling of changes. In contract development, requirement changes mean additional cost and re-estimation. In FDE, if a change is a pattern, we put it into the product roadmap without charging extra; if it is a branch, we absorb it through configuration or decline with a stated rationale. Whether you can say "No" to the customization in a16z's pressure test 3 is only possible if this clause exists in the contract.

Our practice and its limits

I will write honestly. This model cannot be applied to every situation.

There are sites where outcome metrics cannot be set. Work where the person in charge's day cannot be counted—for example, work where the judgment itself is the job and cannot be measured by time. In such cases, we look for substitute metrics such as the number of rework items or the amount of information used for judgments, but if we cannot find them, we do not take on the work under this model.

There are sites where the timeline cannot be kept. Sites where security review takes long, the data owner is undecided, or the person in charge has no time. At sites where the speed conditions I wrote about in the one-week article are not met, the 45-to-90-day cadence cannot be kept. We verify these conditions in the first meeting, and if they are not met, rather than extending the period, we delay the start until they are.

There are cases where no pattern emerges. Work composed entirely of branches unique to that company, where not a single pattern likely to help the next customer will emerge. In such cases, the hours cannot be recovered as an investment, so contract development suits the customer better than we do. We say this honestly during sales conversations.

And then, our capacity. We are a small company, so the number of engagements we can take on simultaneously is limited. Having the person who listens, the person who builds, and the person who returns learnings to the product be the same person is the reason for our speed, but it is also the ceiling on our capacity. We do not hide this limit.

Summary

We can say FDE hours are an investment because outcomes are measured by the person in charge's daily metric, per-customer site hours decrease, patterns return to the product, and are reused for the next customer. The contract is neither a fixed-price nor a man-month contract; it is built from six clauses: a 45-to-90-day period, three-tier outcome metrics, the three-way classification of learning (patterns to the product, branches to the customer, dictionary contents to the customer), the four conditions for write-back, the handling of additional requests, and self-sufficiency as the end condition. Cost is split into product usage fees and time-boxed deployment fees, not determined by accumulating hours.

The boundary with contract development is not building the motivation for extension into the contract. A contract where staying longer generates revenue is contract development; a contract where leaving sooner creates value is FDE.
