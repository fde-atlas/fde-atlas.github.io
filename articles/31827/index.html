Party A: When is it worth bringing in an FDE

Original title: 甲方：什么时候值得引入 FDE

pafa.github.io · 2025-02-10 · 7 min read

Summary

This article addresses buyers (enterprises commissioning work) on when engaging an FDE is worthwhile. It argues the buyer should reframe the need as a concrete task, distinguish three gaps—missing product features, missing engineering connections, and missing understanding of new ways of working—then compare five paths (mature products, internal builds, conventional implementation, platform co-building, and FDE engagement) using the same set of normal and abnormal work orders. It stresses buyers cannot leave three internal roles vacant: deciding business priorities, confirming data boundaries, and arranging operations. Citing the UK AI Playbook and an NS&I review, it recommends a co-building round first to test whether demand persists and capabilities are maintainable, before deciding whether to build an internal FDE team.

Key points

  • Classify the gap first: product features, engineering connections, or adoption knowledge.
  • Compare all five paths on the same normal and abnormal work orders; prices must include run and handover costs.
  • Buyers must retain three internal roles: business decisions, data boundaries, and operations arrangements.
  • The NS&I transformation review shows buying external staff does not equal managing capability.
  • Co-build one round first, then decide whether an internal FDE team is justified.

An equipment service company wants AI to help supervisors organize repair request materials. It faces at least several options: buying new features in work order software, asking its existing implementer to connect the data, having internal engineers build it, or exploring jointly with a vendor. It could also first tidy up its repair request process and hold off on AI for now.

This teaching scenario has no pre-set "correct" procurement answer. If what supervisors lack is a unified equipment numbering scheme, co-developing an intelligent assistant may complicate a simple problem; if the system can already retrieve materials accurately but supervisors still don't know how to act on the suggestions, simply buying another model doesn't solve the adoption problem.

What the client must first buy or build is the capability to change this work. An FDE is one organizational approach to achieving it. Deciding to bring in an FDE, and deciding to maintain a long-term internal FDE team, are two different choices.

First, clarify what is missing

Rewriting "we lack AI capability" as a piece of work that cannot yet be completed makes the choices much clearer. The repair company can first follow a real work order end to end: where the materials come from, where the supervisor gets stuck, who corrects errors, and who ultimately bears the consequences of a mis-dispatched order. At this point, three gaps must be distinguished.

Missing product features means existing tools lack the needed capability—for example, the system cannot bring related equipment records together. Missing engineering connections means the capability already exists but is not correctly connected to the company's data, permissions, and processes. Missing understanding of the new way of working means the team does not yet know which suggestions are useful or when a human should step in, and needs to adjust while using it.

These gaps can coexist, but the people and money needed to solve them differ. Buying a product may fill the first; conventional implementation may fill the second. The third requires sustained collaboration with people who can reach the front line, modify systems, and gather business feedback; if the exploration will also change the vendor's product or the company's own shared platform, then an FDE investment has a clearer place.

The UK Government AI Playbook published in 2025 lists off-the-shelf products, adding AI to existing technology, commissioned development, and co-building with vendors as comparable options; it requires articulating the problem first, rather than prescribing a procurement form up front. 1 This is public-sector guidance; this book borrows its comparison method without transplanting its procurement regime as a general rule for enterprises.

Comparing the options under the same question

The table below is this book's decision analysis. The options compared should be tested against the same set of normal and abnormal work orders; one path must not be given only easy questions while another bears all the hard ones.

Change the process, or adopt a mature productWhether existing features already cover the actual workDeciding work rules, organizing base data, driving adoptionKey steps genuinely exceed product capability, rather than just poor configuration

Conventional implementer or industry partner deliveryWhether clearly specified interfaces, configurations, and processes can be delivered as agreedProviding reliable inputs, confirming delivery and go-live conditionsWhat keeps changing is the problem itself, and fixed specifications cannot absorb the learning
Procure a platform and co-build with a partnerWhether platform capability, industry knowledge, and on-site delivery can be combinedClarifying cross-vendor interfaces and who is accountable for the wholeNo one among platform, partner, and client can decide key changes
Bring in a vendor FDE, or build internal field engineering capabilityWhether unsolved problems can be turned into a usable system while leaving maintainable capability behindProviding business decisions, front-line time, authorization, and handover arrangementsThe work has mostly become repetitive configuration, or the exploration never converges

Price must sit beside this table, but it cannot decide on its own. One quote may cover only initial deployment while another includes operation and handover; the two cannot be compared directly. The UK DDaT Playbook examines internal, external, and hybrid arrangements together when assessing delivery models, and includes build, run, and exit costs in the comparison. 2 Project Economics: Getting Both Sides' Numbers Straight will expand on the calculation; here, first make sure the comparison hasn't omitted the client's own work.

Platform and partner cannot be divided by brand alone. The platform vendor may maintain the software, the industry partner understands the equipment business, the field team handles integration and trials, and the client makes business trade-offs. If an error occurs between these boundaries, each party in the contract has "completed its own part," yet the supervisor still cannot dispatch orders—the client has bought a set of components. Someone must track the entire workflow, be able to require relevant parties to locate problems, and have the resources to resolve interface conflicts.

This integration responsibility can be carried by a capable internal team or explicitly delegated. Even after changing who carries it, the client still must know how to judge whether it has been done.

When outsiders come in, nothing internal can be left vacant

In its February 2026 review of the NS&I transformation, the UK Parliament's Public Accounts Committee noted that the organization lacked the required capabilities, struggled to recruit, relied heavily on consultants, and still had no clear resource management strategy. 3 This is adjacent counterevidence from traditional digital transformation, not a record of FDE project failure; it reminds buyers that purchasing people is not the same as building the capability to manage those people, their knowledge, and their dependencies. That project's replacement difficulties are covered in Boundaries, Risks, and Exit.

Returning to the repair company: even if all technical work is done by the vendor, the client cannot leave three roles vacant: someone decides what the repair work should improve first; someone can confirm the boundaries for using data and systems; someone makes choices about operating arrangements after the pilot ends. The first need not understand models, the second need not build applications, and the third need not be on duty personally—but they must be able to mobilize the corresponding resources.

If no one can currently take these on, don't immediately declare the project permanently infeasible. You can first narrow the scope to material checks that don't change production actions, arrange for internal staff and external experts to jointly identify the gaps, and then decide whether to enter a trial. This preparatory work must not be directly promised as a production launch.

There is also a reasonable alternative: the client may prefer to purchase managed services long term rather than take everything back in-house. In that case, what must be retained internally is the ability to understand the service, supervise outcomes, control permissions, and switch providers; there is no need to replicate every vendor role just to prove "independence." Long-term dependence as a considered choice with clear costs is a different matter from discovering only at project's end that you cannot leave.

Co-build first, then decide whether to build your own team

Internal FDEs deserve separate discussion. If multiple business units repeatedly encounter similar problems, an internal team that can enter the field, modify the shared platform, and bring the experience back may be more suitable than each department buying its own solution. What matters here is the working mechanism; not all enterprise software development counts as FDE just because the team serves internal customers.

Before building such a team, one co-build engagement can test three things. First, whether demand will keep recurring, or end once a single migration is done. Second, whether field work can produce shared capability the company is willing to maintain long term. Third, whether business units consistently provide staff time, rather than handing the project to the technology department after the kickoff meeting.

If the repair company has only one region with known interfaces, conventional implementation plus existing systems engineers taking over may suffice. If dispatch rules across multiple regions keep changing and the internal platform needs to absorb those changes, internal engineers can first complete a round of modification and recovery together with an external FDE, then decide which capabilities should be kept. The co-build contract should allow for this learning period and give internal staff hands-on opportunities; watching a demo is not the same as gaining capability.

Building your own team does not automatically eliminate platform dependence. Even if the team writes its own applications, it may still depend on models, cloud services, specialized tools, and a few people who know the business well. The real questions to answer are: which parts are worth owning, which can be bought, and which must be replaceable when vendors change. Only with clear answers to these boundaries should hiring decisions follow—otherwise the order is reversed.

Before making the commitment, obtain evidence that can change the decision

A procurement comparison can start with a small packet of shared materials: normal work orders, work orders with missing fields, work orders where permissions are insufficient, and the steps supervisors currently take to complete them. Materials may only be used within existing permissions; the absence of usable materials is itself evidence that the conditions for implementation are not yet in place.

Ask each candidate solution to explain the same thing: which steps can be completed right now, which require configuration, which require development, and which remain uncertain. Have the client's frontline staff take part in the operation, and record the added reviews and waiting time, rather than just watching the vendor's polished demo. Also confirm who diagnoses errors when they occur, how to roll back to the original process, and what ongoing maintenance includes.

The resulting decision can be to buy, build in-house, co-build, or first fix the data, narrow the problem, or stop. Every decision should leave behind a rationale that can be reviewed later. For example: "The off-the-shelf system meets the confirmed work, so hold off on building a team"; or "How to embed recommendations into dispatch remains unresolved, so approve limited co-building but make no commitment to automatic dispatch"; or "This region has improved, but not enough to justify a permanent company-wide department."

Whether an enterprise should bring in an FDE lies in these distinctions. It should solve one more real problem than the available alternatives, and the client must be willing to commit resources to the corresponding collaboration, learning, and long-term accountability.
