Decoding the FDE Job Title: Does a Forward Deployed Engineer Write Code?

Original title: FDE 岗位名称解读：前线部署工程师要写代码吗

FDE Times · 2026-10-09 · 4 min read

Summary

This article argues that FDE job titles vary widely across companies and don't reliably indicate what the work involves. Even Palantir, which coined Delta and Echo, admits the two roles blur in practice. The real dividing line is who builds, who owns outcomes, and whether the person writes production code. Comparing Palantir, Salesforce, Anthropic, Rippling, Scale AI, and Ramp, the author suggests reading job descriptions by underlining verbs, and preparing resumes with evidence on both the building and outcome sides.

Key Takeaways

  • The same job goes by many names: Palantir calls it Delta and Echo, Salesforce calls it FDE - Deployment Strategist, Anthropic calls it FDE on the Applied AI team, and Scale AI calls it Forward Deployed AI Engineer.
  • Palantir itself admits that in practice, both tracks blend product, engineering, and strategy.
  • The real dividing line is: who is responsible for building, who owns the problems and outcomes, and whether the person writes production code.

At Palantir, deployment engineers are called Delta, and Deployment Strategists are called Echo.

Outside Palantir, the same work goes by many other names. Salesforce hires for "Forward Deployed Engineer - Deployment Strategist". Anthropic's title is "Forward Deployed Engineer, Applied AI". Scale AI recruits for "Senior Forward Deployed AI Engineer, Enterprise", and Ramp calls it "Software Engineer, Forward Deployed". Only Rippling uses the most straightforward name: Forward Deployed Engineer.

If you are a Forward Deployed Engineer (FDE), or want to become one, this confusing naming carries real costs. Searching job listings only for "FDE" will miss a large number of roles.

Worse, if you assume the title tells you what the job actually is, you might accept a role where you never write a single line of production code from start to finish. The right way to read a job posting is to ignore the name and look for only two things: who is responsible for building, and who owns the outcome.

Palantir admits its own two tracks have blurry boundaries

According to a Palantir blog post cited by Second Talent, in theory Echo leans toward product management and Delta leans toward engineering. Yet the very next sentence in the same article says the opposite: in practice, both roles are part product manager, part software engineer, part strategist.

This shows that even the company that coined these two names cannot keep them distinct. In the field, Delta still has to understand business problems, and Echo still has to understand how systems work. The titles reflect emphasis, not scope of responsibility.

What's worth learning is how they draw the line, not what they call it.

The real axis: who holds the code, who owns the outcome

Second Talent summarizes the difference between a Deployment Strategist and an FDE with one sentence about ownership: one side is responsible for building, the other owns the problems and outcomes. Placing the various titles on this axis makes the picture much clearer.

Anthropic sits at one end. According to Second Talent, it has the clearest division: the person leading deployments there does not write production code. Anthropic's FDEs belong to the Applied AI team and are embedded directly with its most strategically important customers, driving them to change how they use AI. In this model, the two jobs are split between two people.

Salesforce takes a different path. It merges Palantir's two tracks into a single title and describes the role as a hybrid of both. The person in this role leads a group of peer FDEs and coordinates deployments, meaning the same person must be both technically strong and accountable for outcomes.

Rippling, Ramp, and Scale AI lean toward building. Rippling defines an FDE as a software engineer who works directly with customers, turning vague business requirements into clear technical designs and working software.

Ramp puts "Software Engineer" first in the title, but its FDE team works directly with customers and delivers solutions end to end, so the job also extends to the outcome side. Scale AI is looking for frontend-leaning full-stack engineers who build AI-powered applications.

Read the JD the way you'd read vague customer requirements

The table below places six titles on the same axis and lists one question worth asking when interviewing at each company.

AnthropicFDE, Applied AIBuilding, with a separate non-coding ownerWho decides scope: the FDE or the deployment owner?

Imagine you're reading a hypothetical job posting titled "AI Deployment Engineer". From the title alone, you can't tell anything. Underline the verbs: if most of them are "design", "build", "ship to production", you're on the building side, similar to Rippling or Ramp.

If most of them are "lead", "orchestrate", "define success criteria", the role is closer to Echo or Anthropic's deployment owner.

This exercise is exactly what FDEs do every day at customer sites: take a vaguely labeled requirement and figure out the real work behind it. If you can do this with a JD, recruiters will see that you can do it with customers too.

Your resume should cover both ends of the axis

Since every title blends multiple roles, your resume needs evidence at both ends of the axis. A good bullet point should follow the order Rippling describes: the initial vague requirement, the technical design you chose, the software that shipped, and the outcome the customer got.

If you're a backend or full-stack engineer, the building side is already covered. What's usually missing is the outcome side: experiences where you sat down with users yourself, defined what "done" means, or proactively took on work no one assigned. In the coming work week, pick one vague internal requirement and take it from start to finish.

Conversely, if you're targeting an Echo-style role, or a hybrid role like Salesforce's, don't hide your coding ability. Palantir itself says both tracks require part software engineer. Someone who leads an FDE team but can't read code will struggle to earn the team's respect.

Titles will keep evolving, and adding "AI" to the name probably won't be the last change. Whatever the title is, be ready to answer these two questions in your resume and interviews: who holds the code, and who is accountable for the results.
