Product Management Without Product Managers: A Conversation with Rahul Agarwal

FDE Hub · 2026-09-22 · 13 min read

Summary

In this FDE Hub conversation, Rahul Agarwal, formerly of Palantir's forward deployed ML team and COO of Medplum, explains how Medplum ran product management without product managers. He argues FDEs embedded at customers act as high-bandwidth sensors, and that product management is a necessary function even if the separate PM role is not. Medplum used a weekly shared spreadsheet where FDEs rated urgency and impact, then negotiated a stack ranking. The system broke down at scale due to context overload, and he suggests rotating FDEs into the PM role for longer stints. He also covers how the role prepares founders and career paths for those who don't start companies.

Key points

  • Rahul Agarwal argues product management matters but a separate full-time PM role may not, given FDEs' firsthand customer context.
  • Medplum replaced PMs with weekly spreadsheets, self-rated urgency and impact, and enforced team negotiation and stack ranking.
  • The process broke at scale because a rotating delegate couldn't hold context across ten to twenty FDEs' tickets.
  • He calls the FDE role founder school: radical accountability and CEO-like business exposure, minus fundraising and early product-market risk.
  • Non-founder paths include player-coach FDE leadership or operations roles like COO, chief of staff, or GM of operations.

Full text

Rahul Agarwal was my guest on FDE Hub earlier this year, in Sensors in the Field, where he described running Medplum’s FDE team without a single product manager. He spent several years on Palantir’s forward deployed machine learning team, led customer impact at Applied Intuition, and co-founded Medplum, where he was COO. We picked up where we left off: whether product management survives without product managers, what qualifies an FDE to shape a product, what broke when the team scaled, why he calls the role founder school, and where the career goes if you don’t want to found anything.

Last time you said FDEs are sensors in the field, and that Medplum was running product through them rather than through PMs. With some distance, did that experiment work?

In general, yes. But let me revisit the sensors idea first, because it explains why.

One of the major superpowers of the FDE model is that you’re embedded in the problem. You’re not getting low bandwidth feedback from customers about what they think the problem is. You’re inside the organisation, not just doing what they say but watching what they do and iterating off that. That’s the difference from a traditional PLG-led motion, where we try to infer what people want from what they tell us. We solve the problem shoulder to shoulder with the customer, experience it first hand, and develop product around that. A bunch of sensors embedded across the space, rather than one satellite on high taking pictures every so often and trying to make sense of the world.

The challenge is how you take all that high bandwidth, distributed information sitting in the heads of individual FDEs and shape a reusable product out of it. If you talk to Barry McCardle about our time at Palantir, the answer is a spectrum. Palantir indexed on building the product in the field first, maybe two or three versions independently, and then figuring out how to put it all together. On a startup budget we couldn’t afford that much redundancy, so we had to build mechanisms to do product management through our distributed sensors.

So here’s my hot take on my last hot take. Product management is an important function. I’m just not convinced that product managers, as a full time, separate role and profile, make sense in this new world. Especially if you’re building a highly customisable platform for the enterprise and doing the full FDE motion.

When I say that to FDE leaders, the pushback is always the same. Fine, FDEs collect the feedback. But why are they qualified to recognise the signal and shape the product? How did you trust your team with that?

To some extent I’d wonder whether those leaders have hired really solid FDEs. I’ve seen an anti-pattern where a company hires someone they don’t consider a senior architect type engineer, throws them in the field, and that person can’t operate independently with a customer. They get paired with strategists, a product manager, all this armour and padding, so even though they’re assigned to the customer, they’re not accountable to the customer. Then they’re not getting product feedback, because you didn’t hire them to be a person who interacts with customers to begin with.

So it starts at hiring. One of our core values for the FDE team was zero ambiguity, and it had two flavours. The first is the obvious one: leave a meeting with a customer, write everything down, no confusion about what we have to do.

The second is strong intelligence gathering. If it’s unclear why a customer asked for something, that should set off your spidey sense. They ask for a feature, you think it’s kind of silly, a good FDE goes and digs in. Talk to the person who asked, talk to their opposing stakeholders, talk to comparable customers. Why would someone in this industry want this? That informs the why of any enhancement you bring back. It’s also the qualification for getting feedback at all. How many times have customers said things are fine, things are fine, and then churned? If you’re good at zero ambiguity, you see the lack of footprint and go find out what the blocker is.

It makes hiring harder, sure. But people overindex on the E of FDE. For me the letters are in rank order. Forward is the most important, a proactive mentality. Deployed is next: we’re going to embed and work on real problems. I’d even push back on the phrase product feedback, because that’s how traditional PMs think. What sets FDEs apart is that you’re co-solving with the customer. Instead of facing each other, you’re both facing the same way, towards the problem. They’ve experienced it, not heard about it. The engineering, the E, is the building, and that part could be done by APMs or by the pod approach Decagon runs. Those are still valid as long as you’re solving in the field.

Without a PM between FDEs and engineering, how did you stop everyone’s customer problems landing on the engineering team at the same time, all marked urgent?

Start from what product management actually is. Three activities: defining value, why would this change create customer value and leverage; scoping, including deduplicating requests; and alignment, constant prioritisation and grooming. That work has to get done regardless of what roles you have.

At Medplum, once a week the FDEs collected every request from the field into a shared spreadsheet. Each FDE rated their own on urgency and impact, so I was asking them to flex into being a PM. That took a lot of coaching to make crisp. Impact isn’t “the feature will do this”. It’s what that gets us in a business context. Does it unlock the whole account, or does one person stop complaining?

Then the team negotiated and stack-ranked. Nothing can be equal priority. You have to be willing to sacrifice your own priority for someone else’s, and you have to advocate: this is why this affects multiple customer types. Deduplication falls out organically, because you’re forced to talk about each other’s customers. If five customers are all migrating from the same legacy system and each proposed a different fix, you realise what you need is one standard connector.

And we enforced it. No games where I’m best friends with this one engineer, so I grab them at lunch and say, can you please build this thing, it’s super important. It happens, sometimes by accident, an engineer hears something in conversation and just builds it. Even then I’d give a little reprimand: it needs to come through the negotiation process. I wanted to avoid the optics of every FDE being out there for themselves. We’re mostly siloed on our own customers, but we share the same pain and the same scars, and we should be able to accept that another FDE has something more important, even if it means telling your customer they’re waiting two weeks.

That sounds carefully designed. Where did it break?

It was starting to break under scale. One rotating FDE took the stack-ranked list into engineering’s weekly prioritisation standup and explained it, and that delegate had to understand the whole list. It couldn’t be “Bob put this on, Jane put this on, that’s that”. Across ten, twenty FDEs, there’s too much context on each ticket. How much can I hold when I’m only doing this once a week for everyone’s tickets? And how long does that meeting get if every request is properly explained?

People ask whether this would go away at a hundred FDEs. In its current form, as egalitarian as it was, yes. But the knowledge sharing problem is fundamental to FDE. If we weren’t doing it at the feature level we’d be doing it somewhere else. How many customer archetypes do we have? We solved this for that customer this way, now we have a similar one, how do we reuse it? The hard problem of FDE is sharing knowledge in context across hundreds of people. Product management is one facet. You have to fix knowledge sharing anyway, so you fold this into whatever you build for that.

If I did it again, the person acting as PM wouldn’t rotate weekly. Monthly or quarterly, tied to their customer assignment, so you can load balance them off direct customer work and give them brain space. They could go deep, be an internal FDE, ask why we’re asking for these things. When it was forty five minutes a week of someone’s job, they just didn’t get into it.

So the product management work is important. I just don’t know if putting it into a separate role, with a separate hiring profile, is worth the extra hop of communication and context, plus the fact that the person hasn’t solved the problem themselves in the field.

People reading this will wonder whether their company could run this way, or whether it was specific to Medplum being open source.

Not about open source at all. It goes hand in hand with which companies are actually well served by FDE, and that isn’t every company doing FDE right now.

The ones that are have a highly customisable platform, sell to the enterprise, and adopting the product entails organisational transformation and a lot of change management inside the customer. Someone gave me an analogy I like: traditional PLG is screwing in a light bulb, and FDE-led companies are rewiring the whole house so the light bulb will work. If you’re selling something that requires rewiring the house, which tends to be high value, you should do FDE.

And if you’re doing that, the surface area you’re attacking across customers is enormous, so sensors in the field is valuable too. Product management without PMs then flows naturally, because the overhead of translating high context information to someone who wasn’t doing the job outweighs the benefit of dedicated headspace. There is value in a person who isn’t under the gun doing the reconciliation, but that could be an FDE in a rotating model. Even then I’d worry about them losing touch with the field, because as your product matures, what co-solving means changes. Some things are just done by the product now. You’re always pushing the frontier of where the hard bottleneck is.

Someone at my office hours recently asked whether an extrovert who likes working with teammates should become an FDE, and I had to admit it’s a bit of a lone wolf job. Was your weekly negotiation also a forcing function for FDEs to actually talk to each other?

That’s definitely fair. Palantir did this at scale: once a year, a global all-hands of two or three thousand people called HobbitCon. Talks about what people had built, but a lot of it was socialising, with a top-down expectation that customer work is on pause for a week, fires excepted. You’re not solving knowledge management in a three day all-hands, but I’d leave with pointers: some FDE in Australia was doing something similar, let’s follow up. And because you’ve both been in the trenches there’s war story swapping, there’s trust.

The other part is autonomy. At Palantir I had the freedom to fly to a compatriot in London who’d done something similar and jam on a solution. Barry McCardle advocates this even more strongly than I do, but sensors in the field plus autonomy in the field is what makes the FDE magic special. Not every company can afford five versions of the same product before figuring out which one works. But giving people some freedom to pursue ideas and collaboration yields serendipity at scale. It becomes an N squared situation, in a good way.

Rahul has been on both sides now, an FDE for years and then a founder. Last time he mentioned telling his team that the role is founder school. I wanted to know what that means in practice.

What does being an FDE actually prepare you for as a founder, and what does it leave out?

The main thing FDE embeds in your brain, very deeply, is radical accountability. You’re the account manager, the builder, and in a sense the product manager. What the company is saying to you is: this account lives or dies on your work, nothing is beyond your scope, get it done. That’s the single biggest thing about being a founder. The buck stops with you.

At Medplum our FDEs had a mantra: it’s not our fault, but it is our problem. A fire comes up, a stakeholder emerges from the woodwork. It doesn’t matter that we didn’t cause it. Enterprise software plus organisational transformation is a painful, slow moving process with friction all along the way. Our mission wasn’t to deploy the product, it was to achieve the outcome, which for us meant improving healthcare. Anything short of that is a stepping stone.

The second thing is that the wide scope gives you a CEO-like view across a business that a traditional engineer doesn’t get. FDEs get pulled into pre-sales, sometimes into contracts and deal structure. You see the PM process. And you see how architectural choices percolate across customers. I had plenty of moments where I wanted a quick fix, got pushback from core engineering, we did the sound thing instead, it delayed my request by two weeks, and then so many other customers were served more easily because we did it right. You get to put your VP Engineering hat on.

What it leaves out, the glib answer is fundraising. The broader version is that radical accountability for customer value is ninety percent of what a startup should be, but you also have to create the entity that delivers it. Build the organisation, keep it financially sustainable. And at a later stage company you’re coming into an existing product. You don’t get the lost-in-the-wilderness experience of picking a problem, deciding whether to ignore the competition, finding your niche. That early product-market risk has been de-risked before you arrived.

Not every FDE wants to found a company. What does progression look like for the rest?

Not everyone needs to be a founder. There’s a lot of value in high performing people contributing to an existing organisation that has found product-market fit in something. We don’t need a thousand AI agents for fintech when ten will do.

I don’t have a silver bullet, because FDE outside Palantir is all very new, and inside Palantir progression was pretty loose. One path is FDE leadership, which at Medplum was very much player-coach. You’re still doing customers, but only the bigger, strategic ones that need someone with more reps, and you own a portfolio across the people you manage. It’s less about teaching raw skills and more about coaching, feedback, and a high feedback, high performance environment.

The other path, and I have a soft spot here, is COO. The joke is that a CEO’s role in a startup is fairly clear: everything, and usually the commercial side. A CTO’s role is clear too. The COO is really the C-asterisk-O, everything that fills the gaps between what the other Cs are defined as. An FDE with that pluripotent, multifaceted skill set plugs in really well there, or anywhere in the operations funnel, a GM of operations, a chief of staff. Because if you’re doing organisational transformation, you’re rewiring another company’s operations, their ways of working, their tools, how they hand off, which roles interact. You’ve had to understand how organisations work and how they can be improved.

Anything you’d like to add?

One thing that’s been nice for someone like me is the rise of the generalist again. For a long time, if you hadn’t done ten years in just product or just engineering, you were kind of useless. Hiring managers would look at me and say, what do I do with you? You talk to people, but you also code? They didn’t think it was bad, they just didn’t know what box to put me in.

Because of AI, the raw muscle needed for writing code has become less important, and everything else has become more important. Generalists are having a good time right now.

People ask me whether FDE is a fad or structural. I think the rise of the generalist is a secular change. I don’t know whether the FDE title itself sticks. Decagon is doing interesting things with pods, others have other names for it. But certain things have got easier, so there’s more value in someone who can connect the dots.

Rahul Agarwal is an FDE leader and founder, with time at Palantir, Applied Intuition and Medplum. He is currently building his next company in stealth.
