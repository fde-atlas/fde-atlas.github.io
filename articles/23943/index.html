How to Implement FDE | A Four-Step Process, Team Structure, Evaluation Metrics, and How to Avoid Failure

Original title: FDE導入の進め方｜4段階の手順・体制・評価指標と失敗の回避策

FDE総研 · 2026-08-06 · 19 min read

Summary

This article from FDE总研 argues that adopting Forward Deployed Engineers is an organizational redesign involving authority, contracts, and evaluation criteria—not just adding one job posting. It distinguishes FDEs from outsourced engineers, IT consultants, and customer success roles, and proposes five fit conditions (e.g., customer-specific workflows, data residing on-site); companies matching three or more should consider FDEs. It lays out a four-stage process—case selection, minimal deployment of one or two FDEs, templating, and horizontal rollout—with exit criteria and metrics such as days to first value, product contribution rate, and successful handoff rate. It also covers reporting lines, roles of seven internal stakeholders, an eight-item handoff checklist, and common failure modes like becoming a handyman, knowledge silos, and broken feedback into the product.

Key points

  • FDE adoption means redesigning authority, contracts, and evaluation—not just hiring.
  • Five fit conditions; match three or more before adopting FDEs.
  • Four stages: selection, minimal deployment, templating, horizontal rollout.
  • Evaluate FDEs on days-to-first-value and product contribution, not utilization.
  • Common failures: handyman drift, person-dependency, and broken product feedback.

What exactly does implementing FDEs mean?

The FDE Research Institute frames FDE implementation not as adding one more job posting, but as an organizational restructuring that replaces three things: authority, contracts, and evaluation criteria (What is FDE).

FDE (Forward Deployed Engineer) is a role that became widely known when Palantir placed it at the core of its customer deployment model. FDEs embed themselves in the customer's business operations and single-handedly handle everything from requirements definition to implementation and operational adoption. For a detailed definition of the role, see What is an FDE Engineer; this article focuses on the practical work of the organization accepting and integrating FDEs.

The first stumbling block for Japanese companies implementing FDEs is the misconception that "adding one FDE job posting is enough." Even with a job posting, without the authority to make decisions on-site at the customer and metrics to measure on-site results, an FDE becomes just a traveling engineer.

How do FDEs differ from contracted development engineers and IT consultants?

What makes FDEs unique is that the same person moves back and forth between defining business problems and implementing them in product code within the same sprint.

"FDE" is not a blanket term for "engineers close to the customer." Compared with similar roles, the differences are as follows.

The FDE Research Institute narrows the comparison axes used for implementation decisions to two. The difference from IT consultants is "whether they can submit pull requests instead of meeting minutes." The difference from contracted development is "whether they take responsibility for bringing work back as shared features in their own product rather than ending with customer-specific customization." Companies that have implemented FDEs often report the lesson that implementation effectiveness drops significantly if either is missing.

What specifically changes in internal operations when implementing FDEs?

The operational changes from FDE implementation concentrate on three points: how the roadmap is decided, permission design for production environments, and contract structure.

First is how the product roadmap is decided. Until now, product managers collected requests and set priorities. After FDE implementation, you need a mechanism that treats first-hand information brought back by FDEs working on-site as official input. If you place FDEs without deciding the input pathway, their discoveries flow away as individual Slack posts and disappear.

Second is permission design. Because FDEs touch customer data and production-equivalent environments, you need to decide in advance the scope of access, audit logs, and what cannot be taken out. This is work that assumes involvement of legal and information security departments; trying to arrange it after FDEs arrive will inevitably cause delays.

Third is contract structure. If FDE work hours are segmented as "quasi-mandate person-months," customers tend to use up the hours, drifting away from the outcome-linked approach that is the aim of FDE implementation. Decide in advance whether to attach implementation support to the license or to write success metrics into the contract. Key points for checking contract types are organized in FDE Service Agreements.

What are the conditions for companies that need FDE implementation?

Businesses that meet 3 or more of the FDE Research Institute's 5 conditions are worth considering FDE implementation (Comparison of FDE Implementation Cases).

Not every company needs FDEs. FDEs are a high-cost role, so misplacing them only increases fixed costs. Whether to implement should be decided by matching against conditions, not by the feeling that "we want one too."

What kind of business structure makes FDE implementation effective?

FDE implementation is effective in businesses where customer operations are not standardized and the data resides on the customer's site.

Of the following 5 items, count each match as 1 point. If the total is 3 or more, consider FDE implementation. If 2 or fewer, first try the alternative approaches in the next section.

Assessment itemMatching state (1 point)Non-matching state (0 points)
Uniqueness of business flowsBusiness flows differ per customer, so even the same screen is used in completely different waysOperations are nearly identical across customers
Location of dataThe data that is the source of value resides in the customer's site equipment or core systems and cannot be designed from outsideAll necessary data is available on your own side
Cost recoveryContract prices are high enough to recover implementation effort per customer (guideline: annual contract value of several million yen or more)Low prices make it impossible to recover implementation effort per customer
Early value demonstrationWhether value can be shown within the first few weeks of implementation heavily influences the main contract and continuationEarly implementation results have almost no influence on contract decisions
Need for operational changeValue only emerges if on-site operations changeResults move just by installing the system

Another criterion FDE Soken recommends is "the number of variables that can only be determined on-site." For example, in VACAN's service that visualizes congestion and vacancy information in real time, even for the same type of commercial facility, at least three things differ from facility to facility: sensor installation conditions, in-facility traffic flow, and staff operating procedures. In such domains, the judgment tends to be that it is faster to stand on-site and nail down measurement conditions than to decide requirements at a desk. Discussions about FDE adoption become less likely to waver if you start by counting these variables.

Which cases should FDE adoption be postponed?

FDE adoption should be postponed in cases of self-serve, low-unit-price offerings, and cases where the product is pre-PMF and its specifications are not yet settled.

The cases where it is appropriate to postpone or delay are as follows.

How does the decision to adopt FDE change depending on company size and internal preconditions?

FDE Soken holds that the first move in FDE adoption depends not only on company size but on three preconditions: contracting department, PMF, and hiring status (How to Advance In-House DX Development).

Even with the same FDE adoption, the first move differs depending on internal preconditions. Here we organize how to start under each precondition and who within the company partners with FDE.

How should the approach to starting FDE adoption change by precondition?

The approach to starting FDE adoption splits into one of three depending on preconditions: "validate with 1-2 people," "separate the department first," or "validate externally."

Who within the company partners with FDE?

In FDE adoption, seven parties—business owner, legal, information security, field manager, development, CS, and sales—have roles at each stage.

Adoption does not proceed with the FDE alone. Decide the role division of the receiving side in line with the stages of FDE adoption.

What is the procedure for advancing FDE adoption?

FDE adoption proceeds through FDE Soken's four phases (selection, minimal deployment, templating, horizontal expansion), with exit criteria for each phase decided before starting (FDE Adoption Failure Patterns).

An approach that assigns FDEs to all customers at once will almost certainly fail. The standard practice is to prove it with one customer and expand while verifying that the same results can be reproduced. The following table summarizes the structure, tasks, evaluation metrics, and conditions for proceeding to the next phase at each stage. Durations are general guidelines and vary depending on the complexity of the target operations and how quickly access is granted.

What is decided and verified in Phases 1 and 2?

The purpose of Phases 1 and 2 is to create a state in which the FDE can make decisions on-site and to actually measure the delivery of initial value.

The most important thing in Phase 1 is how to choose the project. Rather than "the customer with the highest sales," choose "a customer whose business challenges are clear, whose on-site decision-maker is identifiable, and whose overall business relationship would not collapse even if the project fails." At the same time, agree in writing on access rights to production-equivalent data and on which metrics will be used to evaluate results. Skipping this means most of Phase 2 is consumed by coordination.

In the second phase, narrow the FDE deployment down to one or two people. A small team not only makes decisions faster in the field, but also serves as a benchmark for measuring "how much a single FDE can launch." The goal at this stage is not completeness, but delivering a minimal implementation that moves the customer's business metrics. The longer the first value delivery is delayed, the more the customer's internal champion tends to lose enthusiasm, making their cooperation harder to obtain afterward.

How should standardization and horizontal rollout proceed in phases 3 and 4?

The purpose of phases 3 and 4 is to elevate a single-customer implementation into common functionality, putting the FDE in a position to move on to the next project.

In phase 3, take inventory of the individual implementation built in phase 2 and sort it into "things other customers will use," "things specific to this customer," and "things to discard." Since this is hard for a single FDE to judge, make joint reviews with the product development team a regular practice. If the product side refuses to accept the work, the FDE's output remains as one-off code and becomes a maintenance liability. Documenting acceptance criteria in advance (tests, documentation, externalized configuration) reduces friction.

In phase 4, verify whether the FDE can be detached from the first customer. If detachment is not possible, that is evidence standardization is incomplete, and horizontal rollout is still premature. Once detachment succeeds, FDE deployment shifts from "one person's contribution" to "a replicable system." Check quarterly whether this transition has occurred.

What should be handed over before the FDE withdraws?

Before FDE withdrawal, use the FDE Research Institute's 8-item checklist to verify the handover of code, procedures, points of contact, and permissions.

Whether operations stall after the FDE leaves the field is determined by preparation before withdrawal. Withdraw only after all of the following items are satisfied.

  • Individual implementations have been fully sorted into the three categories: "usable by other customers," "specific to this customer," and "discard"
  • Code to be moved into common functionality meets the acceptance criteria: tests, documentation, and externalized configuration
  • Customer-specific operating procedures exist, and staff other than the FDE can read them and perform the work
  • A handover period under a two-person setup has been completed within the standardization process
  • Intake routes have been agreed with the customer: operational inquiries go to the support desk, and routine data extraction is handled via self-service or CS
  • The scope of the FDE's access rights and audit logs have been reviewed, and post-withdrawal handling of permissions has been decided
  • The contract's scope of support and success metrics have been revised to match the post-withdrawal structure
  • A date has been set three months after withdrawal to verify that operations are continuing without the FDE

How should the FDE deployment structure and evaluation metrics be designed?

Separate the FDE organization from departments measured by utilization rate, and evaluate it using the FDE Research Institute's five metrics, such as days to first value delivery (How to Adopt FDEs).

The success or failure of FDE deployment is largely determined by the design of the structure and evaluation metrics. Most failures stem not from lack of ability, but from design mistakes in where the FDE is placed and what they are measured by.

Where should the FDE's reporting line be placed?

The FDE reporting line should be separated from departments evaluated on utilization rate and placed under the same manager as product development, which is the safest option.

Placement modelAdvantageWeakness
Within the product development organizationFeedback into the product tends to happenDistance from sales in the field tends to grow
Under sales or CSEasy access to deal informationEasily used for short-term sales support
As an independent department directly under the business headCan maintain neutralityProne to isolation when small

Many practitioners say a realistic approach is to start with placement on the product side with regular coordination with sales, and consider independence once the FDE headcount exceeds five. Whichever model you choose, it is critical not to give sales departments authority over FDE performance evaluations.

How should FDE evaluation metrics be designed?

Evaluate FDEs not by working hours or visit counts, but by days to first value delivery and the volume of contributions back to the product.

It is fine to monitor utilization as a supplementary indicator. However, the moment utilization rate becomes the primary metric, FDE deployment turns into contracted development. Among practitioners, there is even a saying: "Whether utilization rate appears on the FDE evaluation sheet tells you whether a company is serious about FDE deployment." That may be overstating it, but it is certainly true that metric design shapes people's behavior.

How should you choose between FDE deployment, SIers, IT consulting, and adding PdMs?

According to the FDE Research Institute, the criterion for choosing FDE deployment is whether you need both on-site requirements definition and contributions back to your own product (Differences Between FDEs and SIers).

FDE deployment is not the only way to solve field-level problems. Compare it with the other options and separate what each can and cannot deliver.

What does each option provide, and what does it not?

SIers and contracted development deliver deliverables built to spec, IT consultants deliver recommendations, and FDEs deliver working implementations in the field plus contributions back to your company.

What do companies poorly suited to FDE deployment have in common?

Organizations that are unsuitable for FDE adoption are those that evaluate people by utilization rate and where the authority over FDE personnel evaluations is held by sales or contracted-development departments.

From practitioners in the field, we often hear that "the more a company has a contracted-development department, the harder FDE adoption becomes." FDEs are professionals who prioritize codifying patterns even at the cost of immediate billings. If placed within an organization evaluated by utilization rate, they will lose out to the evaluation system. Hiring FDEs while leaving things in this state merely results in changing the job title of contracted-development engineers. The proper approach is to separate the department and evaluation metrics first.

What are common failures in FDE adoption and how can they be avoided?

According to the FDE Research Institute's analysis, typical failures in FDE adoption fall into three categories: becoming a handyman, over-dependence on individuals, and a broken feedback loop, with most showing warning signs within three months of the FDE's arrival (Causes of failure and withdrawal criteria).

If you articulate the warning signs in advance, recovery is not difficult.

Failure patternEarly warning signsCountermeasures
Handyman syndromeTroubleshooting and data extraction requests make up the majority of the workSeparate the intake channel for requests and set an upper limit on the FDE's weekly allocation
Over-dependence on individualsA state where only one person can handle a specific customer continues for three monthsBuild a handover period with a two-person setup into the codification process
Broken feedback loopAlmost no commits to the main repository for six monthsMake joint reviews a regular practice and include feedback volume in evaluation metrics
BurnoutLong working hours including travel time become the normInstitutionalize an upper limit on the on-site ratio and buffer periods between projects
Misaligned expectationsThe customer starts treating the FDE as "free development resources"Clearly state the scope of support and performance metrics in the contract and review them quarterly

Why does FDE "handyman syndrome" occur, and how can it be stopped?

FDE handyman syndrome occurs because the FDE becomes the easiest person to rely on in the field, and it can be stopped by separating the intake channels for requests.

FDEs are stationed on-site, understand both technology and business, and can act immediately. From the customer's perspective, they are the ideal consultation partner, and if left alone, requests will pile up. The way to stop this comes down to separating the intake channels for requests. Agree with the customer on a division of labor: operational inquiries go to the support desk, routine data extraction is made self-service or handled by customer success, and the FDE only handles consultations about specification changes.

Do not settle for a verbal agreement; write it into the kickoff materials. If the design relies on the FDE themselves to refuse requests, their desire not to damage relationships will inevitably cause it to break down. Implementing companies repeatedly point out that the responsibility for drawing the line lies with the organization.

How can FDE over-dependence on individuals and burnout be prevented?

Over-dependence on individuals and burnout can be prevented structurally through three measures: an upper limit on the on-site ratio, handover with a two-person setup, and buffer periods between projects.

To prevent over-dependence on individuals, it is effective to include the third-stage codification in the project schedule as a "non-omittable process." Codification yields results that are hard to see, so it is the first thing to be cut when things get busy. The consequence of cutting it is repeating the same struggles on the next project, with knowledge accumulating in the individual FDE and never being externalized.

To prevent burnout, set an upper limit on the on-site ratio (for example, at most 60% of weekly working hours spent on-site). The remaining time is devoted to codification, learning, and collaboration with the in-house team. Furthermore, place a one-to-two-week buffer between projects for retrospectives and extracting common functionality. The appropriate numbers vary by business, but what is common everywhere is that organizations without an upper limit end up exceeding it.

FDE adoption is not a measure of sending excellent individuals into the field. It is a measure of building a circuit that returns field-level firsthand information back into the product. Whether that circuit has started running can be judged by whether you can detach the FDE.
