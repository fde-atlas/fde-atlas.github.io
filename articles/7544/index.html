Prologue · The Fourth Time Hearing "This Time Is Different"

Original title: 序章 · 第四次听见“这次不一样”

FDE 的来路 · 2023 · 7 min read

Summary

Drawing on 18 years in enterprise digitalization, the author recounts four waves of "this time is different"—ERP, cloud, big data, and now large models—and identifies a recurring wall: systems solve information problems, but not who decides, executes, and is accountable. In traditional enterprises, data is often a shadow of the business, unlike internet companies whose operations live inside software. AI agents can now act, raising questions of permissions, approval, and rollback. FDE matters because it consolidates responsibilities once spread across roles. The book deliberately starts not with Palantir but with the overlooked history of China's on-site implementation and data engineers.

Key points

  • Four tech waves promised change, yet systems multiplied while Excel persisted
  • The real gap is accountability, not data quality
  • Traditional firms' data is a shadow; internet firms' data is the business
  • Agents that act raise permission, approval, and rollback questions
  • The book traces FDE's roots in China's field engineers, not Palantir

From ERP, cloud, and big data to AI, enterprises have heard "this time is different" again and again. The opening chapter asks: why, with more and more systems, do we still get stuck between seeing a problem and solving it?

Status: public first draft v0.1.0. Editorial note: the project scenes in this text are composite scenarios drawn from the author's years of observation and do not refer to any specific client; personal experiences are kept only in general terms and are not offered as standalone evidence of industry facts.

Over the years, I have heard "this time is different" at least four times.

The first time was ERP. Back then, people believed that as long as procurement, sales, inventory, and finance were all packed into one system, the enterprise would run like a machine. Once processes were defined, permissions sorted out, and the numbers reconciled, the things that used to depend on people watching, asking around, and keeping notebooks would naturally disappear.

Later came the cloud. No more installing system after system, and enterprises no longer had to maintain so many servers of their own. It sounded like moving old problems to the cloud would make them a little lighter.

Then came big data and the data middle platform. The slogan took another step forward: not just putting the business into systems, but also gathering back the scattered data—unified definitions, unified metrics, unified services. From then on, whatever the business needed, there would be no need to start from scratch every time.

Now it is the turn of large models. Models can read documents, understand human speech, write proposals, and call tools, and people are saying again that the enterprise is about to be rebuilt from the ground up.

Four times may not be an accurate count. In between there were also mobile internet, SaaS, low-code, and RPA—each of them said something similar. And the people saying it were not necessarily bragging. Every wave of technology solved real problems and left behind real things. It is just that after eighteen years working in enterprise digitalization, another fact I have seen is: systems keep multiplying while Excel has barely decreased; reports keep getting prettier, but the number of people who actually act on the reports does not necessarily grow.

That is a bit awkward.

The liveliest moment of a project is usually the kickoff meeting. The conference room is full of people—executives talk strategy, the vendor talks blueprints, and the arrows on the PPT run from data sources all the way to the executive dashboard, finally landing on the words "empowering the business."

A few months later, the system goes live and the project passes acceptance. A while after that, the business department sends over another Excel, asking to re-verify this number.

You ask them, isn't it in the system?

They say, yes it is, but that's not the definition the boss looks at.

You ask again, so which number is actually the real one?

They say, they're all real—they're just used for different purposes.

When I was young and ran into this kind of thing, my first reaction was usually that the data was wrong. The tables weren't built properly, the ETL didn't run correctly, the metric definitions weren't rigorous enough. When technical people hit a problem, they always feel that adding another layer of models, another table, or another round of definition changes will make things clean.

It took me a long time to learn that, in many cases, the data was not wrong at all.

The trouble lies further down the line: the people looking at the numbers have no authority to act, the people with the authority to act don't look at that report, and the people who ultimately bear the consequences are looking at a different set of numbers. The system solved the information problem, but the enterprise still hasn't solved who judges, who decides, who executes, and who is accountable.

This is not the fault of any one generation of technology, yet it is a wall that every generation of technology runs into.

Is Data the Business, or the Shadow the Business Leaves Behind?

By the era of the data middle platform, this wall had become especially visible.

Many internet companies doing data work have a condition that traditional enterprises can hardly replicate: their business was born inside software in the first place.

What users clicked, searched, and bought—the platform can see it all. Change a recommendation rule, and traffic shifts immediately; swap in a new version of a campaign page, and conversion rates give feedback quickly. Data is generated from the business and flows directly back into it. For these companies, data is not just material for analysis—it is inherently part of the product and the operation.

Traditional enterprises are a different story.

A factory's profit lives in raw materials, equipment, processes, delivery schedules, and shop-floor management; a hotel's business lives in its rooms, channels, service, and how guests feel; an insurance company's risk lives not only in its databases but also in contracts, investigations, underwriting, and human judgment.

These enterprises certainly have systems too, and they generate massive amounts of data. But the data is more often a record left behind by the real business—sometimes merely a shadow. Things have already happened on the ground before the system learns about them a beat later; the veteran worker has already fixed the problem, but the cause was never recorded; a report flags an anomaly, yet the person who could change the process may not even be in the data department.

So even when both are called a "data platform," what they do in these two kinds of enterprises is not exactly the same thing.

When an internet company builds its platform well, it may directly change products and revenue. When a traditional enterprise builds its platform well, it often just lets people see the problems more clearly. Whether those problems actually get addressed still has to pass through departments, permissions, budgets, processes, and responsible owners.

This is not to say that the internet company's approach is necessarily unsuitable for traditional enterprises, nor that traditional enterprises are doomed to fail at data. The better question is: what conditions did a method originally depend on? After being transplanted into a different kind of enterprise, do those conditions still hold?

Many digitalization projects in the past stumbled exactly here. We carried over the architecture, the terminology, and the construction methods, but not the business environment behind them. In the end, the platform looked the part, but the results did not.

This Time with AI, Something Really Is a Bit Different

Since none of the previous waves got past this wall, why should we seriously discuss FDE today?

Because this time, AI has genuinely pushed software a step forward.

In the past, systems mostly handled recording, computing, and displaying. When BI turned a metric red, a person still had to judge the cause, gather materials, contact departments, and modify the system. Software delivered the problem to a person's desk, and human work picked up from there.

Today's models can read emails, contracts, images, and chat logs; they can piece together scattered materials and call tools. They don't just tell you "there might be a problem here"—they can also prepare the next action: draft a reply, fill in a ticket, modify an order, notify a customer, even trigger financial and production processes.

Once software starts taking action, the nature of the game changes.

In the past, when a report was miscalculated, the worst outcome was someone slamming the table and rerunning the numbers. Now, when an agent makes a mistake, the email may already have been sent to the customer, the order may already have been changed, the money may already have been paid, and the equipment may already have been shut down. You can no longer just ask whether the model is accurate—you also have to ask what permissions it has, who approves its actions, at what level of error it must stop, and whether the process can be reconstructed afterward.

What's more, the enterprise will keep asking that most basic question: having spent this money, what exactly are we earning more, losing less—or did we just get another demo?

At this point, the traditional division of roles starts to fall short.

Business consultants understand the front lines but may not be able to build the system; engineers can write code but may not know which problems are worth solving; implementation teams handle go-live, product teams handle releases, sales handles contracts, and customer success handles renewals. Everyone completes their own part, yet no one may be fully accountable for the final outcome.

It is in this gap that FDE has become conspicuous.

None of its component capabilities are new: field research, business modeling, data integration, writing code, accompanying clients through go-live—people have done all of these before. What has changed is that responsibilities once scattered across different roles are now compressed onto a single person or a single small team.

He cannot just say "the requirements weren't set by me," nor can he just say "the system has gone live." He has to follow through from whether the problem was correctly identified, all the way to whether the client dares to use it, whether using it produces results, and whether things can be rolled back when something goes wrong.

That is why I am reluctant to simply describe FDE as the next stop after BI.

BI did not grow along a straight line into FDE. FDE's lineage also includes implementation consultants, Solutions Architects, product managers, customer success, and those nameless engineers who spent years embedded on-site.

But I also don't believe FDE is a new species that appeared out of thin air after 2023.

The challenges it faces—inconsistent data definitions, distorted requirements, system silos, departmental barriers, and project-based work—are all pitfalls that enterprise digitalization has been stepping into for decades. Without understanding this lineage, it is all too easy to build agents into reports that talk better, build ontologies into field dictionaries with new names, and turn FDE into pricier on-site outsourcing.

Why This Book Does Not Start with Palantir

When talking about FDE, you can't avoid Palantir.

The official history of this role, how it's organized, and today's industry hype all need to be explained clearly. It's just that this book doesn't intend to start there.

The reason is simple. Others have already done a good job of documenting how Palantir sends engineers to customer sites, and why OpenAI and other model companies are once again emphasizing deployment. If I wrote along the same line again, at best I'd just be adding a few paragraphs of Chinese-language material.

What I'd rather fill in is a different part of the story.

Before the name FDE arrived in China, many people here were already doing work similar to it. They reconciled data definitions in telecom equipment rooms, ran batch jobs in banking projects, sorted out processes in factories, and did business analysis for hotels and retailers. Their job titles might have been implementation, consulting, data engineering, systems analysis, solutions—or sometimes no official title at all.

The experience these people accumulated doesn't become obsolete just because large models have arrived; and the pitfalls they stumbled into don't get automatically filled in just because the job has been renamed with three English letters.

What this book wants to do is walk that road again.

Not to prove that old technology matters more than new technology, nor to hand out FDE certificates to data veterans. Quite the opposite—veterans carry their own baggage: they tend to worship complete architectures, tend to build platforms first and look for business use later, and tend to mistake technical correctness for business effectiveness.

Newcomers not having stepped in old pits is not a flaw. What's troublesome is not knowing where the pits are and still keeping the pedal to the metal.

This book will make judgments, but it won't pretend to have standard answers. Which old skills should be carried forward and which old habits must be let go; which FDE concepts are just old ideas with new names and which have genuinely changed software delivery; which lessons from internet companies can be borrowed and which would get distorted when transplanted into traditional enterprises—on all these questions, I'll do my best to be clear.

In the end, we still have to come back to that wall.

Enterprises have spent decades writing real business into systems, turning the data in those systems into reports, and piling reports into platforms. Today, AI is beginning to try to go beyond reports and participate directly in action.

This last ten meters used to be walked mainly by people. Now machines want to walk it too.

Who guides it, who draws the lines for it, who pulls it back when it goes wrong, and how to prove the trip was worth it once it arrives—this is the real work FDE has to take on.

And to explain this properly, we must first return to the oldest and most easily overlooked step:

Why does an enterprise write a real transaction into a system.
