High-rate, direct-contract project information

For freelance engineers, "rate" refers to the monthly compensation paid by the client. Unlike a full-time employee's "monthly salary," it includes the following elements.

  • Compensation for labor (technical skill and working hours)
  • Social insurance premiums (the client's share is not included)
  • A substitute for paid leave and bonuses

Generally, rates are quoted in the format "X million yen per month (assuming 140–180 hours)." For example, a project listed as "800,000 yen per month, 160 hours worked" translates to an hourly rate of about 5,000 yen.

Rate calculation formula:

時給換算 = 月額単価 ÷ 月間稼働時間
例）800,000円 ÷ 160時間 = 5,000円/時

Also, when overage or shortfall in working hours occurs, a "settlement range" is established; for example, conditions such as "settlement range of 140–180 hours, overage rate = monthly rate ÷ 180, shortfall rate = monthly rate ÷ 140" are clearly stated in the contract. Since the settlement range directly affects take-home pay, it is an item you should always check when choosing a project.

In addition, under the Act on Appropriate Transactions between Freelancers and Business Operators, enforced on November 1, 2024, ordering businesses must disclose transaction conditions when outsourcing work to freelancers, and compensation must in principle be paid within 60 days from the date the deliverables are received (see: Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare, "For those working as freelancers and businesses outsourcing work", Japan Fair Trade Commission's special page on the Freelance Act). Check rates and settlement conditions in the transaction conditions disclosed before signing the contract.

  • 800,000 yen/month ≒ 5,000 yen/hour ≒ 40,000 yen/day

When comparing projects, it is clearest to convert everything to a monthly rate for comparison.

It is said that even for projects with the same work content and required skills, differences in distribution channels alone can create gaps of ¥300,000–500,000 per month (the figures above are theoretical net amounts after margin deductions). If you feel "my rate is too low," it is worth suspecting the distribution channel as well.

[Our company's actual data] Of our 183 published projects (all with distribution channels registered), excluding PM/PMO projects (22 projects) because their rate levels are exceptional and the counts by channel are skewed, the average rates were ¥782,000 for direct end-client (65 projects), ¥760,000 for direct prime contractor (86 projects), and ¥700,000 for third-tier subcontractor (10 projects), confirming the tendency for rates to drop the deeper the channel. However, the difference was at most about ¥80,000, not as large as the theoretical figures above (¥300,000–500,000/month) (the third-tier figure is a reference value based on only 10 projects; listed rates are monthly and are not net amounts after revenue-share deductions). Also, of the 17 projects currently recruiting, 15 (about 90%) are direct end-client.

Working days and work arrangements directly affect rates. As mentioned above, remote 3–4 days/week tends to have a lower rate per project than on-site 5 days/week, but total income can be maximized by running multiple projects in parallel.

Example patterns for maximizing monthly income:

  • Remote 4 days/week (¥600,000/month) × 1 project = ¥600,000/month
  • Remote 3 days/week (¥500,000/month) × 2 projects = ¥1,000,000/month (multiple concurrent projects)
  • On-site 5 days/week (¥850,000/month) × 1 project = ¥850,000/month

However, handling multiple concurrent projects can raise contractual constraints and quality management issues, so it is necessary to confirm contract terms with clients in advance.

In addition to pure technical skills, the following soft skills are essential for high-rate projects.

  • Requirements gathering skills: The ability to translate vague business requirements into technical specifications
  • Documentation skills: The ability to accurately produce design documents, proposals, and meeting minutes
  • Stakeholder management: The ability to communicate with non-technical executives and clients
  • Progress management and reporting: The ability to report issues early and propose solutions when problems arise

If you aim to move into upstream roles (PM, PdM, IT consultant), these skills are valued as much as or more than technical skills.

Even engineers with the same skills and background may be offered rates that differ by 200,000-300,000 yen per month depending on the agent they use. Agents earn the difference between what the end client pays and what the engineer receives as an intermediary fee (margin). Margin rates are generally said to be 10-30%, so choosing an agent with a low margin rate leads to maximizing your rate.

Key points when choosing an agent:

  • Transparency of margin rates (choose agents that disclose them)
  • Ratio of direct end-client and tier-1 projects
  • Specialization in your desired skills and role
  • The agent's technical understanding (whether the representative is a former engineer)
  • Register with multiple agents and compare offered rates

Engineers and PMs who can work with trending frameworks and technologies such as Scrum, Agile, and LLMs have high market value, and those who go independent as freelancers are in a position to easily land high-rate projects.

Contract renewal timing offers the highest success rate for rate negotiations. Below is a practical guide to how to negotiate.

Appropriate timing for negotiation:

  • 1-2 months before contract renewal (most important)
  • Right after acquiring or demonstrating a new technology or skill
  • After delivering significant contributions to a project
  • When you receive a high-rate offer from another agent (competitive proposal)

How to conduct rate negotiations (step by step):

  • Understand market rates: Register with multiple agents and gather information to confirm the market value of your skills
  • Organize your track record: Prepare quantifiable achievements, such as "implemented XX in this project and improved processing speed by 30%"
  • State the basis for your proposal: Present "market rates, offers from other companies, and your achievements" as grounds
  • Offer alternatives: If a rate increase is difficult, propose alternative conditions such as "reduced working hours" or "remote work"
  • Create competition among multiple agents: Use rates offered by other agents as negotiating leverage

Examples of what not to do when negotiating:

  • Asking for "a higher rate" without providing grounds
  • Requesting relaxed working conditions at the same time as a rate negotiation (too many demands)
  • Immediately terminating the contract if negotiations fail (ending it without preparing your next project is risky)

To maximize your rate, securing direct end-client projects (direct client projects) is the most effective approach. There are three main ways to obtain them.

1. Use agents specializing in high-rate, direct end-client projects

Choosing agents with a high proportion of direct end-client projects shortens the supply chain and increases your take-home rate. When choosing an agent, always check the "percentage of direct end-client projects" and "average rate."

2. Obtaining direct offers through social media and technical content

By consistently publishing technical content on X (formerly Twitter), Zenn, Qiita, and GitHub, more and more engineers are receiving direct offers from corporate recruiters and CTOs. Content in the "LLM/AI," "cloud architecture," and "product development" fields in particular generates strong responses.

3. Obtaining projects through networking and referrals

Referrals from existing clients or introductions from fellow engineers tend to offer higher rates since no agent is involved. Participating in communities (study groups, conferences, Slack) and building connections contribute to long-term rate growth.
