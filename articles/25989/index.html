Kickoff to Go-Live: How to Build an Implementation Timeline for Complex B2B SaaS

Quivly · 2026-10-23 · 6 min read

Summary

This Quivly guide argues that complex B2B SaaS implementation timelines slip because they are built backwards from a hoped-for go-live date rather than from the actual phases and dependencies of the work. It proposes a five-phase structure (kickoff, discovery, integrate, validate, cutover), each with a single accountable owner and explicit exit criteria. Milestones should record dependencies and roll forward automatically when dates slip. Duration estimates should come from a vendor's own past projects, grouped by segment and complexity. Go-live should be a decision based on predefined readiness gates covering technical, security, user, and support readiness. The forward deployed engineer leads technical discovery and integration, monitors signals like API error rates, and shares one timeline with customer success so both act on the same facts.

Key points

  • Timelines slip due to missing dependencies, omitted customer tasks, vague phase exits, and static dates.
  • Five phases: kickoff, discovery, integrate, validate, cutover, each with an owner and exit criterion.
  • Estimate durations from past projects grouped by segment; add buffer where dependencies cluster.
  • Go-live is a gate-based decision covering technical, security, user, and support readiness.
  • The FDE owns technical discovery and integration and shares one timeline with customer success.

Ask a customer when they expect to be live and you'll usually get a date. Ask how that date was set and the answer is often less precise: it came from the sales cycle, a board deadline or a rough sense of how long the last one took. A timeline built backwards from a hoped-for date tends to slip, because nobody has checked whether the work in between actually fits.

Complex B2B SaaS implementations need a timeline built the other way round: from the phases and dependencies the work really has, with an owner and an exit criterion for every step. That timeline then becomes the plan both the vendor and the customer manage against, from kickoff to go-live.

This guide shows how to build one. It covers the standard phases of an enterprise rollout, who owns each, how to set milestones and dependencies, the gates that decide whether you are ready for cutover, and where a forward deployed engineer fits. It draws on Quivly's Forward Deployed Engineers, Projects and Onboarding at Scale pages.

Why Implementation Timelines Slip

Timelines rarely slip because a single task took longer than expected. They slip because of how the plan was put together. The common causes are:

  • No dependencies: tasks are listed in order, but nothing records that data migration can't start until access is provisioned.
  • Customer work left off the plan: the vendor's tasks are scheduled, the customer's are assumed.
  • Vague phase exits: a phase ends when the team feels done rather than when a specific condition is met.
  • Static dates: when one milestone moves, the rest of the plan keeps its original dates and looks healthier than it is.

Each of these can be fixed at the planning stage, before kickoff, which is far cheaper than fixing it a week before go-live.

The Five Phases of a Complex Implementation

Most enterprise rollouts follow the same broad shape, even when the product and the customer differ. Quivly's FDE page shows a rollout plan with discovery, integration, validation and cutover phases. Add a kickoff in front and you have a structure that fits most complex B2B SaaS products.

  • Kickoff: confirm goals, scope, stakeholders, success metrics and the communication cadence. Exit when both sides have agreed on the plan.
  • Discovery: complete the technical discovery questionnaire, review the customer's architecture and agree on the integration design. Exit on architecture sign-off.
  • Integrate: set up single sign-on and access provisioning, build the connectors and data mappings, and wire the product into the customer's pipelines. Exit when the core integration is live in a test environment.
  • Validate: run user acceptance testing, data checks and a migration dry run, and train end users. Exit when the cutover gates are met.
  • Cutover: move to production, confirm the first value event and hand the account to its long-term owner. Exit on go-live sign-off.

Not every customer needs every step. A mid-market account might compress discovery and integration into a few days, while an enterprise account might need weeks for each. Quivly lets teams define separate onboarding blueprints by segment, product line or use case and assigns new accounts to the right track automatically.

Assign an Owner to Every Phase and Task

A timeline without owners is a wish list. Every phase should have a single accountable owner on the vendor side, and every customer-side task should have a named person, not a team.

A typical ownership split looks like this:

  • Kickoff: the implementation lead or customer success manager runs it, with the account executive attending to carry over sales context.
  • Discovery and integration: the forward deployed engineer or solutions engineer owns the technical design and build, paired with the customer's technical admin.
  • Validation: shared between the forward deployed engineer and customer success, since it combines technical checks with user training.
  • Cutover and handoff: customer success takes the lead, with engineering on call for production issues.

The rollout plan on Quivly's FDE page reflects this split: discovery and integration are owned by the FDE, validation by FDE and CS together, and cutover by CS. A Projects template example follows the same pattern, with kickoff, discovery and go-live each assigned to a different role.

Build Milestones and Dependencies, Not Just Tasks

Milestones are the checkpoints that tell you a phase is really progressing. Dependencies tell you what has to happen before each one can start. Together they turn a task list into a timeline.

Quivly's onboarding page gives a useful default milestone journey: workspace provisioned, core integration live, first value event, team trained and health review. For a technical rollout, the task list on the FDE page shows the work underneath those milestones:

  • Complete technical discovery and architecture sign-off.
  • Set up single sign-on and team access provisioning.
  • Configure the CI/CD pipeline integration.
  • Run the initial data migration and validation.
  • Conduct end-user training and complete the handoff checklist.
  • Establish success metrics and a review cadence.

For each milestone, record which earlier milestones it depends on. Then make sure the plan updates itself: on Quivly's shared timeline, slipped dates roll forward to dependent milestones automatically, and status rolls up from tasks to the project. That way the go-live date always reflects the real state of the work.

Estimate Durations From Your Own History

The most reliable duration estimates come from your own past implementations, not from the date the customer would like. Before setting dates, look at how long each phase actually took on similar projects.

  • Group past projects by segment and complexity, since an enterprise rollout and a mid-market one rarely share a timeline.
  • Measure each phase separately, including how long customer-side tasks took to complete.
  • Compare planned with actual effort. Quivly Projects tracks logged hours against planned hours on each project, which shows where your estimates are consistently too optimistic.
  • Add buffer where dependencies cluster, usually around security review, access provisioning and data migration.

If the resulting date is later than the customer wants, that is a conversation to have at kickoff, with options such as a smaller first go-live scope, rather than a surprise later.

Define Go-Live Gates Before You Need Them

Go-live should be a decision, not a date that arrives. The way to make it a decision is to define readiness gates during planning and check them before cutover.

Quivly's FDE page ties customer readiness gates to technical checkpoints. Its example rollout tracks readiness across engineering, CS alignment and security review, with cutover gates for single sign-on and provisioning, a migration dry run and a training session. A practical gate list for most complex products includes:

  • Technical readiness: integrations stable, data migrated and validated, access provisioned.
  • Security and compliance: any required review complete.
  • User readiness: key users trained and acceptance testing passed.
  • Support readiness: escalation paths agreed and the post-go-live owner named.

If a gate isn't met, the options are the same every time: fix the blocker, reduce scope or move the date. Agree in advance who makes that call on each side.

Where the Forward Deployed Engineer Fits

In complex implementations, the forward deployed engineer is the person who turns the plan into working software inside the customer's environment. On the timeline, the FDE typically:

  • Leads technical discovery and owns the integration design.
  • Builds and tests the connectors, mappings and pipeline integrations.
  • Monitors technical signals such as API error rates, data sync latency and gaps, and endpoint-level adoption during validation and after cutover.
  • Routes platform-side issues to engineering when they exceed FDE scope.
  • Gives customer success a plain-language view of technical risk.

Keeping the FDE and CS on one shared timeline matters more than any single task. When Quivly detects a technical risk, it sends the FDE an alert with full technical context and sends the CSM a plain-language summary with a suggested customer message, so both act on the same facts without a coordination call. After go-live, the same view shows product adoption, which gives the FDE a proactive agenda for each account.
