What is an FDE? What does a Forward Deployed Engineer actually do, and how does it differ from Sales Engineer, delivery, and algorithm roles

Original title: FDE 是什么？前沿部署工程师到底干什么，和售前、交付、算法岗差在哪

FDE 指南 · 2026-10-26 · 6 min read

Summary

This guide defines the Forward Deployed Engineer (FDE) as an engineer stationed at customer sites who writes code to integrate a company's technology into real business systems and is accountable for post-launch results. The term originated as military language and was brought into business by Palantir roughly two decades ago, later adopted by OpenAI and Anthropic. The article contrasts FDEs with pre-sales, delivery, algorithm, and support roles by location, coding, and accountability, lists four drawbacks of the model, and outlines a three-tier skill framework plus advice on certificates and entry paths.

Key points

  • FDE's three essentials: on-site, hands-on coding, accountable for results.
  • Palantir introduced the role commercially about 20 years ago.
  • Pre-sales owns signing, delivery owns acceptance, FDE owns outcomes.
  • Drawbacks: poor scalability, knowledge loss, heavy travel, outsourcing risk.
  • Entry priority: demonstrable projects > production skills > certificates.

The one-sentence version

An FDE is an engineer who is stationed at the customer's site, connects the company's technical capabilities into the customer's real business systems, and is accountable for the results after go-live.

Three keywords, all indispensable: on-site, hands-on coding, and accountable for results. Missing any one of them, and it becomes a different role.

Where the job title comes from

FDE stands for Forward Deployed Engineer. "Forward deployed" was originally a military term referring to troops deployed on the front line. About twenty years ago, Palantir brought the term into the business world to describe a special kind of engineer: instead of sitting at headquarters building general-purpose products, they are sent to customer organizations to use the company's platform to solve the customer's specific problems.

Later, companies like OpenAI and Anthropic adopted this role when bringing AI into enterprises, and FDE gradually evolved from one company's internal term into a common job title.

The reason it became hot in China in 2026 is straightforward: model capabilities are now sufficient, but the stretch where enterprises actually put AI to use—how to connect the data, how to change the workflows, who is responsible when things go wrong—has no one doing it. FDEs fill exactly that gap.

What they do every day

Different companies use the same title with different emphases, but the job boils down to these four things:

First, go on-site and understand the real workflows. The customer says "we want to use AI for customer service," but the reality may be that tickets are scattered across three systems, the knowledge base hasn't been updated in two years, and frontline staff don't use it at all. None of this shows up in requirements documents; you can only see it on-site.

Second, build it with your own hands. FDEs write code. They write the glue layer that connects models, platform capabilities, and customer systems—not training foundation models, but not just tweaking a few parameters either.

Third, be accountable for the results after go-live. This is the biggest dividing line between FDEs and other roles. If accuracy drops after launch or the customer's business metrics don't improve, the FDE has to go back and fix it.

Fourth, bring on-site problems back to the product. If the same pitfall appears at five customers, the FDE should feed it back to the product team so it becomes a general capability. An FDE who doesn't do this is just doing custom outsourcing.

How it really differs from four adjacent roles

This is where confusion is most likely. The table below breaks things down along three dimensions: "where you work / whether you write code / what you're accountable for":

RoleMainly works atWrites code?Accountable for
FDECustomer site + companyYes, and fixes thingsBusiness results after go-live
Sales Engineer / Solutions ArchitectCustomer meeting roomsUsually not; produces proposalsWhether the deal gets signed
Delivery / Implementation EngineerCustomer siteA little; deploys per a fixed planCompleting delivery per contract
Algorithm / Backend EngineerCompanyYesWhether features are implemented per requirements
Technical Support / Customer SuccessRemote + occasional site visitsBasically notWhether issues get responded to

The most easily confused are Sales Engineer and delivery.

Sales Engineers also understand technology, visit customer sites, and draw up proposals, but a Sales Engineer's endpoint is signing the contract. Whether the system runs well afterward is not part of their evaluation. An FDE's starting point is precisely after the contract is signed.

Delivery engineers are also stationed on-site and deploy systems, but their boundary is "install this thing per the contract." Once installation passes acceptance, the job is done. An FDE's goal is "after installation, the customer's metric actually moves"—if it doesn't, they keep fixing.

So in one sentence: Sales Engineers are accountable for signing, delivery engineers for acceptance, and FDEs for outcomes.

Why the role has downsides

The FDE model isn't without costs. The four most commonly cited in public discussions:

  • Costs scale headcount by headcount—one FDE can only serve a limited number of customers at a time, so economies of scale are poor
  • Knowledge accumulates in people—when people leave, the experience leaves with them
  • High intensity of travel and on-site work—a big impact on lifestyle
  • Can degrade into custom outsourcing—if the fourth task (feeding back to the product) isn't done, an FDE is just a more expensive implementation engineer

These four points mean FDE is not an "easy career switch" but a demanding role that consumes a lot.

What abilities the role requires

Translating "the four daily tasks" into the abilities employers actually assess, you get three layers:

LayerAbilityWhy it's needed
Hard thresholdProduction coding, system integration, deployment and troubleshootingPrototypes must be pushed to production, and when things break you must trace from logs to root cause
New premium itemsPrompt engineering, RAG, evals, agent orchestrationOnly written into job descriptions in 2026; most working engineers are still catching up
Determines your ceilingOn-site discovery, requirements translation, driving customer adoptionThe first layers can be learned; this one only grows out of real projects

To judge whether a role is a true FDE, look at which layer it tests: testing only the first layer means backend/implementation; testing only the third means Sales Engineer/consulting; only a role testing all three is an FDE. The relationship with certificates

Once the role is clear, the standard for judging certificates becomes obvious: whether an FDE certificate is worth it depends on how close what it tests is to the things above.

If it tests pure memorization of concepts, it only proves you've read the material; if it tests scenario judgment and solution design, it at least proves you've thought about real problems.

Note that the role and the certificate are two different things: public job descriptions rarely list any particular FDE certificate as a requirement; hiring looks at whether you can be accountable for results. A certificate's value is systematically organizing knowledge and providing a verifiable starting point—it cannot prove on-site judgment. For prioritizing your path in: demonstrable projects > production engineering ability > certificates.

Another article on this site, How Many Types of FDE Certification Are There, breaks down the four systems currently on the market one by one; that one covers "what exists," this one covers "what the role is."

Common misconceptions

"FDE is just a new name for AI Engineer"—No. AI Engineer is a skills label; FDE is a way-of-working label. An FDE may spend most of their time on data integration and workflow redesign, with the model part just calling existing APIs.

"FDE is an entry-level role"—Quite the opposite. Having to face customers, modify code, and own outcomes usually requires several years of engineering experience as a foundation; it's hard for newcomers to handle directly.

"Get a certificate and you can switch to FDE"—A certificate can prove you understand how the role works, but the core threshold for an FDE is on-site judgment, which can only grow out of doing projects.

"FDE is just an implementation consultant with a new name"—Implementation ends when the contract is delivered; an FDE is accountable for business metrics after go-live, and if the metrics don't move, they go back and fix things. These are two completely different boundaries of responsibility.

Frequently asked questions (FAQ)

What is an FDE? How do you explain it in one sentence?

An FDE (Forward Deployed Engineer) is an engineer stationed at the customer's site who uses engineering means to connect the company's technology into the customer's real business and is accountable for the results after go-live. Three keywords, all indispensable: on-site, hands-on coding, accountable for results.

What is FDE called in Chinese?

The two common Chinese translations are 「前沿部署工程师」 and 「前线部署工程师」, both corresponding to the English Forward Deployed Engineer. 「前线」 is closer to the military origin of "forward deployed," while 「前沿」 is more commonly used in Chinese technical contexts.

What is the difference between an FDE and a Sales Engineer or delivery engineer?

Sales Engineers are accountable for signing the deal, delivery engineers for acceptance, and FDEs for outcomes. The endpoint for pre-sales is signing the contract; for delivery, it's getting the system installed and passing acceptance; for an FDE, it's the customer's business metric actually moving.

Can someone without a technical background transition to FDE?

You can participate in FDE-style delivery (requirements discovery, outcome evals, driving adoption), but to formally hold an engineering role, the layer of production coding and system delivery is usually unavoidable. The realistic path is to start from the business side while building up engineering skills, rather than applying directly for a formal FDE position.

Why did FDE suddenly become hot in 2026?

Because model capabilities are now sufficient, but the segment where enterprises actually put AI into production—how to connect the data, how to change the processes, who is responsible when things go wrong—lacks people to do the work. FDEs fill exactly this gap, which is why they are being mentioned simultaneously by domestic cloud providers, big tech companies, and local governments.

Do FDEs need certifications?

It's not a hard requirement. Public job descriptions rarely list any specific FDE certificate as a must-have. What helps more in getting into the field is demonstrable real projects and production troubleshooting ability; certificates are better suited for systematically organizing knowledge and providing a verifiable starting point.
