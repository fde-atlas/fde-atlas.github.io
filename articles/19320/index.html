Databricks FDE Interview Questions: Spark and Unity Catalog

Original title: Databricks FDE 面试题：Spark 与 Unity Catalog

FDE Times · 2026-10-17 · 5 min read

Summary

Drawing on an Aced.io interview guide and Databricks job postings, this article breaks down the Databricks FDE interview process. It argues that the roughly 60-minute decomposition round, which starts from a vague business goal like customer retention, tests three things: deep Spark knowledge including runtime internals, Unity Catalog governance, and the ability to turn ambiguous requirements into architecture. It walks through examples such as data skew fixes, three-level namespaces, and lineage, and advises tailoring resumes to the Seoul, Zurich, and AI Engineer FDE roles.

Key points

  • The 60-minute decomposition round simulates a real customer meeting.
  • Clarifying questions in the first ten minutes decide the outcome.
  • Data skew answers should cover detection, cause, and remedies like salting.
  • Unity Catalog essentials: catalog.schema.object naming and lineage.
  • Three FDE roles differ in focus; tailor each resume accordingly.

Key Takeaways

  • The FDE role in Seoul requires deep understanding of Spark, including runtime internals; the Sr. FDE (Fullstack) role in Zurich hands the FDE authority over security and scalable architecture decisions.
  • The roughly 60-minute breakdown round starts from a vague business goal, and the clarifying questions at the start are just as important as the design itself.
  • Databricks' three FDE roles have different focuses: Spark and MLOps, production architecture, or GenAI. Pick the role first, then tailor your resume.

Imagine the interviewer opens with just one sentence: "A retail chain wants to use AI to retain customers—please design it." No table schemas, no numbers, and no one mentions deadlines.

According to an interview guide from Aced.io, Databricks' breakdown round looks exactly like this: using about 60 minutes to design a system starting from a still-vague business goal.

This round is hard because it closely resembles the real job. Databricks' Senior Forward Deployed Engineer (Sr. FDE, Fullstack) job description states that engineers build solutions to customers' data and AI problems together with them and push them to production. The responsibilities section is blunt: the FDE is responsible for leading technical projects and delivering production-grade systems.

So grinding LeetCode alone is not enough. What's really tested are three things: deep understanding of Spark, mastery of Unity Catalog governance, and the ability to turn vague requirements into architecture. Each section below comes with an example you can practice yourself.

Five interview rounds, but really only three abilities are tested

Aced.io's guide describes a five-round process: a 30-minute recruiter conversation, a technical screen, a round of coding in a notebook, the breakdown round, and a leadership round. This is a third-party description, not official documentation; the order and duration may vary by hiring team.

More reliable are the requirements listed in the job descriptions themselves. The Seoul role requires candidates to have extensive experience with Apache Spark distributed computing and knowledge of Spark's runtime internals.

It also requires candidates to understand and use MLOps, ML/AI models, and AI APIs, and to write code in multiple languages. The Zurich senior role gives the FDE authority over architecture decisions, provided the solution is secure and scalable.

Putting these two sources together: the notebook round tests Spark, the breakdown round tests architecture and governance, and the leadership round tests how you collaborate with customers. Prepare around these three abilities; don't split your time evenly across the five rounds.

Spark questions test what lies beneath the API

The phrase "runtime internals" means the interviewer wants you to explain why a job runs slowly, not just write a

join

. The classic example is data skew.

Suppose you use

customer_id

to join a

orders

table with a

customers

table, and Spark splits the data into 200 partitions after the shuffle. If one wholesale customer accounts for 40% of the rows in

orders

, that 40% all lands in the same partition. The result: 199 tasks finish within seconds, but the whole job waits on one task carrying nearly half the workload.

A good answer has three steps. First, detection: in the Spark UI, one task in the same stage runs far longer than the others. Second, the cause: a shuffle sends all rows with the same key to the same place.

Finally, the fix: broadcast the small table to avoid the shuffle; add "salt" to the skewed key to spread it across multiple partitions; or enable Adaptive Query Execution so Spark splits oversized partitions on its own.

Don't just read—practice. Create a skewed table in a notebook and record the job's runtime before and after the fix. Presenting numbers you measured yourself in the interview is far more persuasive than reciting definitions.

Unity Catalog is the answer for people who care about security

Databricks documentation defines Unity Catalog as a unified governance layer for data and AI built into the platform. When asked about it, there are two points you must be able to state off the top of your head.

The first is the three-level namespace

catalog.schema.object

. In the retail problem above, the customer features table could be named

retail.churn.customer_features

. Write the names clearly on the whiteboard to show you've thought about where the data lives and who can access it.

The second is lineage. Unity Catalog automatically tracks how data and AI assets flow and relate, from source data all the way to models, serving, and dashboards. The benefit is concrete: when the marketing director asks where a number on the dashboard comes from, you can trace it back to the source table without reading code.

The first ten minutes determine success or failure in the breakdown round

Aced.io's guide recommends using the first few minutes to clarify the goal, because this round simulates a real customer meeting. For the "retain customers" requirement, ask questions in this order:

"How is retention measured: the share of customers who repurchase within 90 days, or average order value?" "Who uses the output: the marketing team that sends weekly offers, or store staff?" "Where is the transaction data stored now, and how often is it updated?" "Is there any personal data that only certain people are allowed to view?"

Suppose the answers are: the marketing team runs a campaign once a week, the data is updated nightly, and customer phone numbers are sensitive information. Under these conditions, the architecture almost takes shape by itself.

The data is loaded nightly into a

retail

catalog, Spark computes features, the model scores churn likelihood, and the results are written to a table readable by the marketing team. The phone number column is accessible only to the campaign operations group.

Drawing the architecture diagram is only half the job; the other half is explaining why you chose this solution over alternatives. For example, choosing batch processing over streaming because campaigns run weekly—real-time scoring would only add cost, not value.

Adding MLOps-style model monitoring is because shopping behavior changes with the seasons. Pointing out lineage is to show the system is auditable.

Before applying, pick the right role

Databricks' three FDE roles have clearly different focuses, and your resume should be written separately for each.

FDE（首尔） Sr. FDE – Fullstack（苏黎世） AI Engineer – FDE
FocusSpark distributed computing, runtime internalsProduction-grade systems, architecture decisionsGenAI applications
Experience to showcaseDeep Spark; MLOps, ML/AI models, AI APIs; multilingual programming6+ years in data engineering, data platforms and analytics, or software engineeringRAG, multi-agent systems, Text2SQL, fine-tuning
Cadence with customers—Lead customers' technical projectsVisit customers every 4–8 weeks as needed

Whichever role you apply for, every line on your resume should state three things: a system you pushed to production, a measured number, and how you handled access control or data lineage. For example, "reduced join runtime from X to Y by fixing skew" carries far more weight than "proficient in Spark."

Mistakes that trip up even strong candidates

The most common mistake is starting to draw the architecture as soon as the requirements are heard. The interviewer is playing the customer; skipping the clarifying questions is like skipping requirements gathering when facing a real customer.

The second mistake was answering Spark questions at the API level, when the Seoul role explicitly requires runtime knowledge. The third mistake was only mentioning governance when pressed, while the senior Fullstack role requires solutions to be secure by design from the very beginning.

In the leadership round, a generic story like "I'm great at teamwork" can't compete with a story that has a specific customer, a specific conflict, and a decision you personally made.

You don't need to memorize every Databricks feature. You need to convince the interviewer that if you were sent tomorrow to meet a customer with a vague requirement, you would ask the right questions, choose the right architecture, and leave behind a system others can trace.
