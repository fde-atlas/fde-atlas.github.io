Scope Creep vs Discovery: Three Tests for FDE Projects

FDE Times · 2026-10-08 · 7 min read

Summary

This article addresses a core dilemma in forward deployed engineering: distinguishing legitimate discovery from scope creep. Freezing the SOW and charging for every change fits poorly with FDE work, but accepting every request erodes margin and delays go-live. The author proposes three tests: does a request clarify the outcome already bought or add a new one; does it shift time, cost or resources without adjustment; and can it be checked against signed documentation. Every significant change should get a same-day written summary ending in one of three decisions: approve, reject, or defer. Unacknowledged scope expansion breeds technical debt, since code quality is what flexes when deadlines stay fixed.

Key points

  • Three tests separate discovery from scope creep: objective, adjustment, and documentation.
  • Apply the tests to any request exceeding one day of work.
  • Good scope management starts before day one, with explicit out-of-scope lists and definitions of done.
  • Every significant change needs a same-day written record with one of three statuses.
  • Quietly absorbed scope expansion causes technical debt and margin loss.

Say yes to everything and the margin disappears; say no to everything and the client loses the reason it hired you instead of buying a standard product.

In brief

  • Freezing the SOW and charging for every change does not suit FDE work, but agreeing to everything erodes the margin and delays go-live.
  • Do not ask whether a request has changed. Ask whether it clarifies the outcome already bought or adds a new one, whether it shifts time and resources, and whether you have documentation to check it against.
  • Every significant change needs a short written summary on the same day, ending in one of three decisions: approve, reject or defer.

Picture yourself in the third week of a project. The invoice-reading agent you deployed for a client is running on real data, and their head of operations is delighted. Then she asks for one more thing: “While you’re at it, could it also check them against our supplier contracts? It shouldn’t take long.”

Questions like this decide the fate of a good many FDE projects. Say yes, and you may have just taken on two weeks of work nobody is paying for. Say no, and you may have just closed the very door the client opened to help you understand their problem more deeply.

This chapter shows how to answer that question on solid grounds rather than on instinct or awkwardness.

Why can’t an FDE simply freeze the SOW?

As Tandem describes it on its blog, an FDE spends roughly half their time with the client understanding the problem and the other half building a solution for that specific client. Under that model, requirements changing mid-project is normal, because sitting next to the client is exactly what produces new understanding.

That is also why the standard professional-services response, freezing the SOW and charging for every change, is, according to Tandem, a poor fit for FDE work. Going to the other extreme is just as dangerous, and both mistakes have very concrete consequences.

If you label every change “discovery”, the engagement gradually absorbs blocks of work nobody has priced, until the margin is gone and the go-live date slips. If you label every change scope creep, you turn the client into the user of a standardised product, when they chose you precisely because you would build something for them.

Three questions instead of a gut feeling

Tandem proposes a set of tests to replace the question “has the request changed?”. The three below are enough to handle the example in this chapter.

The objective test asks: does the new request clarify the outcome the client has bought, or add a new one? The adjustment test asks: does the request shift time, cost or resources without a corresponding adjustment?

The documentation test asks: can you point to what was scoped, what was said and what was delivered?

Tandem recommends running the tests on any request that takes more than a day of work. The threshold makes sense: smaller tasks you simply do to keep things running smoothly, while larger ones have to go through the filter.

Good scope is written before day one

The documentation test only works if you have something to check against. So stopping scope creep starts when you write the scope, not when the new request arrives.

An article from TimeTackle stresses that a good SOW must state clearly, without diplomatic softening, what you will not do. The out-of-scope list is often as important as the list of deliverables. That scope should be signed off by the stakeholders before work begins.

Baytech Consulting adds another piece: every feature and every milestone needs objective criteria for confirming that it is “done”.

For the invoice project above, you might write: “The agent correctly extracts the amount, date and supplier code from the sample invoice set provided by the client; out of scope: matching against contracts, payment approval, handwritten invoices.”

A worked example from start to finish

Suppose you receive two requests in the same week. Request A: “The agent misreads invoices that were scanned at an angle. Please fix it.” Request B: the contract-matching question from the start of this article.

For request A, the objective test gives a clear answer: skewed scans are still part of the invoice set the client supplied, so this clarifies the outcome already bought. It may take a few days, but it sits within the plan to meet the signed definition of done, so the adjustment test raises no alarm either.

The documentation test is fine too, because the scope document states exactly which dataset is covered. This is discovery. Do it.

Request B is a different matter. Contract matching is a new outcome, sits squarely on the out-of-scope list, and will push back go-live unless more time or people are added. This is scope creep, but that does not mean it must be refused. It needs to be put on the table and decided openly.

The change-control process TimeTackle describes requires an analysis of the impact on timeline, budget and technical risk before a decision is made. Baytech routes every request through a standard form and records the outcome: approved, rejected or deferred to a later phase. Put together, your change log might look like this:

Field Request A Request B
Description Read invoices scanned at an angle Match against supplier contracts
Objective test Clarifies the outcome already bought Adds a new outcome
Adjustment test Within the plan to meet the definition of done Delays go-live without extra resources
Documentation test Matches the signed dataset On the out-of-scope list
Impact A few days, low risk Timeline, budget and technical risk need a separate estimate
Decision Approve, start now Defer to phase 2, pending client confirmation

When you send this table, you are no longer saying “no” to the head of operations. You are saying “yes, here is the cost and here is when”.

Write it down the same day, even if it is just a message

According to FDE Academy, good FDEs turn every significant scope change into a short written summary on the same day, even if it is only an informal note.

A message along the lines of “Summary of this afternoon’s meeting: contract matching moves to phase 2, we’ll send an estimate on Monday, could you please confirm?” is enough.

The reason to do it immediately is technical debt. FDE Academy also notes that technical debt often arises when expanded scope is quietly absorbed rather than acknowledged. It is easy to see why: when nobody acknowledges the extra work, the old deadline stays put, and the thing left to flex is usually code quality.

When you have to take a shortcut, write one sentence on the spot about what you skipped and why. For example: “Skipped retry on accounting API timeouts because the client needs a demo on Friday; must be added before go-live.” A sentence like that rescues whoever takes over, and sometimes rescues you three months later.

Common mistakes

The most common mistake is running the tests in your head without writing them down, so when a dispute comes there is nothing to point to. The second is writing a scope that lists only the work you will do and leaves the out-of-scope section empty, which makes every new request look as though it is “probably in scope”.

The third is treating every change as the enemy. Request A in the example is the kind of feedback that makes the product better, and refusing it throws away the reason the client hired an FDE. The last is leaving decisions hanging: each request needs exactly one status, whether approved, rejected or deferred.

Where to practise this skill, and how to show it

You do not need an FDE title to practise. If you work in outsourcing or on a product team, every “can you just add this” ticket from a PM or client is an exercise in running the three tests.

When reading FDE or solutions engineer job descriptions, look for lines about working directly with clients or managing change requests; that is where it pays to have a story about this skill ready.

On your CV, instead of writing “worked with clients”, write a sentence with an outcome, such as how you moved an unplanned request to a later phase and still hit the go-live date.

In interviews, describe a time you told a client “not now” and the relationship got better. That story is more persuasive than any claim to be “a good communicator”.

Clients will always ask for more, and a good FDE project should attract those requests. Strong practitioners do not dodge them: they answer quickly, put it in writing, and every time can point to exactly which outcome the client bought.
