What Is Stakeholder Management for an FDE? Why Does It Matter?

Stakeholder management for an FDE is the practice of identifying stakeholders in the customer organization and continuously designing expectations and agreements.

An FDE (Forward Deployed Engineer) is a role popularized by Palantir: an engineer who embeds in the customer's workplace and takes responsibility for solving problems through to completion. Coding skills are of course essential. However, whether results are achieved depends heavily on understanding "who decides what, and who feels anxious about what." FDEs often stand between the customer's business departments, IT departments, executives, and frontline staff. Even a technically correct solution will never reach production without consensus.

How Does This Differ from Typical Project Management?

The difference lies in the fact that FDEs work inside the customer organization, engaging simultaneously with multiple departments whose interests do not align.

The PMI's PMBOK Guide, 6th Edition (2017), the standard for project management, defines stakeholder management as an independent knowledge area, divided into four processes: "Identify Stakeholders," "Plan Stakeholder Engagement," "Manage Stakeholder Engagement," and "Monitor Stakeholder Engagement" (Source: PMI, A Guide to the Project Management Body of Knowledge, 6th Edition). FDE practice can be organized within this framework, but the situation FDEs are in is different.

Why Does It Determine an FDE's Results?

Because an FDE's value is only realized when the solution is actually used, stakeholder buy-in determines outcomes just as much as technical quality does.

From my experience working in the field, projects tend to stumble not because of technical difficulty but because of stakeholder-related issues such as "the data owner wasn't cooperative," "the decision-maker changed midway," or "the frontline staff didn't use the new tool." FDEs are positioned to spot these risks early and act on them themselves. This is both a strength of the role and a heavy responsibility.

What Is the Process for Stakeholder Management?

The process consists of five steps: "Identify → Analyze → Plan → Execute → Monitor," and the basic approach for FDEs is to cycle through them repeatedly in short iterations.

The following five steps add "Analyze," which is especially important in FDE practice, as an independent step to the four PMBOK processes. They are not meant to be completed once in order. Every time a new stakeholder is discovered in the field, you return to the first step.

What Should You Prioritize in the First Two Weeks?

Right after starting, you should prioritize building the stakeholder list and understanding the decision-making routes before any other work.

FDEs are eager to get their hands on data and systems as soon as they arrive, but mapping the org chart against the actual flow of decision-making up front greatly reduces rework later. It is not uncommon for formal titles on the org chart to differ from actual influence. For example, "the specs of this core system are effectively decided not by the section manager but by a long-tenured staff member." In interviews, it is effective to always ask the following three questions:

  • When changing this data (system), whose final approval is required?
  • Have similar initiatives stalled in the past? If so, where did they stall?
  • If this initiative succeeds, whose work will change the most?

How Do You Build Consensus?

Rather than securing agreement in one big meeting, it works better to accumulate small confirmations and formalize them at the end.

What works well in FDE practice is "show-and-verify" agreement using a working prototype. Compared to explaining with slides, showing a screen running on real data surfaces the points stakeholders genuinely care about much faster. Before the decision-making meeting, the ideal approach is to talk individually with high-influence stakeholders to resolve their concerns, so the meeting becomes a place of confirmation. Document agreed content by clarifying roles with RACI (Responsible, Accountable, Consulted, Informed) and record it in an agreement log. Verbal agreements disappear when staff are reassigned.

How Do You Analyze Stakeholders? How Do You Use Influence and Interest?

In stakeholder analysis, you divide stakeholders into four quadrants along two axes—influence (power) and interest—and adjust your approach for each quadrant.

This two-axis analysis is known as the "Power/Interest Grid" proposed by Aubrey Mendelow in 1991, and it is introduced in the PMBOK as a representative analysis method (Sources: Mendelow, A. 1991, Proceedings of the 2nd International Conference on Information Systems; PMI, PMBOK Guide 6th Edition).

How should you engage with each of the four quadrants?

Each of the four quadrants has a standard way of engaging, and the FDE adapts it to the circumstances on the ground.

QuadrantInfluenceInterestBasic policyTypical example in FDE fieldworkSpecific approach
Manage closelyHighHighCollaborate closelyDivision manager, project ownerWeekly progress sharing, involvement in decision-making

MonitorLowLowKeep an eye on the situationPeripheral departmentsShare information as needed

From an FDE perspective, it is important not to treat field staff in the "Keep informed" quadrant lightly. Even without decision-making authority, they are the ones who ultimately use the tool, and whether it takes hold depends on them. Also, since the IT department often holds approval authority over security and data integration, even if their day-to-day interest is low, it is safe to assume they may suddenly move to "Manage closely" at some point.

How do you measure current versus desired engagement?

Engagement is managed by the gap between "current" and "desired" levels, prioritizing action for those with the largest gaps.

The PMBOK 6th Edition introduces the "Stakeholder Engagement Assessment Matrix," which evaluates stakeholder engagement levels on five levels: "Unaware," "Resistant," "Neutral," "Supportive," and "Leading." The FDE turns this into a simple table and records "Current (C)" and "Desired (D)" for each stakeholder.

The further a stakeholder's current position is from the desired one, the more time you need to invest in engaging them. Not every target needs to be "Leading." Set goals realistically—for example, for the IT department, reaching "Supportive" may be enough.

When should you update the analysis?

The analysis is not a one-time deliverable; update it whenever the organization changes or at key milestones.

Triggers for updating include personnel transfers and organizational changes, budget review periods, decisions to move from PoC to production, and the occurrence of failures or incidents. In practice, I find that a lighter routine—reviewing the grid about every two weeks to check whether anyone has moved quadrants—is easier to sustain.

What challenges are unique to FDEs? What are common failures and countermeasures?

The difficulty of being an FDE stems from the dual position of being an outsider while working inside the customer.

The deeper an FDE embeds in the customer's field operations, the more they can hear field staff's honest opinions. At the same time, they carry the interests of their own company and the scope of the contract. If this position is not handled well, the FDE ends up leaning toward either "doing whatever the customer says" or "imposing their own company's convenience."

What are the three challenges unique to FDEs?

The main challenges are threefold: "duality of position," "fluidity of requirements," and "interpreting between technology and business."

  • Duality of position: Acting as a member of the customer's side while also adhering to the policies of your own company (for example, a provider like Palantir or a business company like VACAN)
  • Fluidity of requirements: Every time a new issue is found in the field, the scope expands and drifts from what was agreed with the original decision-maker
  • Interpreting between technology and business: Explaining the same outcome in different words for each audience—ROI for executives, ease of use for field staff, security and operational burden for the IT department

What are common failures and countermeasures?

Most failures start with "only listening to the loudest voices," so preventing them through a structured analysis process is effective.

To add one field-level insight: stakeholders who oppose you are not "enemies"—in most cases, they are "people who know about risks that haven't been heard yet." When you carefully listen to the reasons for their resistance, information essential to the design often emerges, such as data quality issues or past failures. Stakeholder management for an FDE becomes easier to approach if you think of it not as a lobbying technique but as part of the engineering work of gathering field knowledge and shaping it into agreement.
