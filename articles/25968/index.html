Forward Deployed Engineer Career Path: What Comes After FDE

Falkster.AI · 2026-10-13 · 11 min read

Summary

Part 8 of The FDE Transition series argues that the FDE seat is a tour of duty with an unsettled career ladder. Companies try to fix retention by adding internal rungs (senior, staff, architect), but the author says rungs should be defined by what the product absorbed from deployments, not account size or tenure. He identifies three exits—into product, up to running the function, or founding a company—and urges writing them into offers. Evidence includes Palantir alumni founding 111+ companies raising $11.6 billion, and Wonderful hiring ex-founders for one in six to seven FDE roles.

Key points

  • Ladder rungs should measure what the product absorbed, not years on site.
  • Three exits: into product, up to managing the function, or founding a startup.
  • Miku Jha warns against FDEs becoming permanent glue between customer and product.
  • Palantir alumni founded 111+ companies raising $11.6 billion by 2024.
  • After the third deployment, FDE and manager should confirm the next seat in writing.

The one piece of forward deployed engineer news I found this week came from Seattle, and it was about founders.

On October 6 Todd Bishop at GeekWire reported that Foundations, a startup community with more than 300 founders, launched a service that hires those founders out to businesses to plan, build, and implement AI systems. It's priced per project. The service is called Foundations Design and Engineering, and Bishop notes the initials are a play on the job this series is about. Aviel Ginzburg, the co-founder: "I think the world of traditional consulting and startups is just absolutely going to merge."

Otherwise it was quiet. No new headcounts, no new academy. After last week I'll take it, and I'm not going to dress up a quiet week as a busy one. I'll use it to write the part the Foundations story points at.

Founders are walking into the seat for paid work. That isn't only a Seattle thing. Boaz Francis, who heads forward deployed engineering at Wonderful, told an Insight Partners panel in July that between one in six and one in seven of his new hires are ex-founders. His reason, as Santosh Iyer and Samma Hafeez wrote it up: "Those same people who can make a company out of nothing," he said, "will do well as an FDE."

The traffic the other way is old. Concept VC's count of Palantir alumni (undated, no byline, built on getPIN's data) found over 111 companies and $11.6 billion in capital raised by 2024, with "Forward Deployed Engineer being the most common background that founders had."

So the seat has one door that swings both ways. What it mostly lacks is any other door with a sign on it.

Rupanjana Bhattacharjee wrote the plainest version in August: "The career ladder is genuinely unsettled." Her warning is that you can do two years of strong work and then find "the promotion criteria were never defined, or were defined after the fact." And the way onward, she writes, "is frequently sideways into solutions architecture, product, or founding-engineer roles."

This is part 8 of The FDE Transition. Part 7 was the scorecard. This part is the career out of the seat: what an FDE becomes, and what the company owes them in writing before they start.

The short version

  • The FDE seat is a tour of duty, and most companies are trying to fix its retention problem by drawing a ladder inside it: senior, staff, architect.
  • A ladder inside the seat works only if each rung is defined by what the product absorbed, never by how big the account got or how long the engineer has been on site. The exits matter more than the rungs.
  • I count three, and I'd write them into the offer: into product, as the person who answers the handback packets you used to send; up, running the function without a bookings number; and out, to found a company on the problem you saw at three customers.
  • Staff FDE is a real fourth choice when it means owning a class of customer and the hours-per-deployment line for it.
  • The failure has a name, from Miku Jha at ServiceNow: "the permanent glue between the customer and the product." If your best FDE has been on the same account for a year with no next seat named, you're making glue.

A ladder inside the seat, and the rule for it

The Insight Partners panel is the best public look at what companies are doing about this. Bhaskar Roy, chief of AI products at Workato, has built a career ladder that climbs to an "architect" FDE. Miku Jha, who runs applied AI forward deployed engineering at ServiceNow, calibrates FDE pay to the top of the engineering ladder. Both are sensible. Neither says what an FDE is supposed to become.

Jha is the one who names the risk. The fastest way to lose an FDE, she warned, is to make them "the permanent glue between the customer and the product," or a "customer-facing coder" who goes from project to project. Her fix is to keep the work "intellectually compounding," and on her team's scorecard a productized asset counts as much as the customer outcome.

That's the generalization rate from part 7, used for retention. I think she's right, and I'd push it into the ladder itself.

Here's why. Bhattacharjee describes the engineer who gets so good at one customer that they're assigned it again, and then: "When you leave, much of what you learned leaves with you." Now promote that person for it. A senior title awarded for being irreplaceable at one account is a raise for being stuck. The company has paid extra to keep its best field knowledge in one head, at one site.

So if you draw the ladder, define the rungs by what left the site. An FDE lands deployments. A senior FDE lands them and the handback packets get written decisions. A staff FDE owns a class of customer, and FDE hours per deployment for that class fall. Every rung is a line on the scorecard you're already keeping. None of them is a count of years.

Three exits

Into product. This series started from the claim that the FDE is the Product Builder arriving from the services side. If that's true, the most natural next seat is the one on the receiving end of the handback: the named PM who reads the packet and owes a written answer in two weeks. Nobody is better placed to hold that job than someone who has sent packets into silence. Marcello Pedersen described Databricks rotating engineers between the FDE organization and the product team in both directions. Last week I argued with Paavany Jayanty's case for sending every PM on an FDE tour. This is the same road driven the other way, and I think it's the better direction, because the FDE arrives in product already knowing what three customers did with the thing.

I've hired a lot of product managers as a CPO. The hardest thing to learn in an interview is whether the person has really sat with customers or has read about them. A former FDE's deployments answer that before the interview starts. What they still have to learn is the editor's habit: saying no to a customer they like, for the sake of ten they've never met.

Up. Somebody has to run the function, and the org design chapter says who: the person whose job is to close the loop, with no bookings number. Nehal Vyas is honest about what the job is. "You think you'll be technical. You're not. You're an ops manager with an engineering title." Take that exit if you'd rather fix the system than the customer. The manager's product is the whole scorecard, all four numbers, and the hard one is hours per deployment, because making it fall means shrinking the thing you run.

Out. Concept VC's read of the Palantir founders: "It's no surprise that many FDEs went on to found companies addressing similar problems they saw firsthand." Vyas has the cold water: "Most FDEs don't become founders. Most become managers or stay as senior individual contributors." Both are true. The founder exit is the count of three with your own name on it. You saw the same problem at three customers, you sent the packet, and product said kill. Fine. That can be a company.

And as of this week the door plainly opens inward too. An FDE stint is now something a founder does between rounds, and something a hiring manager like Francis goes looking for.

Write the exit into the offer

A tour has an end. Almost no FDE offer says where.

I'd set the checkpoint by deployments, not months. After the third landed deployment, the FDE and their manager have one written conversation with four possible answers: another tour, staff, product, or manage. The manager has to bring a real opening for at least one of the last three. If there's never an opening, the ladder and the exits are decoration.

Pedersen's line about each engagement is that the job is "to work ourselves out of a job." That holds for the seat as much as for the engagement.

There's a clock on this. Anthropic's academy expects its first Claude Frontier Deployed Engineer badges in early 2027, and the announcement says nothing about what a badge holder does next. That's fair, it's a training program. But the 10,000 people it plans to train will ask their employers, and most employers have no answer yet.

Matthew Mayo at KDnuggets wrote the sentence I'd plan a career around: "The title may splinter or fade; the skill set it names will not." So plan around the skill, and treat the title as something you held for a while.

What this means in the seat, and at the top

If you're the FDE: keep your own record. Deployments landed, packets sent, decisions received, and what shipped for everyone because of you. It's the résumé for all three exits, and nobody else is keeping it.

If you run product: for your next PM opening, interview an FDE first.

If you run the company: ask how many FDEs moved into product in the last year. If the answer is none, your loop is made of documents and has no people in it.

One thing to try this week: list your FDEs. Next to each name write the account they've been on longest, how many months, and which exit they'd pick if you asked. Then ask. A year on one account and a blank in the last column is glue, and Jha already told you what happens to glue.

Frequently asked

What is the career path for a forward deployed engineer?+

There is a ladder inside the seat and there are three exits from it. The ladder, where it exists, runs FDE, senior, and staff or architect, and it should be defined by what the product absorbed from the engineer's deployments, not by account size or years on site. The exits are into product, as the person who answers the handback packets the FDE used to send; up, managing the FDE function without a bookings number; and out, founding a company on a problem seen at three customers. Rupanjana Bhattacharjee wrote in August 2026 that the career ladder is genuinely unsettled, so the practical advice is to get the exits written into the offer.

What do forward deployed engineers do after the role?+

Matthew Mayo at KDnuggets listed the common moves on October 2, 2026 as product management, founding a company, applied AI engineering, and solutions leadership. Nehal Vyas wrote in April 2026 that most FDEs don't become founders and that most become managers or stay as senior individual contributors. Concept VC's count of Palantir alumni found over 111 companies founded and $11.6 billion raised by 2024, with forward deployed engineer the most common background among those founders.

Should a company build a career ladder for FDEs?+

Yes, with one rule: define each rung by what left the customer's site and reached the product. An FDE lands deployments. A senior FDE lands them and gets written decisions on the handback packets. A staff FDE owns a class of customer, and FDE hours per deployment for that class fall. A ladder measured by account size or tenure on site rewards being irreplaceable at one customer, which is the thing Miku Jha at ServiceNow warned about when she said the fastest way to lose an FDE is to make them the permanent glue between the customer and the product.

Is forward deployed engineer a good path into product management?+

It is the most natural exit from the seat. The product role that receives the field's handback has to decide what gets generalized, kept as configuration, or killed, and a former FDE knows what those packets cost to write. Marcello Pedersen described Databricks rotating engineers between the FDE organization and the product team in both directions. The one thing an FDE has to add is the ability to say no to a customer they like, for the sake of customers they have not met.

Do most FDEs become founders?+

No. Nehal Vyas wrote in April 2026: 'Most FDEs don't become founders. Most become managers or stay as senior individual contributors.' The founder exit is real anyway. Concept VC found forward deployed engineer was the most common background among founders who came out of Palantir, and the traffic now runs the other way too. Boaz Francis said about one in six to seven of Wonderful's new hires are ex-founders, and on October 6, 2026 GeekWire's Todd Bishop reported that Foundations launched a service hiring its founders out to build AI systems for businesses.

The one new item I found was Todd Bishop's October 6 GeekWire report on Foundations Design and Engineering, a service from the Seattle and San Francisco startup community Foundations that connects businesses with its founder members to plan, build, and implement AI systems, priced per project. What is The FDE Transition series?+

A twice-weekly series on falkster.com for forward deployed engineers and the leaders building around them. Part 1 is the thesis that the FDE is the Product Builder arriving from services.
