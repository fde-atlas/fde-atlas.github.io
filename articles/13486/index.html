Palantir Forward Deployed Engineer Interview: Decomposition

Artificial Intelligence Uni (AIU.ac) · 2026-11-08 · 6 min read

Summary

Drawing on anonymous Glassdoor candidate reports, this article explains the decomposition round in Palantir's Forward Deployed Engineer interview, where prompts like designing a food delivery app or Pacman test scoping rather than technical depth. It argues candidates should spend the first third of the time defining the goal, user, success measure, and assumptions before proposing architecture, illustrated with a manufacturing defect-reduction example. It also lists common mistakes and connects the skill to AIU's seven-stage deployment lifecycle and its CFDE certification.

Key points

  • Candidate reports describe the round as high-level system design; Palantir publishes no format.
  • The Delta role, created in the early 2010s, measures success by customer impact, not specs.
  • A six-step method: restate goal, find user and decision, fix metrics, state assumptions, set boundaries, split components.
  • Common failures: diagramming first, decomposing by tech stack, never stating what you refuse to build.
  • In 2026, Anthropic, OpenAI, AWS, Microsoft, and Google Cloud launched forward-deployed engineering divisions.

The palantir forward deployed engineer interview includes a decomposition round, and it rewards one habit above all: scoping the problem before solving it. You get a deliberately vague prompt. You agree what success means, state your assumptions aloud, then split the work into parts someone could actually build. This article shows how to do that in order.

Key takeaways

  • What the decomposition round looks like, according to candidate reports.
  • A step-by-step method for scoping a vague prompt, with a worked example.
  • The mistakes that sink otherwise capable candidates, and how the skill carries into real deployments.

What the decomposition round asks of you

Palantir does not publish its interview format, so the best evidence is candidate testimony. It is anonymous and anecdotal, so read it as a pattern, not a specification. In one October 2025 account on Glassdoor, the decomposition round was described as very high level system design, for example how to build a food delivery app. The learning round looks at how you approach code you have never seen, and a separate coding round follows.

The prompts vary widely. Other Glassdoor reviewers report being asked how to implement Pacman, and another Glassdoor entry lists building a concert hall where every performance ends in a standing ovation. The domain is rarely the point. A separate Glassdoor review says you could take the question in any direction and explain your assumptions. Direction and assumptions are what get assessed.

Why Palantir tests scoping first

The role was built around goals, not specifications. The Pragmatic Engineer reports that the Forward Deployed Software Engineer role was created at Palantir in the early 2010s and named Delta. It also quotes Palantir describing deltas as people who measure success in terms of impact on the customer’s goal, with the example of manufacturers wanting fewer defective products coming off the assembly line. Nobody hands you a requirements document for that. Someone has to turn it into one.

That is why the interview looks the way it does. According to First Round Review, former Palantir recruiting lead Shilpa Balaji says Palantir oriented FDE interviews around the real technical challenges the company was trying to solve for customers. The role has since spread. Wikipedia notes that Anthropic, OpenAI, Amazon Web Services, Microsoft and Google Cloud announced new divisions oriented around forward-deployed engineering in 2026, so the same skill is now tested well beyond Palantir.

How to work a palantir forward deployed decomposition case

The angle is simple: scope before you solve. Spend the first third of the time on the problem, not the architecture. A workable order:

  • Restate the goal in one sentence and ask whether that is what the customer wants.
  • Find the user and the decision. Who acts on the output, and what do they do differently because of it?
  • Fix a measure of success and a baseline, even a rough one.
  • State assumptions out loud and mark which ones you would check first.
  • Draw the boundary: what data comes in, what leaves, what is out of scope.
  • Split into components, order them, and name the thinnest slice you would ship first.

A worked example

Take the manufacturing goal above: reduce defective products from an assembly line. Scoping questions come first. Which defect types matter most? Who sees a defect today, and how late? What inspection or sensor data exists, and who owns it? Then the split: get data access and clean it; link defect records to line events; detect or predict; put the result in front of the line supervisor; capture whether the supervisor acted. The first slice might be one line, one defect type, read-only. That ordering is illustrative, not a reported Palantir answer, but it shows the shape: outcome, user, data, then components.

Notice what the example does not do. It does not pick a model or a database in the first five minutes. Those choices depend on answers you do not have yet.

Mistakes that sink strong candidates

The commonest is jumping straight to boxes and arrows. A diagram drawn before the goal is agreed looks confident and proves little. The second is decomposing the technology (frontend, backend, database) instead of the outcome. The third is never saying what you would leave out.

The fourth is about how you use the interviewer. One candidate on Glassdoor wrote that they stopped at several points to discuss the approach with the interviewer as they would a colleague, and concluded that the interview may still be designed to gauge a capacity to work independently. That is one account, so do not over-fit to it. A sensible middle path is to narrate your reasoning continuously and check in at deliberate checkpoints, such as after scoping and after the split, rather than after every step.

Leaving things out matters more than it sounds. The Pragmatic Engineer piece says the ability to say no is part of doing well in the role. Use the Refusal Test as a rehearsal prompt when you practise a case:

What did your system refuse to build, and what does the refusal look like?

If your plan has no refusals, it probably has no scope.

From interview skill to deployment practice

The interview samples a compressed version of real work. The Glassdoor data is a reminder that hiring loops are short while deployments are not, so the round can only test the habit, not the full practice. The Certified Forward Deployed Engineer (CFDE)™ exists because scoping, like the rest of the role, can be taught and assessed against criteria, not left to instinct.

In AIU’s framing, scoping is one stage of a longer sequence:

AIU teaches the deployment lifecycle in seven stages: post-sale handoff, discovery, scoping, build, deploy, iterate and hand-back.

Scoping sits after discovery and before the build. Discovery tells you what the customer says the problem is. Scoping turns that into a bounded commitment: what will be built, for whom, what it will not do, and what has to be true for it to work. Your decomposition answer is a ten-minute version of that commitment. CFDE treats the course as the place to learn it and the separate examination as the place it is certified.

To practise, pick any vague goal from your own work and run the six steps against it, aloud, with a timer. Then ask a colleague what you assumed without saying so.

Frequently asked questions

What is the decomposition interview in a Palantir forward deployed engineer interview?+

It is a round where you take a broad, vague prompt, such as designing a food delivery app, and break it into components while explaining your assumptions. Candidate reports on <a href="https://www.glassdoor.com/Interview/Palantir-Technologies-Foward-Deployed-Engineer-Interview-Questions-EI_IE236375.0,21_KO22,46.htm">Glassdoor</a> describe it as very high level system design, so the quality of your scoping matters more than technical depth.

How should I practise a decomposition case?+

Take a vague goal, restate it, identify the user and the decision, fix a measure of success, state assumptions, draw the boundary, then split into ordered components with a first slice to ship. Do it aloud against a timer, and say what you would refuse to build.

Does the interview include coding as well?+

Candidate accounts on Glassdoor describe several parts: a decomposition round, a learning round on unfamiliar code and a coding round. Formats change and are not published by Palantir, so treat these accounts as a guide, not a guarantee.
