Forward deployed engineer travel and remote work

Kernavique · 2026-11-20 · 19 min read

Summary

Kernavique reviewed 34 employer job ads for forward deployed engineers and adjacent roles on November 20, 2026, examining travel requirements and work location. Only 9 ads state a travel figure, with 25% the most common (5 ads); Deloitte's three ads each say 50%, and Accenture's says 0 to 100%. Sixteen ads mention no travel at all, and 8 use vague wording like "as needed." Among 298 US postings across 177 public job feeds, only 16 fall in a Remote, United States bucket at 12 employers, and 6 of those carry hybrid or onsite wording. The article warns that remote does not mean no travel—Monte Carlo's remote role requires roughly 25% travel, and a C3 AI ad listed as "Remote" is actually full-time onsite at a secure government facility. The authors advise candidates to ask what travel figures measure, who decides, destinations, longest single stretch, office rules, and relocation terms, and recommend employers state these six items explicitly in ads.

Key points

  • Only 9 of 34 ads state a travel figure; 25% is most common.
  • Deloitte's three FDE ads each state 50% travel on average.
  • 16 of 298 US postings are remote-US roles across 12 employers.
  • Remote listings can still require travel or even full-time onsite work.
  • Candidates should ask what travel figures measure and about office rules and relocation.

The headline counts

We read 34 employer ads on November 20, 2026 for what they say about travel and where the work is done. Most are for forward deployed engineers. A few are for neighboring roles on the same teams: applied AI engineers, a deployment strategist, a product manager and a lawyer.

Of the 34 ads:

  • 9 give a travel figure. The most common is 25%, in 5 of the 9. Three say 50%, and one says 25 to 75%.
  • 1 more gives a range of “0 to 100%”, which is a figure in form only.
  • 8 mention travel without a figure, in words such as “as needed”.
  • 16 say nothing about travel in our read.

On where the work is done:

  • 3 are fully remote in the US, and 3 more offer a remote option beside office hubs.
  • 6 state an explicit office rule: three days a week in one, four days a week in two, three to five days in one, and 25% of the time in two.
  • 1 mentions relocation.

Two cautions. “Nothing” means our read of the ad did not return a travel line, which is weaker than proof of silence, so open the ad before relying on it. And the 34 are not 34 employers: two of the five 25% figures are Palantir’s, and all three 50% figures are Deloitte’s.

One third party gives a market figure. Bloomberry, which analyzed 1,000 postings, says 68% of roles require travel (Bloomberry, updated January 25, 2026). It states no method.

A wider check. A separate set of 42 ads with published pay, read the same day, carries a travel column too. In it, 24 of 42 state travel in some form and 18 of 42 do not. The heaviest and lightest stated figures:

  • Anduril, Air Defense: up to 80% of the year, up to two months at a time, and not eligible for remote or hybrid work.
  • Dataminr: two figures in one ad, “approximately 25–75%” and “50-75% to secure customer sites”.
  • Caylent: up to 50%. Deloitte, Frontier GenAI: 50% on average. Glean: 25 to 50%.
  • Databricks: 20%. Guidehouse: up to 10%.

Remote roles

On the 177 public job feeds we read on November 20, 2026, 16 of 298 US postings titled “forward deployed” fall in a bucket we call Remote, United States. They sit at 12 employers. One of the 16 mentions a security clearance.

How the bucket is defined. A posting goes in when one of its listed locations contains both “remote” and “United States”, “US” or “USA”, or when a location is the single word “Remote” and the feed’s country field says United States. A posting can be in this bucket and in a city at the same time.

Read the last column with care. Six of the 16 postings carry “Hybrid” or “OnSite” wording in the same feed record that lists a remote location. The bucket tells you a remote US location is listed. It does not tell you the job is remote for everyone.

Other limits:

  • Not every title is an engineer. The 16 include a product manager, a strategist, two researchers and a role titled for marketing.
  • Reddit’s senior ad serves internal teams. Its customers are Reddit’s own sales, marketing, business development and finance staff.
  • Onebrief’s ad mentions an active Secret clearance.
  • Some remote wording falls outside the bucket. Two Databricks postings say “Remote - California” and one Monte Carlo posting says “Remote, Americas”. They are listed as written and not counted here. Eleven postings at Cloudflare, Vannevar Labs and Tines name no country and are not in the US count at all.
  • The feeds are a convenience sample. Amazon, ServiceNow, Deloitte and employers on Workday are not in them.

Remote does not mean no travel. Monte Carlo’s remote ad states roughly 25% travel to customers. Glean’s says the role is remote and requires 25 to 50% travel. Dataminr’s ad, on a Workday board outside our feeds, is listed as US remote and states 50 to 75% travel to secure customer sites.

On-site at the customer, and relocation

Travel figures cover trips. Some ads describe something closer to a posting.

  • C3 AI, Lexington Park. The job board’s location field says “Remote”. The ad says the role is performed full time on-site in a secure government facility in Southern Maryland.
  • OpenRouter. The engineer must live in the San Francisco Bay Area and work regularly from customers’ offices across it.
  • Turing. Three to five days a week in person, at the client site or a planned Manhattan office, depending on client requirements.
  • Anduril, Connected Warfare. Embedded on-site at customer locations at least 50% of the time.
  • Anduril, Strategic Defense. Travel to the Asia Pacific region for up to a month at a time, up to 50% of the year.
  • Stripe, Privy. At least 50% of the time in a given month in the local office or with users.

The office rule is a separate question from customer travel, and an ad can set both. Anthropic’s Staff Forward Deployed Engineer ad asks for 25% travel to customer sites and at least 25% of the time in an office.

Relocation. Of the 34 ads, one mentions it: Palantir’s New York ad lists “Relocation assistance” as a benefit. Among the 42 ads with published pay, Scale AI’s public sector ad is the only other. It states 25% travel, up to 50% as necessary, “and/or relocation to Washington DC or St. Louis”. In one ad relocation is a benefit, and in the other it is a possible condition.

What to ask before you accept

The ads leave gaps. These questions close them, and each comes from something the ads above show.

  • What does the figure measure? No ad we read says whether 25% counts days, nights or weeks. Deloitte’s 50% is “on average”. Palantir’s 25% is “up to”.
  • Who decides? Palantir’s ad says travel is flexible by personal preference. Accenture’s and Turing’s tie it to client requirements.
  • Where does the travel go? One city of customer offices, as at OpenRouter, is a different life from a month at a time overseas, as in Anduril’s Strategic Defense ad.
  • Is it trips or a posting? Ask about the longest single stretch. Anduril’s Air Defense ad states up to two months at a time.
  • What is the office rule when you are not traveling? The ads we read state three days, four days, three to five days, and 25% of the time.
  • Does “remote” in the listing match the ad text? C3 AI’s Lexington Park ad shows why to check.
  • Is relocation a benefit or a condition? Two ads mention it, one each way.
  • Is the customer site a secure one? Dataminr’s ad ties its heavier travel figure to secure customer sites, and C3 AI’s secure-facility role is full time on-site.

These fit naturally into the first conversation. Our forward deployed engineer interview guide covers what else is public about the process.

What a company should state in the ad

In our read, 24 of the 34 ads gave no travel figure: 16 with no travel line and 8 with words only. An applicant comparing two offers cannot weigh “as needed” against a published number, so the ad with a figure answers the question first.

State six things:

  • A figure, and what it measures. “Up to”, “on average” or “typically”, in days or weeks.
  • Where the travel goes. Local customer offices, US-wide or international.
  • The longest single stretch, if any assignment runs past a week.
  • The home base and the office rule. A number of days, as Tennr and Intercom give.
  • Whether remote is real. Make the location field and the ad text agree. If the role is remote with travel, give both, as Monte Carlo and Glean do.
  • Relocation, as a benefit or as a condition, in those words.

A range of “0 to 100%” is accurate for a consultancy with many clients, and it tells an applicant nothing. If travel depends on the engagement, say what the last year looked like on the team that is hiring.

Travel is one of the checks that separates this role from its neighbors. The others are in forward deployed engineer vs solutions engineer. What the AI and agent versions of these ads ask for is in forward deployed AI engineer and agent engineer roles.

In practice

If you are an engineer, treat the travel line as the start of a question, not the answer. Read the location field against the ad text, take the eight questions into the first call, and write down what you are told. For what the job involves beyond travel, see our forward deployed engineer role page. If you want roles that match the travel you can accept, send us your details and résumé and say what that is.

If you are hiring, decide the travel load before you write the ad, not during the offer. Put the figure, the destination, the office rule and the remote terms in the ad itself. If you want help setting those terms against what other employers publish, ask Kernavique for help.

What we left out

  • An average travel figure. The ads use different measures, and most give none. Averaging them would invent precision.
  • Any claim that an ad is silent. “Not stated” means our read did not return the item. Open the ad before relying on an absence.
  • Long quotations. Our reading tool shortens sentences, so we paraphrased and kept quotations to a few words.
  • Two copies of closed ads. A Texture ad on an investor’s job board and a Northslope ad on an aggregator both carried travel wording. Neither is a live employer ad, so neither is in the 34.
  • Contract postings from agencies. Four carried travel wording. They are not employer ads.
  • On-site and hybrid wording for many Greenhouse employers in the feed counts. Greenhouse feeds have no workplace field, and the ad text was not searched for it.
  • Remote counts for employers outside the 177 feeds. Amazon, ServiceNow, Deloitte and Workday employers are not in the 16.
  • What travel is like in practice. No employer we read publishes days traveled per engineer. We report what the ads promise, not what happens.
  • Bloomberry’s 68% as a market fact. It is one third party’s figure with no stated method.

Questions

  • How much do forward deployed engineers travel?
  • Where the ads give a figure, 25% is the most common: 5 of the 9 ads with a figure, among 34 employer ads read on November 20, 2026. Deloitte’s three ads say 50% on average, and Palantir’s deployment strategist ad says 25 to 75%. In a separate set of 42 ads with published pay, stated travel runs from up to 10% at Guidehouse to up to 80% of the year at Anduril.
  • Can a forward deployed engineer work remotely?
  • Some can. On 177 public job feeds read on November 20, 2026, 16 of 298 US postings titled “forward deployed” list a remote US location, at 12 employers. Several of those also list a city, and the feed marks some as hybrid or on-site, so read the ad itself. Remote does not mean no travel: Monte Carlo’s remote ad states roughly 25% travel and Glean’s states 25 to 50%.
  • Do forward deployed engineers work on-site at the customer?
  • Several ads read on November 20, 2026 say so directly. C3 AI’s Lexington Park ad says the role is performed full time on-site in a secure government facility. OpenRouter’s ad asks for regular work from customers’ offices across the San Francisco Bay Area. Anduril’s Connected Warfare ad describes being embedded at customer locations at least 50% of the time.
  • Do employers pay relocation for forward deployed engineers?
  • Rarely, on the evidence of the ads. Of the 34 ads we read, one mentions it: Palantir’s New York ad lists “Relocation assistance” as a benefit. Among the 42 ads with published pay, Scale AI’s public sector ad is the only other, and there relocation to Washington DC or St. Louis is a possible condition of the job.
  • What does “travel up to 25%” mean in practice?
  • The ads do not define it. None of the ads we read says whether the figure counts days, nights or weeks, or whether it is an average or a ceiling. Deloitte says 50% “on average” and Palantir says “up to 25%”, which are different promises. Ask the employer how the figure is measured and what the last year looked like on the team you would join.
  • What should a company say about travel in the ad?
  • A figure, what it measures, where the travel goes, and the office rule. In our read, 16 of 34 employer ads said nothing about travel, and another 8 mentioned it without a figure. An ad that states the figure and the home base answers the first two questions an experienced engineer will ask.
