FDE vs Sales Engineer: From "Signing the Contract" to "Delivering in Production"

Original title: FDE vs 售前工程师：从「签下合同」到「交付落地」

FDE中国 · 2026-08-14 · 10 min read

Summary

This article contrasts pre-sales engineers with Forward Deployed Engineers (FDEs), arguing the key difference lies in where responsibility closes: pre-sales work ends at contract signing, while FDE work ends when a system runs reliably in production and delivers business value. It compares the roles across mission, timeline, skill stack, customer relationships, and deliverables, noting that AI projects are blurring the boundary between pre-signature validation and post-signature delivery. It cites Anthropic's FDE role split of roughly 40% full-stack building, 30% architecture, and 30% customer discovery, and Palantir's bootcamps that replace lengthy POCs. The piece adds a China-specific angle: pre-sales solution roles are the closest existing talent pool for FDEs, and xinchuang plus private deployment make post-signature engineering heavier than overseas. Readers are advised to split a recent project's milestones into pre- and post-signing phases to gauge their distance from FDE work.

Key points

  • Pre-sales is accountable for winning deals; FDEs for production outcomes.
  • Anthropic FDE work: ~40% building, 30% architecture, 30% customer discovery.
  • Palantir bootcamps validate with real customer data in days, replacing long POCs.
  • China's private deployment and xinchuang make post-signature engineering heavier.
  • Doing POCs is not FDE capability; production delivery needs evals, security, ops.

Amid the AI toB hiring boom, the Sales Engineer is probably the role most often compared with the Forward Deployed Engineer (FDE). The reason is straightforward: both face customers, both need technical knowledge, and both do demos and solution design. But if you put the two job descriptions side by side and read them carefully, you'll find they answer "what counts as success" completely differently—the Sales Engineer's answer is "the customer signed the contract," while the FDE's answer is "the system is stably generating business value in the customer's production environment." This one difference gives rise to an entire set of divergences in work content, skill stack, evaluation methods, and even career exits. This article breaks down these differences clearly to help you decide whether to keep deepening your pre-sales career or migrate toward FDE. If you'd also like to compare another similar role, see FDE vs Solutions Engineer on this site.

One-sentence positioning: one is accountable for winning the deal, the other for making it land

The Sales Engineer is the technical arm of the sales organization. Its core mission is to help sales win deals: understanding customer needs, designing solutions, doing demos and POCs, responding to bids, and surviving technical reviews. Once the contract is signed, the Sales Engineer's main battle is over, and the project is handed off to the delivery or implementation team. In other words, the Sales Engineer's responsibility ends at the moment the commercial commitment is made.

The FDE's core mission begins exactly at that moment. The FDE role was invented by Palantir, inspired by "forward deployed soldiers"—engineers stationed directly in the customer's environment, using real data and real constraints to make the solution actually run. Today, OpenAI, Anthropic, Google Cloud, and other companies are hiring FDEs at scale (see FDE role evolution analysis 2023-2026). What the FDE takes over is precisely the commitment left by pre-sales: the capability you promised the customer, you deliver with your own hands in the production environment. Its responsibility ends at the moment business value is validated, not at contract signing.

Dimension one: core mission and evaluation signals

Evaluation methods are the fastest ruler for understanding the difference between the two roles. A Sales Engineer's year-end review usually revolves around a set of business numbers: how many bids participated in, how many won, the POC conversion rate, whether the solution dragged down gross margin. These metrics determine the Sales Engineer's optimal strategy: keep commitments within "deliverable boundaries" and shorten the pre-signing cycle as much as possible.

The FDE's evaluation, by contrast, revolves around delivery quality: the delivery cycle from first prototype to stable production, acceptance pass rate, post-launch business metrics (e.g., how many labor hours a business process saved), and whether the customer renews or expands usage. Third-party research shows that Anthropic's FDE role explicitly breaks down its work into roughly 40% full-stack building, 30% architecture, and 30% customer discovery (per tryexponent.com's tracking)—a ratio that itself embodies "accountability for outcomes." Putting customer discovery into the job description means the FDE must constantly calibrate "is what I'm building what the customer actually needs," rather than just building to spec.

For practitioners, this difference carries an important hint: the Sales Engineer's sense of fulfillment comes from "persuasiveness validated by the market," while the FDE's comes from "engineering validated by the business." Both are real, but the temperaments are completely different. If you enjoy telling a complex technical story until the customer's decision-makers nod, pre-sales is your home turf; if you enjoy watching the code you wrote produce results every day on the customer's production line, FDE is your home turf.

Dimension two: the timeline—which part of the contract lifecycle does the work happen in

Lay out a toB project from start to finish: requirements discovery, solution design, POC validation, commercial negotiation, contract signing, development and integration, evals and acceptance, launch and operations, expansion and repurchase. The Sales Engineer's home turf is the first half; the FDE's is the second half.

But this timeline has undergone a structural change in the AI era: the wall between the first and second halves is being torn down. The reason is the uncertainty of large model projects—many capability boundaries can only be known by trying them on real data, and no matter how beautiful the POC is, it isn't enough to prove production-environment performance. As a result, leading companies generally put "pre-signing validation" and "post-signing delivery" in the hands of the same team. Palantir's approach is to replace lengthy POCs with bootcamps: build a runnable application on the customer's real data within days, and after validating value, the contract starts small and expands gradually (per everestgrp.com's analysis). Under this model, there is no position where "pre-sales finishes and leaves"—validation and delivery are completed continuously by the same person or the same small team.

This also explains why domestic customers often complain that "the demo is one thing, the delivery is another": the traditional two-stage pre-sales/delivery division leaves a gap precisely at the capability validation stage where AI projects are most uncertain. The FDE model is essentially an organizational response to this gap. For how to measure the delivery phase, see FDE delivery metrics on this site.

Dimension three: the skill stack—demo engineering and production engineering are two different crafts

Pre-sales technical capability is built around "credible demonstration": quickly building a stunning demo, translating industry jargon into language the customer understands, and withstanding expert questioning at bid defense sessions. Code is the means; persuasion is the goal, so pre-sales code is usually scripts and glue, with a quality standard of "the demo doesn't crash."

The FDE's technical capabilities are built around "reliable operation": system integration, data pipelines, permissions and security, evals systems, observability, and failure rollback. When facing large models, a Sales Engineer wants "this scenario can produce good results," while an FDE wants "this scenario can continuously produce good results within the customer's intranet, the customer's data governance maturity, and the customer's operations system." The overlap between the two is only foundational technical understanding; beyond that, they diverge very quickly.

It's worth emphasizing that an FDE is not simply "a Sales Engineer who can write code." Production-grade delivery requires judgment on engineering details—for example, how to set eval baselines, how to attribute bad cases, and how to trim context. These skills require dedicated practice; you can refer to the on-site Eval Baseline Setup Manual. Conversely, the Sales Engineer's ability to "explain technology to non-technical decision-makers" is also needed by FDEs, and is often the scarce skill. So real career migration is not a one-way street; rather, each side fills in the other's core competencies.

Dimension Four: Depth of Customer Relationships and Length of Engagement

The Sales Engineer's relationship with customers is "project-based": intensive contact during the bidding period—meeting three times a week is normal; after the contract is signed, the customer mainly deals with the delivery team, and the Sales Engineer only returns when expanding the deal. This relationship model requires Sales Engineers to build trust quickly and read organizational politics quickly, and it also means their understanding of the customer's business often stays at the depth of "enough to design a solution."

The FDE's relationship with customers is "symbiotic." Being on-site means you use the customer's internal systems, attend the customer's morning stand-ups, and get to know the customer's frontline operators. The granularity of problems you learn about is completely different—the Sales Engineer knows "the customer's data quality isn't great," while the FDE knows "one of the customer's core tables has 3% dirty data and gets locked every Friday during batch updates." The latter kind of knowledge cannot be obtained through interviews; it can only grow from being on-site, and that is precisely the source of the FDE's irreplaceability.

In the Chinese context, this dimension has an additional special variable: private deployment and intranet delivery. When model weights, inference services, and compute all reside in the enterprise's own environment and data never leaves the intranet (per xhby.net's definition of private deployment), the on-site engineer's access to the customer's core systems goes far deeper than in the overseas SaaS model, and customers become especially sensitive to "whose person this is." This is also why, in domestic discussions of FDEs, "the difference from outsourced on-site staff" is always a hot topic—the closed loop of responsibility is the dividing line: outsourced teams leave as soon as delivery is done, while FDEs must follow up and take responsibility the moment the system has problems. For a detailed breakdown of this controversy, see The Chinese Version of the FDE: The Real Shape of the Local Role.

Dimension Five: Deliverables and the Closed Loop of Responsibility

The Sales Engineer's deliverables are documents and demos: solution proposals, pricing configurations, demo environments, POC reports, and bid documents. The common feature of these deliverables is that they "serve decision-making"—the customer uses them to decide whether to buy or not.

The FDE's deliverables are systems and evidence: applications running in production, quantified eval reports, post-launch monitoring dashboards, and knowledge documents handed over to the customer's team. The common feature of these deliverables is that they "serve operation"—the customer lives with them day to day. The closed loop of responsibility also differs accordingly: a Sales Engineer's mistake manifests as "promising something that can't be done," which usually only surfaces at the delivery stage with ambiguous accountability; an FDE's mistake manifests as "the system didn't meet the standard," exposed on the spot and fixed on the spot, with no room for blame-shifting.

There is an interface issue both sides should remember: when a Sales Engineer hands over to an FDE (or delivery team), the most likely troublemaker is "implicit promises"—parts temporarily hardcoded in the demo for effect, out-of-scope requirements verbally agreed during bid responses. These never appear in the contract, yet they become your bill on the first day on-site. Whichever role you're in, putting every pre-signing commitment in writing is the lowest-cost risk control.

The Boundary Is Blurring: Sales Engineers Extending Backward, FDEs Penetrating Forward

Two roles that are clearly distinct in theory are penetrating each other in practice. On one hand, the Sales Engineer role is being forced to extend backward in AI projects: customers increasingly demand POCs built with real data and real integrations, the credibility of "demo environments" keeps depreciating, and Sales Engineers are effectively starting to take on small-scale delivery work. On the other hand, FDEs are also penetrating forward: Anthropic allocates 30% of FDE working time to customer discovery, and Google Cloud's FDEs are paired with system integrators to serve customers (per fdeinterviews.com), all participating in solution design before the contract is signed.

Even more telling are the moves by consulting firms: BCG X, Deloitte, McKinsey QuantumBlack, and others have all established FDE or similar roles (as compiled by fdeinterviews.com), showing that "delivering by writing code" is entering the territory traditionally dominated by "delivering by writing proposals." The trend can be summarized in one sentence: pre-signing and post-signing engineering activities are merging into one continuous chain, and those standing on it must either extend toward both ends or be squeezed into a narrow strip.

How to Choose: Migration Advice for Three Types of People

If you are a Sales Engineer: your greatest assets are demand insight and fluency in the customer's language; your biggest weakness is usually production-grade engineering. The priority move for migrating to FDE is not learning new frameworks, but turning your POC into "a deliverable first version"—filling in the evals, error handling, and data integration that demos always skip. For a concrete path, see FDE Transition Path.

If you are a backend or full-stack developer: what you lack is not coding ability but exposure to customer scenarios. You can start by accompanying Sales Engineers on a few customer visits or taking over the customer-facing module of a delivery project, training your ability to translate vague business needs into technical solutions.

If you are on the client side in IT or an implementation team: you understand customer workflows and data governance, which is a scarce asset in the FDE context, because the most frequent cause of AI project failure is precisely data permissions and process friction rather than model capability. Your migration focus should be on the engineering methodology of large-model applications; the on-site FDE Knowledge Guide is a suitable starting point.

Summary

The divide between Sales Engineers and FDEs can be compressed into a single question: the moment the contract is signed, do you breathe a sigh of relief, or feel that the real hard battle has just begun? The former is the Sales Engineer's professional instinct; the latter is the FDE's professional destiny. Neither role is superior to the other, but when it comes to AI implementation, they must connect more tightly than ever before.
