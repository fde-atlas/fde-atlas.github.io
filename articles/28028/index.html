What is an FDE (Forward Deployed Engineer)?

Original title: 什么是 FDE（前沿部署工程师）？

wangjunjian.com · 2026-10-29 · 11 min read

Summary

Based on interviews with three practitioners from QuantumBlack, McKinsey's AI unit, this article explains what forward deployed engineers (FDEs) do: they embed themselves in client systems, co-define problems, rebuild workflows, and leave behind working products rather than just advice or pre-specified software. The author argues FDE and consultant roles are converging, and that in the AI era LLMs alone are insufficient—they must be tailored to specific business problems to produce profit impact, not just rising token spend. The piece also covers how AI changes the craft (less coding, more specification writing, agentic AI products), why organizational adoption is hard, and what traits make a good FDE.

Key points

  • FDEs work inside client systems and deliver runnable products that stay there.
  • FDE and consultant roles are merging into one, per Nick Robson.
  • LLMs alone don't create value; tailoring to business problems does.
  • AI shifts FDE work toward specifications and agentic AI systems.
  • Hardest part is changing how people work; adoption requires real usage.

What is a Forward Deployed Engineer?

What is a forward-deployed engineer?

Holger Hürtgen Distinguished Partner · Düsseldorf

First of all, he's an engineer, so essentially you're doing data science, data engineering, software engineering. You work with data, and you work with technology. The "forward deployed" part is what's interesting to me personally—it means you work directly on the client's systems. You're not working in a lab. You're facing real problems.

Martin Harrysson Senior Partner · Bay Area

He's the person in an enterprise whose main task is to make technology actually work, solve problems, and achieve the outcomes we want. So, unlike a standard engineer who might be working on a product and just wants to get the technology running, a Forward Deployed Engineer's primary goal isn't to make the solution "work"; it's to reach the right people for the job and achieve some outcome in a different way. I think the term "forward deployed" probably comes from its military meaning, but more fundamentally—it's that they are embedded inside the company.

Nick Robson Associate Partner · Atlanta

For me, a Forward Deployed Engineer isn't just someone who can fit software onto a business problem, or squeeze it into a company's existing business processes. Going a step further, he's someone who can work hand in hand with the client to figure out how to transform the business process along with the software, technology, and tools, creating an entirely new way of working. It's a unique combination of skills that requires a great deal of creativity, as well as a fairly "generalist" toolbox.

How is an FDE different from a consultant?

How is an FDE different from a consultant?

Martin Harrysson Senior Partner · Bay Area

In the traditional sense of a consultant, the incentives probably revolve more around a recommendation, or coming up with a plan of action. Something like: "Here's our recommendation, here's the direction we should take." A Forward Deployed Engineer, on the other hand, is more about implementing a technology—it changes the way you do things and has a more direct impact on a specific workflow.

Holger Hürtgen Distinguished Partner · Düsseldorf

One difference between an FDE and a consultant is that, as an engineer, you have to work within the client's systems and develop a product, so you leave that product behind there. And the difference between an FDE and a software developer is that the product a developer builds is predefined; a Forward Deployed Engineer, like a consultant, first needs to understand the problem and discuss with the client what the solution should look like.

Nick Robson Associate Partner · Atlanta

Today the two roles are converging significantly, and the Forward Deployed Engineer and the consultant are becoming the same role. You can't just go in, deliver some insights, and leave. You have to work with the client, dissect the business problem, figure out who to collaborate with, drive the technology into place, and restructure processes. I don't see it as two roles. I think a really good consultant makes a really good FDE.

"I don't think about it as two roles. I think a really good consultant makes a really good forward-deployed engineer." — Nick Robson, Associate Partner at McKinsey

Why are we hearing more about FDEs right now?

Why are we hearing more about FDEs right now?

Holger Hürtgen Distinguished Partner · Düsseldorf

I've been doing this job for 20 years, and I'll admit, the term FDE itself may be a bit of a buzzword. Yet the concept is more relevant than ever, because new LLMs on their own aren't enough. The key is tailoring them to the problem at hand and making sure you get business impact from them. Otherwise, you're implementing AI for AI's sake. You'll see token consumption rising steadily, but no impact on the bottom line. That's why "FDEs actually solving the problem" matters so much.

Nick Robson Associate Partner · Atlanta

I think people are talking about it more than ever, and demand for QuantumBlack (McKinsey's AI unit) is higher than ever. It's the longest waitlist for this kind of talent I've ever seen, partly because clients are genuinely seeing its value. We're able to truly "meet clients where they are." The gains from adoption and the client experience are astronomical.

Three years ago, everyone was installing enterprise AI platforms. But then they realized they weren't getting value from them, and people weren't using them. So today, my clients realize: if we can restructure an entire business domain end to end, completely reimagine a business process, that's what drives real adoption.

Martin Harrysson Senior Partner · Bay Area

Getting technology to do something useful and productive for the company and for our clients—that core question isn't new. What's new is that AI has amplified many of these problems. We have more technological tools to solve more problems, but they don't come packaged and ready to use out of the box. Clients will launch a new AI tool and expect the impact to happen on its own, and it doesn't.

The leap from individual impact, to team impact, to organization-wide impact doesn't happen automatically, because you need collaboration. Just because I can do one part of a problem faster doesn't mean we've figured out how to run meetings more efficiently, or communicate faster.

The amplified gap

The leap Harrysson says "doesn't happen automatically": one person's efficiency gains don't spread on their own into gains in organizational output.

The missing link: There's no escalator from the first level to the third. That's exactly where the FDE stands—pushing technology, processes, and people across this gap together.

How has AI changed the job of an FDE?

How has AI changed the job of an FDE?

Holger Hürtgen Distinguished Partner · Düsseldorf

AI is changing the job in two ways. One is how you develop software—writing code with agentic AI and agent engineering. I write less code than I did 20 years ago; AI does it for you. You need to think more about specifications, that is, what you want the AI to do.

The other is that the end product itself is becoming AI. So what you're building is agentic AI systems, not just the machine learning systems of a few years ago.

Nick Robson Associate Partner · Atlanta

In the past, we would staff a team made up of many computer engineers or data scientists; but with today's technology—AI, analytics, software engineering—these are genuinely being democratized. Today's QuantumBlack business analysts are almost acting as managers of AI. What was a manager two years ago now operates like a director, leading a group of people who each lead their own teams.

Martin Harrysson Senior Partner · Bay Area

AI has, to some extent, changed what "deploying technology" looks like. In the past, it meant deploying more prepackaged software at the client, maybe doing a bit of customization, or configuring it in certain ways. AI is slightly different, because in some ways it is a general-purpose tool. But you have to piece a few things together:

You usually need a model, but you also need a data layer, and some understanding of the organization. You need to build a harness to make sure it behaves the way you expect. And you usually need to change workflows, getting people to do things differently, in order to capture the full value.

"You usually need a model, but you also need a data layer and an understanding of the organization. You need to build a harness to make sure it behaves the way you expect. And you usually need to change workflows." — Martin Harrysson, on how "deploying AI" differs from traditional software deployment

What do FDEs spend their first few weeks on the job doing?

What does an FDE do in the first few weeks of a job?

Martin Harrysson Senior Partner · Bay Area

A typical first week or initial phase of an FDE's work usually involves sitting next to someone who is performing a task. Say we have a team doing supply chain planning: they receive a bunch of data, existing systems are running, and they are making decisions—whether to reroute ships or trucks to make sure the right things show up in the warehouse.

What the FDE does is embed themselves in that team and try to understand who is making decisions, what actually makes a difference, and which data matters. Truly immersing yourself to understand how the thing works, because much of it may never have been well documented.

Holger Hürtgen Distinguished Partner · Düsseldorf

It's hard to describe a typical FDE job, because it really depends on the type of project. What stays constant is: start by defining the problem—what do we want to solve—and then find the data. That means identifying the core systems where the data lives in the client's environment, and then deciding how to extract the data and put it into, say, cloud infrastructure. That's more the data engineering part.

At some point, you also add a software engineering or front-end engineering component, because ultimately you want to build a real tool. Then comes iteration—because you need to keep demoing what you've built to the client, getting feedback, and possibly even starting over from scratch.

Nick Robson Associate Partner · Atlanta

This role is more focused on the business problem. How do we scope it? How do we know what will drive business impact? In the early part of their time, FDEs spend most of their effort defining the problem, dissecting the business itself, spending time with people, figuring out where the value is, and so on.

What problems do FDEs solve?

What problems do FDEs solve?

Nick Robson Associate Partner · Atlanta

One of my clients is an industrial company with complex products and a highly specialized supply chain. Due to the geopolitical situation, demand for their products is multiplying, many times their previous delivery volumes. They needed something tailored to their supply chain: something that provides visibility—whether they have the right parts at the right time; that gives a sequence of actions; that drives accountability; and that can do complex analytics.

On Tuesday we were asked to build this capability, and by Thursday we had it running. This tool, which we built in roughly a day and a half, would previously have taken hundreds of people many years to build.

(Proposed on Tuesday, delivered on Thursday)

(According to the interviewee)

Holger Hürtgen Distinguished Partner · Düsseldorf

An FDE has to be able to exercise judgment. A consultant might identify a problem and define what the best solution should look like. But as an FDE, what you should decide is what the best methodology is. What actually fits? Is generative AI the best tool? Is it agentic AI? Or machine learning? Or just a very simple regression? It doesn't matter how sophisticated the tool is; what matters is whether it has impact.

The focus now is on agentic AI systems. For example, we all know how frustrating dealing with call centers can be. We essentially "agentified" a utility company's call center. Solving that problem was quite interesting: making sure the agent always has the right answers, doesn't hallucinate, and that customers actually like it. That was a real call center you could dial into, with the agent supporting you as you go through the call center journey.

Why is changing how people work so difficult?

Why is changing how people work so difficult?

Martin Harrysson Senior Partner · Bay Area

Ironically, you could say the harder part is often the human side. People aren't always open to change or to new things. That can sometimes be trickier than actually getting the technology to work.

I think an interesting example recently came from a client. We were transforming how they develop software, getting them to use agents more. One of this client's most experienced software developers attended agent training and then went back to work. He started using agents, but they didn't work. Because he is a truly experienced, senior engineer, he set the agent part aside, opened his IDE, and fixed the code himself. But the agent path got derailed.

The next time, he might not even try using AI and agents for that again. Sometimes, if the people working under you are really strong at what they're good at, making this kind of change is actually harder.

Holger Hürtgen Distinguished Partner · Düsseldorf

You have to solve the problem with real data, in real systems, and make sure the people who are supposed to use it like the solution and actually use it. That's the only way to get business impact.

Nick Robson Associate Partner · Atlanta

We had a client who was resistant to agentic engineering or agentic coding. He told us very clearly: "I don't really trust this stuff. I don't like AI." We spent an hour and a half teaching him some AI fundamentals and how to apply them to a specific problem he had to solve within 24 hours.

The next week, he came in and said: "I've used up all my tokens." He also asked us to teach his entire team how to use AI, so they could become their own Forward Deployed Engineers.

What kind of person is good at this job?

What kind of person is good at this job?

Holger Hürtgen Distinguished Partner · Düsseldorf

If you're like me, an "extroverted geek"; if you enjoy teaching others; if you like not just the server room but also talking to people—I think that's the perfect profile for an FDE. It's the perfect embodiment of that combination: coding in the server room, explaining what you've built to people, collecting feedback, and then doing it all again. That's the beauty of this job.

Martin Harrysson Senior Partner · Bay Area

You need to be something of a "multi-project athlete," or like a bilingual or trilingual person. I don't mean that it's necessarily about multiple languages; you just need enough technical depth. To some extent you have to be an engineer, meaning you understand how a technical system works.

But if you only have the technical side and can't dig out how decisions are made and the informal connections that are happening, in order to solve problems for clients, you won't be very good at solving problems. That requires far more experience working with people: communicating well, asking the right questions, and having empathy.

Nick Robson Associate Partner · Atlanta

I think it's a combination of several different things. One is genuine content knowledge and content expertise. The second is strong interpersonal skills—delivering business impact and doing consulting is fundamentally a "people sport." Finally, technical ability, yes, but the more important skill is the ability to learn quickly.

I think the best FDEs are people who can be extremely creative, or who can think outside the box about things that have never existed before. And a big part of the job is inspiring clients.

"The best FDEs are people who can be extremely creative, who can think outside the box about things that have never existed before. And a big part of the job is inspiring clients." — Nick Robson, on the FDE skill set

Terms in this piece

How key terms from the original are translated and what they mean

Below are several terms that appear in the piece, provided for reference while reading; the definitions are based on the original context and are not expanded upon.

  • Forward Deployed Engineer — forward-deployed engineer / FDE
  • The protagonist of this piece. "Forward-deployed" was originally a military term (forward deployment); here it refers to engineers sent to the client site who work directly on the client's systems and data. Chinese translations also include "前置部署工程师" and "前线部署工程师"; this translation consistently uses "前沿部署" to match the original meaning of forward (advancing ahead, at the frontier).​
  • Agentic AI — agentic AI
  • The original uses this term to distinguish from traditional machine learning systems: AI systems that autonomously execute multi-step tasks and invoke tools. In the piece it appears in two places—"how code is written" and "the form of the final delivered product."
  • Agentic engineering / agentic coding — agentic engineering / agentic coding
  • An approach to software engineering in which agents are the executing subjects. Hürtgen's judgment: the share of writing code is declining, while the share of writing specifications is rising.
  • Harness — harness
  • Original quote: "You need to build a harness to make sure it behaves the way you expect." This refers to the layer of engineering structure wrapped around the model that constrains and orchestrates its behavior—directly related to "making AI run reliably inside the business" in FDE delivery.
  • Token spend — token spend
  • The point of contrast in the piece: when you adopt AI without attending to business impact, "you'll see token spend go up, but you won't see any impact at the profit level."
  • QuantumBlack — QuantumBlack
  • McKinsey's AI unit, the organization where the three interviewees in this piece work.
