Salesforce Agentforce FDE Interview Questions and Preparation Guide

Original title: Salesforce Agentforce FDE 面试题与准备方法

FDE Times · 2026-11-09 · 5 min read

Summary

This guide explains how to prepare for the Salesforce Agentforce Forward Deployed Engineer (FDE) interview, combining candidate reports from Blind with Salesforce's own job posting. The role spans both engineering and consulting: candidates must pass a timed coding test while also articulating AI project stories with real eval numbers. The article maps each job-requirement line to likely questions, outlines a design-question order (data and guardrails first, then tool calls, evals, and post-launch operations), and lists common mistakes, such as lacking eval metrics or forgetting guardrails. It also notes team structure (two FDEs plus a Deployment Strategist), roughly 25% travel, and level-specific expectations (3+ years for base, 6-10 for senior).

Key points

  • Blind reports: first round focuses on AI experience; onsite feels consulting-style; HackerRank matters.
  • Job posting requires one of Python, JavaScript/TypeScript, Java, or Apex plus LLM experience and rigorous evals.
  • Project stories should follow: problem, measurement, root cause, fix, before/after numbers.
  • Design questions: start with data and guardrails, then tool calls, evals, and post-launch monitoring.
  • Teams pair two FDEs with a Deployment Strategist; ~25% travel; tailor stories to job level.

Key Takeaways

  • According to candidate feedback on Blind, one round focuses specifically on your AI experience, the onsite leans toward a consulting style, and HackerRank-style coding questions are also worth practicing.
  • The job posting emphasizes agent design, integration with customer systems, and rigorous evals of AI output.
  • Design questions should start with data and guardrails, and end with an operations plan for after the agent goes live.

You've just received an interview invitation for the Salesforce Agentforce Forward Deployed Engineer (FDE) role. You check Blind for insights and see two seemingly contradictory pieces of advice: one person says the onsite interview is more like a consulting role than an engineering one, while another warns that you must do well on the HackerRank.

The two don't conflict; together they accurately describe the job: an FDE must both write code that runs in production and be able to sit down and talk with customers. Prepare for only one side, and you're likely to stumble on the other.

Blind posts are just individual experiences. A more reliable study guide is Salesforce's own job posting, "Forward Deployed Engineer (All Levels)". Read it carefully, and nearly every line corresponds to a category of questions you need to prepare for.

Every Line of the Job Posting Is an Interview Question

The job posting states that the core responsibility is designing an agent's "intelligence": prompts, reasoning, tool calling, and integration with customer systems.

Candidates must be proficient in at least one of Python, JavaScript/TypeScript, Java, or Apex, and have experience with large language models (LLMs). The posting also requires rigorous evals of AI output, with the same methodological rigor as testing code.

Levels are also clearly stated: the base level requires three or more years of experience, while the senior level requires six to ten. When interviewing for a senior role, interviewers want to hear that you made design decisions yourself, not just executed plans handed to you. Choose stories that match the level you're applying for.

Round One: Talk About Your AI Projects Like an Engineer

One candidate on Blind said the first round mainly asks about your AI experience and the difficulties you encountered in AI projects. The question sounds simple, but many people only list a string of technologies they've used. What the interviewer wants to hear is: what problem you ran into, how you measured it, and how you solved it.

Here's an example. You built an agent that answers internal questions and put together an eval set of 200 questions. The agent answered 170 correctly, or 85%. That looks good, but on the 30 questions related to a new policy, the agent only got 18 right, or 60%.

The reason was that data on the new policy hadn't been added to the agent's retrieval sources. After fixing the data pipeline and rerunning the evals: the new-policy group rose to 27 out of 30 correct, or 90%, and the overall score rose to 179 out of 200. These numbers are just examples; use real numbers when telling your own story.

This kind of story showcases exactly the abilities the job posting requires: not trusting the average score blindly, breaking evals down by group, and tracing the cause back to the data instead of rushing to blame the prompt. Prepare two stories using this structure: problem, measurement, cause, fix, before-and-after comparison.

Design Questions: Talk Data First, Then Prompts

When announcing the FDE partner network, Salesforce wrote that pushing AI into production is a disciplined engineering effort.

The announcement also described FDEs as people who, before starting to build, first figure out how an agent connects to enterprise data and what guardrails are needed. That's also the best order for answering design questions.

Suppose the question is: a retailer wants to use an agent to handle refund requests. Many candidates jump straight to writing prompts. The better approach is to ask questions first: where is the order data stored? Which fields can the agent read? Can it write to the system?

When a refund amount exceeds a customer-set threshold, the agent must escalate to human approval. The agent must never modify prices on its own.

Once data and guardrails are clear, move on to tool calling. For example, the agent has a tool to query order status and a tool to create a refund request, and the latter can only be called after the condition checks pass.

Next comes evals: split the question set by request type, and set aside a separate group of cases the agent must refuse.

The last part is what many people forget. According to Salesforce, launch day is the starting point, not the finish line. An FDE's job also includes monitoring and handling incidents in production. Use the final minute to explain which metrics you'd watch after launch, and what you'd do if the rate of escalations to human approval suddenly spiked.

Why Does the Onsite Feel Like a Consulting Interview?

Salesforce organizes Agentforce FDEs into small teams, each with two FDEs plus one Deployment Strategist, to move quickly from prototype to production. The job posting also mentions about 25% travel, working directly with customers. So the "feels like consulting" comment on Blind isn't surprising.

One Salesforce FDE has said the role has a big impact, not just on customers but on the future of Agentforce itself. That quote hints at how to frame your project stories.

When describing a project, add one detail: what you brought back from the front lines to the product team. With that detail, the story shows you understand that the FDE is a two-way bridge between customers and product.

Don't Let the Coding Test Knock You Out

"Do well on the HackerRank" is just one comment on Blind. The post doesn't confirm which round the coding test is in, so treat it as a possibility to guard against, not a syllabus. Even so, if you spend all your time on the consulting side, this is where you're most likely to lose points.

Pick one language from the four listed in the job posting and practice on a fixed schedule. One suggestion: two problems a day, 45 minutes each, without looking up syntax. Prioritize patterns common in integration work: string and array manipulation, counting and grouping with hash maps, sorting, and traversing nested data like JSON.

After each problem, write a few tests for edge cases like empty inputs or missing fields, then spend two minutes explaining your approach out loud. This habit trains exactly what an FDE needs: writing correct code under time pressure and articulating your choices clearly.

Mistakes That Trip Up Even Strong Candidates

A common mistake is presenting a project without eval numbers. When the interviewer asks "how do you know it worked," "the client was happy" isn't enough. Another mistake is designing an AI agent while forgetting guardrails—that is, forgetting who has the authority to stop the agent, and when.

The story also shouldn't stop at launch day, because Salesforce sees that day as the start of the work. The last mistake is on the resume: don't just write "built agents with LangChain."

Be specific, for example: "Took an agent integrating two customer systems to production, built a 200-question eval set, and was on incident call for the 3 months after launch." The numbers in the example are for illustration only—replace them with your own real data.

According to Aced, as of September 2, 2026, Salesforce has roughly 65 positions with the FDE title, spread across six continents. So the story you prepare now can likely be reused again and again. It should withstand scrutiny the way a good AI agent does: with data, with guardrails, and with a plan for after launch day.
