CS50's Introduction to Cybersecurity: A Must-Take for FDEs

Original title: CS50 网络安全入门课程：FDE 安全必修

FDE Times · 2026-11-17 · 4 min read

Summary

This article reviews Harvard's free CS50's Introduction to Cybersecurity course, arguing it is essential preparation for FDEs before touching customer systems. The five-week course, taught by David J. Malan at 2-6 hours per week, covers accounts, data, systems, software, and privacy. The author highlights three takeaways: security is a relative risk-benefit tradeoff; every layer of protection costs convenience and should be explained to customers upfront; and passwords are only safe when properly hashed and salted. Experienced engineers may find parts basic and can start with weeks 0 and 4, while the for-credit CSCI E-159 version adds live small-group sections and a final project.

Key points

  • Free five-week Harvard course by David J. Malan, 2-6 hours weekly.
  • Security is relative: weigh risk against benefit, not maximize controls.
  • Each protection layer costs convenience; document tradeoffs in deployments.
  • Unsalted password hashes let attackers match dictionary entries across users.
  • For-credit CSCI E-159 adds live sections and a final project.

Harvard's free CS50 cybersecurity course teaches the habits an FDE needs most: weigh risk against convenience before customers grant you access.

Key Takeaways

  • Harvard's free course, taught by David J. Malan, runs five weeks at 2 to 6 hours per week and is organized around four layers: accounts, data, systems, and software, plus privacy.
  • Three ideas worth remembering: security is relative; every additional layer of protection makes the system less convenient; passwords are only safe when properly hashed.
  • Experienced engineers may find the content fairly basic; the for-credit version, CSCI E-159, adds live small-group sessions and a final project.

Harvard's introduction to security runs five weeks at 2 to 6 hours per week, at your own pace, and the course page marks it as free. The instructor is David J. Malan, Harvard's Gordon McKay Professor of the Practice of Computer Science and creator of the CS50 course series.

For a Forward Deployed Engineer (FDE), these five weeks are worth the investment, because this job happens where product engineers rarely go: accounts issued by customers, customers' real data, servers behind another organization's firewall. In that environment, a small mistake is no longer an internal bug but a customer incident.

CS50's Introduction to Cybersecurity won't make you a security expert. What it gives you is a shared vocabulary and the most basic reflexes, so you won't become the weakest link in a deployment.

Five Weeks, Four Layers of Protection

CS50 positions this course for people with both technical and non-technical backgrounds. The content spans five weeks: Week 0 covers account security, Week 1 data security, Week 2 systems security, Week 3 software security, and Week 4 privacy.

The course topics are very concrete, including social engineering and phishing, passwords and brute-force and dictionary attacks against them, biometrics, multi-factor authentication (MFA), and password managers.

The course also covers hashing, salting, symmetric-key encryption and public-key encryption, and digital signatures. The problem sets are all based on real incidents. Anyone can take the course for free through CS50's OpenCourseWare, and it is also available on edX.

Security Is a Relative Calculation

The most memorable idea appears right in the course introduction: CS50 asks students to treat cybersecurity as relative, not absolute—a trade-off between risk and benefit. It sounds obvious, but it is exactly the judgment an FDE makes every week.

When a customer asks what controls a data pipeline that reads internal data needs, "the more the better" helps no one.

A better approach is to ask back: how much damage would a leak of this data cause? Who has an incentive to take it? And how much effort would it take to stop them?

Every Layer of Protection Sacrifices Convenience

The second idea follows from the first: the course treats security as a trade-off against convenience. Turn on MFA, and users take an extra step at every login; rotate keys, and someone has to update the configuration.

The practical lesson for FDEs is: explain these trade-offs up front. If you lock down an environment without explaining why, users on the customer side will always find ways around it, and those workarounds are often less secure than no lock at all. Write every control, along with its cost, into the deployment documentation.

Passwords Are Only Safe When Stored Properly

The third idea is the most technical. CS50's notes explain that a system should only store password hashes, never plaintext. Understanding why salts matter just as much is enough to let you audit a system yourself.

Imagine a scenario: you connect to a customer's user table and find that the password column is hashed but not salted. Then every user who chose "123456" will have an identical hash string. An attacker only needs to hash a dictionary of common passwords and compare it against the whole table.

If every user has their own salt, the attacker has to rehash the entire dictionary for each user, and identical hashes no longer reveal a group of people sharing the same password. Before that table enters any data flow, this point should go into the report you give the customer, along with a remediation recommendation.

In What Order Should You Study, and Who Can Skip?

The most persuasive criticism comes from students. On OpenCourser, this course has only 3 reviews, averaging 4.7, and one reviewer noted that parts lack technical depth and experienced engineers may find it too basic. The sample is too small to draw conclusions, but the point is fair.

So, if you're already familiar with backend work, start with Week 0, because accounts are the first thing customers hand over to you. Then jump to Week 4 for privacy, and go back to fill in the weeks where you're weakest. If you're moving into an FDE role from a less technical position, take it in order from start to finish.

If you need someone to keep you accountable, consider the for-credit version: Harvard Extension's CSCI E-159, 4 credits, also taught by David J. Malan, scheduled for the Spring 2027 term.

Its biggest difference from the free version: every lecture is paired with a 60- to 90-minute live small-group session led by a teaching assistant, cameras on throughout; the course also ends with a final project.

If you know you tend to abandon self-paced courses halfway through, that's the reason to choose it; otherwise, the OpenCourseWare version is enough.

When writing your resume, don't just list the certificate. Write a line that proves you applied what you learned—for example, that you audited how credentials were stored while integrating with a customer system, and what recommendations you made. A line like that demonstrates your judgment, which the certificate name or grade alone cannot.

Five weeks won't teach you how to stop a sophisticated attack, but it's enough to keep you from personally opening the door to the systems customers entrust to you.
