FDE Unpacked: 9 Critical Questions on AI Delivery Roles, Org Design & China's Unique Path

BestHub · 2026-10-26 · 13 min read

Summary

This roundtable-style article examines the FDE role through nine questions. Chinese tech firms have built FDE teams, but most work still applies new technology to old problems in finance, customer service, and office scenarios. Key delivery pitfalls include wrong problem selection, missing value quantification, and lack of bidirectional capability to feed field experience back into products. The author argues FDE reflects a deeper redefinition of division of labor: requirement, technical, and schedule judgments must fuse in one person on-site, dissolving the traditional PM/BA/TM iron triangle. Cost reduction depends on platformization (citing Palantir's Foundry, AIP, Apollo) and AI-tool leverage. Team maturity is measured by organizational leverage and evaluation-driven iteration, and China's path diverges from Palantir's model.

Key points

  • Most Chinese FDE work solves old problems; no true new-problem cases yet
  • Delivery pitfalls: wrong problem choice, no value quantification, broken feedback loop
  • Iron triangle fails; three judgments must fuse in one on-site person
  • Cost reduction comes from platformization and AI-amplified individual leverage
  • Team maturity measured by asset output and falling customization ratios

This article analyzes the Forward Deployed Engineer (FDE) role through nine roundtable questions, covering real-world deployment scenarios in Chinese tech, common delivery pitfalls, organizational structure shifts from iron triangles to fused roles, talent models, cost reduction via platformization, AI PM skill evolution, team maturity metrics, AI-native collaboration patterns, and why China's FDE path diverges from Palantir's model.

Fighter's World

Note: All topics/questions were prepared by the host; personal views only, not representing any company or team.

Question 1: Real FDE Deployment Extent and Mainstream Scenarios in Chinese Enterprises

The author examines two layers: observed FDE team building across major companies, and first-hand experience.

Observed: Large firms have formed FDE teams evolved from delivery or product R&D teams. Core problem: making LLMs land in specific domains. Typical scenarios: finance, autonomous driving, enterprise customer service, enterprise office — essentially "using new technology to solve old problems," with titles simply changed to FDE.

First-hand: Alibaba Cloud intelligent customer service system for complex large-customer scenarios, exploring how to ground general LLMs, domain LLMs, and supporting products into specific clients. Digital employee construction: requirements differ per industry/enterprise beyond basic capabilities, involving permission management, risk control; architectural paths include self-built on LLMs, building on Anthropic/ChatGPT, or on coding-agent platforms like Alibaba QoderWake.

Overall judgment: Current cases still solve old problems with stronger tech foundations. No exciting attempts yet at solving previously undefinable/unsolvable new problems. If FDE value is only faster/better old-problem solving, its ceiling is limited; whether FDE becomes a new species depends on when "new problem" cases emerge.

Question 2: Pitfalls in Actual FDE Delivery

Pitfalls form a progressive chain:

1. Wrong problem selection. Two cases: forced by signed contracts with no flexibility; or team lacks rigorous judgment and dives in without validating true pain points — the latter is avoidable, a judgment/process failure.

2. Decision bias + value quantification gap + expectation-capability gap. Customer expectations vs. actual technical boundaries have huge gaps. Gap identification/management determines success. Compounding issue: no clear value assessment standards — after delivery, unclear how much problem was solved, ROI, etc. This exists in traditional AI too but amplified now. Direct consequence: massive acceptance disputes — no shared criteria for "done."

3. Lack of "bidirectional working" capability. Core criterion: can the person solve customer problems with product/tech while abstracting practical experience back to product team? In China, almost no individuals/teams truly have this bidirectional ability — not intelligence, but org design, role division, and capability models don't connect. Result: broken loop — customer problems solved but experience not沉淀, no product feedback, no scale economies, high cost, eventual doubt on viability.

These pits chain: wrong problem amplifies gap; quantification gap + vague gap cause scope creep until acceptance explosion; missing bidirectional ability prevents lessons from becoming experience.

Question 3: Capability Gap-Filling vs. Redefining Enterprise Division of Labor

Gap-filling: Yes. Palantir ex-exec Bob McGrew defines FDE as "on-site filling gap between product capability and customer need." Gap is real; FDE is capability补齐.

But deeper: division-of-labor rules changed. Traditional project "iron triangle": PM (schedule/resources), BA (business understanding → specs), TM (tech implementation). Handoff via BA docs → TM build → periodic reviews for alignment. Hidden premise: requirement-tech deviation can be caught by periodic alignment rhythm.

AI projects break this premise. Requirements surface only via collision with real systems/data. Customer-stated pain points? Model actual capability? Answers emerge only during hands-on, not from BA docs. Deviation between requirement judgment and tech judgment changes faster than weekly reviews. "BA thinks → TM builds → meeting corrects" chain too slow.

New logic: Consolidate requirement judgment, tech judgment, schedule judgment into one person on-site, adjusting in real time, no meeting-dependent alignment. This person does BA, TM, PM work simultaneously — not willingness but necessity because three judgments inseparable; splitting them + process alignment loses to reality speed.

Palantir's Sankar emphasizes "Total Ownership of Implementation." Not "one person does three jobs" but in real-time shifting requirement-tech deviation scenarios, judgment authority must sit whole in one person; once split across roles + process alignment, feedback loop breaks due to latency. Iron triangle dissolution → FDE fusion is just organizational shadow; real change: "can three judgments be pre-split across roles and aligned via process" no longer works.

Combined answer: Gap-filling is surface; division-of-labor redefinition is deeper. McGrew explains why FDE appears; Sankar explains why FDE must take this shape — not FDE forcing rule change, but rules failing first, FDE emerging under new logic.

Question 4: Internal FDE Role Setup and Boundaries

Three dimensions: capability model, org affiliation, boundaries with SA/delivery/PM/R&D.

Capability model: Many FDEs converted from traditional delivery; if work style, capability matrix, product-team relationship don't fundamentally change, conversion fails. Three-layer "engineering perspective": (1) understand customer/business needs — hear real pain points, not just execute vague requests; (2) hands-on problem solving — customize code on models/products, independently deliver, not just draw diagrams/write PRDs; (3) abstraction capability — productization mindset during delivery, distinguish client-specific vs. cross-client common needs, abstract latter. These three layers = bidirectional working capability at individual level.

Org affiliation: Prefer placing FDE in product-tech R&D system, at least engineering line. Logic: FDE core value = "fill gap + feedback product"; under sales/delivery → old delivery renamed; under product R&D → connects "discover problem → abstract → feedback product" chain. References Palantir's Echo/Delta modes: traditional iron triangle (BA/TM/PM) specialized; today roles must fuse, not mechanically split. Echo/Delta need not be strict; can have emphasis, many-to-many. Two archetypes: (a) domain experts in high-professionalism scenarios — may not master tech, acceptable; (b) "full-stack special forces" — strong engineering hands-on, background in engineering/algorithm (e.g., model tuning), fuzzy boundaries, core ask: "one vs ten."

Boundaries: Real clarity is responsibility boundaries — who owns what outcomes. Role capability overlap grows (fusion trend); but outcome ownership must be clear. vs SA: SA owns top-level architecture design (often not landing); FDE owns making solution run on-site — capability overlap in design, different result ownership. vs traditional delivery: delivery owns contract fulfillment/acceptance end; FDE owns "delivery is start" + experience feedback loop. vs PM: PM owns global product definition/roadmap/commercialization; FDE owns first-hand scenario signals + local abstraction — overlap in demand discovery, but global judgment responsibility stays PM. vs R&D: R&D owns product core code/system architecture; FDE owns client-side integration/adaptation — both write code, but one for product core, one for client landing.

Ideal FDE team: strong engineering-perspective special forces: clear capability model (3-layer engineering view), clear org home (product-tech R&D/engineering line), flexible role setup (no mechanical Echo/Delta split, many-to-many), roles fusing but responsibilities clear (overlap with SA/delivery/PM/R&D but distinct outcome ownership). Reality challenge: such talent scarce; how many engineering-perspective people willing to do frontline delivery? Confirms "talent gap not fillable by training programs quickly."

Question 5: Avoiding Project-Mode Slide & Solving Heavy Labor Cost

1. Avoiding project-mode slide: Admit most FDE work today is project-mode. Causes: (a) vague customer needs, scope creep, high customization → hard to沉淀 reusable productized assets; (b) team lacks abstraction/engineering capability → passively slides. Natural breakout: productization/platformization for reuse/scale. Reference Palantir product matrix: Foundry (data integration/ontology modeling platform) — FDE models business rules per client, heavy labor; once validated general, productized into platform standard capability, next same-industry client only needs "configuration" not redevelopment. AIP (AI platform) connects structured ontology capabilities with LLMs, letting FDE orchestrate on deterministic structures instead of hand-coding agents from scratch, drastically cutting labor to put LLM into production. Apollo (private deployment platform) enables software stack continuous updates/auto-scaling in diverse private envs. These three solve "repeated modeling," "agent orchestration," "repeated ops" costs. Real FDE labor cost reduction comes from platform infra — what Chinese companies lack most.

2. Solving heavy labor cost/low marginal returns: Root: high customization, scope creep, high investment, no沉淀 → low marginal returns. Two core views: (a) Increase productization ratio — raise product/platform capability share, reuse via asset沉淀, cut repeat investment. Two metrics: unit FDE standardized asset output rising? later-client customization ratio falling? If improving, flywheel turning; else just premium outsourcing repackaged. (b) Individual tool efficiency — how FDE individuals leverage AI tools for daily efficiency, directly cutting labor-time cost. Difference from early Palantir: past FDE relied on human labor; today FDE should be special forces equipped with advanced AI tools, fewer people doing more. Special note: Palantir's AIP includes AIFDE — AI Agent doing FDE work. Can reverse-supplement human FDE: human FDE not just uses scattered AI tools but invokes AIFDE-type AI FDE capabilities, becoming "super FDE individual" — one human + AI FDE, still individual efficiency but leverage amplified. Key variable for China to compress Palantir's 20-year flywheel to 3-5 years.

3. Key execution capabilities: (a) Clear boundaries, learn to say no — judge what cannot be done today, timely Say No; survival pressure often prevents refusal, but this gate cannot be skipped — entry control. (b) Abstraction capability — without abstraction no沉淀; starting point of productization. During delivery with productization mindset, extract common capabilities from concrete projects, separate client-specific vs. cross-client common needs — process conversion. Both levers needed: entry control + process conversion.

Question 6: AI PM Capabilities to Strengthen When FDE Becomes Mainstream

Framework: "Three-stage convergence" — problem→tech, tech→product, product→commodity. Entire AI landing value chain splits into three segments with different nature:

Segment 1: problem-oriented — deep on-site, understand real problems.

Segments 1+2: product-oriented — turn solutions into reusable product capabilities.

Segment 3: business-oriented — scale, go-to-market, marketing.

FDE and AI PM focus differently across segments.

FDE focuses on first two, especially segment 1. Segment 1 "problem→tech convergence" — identify true business problems on-site, judge which solvable with today's models/how — FDE's core battlefield; knows best where models work/fail. Segment 2 "tech→product convergence" — abstract field-validated tech solutions into product capabilities — FDE contributes importantly, holds first-hand scenario signals, distinguishes client-specific vs. cross-client common needs.

AI PM focuses on last two. Segment 2: PM reviews from global product definition/roadmap — multiple FDEs find inconsistent signals across clients, PM makes unified judgment/trade-offs globally. Segment 3 "product→commodity convergence" — tests not product capability but commercialization — scale, GTM, marketing — PM's most irreplaceable decisions. Segment 1 PM participates (early product definition needs customer/scene understanding) but not main battlefield.

Key: segment 2 "tech→product convergence" is overlap. FDE abstracts bottom-up from field; PM defines top-down globally. Overlap not a problem, necessary — without FDE first-hand signals, PM definitions detach from reality; without PM global judgment, FDE abstractions fragment, lack direction.

If FDE becomes mainstream and excels in first two segments, AI PM focus sharpens to segment 3 "product→commodity convergence." Not that PM exits first two, but core value shifts backward — FDE already pushed demand-product capability conversion far; PM must strengthen business-oriented capabilities: scale formed product, design GTM strategy, execute marketing. This differentiates PM from generic "judgment/abstraction" talk — true PM irreplaceability lies in segment 3 commercial capability.

Question 7: How FDE Team Analyzes Own Capability Maturity

Team maturity ≠ sum of individual capabilities. Strong individuals without org沉淀/reuse/scale = immature team, just strong individuals temporarily together. Two dimensions from org granularity:

Dimension 1: Organizational Leverage Rate. Key maturity judgment, two layers:

Individual level: each FTE truly leverages current AI tools to boost personal productivity — same person with AI tools/agents covers more clients/scenarios continuously expanding. Not simple headcount stacking, but same headcount with amplified AI leverage covers wider range.

Team level: team converts field experience into productized/asset沉淀 capabilities, generates scale effects via product/platform leverage, raising org leverage rate. Two angles: "product conversion" — turn single-client experience into reusable product capabilities; "asset沉淀" — do converted things become org-level standardized assets? Concrete criteria: not headcount, not client count — only two external signals: unit FDE standardized asset output rising? subsequent client customization ratio falling? If improving, productization conversion real, flywheel turning; no improvement, scale however large = premium outsourcing repackaged.

Individual + team together = complete org leverage picture — individuals not using AI to amplify themselves makes team scale talk empty; team not沉淀 product/assets traps individuals in single points, no org-level leverage.

Dimension 2: Capability Maturity. Critical in AI era, manifests in real/valid/effective evaluation across scenarios, two layers:

Layer 1: Overall evaluation capability — evaluation data, methodology, tools/platforms — team accumulated evaluation data, built frameworks/technical means across industry scenarios. Foundation. Without this, team cannot answer "can today's model actually do this task" — direction judgment becomes guesswork.

Layer 2: Iteration improvement plans based on evaluation results — evaluation-surfaced problems, bad cases, baseline comparisons truly digested into next-round solution iteration inputs, not evaluated then shelved, next project starts from zero. This layer closes evaluation loop: evaluation not purpose, purpose is letting team continuously correct/iterate along evaluation results, forming a "North Star" mechanism — with it, team judges direction correctness from project start at lower cost, avoids detours.

Both layers together = complete capability maturity: evaluation without iteration = decoration; iteration without real evaluation = blind tuning.

Two dimensions together = org capability maturity: org leverage rate measures "individual+team truly turned AI+productization into scale effects"; capability maturity measures "mechanism won't drift, can self-correct." Missing either makes maturity judgment incomplete.

Question 8: AI-Native Organization Collaboration Model Transformation

Answer not limited to FDE role; all AI-native orgs will experience this collaboration transformation — FDE just first pushed to front, most observable sample.

AI-native org shape judgment: Future AI-native orgs resemble lean "special forces squads" not hierarchical large armies. Four traits: extremely high talent density, extremely strong judgment (decision insight), extremely short decision chains, extremely low coordination costs. Causal order: high talent density → strong individual judgment → org dares shorten decision chains (no layers of approval for safety) → shortened chains → coordination costs drop. Why org reliance on individual judgment grows: short chains, few approvals, no one to catch/correct deviations — everything points to "does this person judge accurately." Ideal FDE team = AI-native org shape instantiated in "frontline delivery" scenario.

Two concrete forms: "Super-individual" and "Super-organization."

Super-individual: natural person, leverages extreme AI tools/tech, brings agent matrix to work — compresses past team-level output into one person + their agent matrix.

Super-organization: many people + many agents forming complex collaboration. At least four collaboration layers: human-human, human-own-agent, human-other's-agent, own-agent-other's-agent. Latter three are new, AI-era-specific org forms — past org design assumed unit always "human," at most "human+tool"; super-org must simultaneously design human-own-agent, human-other's-agent, agent-agent three new collaboration interfaces — more complex than any past org transformation.

Structure change is surface; core change = two points: org composition, collaboration mode.

1. Org composition changed. Past: only human employees. Today: two unit types — human employees and digital employees (agents). Not just "added efficiency tools" — agents become new member type counted in org composition — they undertake specific tasks, produce specific results, may get decision rights — different from past "human uses tool" relationship.

2. Collaboration mode/relationships reconstructed. Past: only human-human collaboration via meetings/processes/reporting lines. Today: two coexisting — human-human, and new human-agent collaboration — every person brings own agent to work, collaboration object not just another human but "human+agent" composite, even composite collaborates with another's composite. This collaboration relationship didn't exist, needs redesign — not just give everyone a tool, but redefine "who interfaces with whom, who reports to whom, who can invoke whom" interfaces.

Org composition change enables collaboration reconstruction; collaboration reconstruction is inevitable result of composition change. Together they map to super-individual vs super-org: super-individual = "human+agent" new collaboration at individual extreme compression; super-org = many "human+agent" composites rewoven into network containing both human-human and human-agent collaboration relationships.

Question 9: Will Domestic/International FDE Landing Models Converge? Localization Challenges

Clear judgment: won't fully converge, but forms will differ.

Why not converge? Three structural differences in China prevent Palantir-style heavy model:

1. Payment inertia — Chinese enterprises' willingness/habit for pure-service payment differs from Silicon Valley.

2. Career hierarchy stigma — "on-site deployment" ranks bottom in traditional career ladders, directly affects talent flow.

3. Platform maturity gap — Palantir spent 20 years building Foundry/Ontology/AIP platform infra; China has no counterpart.

These three differences combine → China forms several distinct FDE ecosystems, not single heavy-model replication:

Type 1: Big-tech FDE teams. Core positioning: FDE as model capability validation ground — verify own models/products on frontier clients. This part converges with Silicon Valley.

Type 2: Vertical industry boutique firms. Can't do platform route, but go deep in specific industries, trade industry depth for client LTV — moat from single-industry understanding, not scale.

Type 3: AI-armed individual FDEs. AI-era unique — one person with AI tool matrix drives delivery cost near zero, essentially ultra-light model.

Only Type 1 converges with Silicon Valley; Types 2-3 are China-specific paths. Judgment: China may not wait for a Palantir that can sustain FDE teams for 20 years — like skipping credit cards to mobile payments, China likely jumps straight to light models.

Top localization challenges to prioritize:

1. Talent challenge (possibly biggest). Ideal/excellent FDE demands extremely high individual capability — engineering hands-on + business understanding + abstraction. Such composite talent scarce whether internal cultivation or external hire; needs high cost, long time; not solvable by job postings + training programs.

2. Voice & deep participation challenge. Problems/signals FDEs discover on-site — can they truly convert into product voice? Can they deeply participate in product evolution, feature iteration, roadmap formulation, not just deliver existing product then drop suggestions with no follow-through? If not, FDE remains one-way delivery mode, same as "bidirectional working capability" — talent cultivation solves "supply of people who can do it"; voice solves "whether org takes what this person does seriously" — one supply, one mechanism; missing either stalls China FDE localization.
