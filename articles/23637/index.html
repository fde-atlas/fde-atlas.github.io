Legal Liability of AI Agents and FDE Approval Gate Design

Original title: AI 智能体法律责任与 FDE 审批关卡设计

FDE Times · 2026-10-22 · 6 min read

Summary

This article argues that courts refuse to let AI agents absorb legal liability: a British Columbia tribunal held Air Canada responsible for its chatbot's misinformation, and a California federal court allowed Mobley v. Workday to proceed under an agency theory, meaning vendors can also be liable. Yet standard SaaS contracts push agent-error risk onto customers, who struggle to prove breach. The EU's new Product Liability Directive covers AI systems, and missing technical documentation can create a presumption of defect. The author concludes that approval gates, tested escalation thresholds, and contextual logs are the FDE's job, and recommends showcasing governance work on resumes.

  • Courts hold deployers, and sometimes vendors, liable for AI agent errors.
  • Standard contracts leave agent-error risk with customers, who rarely prove breach.
  • Under the EU directive, missing version and update records can imply defect.
  • Approval gates must actually run: real oversight, tested thresholds, contextual logs.
  • FDEs should document governance decisions and explain stop points in interviews.

After the Air Canada chatbot case and the Workday lawsuit, the approval gates and logs built by FDEs are becoming evidence in disputes.

Key takeaways

  • A tribunal in British Columbia and a federal court in California both refused to let AI serve as a shield: deployers, and even vendors, can still be held accountable.
  • Under standard contracts, customers bear the risk when agents go wrong, and customers find it hard to prove that a vendor breached the contract.
  • Bristows advises customers to require vendors to provide approval gates for high-risk operations; the EU's new directive makes version and update records admissible as evidence.

Air Canada once put forward a defense that many companies using AI have probably contemplated: that its chatbot was a separate legal entity. In February 2024, a tribunal in British Columbia rejected this argument and ruled that the airline was responsible for the misinformation the chatbot provided to customers.

The tribunal's reasoning was simple. According to an analysis by McCarthy Tétrault, the tribunal held that it made no difference whether the information came from a static webpage or a chatbot; Air Canada was responsible for all information on its website.

For Forward Deployed Engineers (FDEs), this ruling directly affects their daily work. It is the FDE who connects agents to customers' real systems, sets thresholds, and decides which operations require human approval. Legal and contractual trends are turning these technical decisions into evidence when disputes arise.

Courts won't let AI be a shield

The law firm Torkin Manes distilled a general principle from Moffatt v. Air Canada: as long as misinformation reaches consumers through any AI tool, the business remains liable. It is not the agent that bears responsibility, but the deployer.

The engineering conclusion is quite clear. Since a chatbot is treated like a page on a website, its answers must be controlled like official content: use an evals set to check answers against source policies, and be able to detect when answers drift. This kind of work usually sits right on an FDE's to-do list.

In the US, liability can even extend to vendors. In Mobley v. Workday, a federal court in California denied a motion to dismiss, finding that Workday could be liable under an agency theory, on the grounds that the customer had allegedly delegated its hiring function to it.

Duane Morris summarized the lesson vendors should take away: vendors that use AI to screen candidates may be liable for discrimination claims. In other words, handing a task to software does not make the legal liability attached to that task disappear.

But contracts pull in the opposite direction

After reading these cases, you might assume vendors will bear more risk. But common contract templates do not allocate risk that way.

Mayer Brown describes the logic of traditional SaaS terms: the vendor is responsible for maintaining the platform, and users are responsible for every action taken on the platform.

This usually comes with the familiar disclaimer: the service is provided as is, with no guarantee of being defect-free. The firm believes that as agents are sold as services, this logic is being stretched to its limits.

The law firm Bristows puts it more bluntly: under standard contracts, customers bear the risk when agents misbehave or make errors. Worse, when an agent causes an error, it is hard for the customer to prove breach of contract, and they must also prove causation.

Putting the two sides together reveals a gap. Courts say deployers cannot shift responsibility to AI, while contracts leave deployers alone to bear the agent's behavior. When something goes wrong, the decisive question will be: whether human oversight genuinely existed and actually worked.

Bristows therefore advises customers not to accept sole responsibility for oversight where it is inappropriate or technically infeasible, and to require vendors to provide approval gates for high-risk operations. Such gates don't appear on their own; someone has to configure them in the customer's systems.

In Europe, version records become evidence

The EU's new product liability directive extends the definition of "product" to software and digital services. Baker McKenzie confirms that the scope covers AI applications and AI systems.

Two accompanying mechanisms make logs crucial. Courts can order parties to disclose relevant evidence; according to HLC, if the defendant cannot provide technical documentation, the product is presumed defective. In addition, if a defect stems from a software update within the manufacturer's control, the manufacturer is likewise liable.

Applying these two mechanisms to a concrete agent requires being able to answer: at the moment the damage occurred, which model, which prompt, and which configuration were running, and what was the most recent update? Without an answer, there is no defense, and you may be found at fault simply for lacking records.

Approval gates are an FDE's job

Imagine an agent that handles refund requests for an e-commerce company. The contract places oversight responsibility on the customer. But in actual operation, the customer service team is overwhelmed, so all refunds are automatically approved.

On paper, the risk already belongs to the customer. If the agent issues erroneous refunds in bulk, the system has no proof of any oversight step; by Bristows' logic, the customer would also struggle to push responsibility back to the vendor. Both sides would end up arguing over a control measure that was never built.

Now consider a different configuration. The agent handles small refunds on its own, but stops and waits for human approval when the amount exceeds a threshold set by the customer, with every approval logged along with the agent version running at the time. This is exactly the kind of approval gate for high-risk operations that Bristows advises customers to seek from vendors.

Three pitfalls to avoid

The most common pitfall is oversight that exists only on paper: the contract and design documents say "human review," but in actual operation no one ever opens the approval queue. Verify with data, for example: of last week's operations that exceeded the threshold, what proportion were actually approved by a human.

The second pitfall is thresholds that have never been tested. An escalation threshold written into the configuration is not yet a control measure unless test cases prove the agent will stop at the right point. Add a few over-threshold scenarios to your evals set and rerun them after every update.

The last pitfall is logs that lack context. A log that only says "approved," without recording who approved it, when, and which agent version and configuration it corresponds to, will be of little use when a court orders the disclosure of technical documentation.

On every deployment, ask early during requirements gathering: where does the contract assume oversight happens? Which agent operations could harm end customers, candidates, or buyers?

Put this skill on your resume

Many engineers write on their resumes: "Deployed an AI agent to handle refunds for an e-commerce client." This sentence only states what the agent can do, but omits the hardest part of deployment.

A stronger way to write it: specify which operations required waiting for approval, who agreed on the thresholds, what the logs recorded, and which evals were used to check accuracy against official sources.

When reading FDE job descriptions, watch for terms like governance, human review, approval workflow, and audit trail, and prepare concrete examples from your own projects that match them. When asked in an interview about a particular deployment, explain clearly why you placed the stopping point where you did.

Software engineers might treat liability clauses as a matter for lawyers. An FDE cannot: approval gates written into a contract only protect people when they actually run inside the client's systems.
