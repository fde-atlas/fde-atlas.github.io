This long-form Japanese guide by Shohei Tokumitsu, CEO of DeFactory, walks through the six stages of software development (requirements, design, implementation, testing, release, operations) and compares major methodologies: waterfall, agile (Scrum/Kanban), spiral, and DevOps/CI/CD. Citing Boehm's law, it notes that defects found after release can cost up to 1,000 times more to fix than those caught during requirements. The latter half shifts to career advice, arguing engineers who can cover upstream processes command freelance rates of 800,000-1,500,000+ yen per month, and that hybrid approaches mixing methodologies are common in practice.

Key points

  • Six development stages: requirements, design, implementation, testing, release, and maintenance.
  • Requirements flaws drive most failures; post-release fixes can cost up to 1,000x more.
  • Waterfall suits fixed-requirement government/finance projects; agile suits fast-changing web products.
  • Engineers covering upstream work can earn 800,000-1,500,000+ yen monthly as freelancers.
  • Real teams often use hybrid approaches, e.g., waterfall-style design with Scrum implementation.

Software development has a "correct flow" that determines the success or failure of a project. Proceeding with development without understanding the processes dramatically increases risks such as rework in later stages, quality issues, and cost overruns.

This article systematically explains software development from the basics to the concrete steps of each process, and the comparison and selection of major development methodologies, all from a practical perspective. It provides on-the-job knowledge for those considering a career in or transition to the IT industry, those participating in a development project for the first time, and those thinking about advancing their careers as engineers.

Software is a general term for programs that run on computers and their related data. It is a concept contrasted with hardware (such as PCs and smartphones themselves), which can be physically touched, and it operates by giving "instructions" to the hardware.

Software can be broadly classified into the following three types.

ClassificationOverviewExamples
System software (OS)Controls hardware and provides the foundation for other software to runWindows, macOS, Linux, iOS, Android
Application softwareUsed by users for specific purposes; runs on top of the OSMicrosoft Office, Slack, Chrome, various business systems
MiddlewarePositioned between the OS and applications, handling database integration, communication processing, etc.Apache, MySQL, Redis, Docker

Software development refers to the entire series of processes involved in planning, designing, implementing, testing, and releasing software. It is not merely "writing code," but a comprehensive engineering activity that includes requirements definition, design, quality control, and operations.

"Software development" and "system development" are often confused, but strictly speaking, they have different meanings.

Software development is a concept focused on creating working programs (software) themselves. On the other hand, system development refers to building an "entire information system" that combines hardware, software, networks, people, business processes, and more.

In other words, software development is often positioned as part of system development, and it is easiest to understand it as "the process of creating programs within system development."

Related article: What Are the Processes (Flow) of System Development? Basic Knowledge of Major Development Methods and Procedures | Summary of Technical Terms

In practice, the terms are used differently in the following situations.

  • Software development: Developing programs for smartphone apps, web services, and business tools
  • System development: Core business systems, ERP implementation and construction, and overall maintenance including internal infrastructure

Software development is broadly divided into three areas depending on its purpose and operating environment. Understanding the characteristics of each is important both for choosing development methods and for career choices.

AreaCharacteristicsRepresentative productsMain technologies used
WebAccessed via the internet through browsers and apps; relatively easy to modify and deployEC sites, SaaS, social networksJavaScript/TypeScript, React, Python, Ruby on Rails
Enterprise (business systems)Aimed at improving internal business efficiency and core processing; requires high stability and securitySales management, accounting systems, CRMJava, C#, SAP, Oracle
EmbeddedBuilt into and running on specific hardware; real-time processing and low power consumption are criticalAutomotive control, medical devices, IoT devicesC, C++, RTOS (real-time OS)

Web development tends to have fast release cycles and works well with agile development, while enterprise development often uses waterfall-style approaches where specifications are strictly finalized before development. Embedded development has strict resource constraints and also requires specialized hardware knowledge.

Software development basically consists of the following six processes. Details vary depending on project scale and methodology, but understanding this framework is the first step toward grasping development as a whole.

Requirements definition → Design → Implementation (coding) → Testing → Release → Operations and maintenance

Let's look at the details of each process in order.

Requirements definition is the most important process, serving as the starting point of development. Through interviews with clients and business departments, it clarifies "what is being built, for whom, and why."

Main items to decide in requirements definition

ItemContentExamples
Functional requirementsFunctions the system must realizeUser registration, product search, payment processing
Non-functional requirementsRequirements related to quality, such as performance, reliability, and securityResponse within 3 seconds, 99.9% uptime, SSL/TLS support
ConstraintsTechnical, legal, and budgetary limitationsDesignated programming languages, integration with existing systems, compliance with the Personal Information Protection Act
ScopeThe range of what is to be developed (what will and will not be done)E.g., smartphone support is out of scope for the initial release

How to proceed with requirements definition (typical steps)

  • Current state analysis and issue identification: Understanding existing business workflows and extracting pain points
  • Interviews with stakeholders: Gathering requirements from multiple perspectives, including users, clients, and operations staff
  • Organizing and prioritizing requirements: Using methods such as MoSCoW (Must/Should/Could/Won't)
  • Creating and reviewing the requirements specification: Building consensus among all stakeholders
  • Approval and baseline establishment: Setting the baseline that serves as the standard for change management

Poor quality requirements definition leads to frequent rework in later stages. Many project failures are said to stem from deficiencies in requirements definition, and as much as 40-50% of total development costs can be spent on rework at this stage.

Deliverables: Requirements specification, business flow diagrams, use case diagrams, screen transition diagrams (drafts)

Main roles involved: Project Manager (PM), Business Analyst (BA), upstream engineers

Once requirements definition has determined "what to build," the next step is the design phase, which determines "how to build it." Design is broadly divided into two stages: "basic design (external design)" and "detailed design (internal design)."

Basic design (external design)

In basic design, the external specifications of the system visible to users are defined. This phase often proceeds with reviews involving clients and users, and it is a critical phase that determines the system's usability.

DeliverableDescription
Screen design documents and wireframesLayout, input fields, button placement, etc., for each screen
Feature listA list and overview of all features the system will have
Business flowchartsHow each feature is used within business processes
Data input/output designDefinitions of data input and output for each feature
External interface designAPI integration specifications with external systems

Detailed design (internal design)

In detailed design, the internal structure of the program is defined to a level where engineers can begin coding.

DeliverableDescription
Database design document (ER diagram)Table definitions, column definitions, relationships
Class design documentResponsibilities and relationships of each class in object-oriented design
Sequence diagramsMessage exchanges between objects and processing order
Batch processing design documentLogic and execution schedules for periodic processing
API design documentEndpoints, request/response formats

Main roles involved: System architects, tech leads, senior engineers

During the design phase, "architectural decisions" that are difficult to change later are made. For example, decisions such as whether to use a microservices or monolithic architecture, and which cloud infrastructure to use, are finalized here. Mistakes in these decisions become difficult to correct even in the later stages of development, so the involvement of experienced engineers is essential.

Based on the detailed design documents, the process of actually writing programs is implementation (coding). In large-scale projects, it is common to split into multiple teams, such as frontend, backend, and infrastructure, and proceed in parallel. In the implementation phase, the fundamentals of maintaining quality are adherence to coding standards, conducting code reviews, and using version control systems (Git). In recent years, the spread of AI coding assistance tools such as GitHub Copilot has improved implementation speed, but the ability to accurately understand code quality and intent has become even more important.

In addition, more teams are adopting "Test-Driven Development (TDD)," in which unit tests are written alongside implementation. Having test code in place beforehand offers the benefit of early detection of regressions (functional degradation).

Main roles involved: Frontend engineers, backend engineers, full-stack engineers, mobile engineers

Once implementation is complete, the process moves to the testing phase, which verifies that the software operates according to the design documents. Testing is essential not only to "ensure quality" but also to "prevent post-release problems before they occur."

Testing is conducted in stages and is broadly classified into the following four types.

Test TypePurposePerformed ByTest Target
Unit TestingVerify that individual modules and functions work correctly on their ownDevelopers themselves, QA engineersFunctions, classes, components
Integration TestingVerify that multiple modules work together correctly when combinedQA engineers, development teamsInterfaces between modules
System TestingVerify that the entire system operates according to the requirements definitionQA engineersThe entire system
Acceptance Testing (UAT)Verify that the system is acceptable from a business perspective for actual users and clientsClients, end usersThe entire business workflow

Details of Each Test

① Unit Testing

This verifies whether the smallest units of a program work correctly. It is common to write test code using test frameworks such as JUnit (Java), pytest (Python), and Jest (JavaScript) and run it automatically. By incorporating it into a CI/CD pipeline, you can set up a mechanism that automatically runs tests every time code is changed.

② Integration Testing

This verifies whether modules that have passed unit testing work correctly when combined. In particular, bugs tend to occur in interface areas between modules, such as integration with external APIs and interactions with databases, so these are checked with particular focus.

③ System Testing

The entire system is run and verified in a test environment close to the production environment. In addition to functional testing, the following non-functional tests are also performed.

  • Performance Testing: Whether response speed can be maintained even with a large number of simultaneous accesses
  • Security Testing: Whether there are no vulnerabilities such as SQL injection or XSS
  • Load Testing: Whether the system operates stably under high load
  • Regression Testing: Whether fixes have not affected other features

④ Acceptance Testing (UAT: User Acceptance Testing)

This is testing performed by the client or actual end users. They operate the system using the actual business environment and data to make a final check of whether business requirements are met. If problems are found here, the work is sent back to the development team for fixes.

Main Roles Involved: QA engineers, test engineers, development engineers (unit testing), client representatives (UAT)

Once all tests have been passed, it is finally time for release (deployment to the production environment and delivery). Since a release is not "the end of development" but "the starting point of the service," it must be carried out carefully.

Main Pre-Release Checklist Items

  • [ ] Whether the production environment configuration files are set up correctly (API keys, database connection destinations, etc.)
  • [ ] Whether test data or dummy data has been mixed into the production DB
  • [ ] Whether logging settings are set for production (whether debug logs are not being output)
  • [ ] Whether the SSL certificate is valid and communication is encrypted
  • [ ] Whether a backup system is in place
  • [ ] Whether rollback procedures are documented
  • [ ] Whether monitoring and alert settings are complete
  • [ ] Whether post-release verification procedures (smoke tests) are prepared

Types of Release Methods

MethodOverviewAdvantagesDisadvantages
Big Bang ReleaseAll features are released at onceSimple and easy to manageLarge impact scope in case of failure
Blue-Green DeploymentOld and new environments run in parallel and are switchedImmediate rollback is possibleInfrastructure costs temporarily double
Canary ReleaseThe new version is deployed to only a subset of usersMinimizes risk while rolling out graduallyRelease management becomes complex
Feature FlagsCode is already deployed, but feature on/off is controlled by settingsFeatures can be enabled or disabled at any timeCost of managing flags

In recent web service development, it has become mainstream to use CI/CD (Continuous Integration/Continuous Delivery) to release small changes frequently. This makes it possible to deliver value quickly while distributing release risk.

A release is not the goal; it is the start of the long-term phase of operation and maintenance. Software does not end once it is released; continuous improvement, feature additions, and bug fixes are required in response to how users use it.

Main Operation and Maintenance Tasks

TypeDescriptionExamples
Incident ResponseIdentifying and fixing the causes of problems and errors in the production environmentHandling server outages, fixing critical bugs
Preventive MaintenanceRegular maintenance performed before failures occurOS and library version upgrades, applying security patches
Adaptive MaintenanceModifications to respond to changes in the external environmentResponding to OS and browser version upgrades, responding to legal revisions
Perfective MaintenanceFeature improvements and additions reflecting user feedbackUI improvements, adding new features, performance improvements

In operation and maintenance, it is important to use monitoring tools (such as Datadog, New Relic, and CloudWatch) to grasp the system status in real time and establish a system that can detect anomalies early. Also, preparing incident response procedures (runbooks) and escalation flows in advance leads to rapid recovery.

Main Roles Involved: SREs (Site Reliability Engineers), infrastructure engineers, DevOps engineers, maintenance engineers

Software development has multiple "development methodologies (development models)." The choice of methodology greatly changes how the team works, schedule management, and the approach to ensuring quality. The five main methodologies are explained below.

Definition: A development methodology that proceeds sequentially from top to bottom through the phases of requirements definition → design → implementation → testing → release. Since each phase must be fully completed before moving to the next, it is likened to the image of a waterfall flowing from top to bottom.

Characteristics

  • Completion criteria and deliverables for each phase are clear
  • Each phase has an approval process, so change management is strict
  • Schedules and costs are easy to forecast

Advantages

  • Progress is easy to visualize, making management easier
  • Even with large teams, role division is clear and parallel work is easy
  • Specifications and design documents are comprehensive, making it easy to handle personnel changes

Disadvantages

  • Difficult to accommodate specification changes mid-project (high change costs)
  • Since working software is only visible during the testing phase, rework tends to be large-scale
  • The overall development period tends to be long

Suitable projects

  • Projects where requirements are clearly defined from the start
  • Large-scale systems requiring strict specification management, such as government agencies and financial institutions
  • Projects with many stakeholders requiring phased approvals

Definition: A general term for development methodologies that progressively complete software by repeating short development cycles (iterations). It emphasizes adapting to change and assumes continuous improvement.

Representative agile frameworks include "Scrum" and "Kanban."

Scrum

A methodology that repeats development cycles called "sprints" of 1 to 4 weeks. A working deliverable (increment) is released at each sprint, with continuous improvement through reviews and retrospectives.

RoleResponsibilities
Product Owner (PO)Managing product backlog priorities, maximizing business value
Scrum MasterSupporting Scrum practices, removing team impediments, facilitating processes
Development TeamSelf-organizing design, implementation, and testing within sprints

Kanban

A methodology that visualizes tasks as cards on a board and optimizes flow by limiting the amount of work in progress (WIP: Work In Progress). Unlike Scrum, it has no fixed sprint cycles and continuously flows tasks through.

Advantages of Agile Development

  • Can flexibly respond to specification changes
  • Working deliverables are visible early, making it easy to prevent misunderstandings with clients
  • Team autonomy increases, often leading to improved member motivation

Disadvantages of Agile Development

  • Overall progress and costs are hard to forecast
  • Does not function well without experienced Scrum Masters and Product Owners
  • Documentation tends to be sparse, which can make handovers difficult when personnel change
  • Startup product development
  • Web services and smartphone app development where specifications change frequently
  • MVP development where rapid user feedback is desired

Related article: What Is MVP Development? Explaining 3 Benefits and Development Examples

Definition: A methodology that advances development step by step in small cycles (spirals) while repeatedly performing risk analysis. It combines the characteristics of the waterfall and prototype development approaches.

Each spiral consists of four phases: "planning → risk analysis → development/testing → evaluation," and the system's completeness improves with each repetition.

Advantages: Risks can be detected and addressed early. Suitable for large-scale projects with uncertain requirements.

Disadvantages: The process is complex and management costs are high. Requires specialized knowledge of risk analysis.

Suitable projects: Large-scale systems with high requirement uncertainty, R&D projects using new technologies

Definition: DevOps is a culture and practice that integrates Development and Operations, running everything from development to operations and monitoring through continuous, automated processes. CI/CD (Continuous Integration/Continuous Delivery) is a representative technical practice for realizing DevOps.

CI (Continuous Integration): A mechanism that automatically runs builds and tests every time code changes are integrated into the repository, detecting problems early.

CD (Continuous Delivery/Continuous Deployment): A mechanism that automatically deploys code that passes tests to production or staging environments.

Representative tools

CategoryTool examples
CI/CD platformsGitHub Actions, GitLab CI/CD, CircleCI, Jenkins
ContainersDocker, Kubernetes
Infrastructure automation (IaC)Terraform, Ansible, AWS CloudFormation
Monitoring and observabilityDatadog, Prometheus + Grafana, New Relic

By adopting DevOps and CI/CD, it becomes possible to increase release frequency while maintaining quality. It is said that tech companies with high-frequency releases (Google, Amazon, etc.) deploy hundreds of times a day.

Suitable projects: Continuous improvement of web services, cloud-native system development, teams wanting to promote a DevOps culture

In actual practice, it is rare to adopt just one methodology exclusively, and hybrid approaches—such as "solidifying the basic design in a waterfall style while running the implementation phase with Scrum"—have become increasingly common.

The most common cause of software development failure is deficiencies in the requirements definition phase. The mindset of "we can change it later" carries extremely high risk, as correction costs grow exponentially as the project progresses.

Image of increasing rework costs (Boehm's Law)

Discovery phaseEstimated correction cost (with requirements definition as 1)
Requirements definition1x
Design5-10x
Implementation10-20x
Testing20-40x
After release40-1,000x

In other words, some estimates suggest that if a requirements-level mistake is discovered after release, the correction cost can be up to 1,000 times higher. This is the basis for the claim that "spending time on requirements definition ultimately leads to cost reduction."

Common failure patterns in requirements definition

  • Insufficient interviews with stakeholders, leading to later perception gaps such as "I was never told about that specification"
  • Setting scope based on a vague sense that "it seems doable," leading to "scope creep" where requirements balloon as development progresses
  • Non-functional requirements (performance, security) not clearly defined, with problems only discovered during the testing phase
  • Only listening to the client (the ordering party) rather than the actual users, so end-user needs are not reflected

Choosing the wrong development methodology is also a major cause of project failure.

Case 1: Applying waterfall to changing requirements

In developing a startup's web service, waterfall development was chosen despite "requirements not being settled." It took six months to complete the design documents, but during that time market conditions changed and the specifications became obsolete even before development began. In the end, the design had to be substantially redone, consuming most of the budget.

Case 2: Introducing agile development with no one experienced in Scrum

Only the concepts of agile were adopted, but with no Scrum Master, the purpose of each sprint was vague. It devolved into "just vaguely meeting every two weeks," with no backlog prioritization, ultimately falling into a state known as "fake agile."

Case 3: Underestimating the testing phase

To make up for schedule delays, the testing phase was drastically shortened before release. Serious bugs occurred frequently immediately after release, and while scrambling to handle emergencies, the team lost significant trust and the relationship with the client deteriorated.

To prevent failure patterns like those above, talent capable of handling "upstream processes" such as requirements definition, design, and project management is essential, not just technical skill.

The "implementation ability" to write code is an important foundation, but adding the ability to understand the overall flow of a project and make judgments from upstream greatly increases an engineer's market value.

Most of the projects companies order from freelance engineers arise from backgrounds such as "we are already short-handed" or "we have no one in-house with a particular skill." Among these, especially in demand are engineers who can work with an understanding of the entire software development flow.

While a typical development engineer can only handle "coding in the implementation phase," an engineer who can be involved end-to-end from requirements definition through design, testing, and operations offers clients the major benefit of "covering many phases with fewer people."

In particular, the following profiles command high valuations in the freelance market.

  • Those with hands-on experience in agile and Scrum development: Talent who can function as Scrum Masters or Product Owners
  • Those with experience building and operating DevOps and CI/CD environments: Talent who can maintain pipelines using GitHub Actions, Terraform, Docker, and the like
  • Engineers who can be involved from requirements definition and basic design: Talent who can engage from upstream as PMs or tech leads
  • Those with development experience combining AI and machine learning: Demand surging on the back of the recent AI boom

To continuously win high-rate projects after going independent as a freelancer, in addition to technical skills you need "the ability to articulate business value" and "a system for building trust."

In this article, we systematically explained the fundamentals of software development, the six development processes, five major development methodologies, and the keys to project success. Finally, let's summarize the key points.

The Six Processes of Software Development

  • Requirements Definition: Deciding what to build. The most critical process that determines project success or failure
  • Design (basic design and detailed design): Deciding how to build it. Deliverables are the set of design documents
  • Implementation: Coding based on the design documents. Languages and frameworks used depend on the project
  • Testing: Ensuring quality through four stages: unit → integration → system → acceptance
  • Release: Deployment to the production environment. Choosing the release method is also important
  • Operations and Maintenance: Continuously handling incidents, preventive maintenance, and feature improvements

How to Choose a Development Methodology

  • If requirements are already fixed → Waterfall
  • If you prioritize change and speed → Agile (Scrum)
  • For large-scale, high-risk development → Spiral
  • If you prioritize frequent releases and automation → DevOps/CI/CD

Engineers and PMs who systematically understand the software development flow and can be involved end-to-end from upstream to downstream hold extremely high value in today's market. Professionals who can work with these frameworks and development methodologies in practice can more easily secure high-rate projects of 800,000–1,500,000 yen or more per month when going independent as freelancers, and are more likely to build ongoing relationships with multiple companies.

Stepping up from "being able to write code" to "being able to lead projects" is one of the fastest paths to maximizing your market value as an engineer. If you are considering a freelance career, take stock of your skill set and actively seek opportunities to get involved in upstream processes.
