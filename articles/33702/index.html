Will the FDE role disappear? Career prospects for Forward Deployed Engineers

Original title: FDE 岗位会消失吗？前线部署工程师职业前景

FDE Times · 2026-11-20 · 5 min read

Summary

The article addresses whether the FDE role will disappear, arguing that while individual deployments are temporary, the function of feeding field learnings back into product is permanent. Citing Insight Partners, a16z, Rocketlane, Umbrex, and PostHog, it explains that ownership transfers to product teams once solutions work, and that larger companies absorb repetitive custom work. The author advises measuring an FDE career by patterns turned into product features, not customers served, and warns job seekers to distinguish learning-loop roles from rebranded custom development work.

Key points

  • Insight Partners states FDEs are not permanent; ownership moves to product teams after solutions work.
  • What fades is per-customer custom work; the field-to-product learning loop persists.
  • As companies mature, products absorb repetitive FDE work, shrinking but not eliminating the role.
  • Career value should be measured by patterns productized, not number of clients served.
  • Job postings signal whether a role is a learning loop or disguised custom development.

The bigger the company, the more the FDE role shrinks—but the ability to turn frontline experience into product will stay with you forever.

Key Takeaways

  • Insight Partners makes it clear that FDE is not a permanent role: once a solution works, ownership is handed over to the team responsible for productization.
  • What tends to disappear is the custom development repeated for each customer; the learning loop from the frontline back into the product is what remains.
  • For a long-lasting career, the measure of yourself should be how many patterns you turned into product, not how many customers you served.

In June 2025, a16z called the Forward Deployed Engineer (FDE) the hottest role at startups. Thirteen months later, Insight Partners wrote a sentence that poured cold water on the hype: FDE is not a permanent role.

If you're considering moving from a stable software engineering job to FDE, that sentence deserves a close read. At first glance, it sounds like a warning: this role is just a hiring fad. Read carefully, and it's really about how to do the job so that your value grows over time.

The key is to separate two things that are often conflated. An FDE's presence at a given customer is temporary; the function the FDE performs—the learning loop from the frontline back into the product—is not. Where your career goes depends on which side you tie yourself to.

Temporary for whom?

Insight Partners is quite specific: once a solution works, ownership is handed over to the team responsible for productization and scaling operations, while the capabilities the FDE created are absorbed into the core product, accumulating rather than disappearing.

A week later, the same firm advised companies not to treat FDE teams as a temporary stopgap that lasts only until customers can run AI on their own. These two statements aren't contradictory: specific FDEs leave specific deployment projects, while the FDE team itself is a long-term institution.

Frontline practitioners describe this loop even more bluntly. Insight Partners quotes Marcello Pedersen of Databricks: the goal of every deployment is to make yourself redundant there. PostHog says core product teams turn the experience FDEs bring back into general-purpose solutions for other customers.

Rocketlane draws the division of labor clearly: FDEs surface insights and validate patterns, while platform teams build them into the product. Temporary connectors are the easiest example to understand.

Suppose you write a connector for Customer A that reads data from their legacy ERP system, and then Customers B and C ask for exactly the same thing. The most valuable move is to document the pattern and hand it to the platform team. Copying the connector a third time just piles up more technical debt.

Umbrex's playbook states it outright: once there's an approved integration, the temporary connector should be removed. If the connector you wrote gets retired because the product now has a replacement feature, you did the job right.

The bigger the company, the more the role shrinks

The "temporary or permanent" question has a second layer, at the company level. First Round Review argues that the FDE model only makes sense at certain stages of a company's development—which means it doesn't make sense at others.

Early stages are the best fit. a16z argues that for young AI companies, high-touch services help land bigger contracts, and early services deals also test whether customers are willing to pay. Companies accept thin margins in exchange for a hard-to-replicate advantage.

Next, the product gradually catches up. Rocketlane's signal is simple: if many customers ask for the same feature, that work probably belongs in the product. Each time it happens, another chunk of FDE work is handed to the platform team.

In mature organizations, whether each piece of frontline work stays or goes is a deliberate decision. Umbrex lists the options: productize immediately, defer, convert into an approved pattern, document it, decline, keep watching, or stop. The role shrinking is a process, not a sign that the profession is dying.

As the product gradually absorbs repetitive work, what mature companies leave for FDEs are the problems the product doesn't yet cover.

When distinguishing FDEs from consultants and Sales Engineers, Insight Partners also places FDEs in this position: taking on ambiguous, undefined-scope problems, building last-mile solutions, and owning the outcome.

The real risk lies elsewhere

What could actually damage this profession is FDEs being used in the wrong way. Rocketlane warns that repeatedly building the same custom solutions only produces expensive one-offs, while the product gains no scalable improvement.

That's first a risk to the company, but it spills over to you. A role that only produces code written for a single customer adds nothing to the product; when the company needs layoffs, the person in that role will struggle to prove their value.

So two job postings that both say "Forward Deployed Engineer" can describe completely different jobs. One treats the FDE as the company's learning loop; the other treats the FDE as a resident custom development team. The image in this article summarizes several signals to help you tell them apart before accepting an offer.

Agents are getting easier to build—who still needs FDEs?

One view on Latent Space pushes the role even further: if you're an FDE, you should go build the product itself. The same source also argues that agents will get easier and easier to build, but people who can work directly with customers will always have a place.

These two views converge on the word "own" in Insight Partners' definition. In the long run, your work only counts once it's in the product. And as the tools for building agents get cheaper, the most valuable work is sitting next to the customer, turning ambiguous problems into something buildable.

What this means for your career

If your presence in every deployment is temporary, the way you measure your career should change too. How many customers you've served says little. The number that really matters is how many patterns you validated and pushed into the product, evidenced by concrete features or modules.

On a resume, a line like "completed deployments for multiple enterprise customers" carries almost no information. Write it in a three-part structure instead: the ambiguous problem you started with, the last-mile solution you built, and the part that eventually became a general-purpose feature. Hiring managers who see FDE as a learning loop will spot at a glance how well you understand the role.

When reading job postings, watch for phrases like "feed back into the product," "work with the platform team," and "ambiguous problems." If the description is all configuration, customer onboarding, and after-sales support, it's most likely repetitive custom work under a different name. That work isn't necessarily bad, but you should know exactly what you're signing up for.

This role also leads down another path. PostHog observed that many former Palantir employees went on to found their own companies. That's no surprise: years of FDE work train exactly the skills a product builder needs—hearing raw problems, recognizing the patterns in them, and judging which ones are worth turning into a product.

"FDE is not a permanent role" shouldn't scare you off. It's a reminder of how to do the job: with each deployment, make yourself less needed there, and more indispensable on the next hard problem.
