What Does a Palantir FDE Actually Do? The "Ability to Choose the Right Problem" That Matters in the Field

Original title: PalantirのFDEは何をしているのか。現場で問われる「課題を選ぶ力」

株式会社本質 · 2026-09-17 · 3 min read

Summary

This article from 株式会社本質 draws on a November 2020 Palantir blog interview with Brian, an FDSE (Forward Deployed Software Engineer), to explain what FDEs do: working on-site with customers, solving problems on Palantir's existing platform, implementing alongside users, managing production quality, and feeding field insights back to product teams. Brian identifies choosing the highest-value problem from many candidates as the hardest part. The author proposes that SES/SIer firms training FDEs should start with a one-page problem statement covering who is affected, needed information, success metrics, and scope, plus final presentations judged by both technical and business-role reviewers with a revision round.

Key points

  • Based on a 2020 Palantir interview with FDSE Brian.
  • FDEs solve customer problems on-site using the existing platform.
  • Hardest skill: selecting the highest-value problem among many.

Full text

If you want to develop FDEs, in addition to training on AI tool operation, you need practice in deciding "which problem to solve." As material for thinking about that role, this article introduces an interview with Brian, an FDSE (Forward Deployed Software Engineer) published by Palantir on November 2, 2020. As a piece published before generative AI became widespread, it is an article for reading the foundations of FDE work.

Key points of the original text and short quoted translations

Brian describes the FDSE as a software engineer who goes into the customer's workplace and solves problems using Palantir's existing platform. He also touches on implementation alongside users, quality control in production environments, and feeding insights gained in the field back to the product team.

"The FDSE focuses on enabling a single customer organization to make use of multiple capabilities"

This is our translation of a passage from the original text. The difficulty he himself cites is choosing the most valuable problems from among many. Original: Palantir official blog (November 2, 2020)

Our view of the essence: make the first deliverable a "one-page problem statement"

When SES companies and SIers begin developing FDEs, if the entry point of training is "completing a specified application," practice in problem setting gets left out. So we propose an exercise in which, before implementation, the following four points are summarized on a single page.

  • Who is struggling, and in what situation?
  • What is the current work, and what information is needed to make decisions?
  • What will be used to verify whether things have improved?
  • What is the scope to be handled this time, and what will be deferred to later rounds?

For example, with a request like "we want to use AI for handling inquiries," before immediately building a chat screen, you would classify the inquiries. The required mechanism changes depending on whether the time spent searching past answers is long, or whether the wait for answer approval is long. For the former, search and drafting assistance are candidates; for the latter, reviewing approval procedures and permissions.

This is a hypothetical example for illustration. Even when "reduce handling time" is set as the goal, you should check not just the overall average, but also the number of cases in the target work, the working time, and rework separately. The narrower the scope, the easier it becomes to judge the changes before and after prototyping and the problems that remain.

Incorporate explanations to customers into development evaluations

In the final presentation of the training, in addition to something that works, have them explain "why this problem was chosen" and "what was changed based on user feedback." Even a technically excellent implementation leaves questions to ask if the users and usage scenarios are vague.

For evaluators, it is good to have an engineer acting as instructor and someone playing the business owner role. The former checks maintainability and permission design; the latter checks whether it can be used in daily work. Also give trainees time to make one round of revisions based on both parties' feedback. Rather than ending with a single presentation, they accumulate experience in correcting gaps in understanding.

Also, do not demand that a person possess, from the start and on their own, the business knowledge specific to each customer. Evaluate whether they can record unclear points, ask the appropriate person, and reflect the answers in the design. Those responsible for development need to prepare problems that allow such behavior to be observed.

In the first training, take one actual business task as the subject and run through a full cycle: interviews, a one-page problem statement, a small implementation, and verification by users. That becomes a concrete first step toward an FDE who handles everything from discovering customer problems to implementation and adoption.
