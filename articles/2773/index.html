FDE struggling in China? AI coding makes individual developers the new main force

Original title: FDE中国水土不服？AI编程让个体开发者成新主力

虎嗅 · 2026-07-13 · 9 min read

Summary

The article argues the debate over whether FDEs fit China asks the wrong question: what gets transferred is the function, not Palantir's heavy model. The US model depends on clients paying for on-site work, subscriptions, and patient capital—conditions China's enterprise software market lacks, where clients won't pay for understanding the business and lowest-bid rules drive price wars. But AI coding and agent infrastructure have slashed customization costs, letting individual developers carry the FDE function without a platform. China may skip the Palantir stage and jump straight to a lightweight, individual-based model.

Key points

  • Both camps in the FDE debate assume it needs a well-funded software company
  • Enterprise AI fails on language gaps, tacit knowledge, and employee resistance
  • Palantir took 20 years to profit; FDE economics only work on large contracts
  • Chinese clients won't pay for business understanding; lowest-bid rules worsen it
  • AI coding plus agents lets individuals deliver what once needed whole teams

Not long ago, a logistics company in Shenzhen approached Li Fei, a former big-tech programmer, hoping to use AI to optimize its business processes. He went to take a look: in this company of forty to fifty people, twenty or thirty of them did the same thing every day—customers sent PDFs, and staff manually extracted tracking numbers and shipping addresses and entered them into Excel. He built a demo with AI coding tools: work that took a person several minutes per document was parsed by AI in seconds, and the client signed a letter of intent on the spot. Now he spends his mornings visiting companies for research, his afternoons on-site observing, squeezing in questions during the sales reps' water breaks, and his evenings back home sorting out requirements and writing code. "I used to sit in the office waiting for others to bring me requirements; now I run all over Shenzhen."

Li Fei's new role is called FDE (Forward Deployed Engineer) in Silicon Valley—an engineer sent into the client's site to embed AI into business processes, currently one of the most sought-after types of people in the global AI industry.

In the spring of 2026, OpenAI and Anthropic almost simultaneously established dedicated "deployment companies." Anthropic brought in Blackstone and Goldman Sachs to build an AI-native enterprise services company; OpenAI's new company received an initial investment of over $4 billion, and according to Reuters, it promised private equity investors a 17.5% annual return within five years. A LinkedIn report from January this year showed that the number of new FDE positions surged 42-fold from 2023 to 2025, while the much-hyped AI engineer role grew only 13-fold over the same period.

The buzz quickly crossed the Pacific to China, where domestic discussion split into two camps. One camp, fixated on job postings with million-yuan salaries, hails the FDE as "the hottest job in the AI world"; the other asserts it won't take root in Chinese soil—clients won't pay for the exploration process, companies can't afford long-term on-site teams, and an industry where "the vendor is worse off than a dog" can't retain versatile talent.

Many people have started debating whether the FDE can actually work in China. But this may be the wrong question.

The two camps actually share the same assumption: that the FDE must be sustained by a high-gross-margin software company, stationed at the client's site for months at a time, with costs slowly recouped through platform accumulation.

This assumption may not hold. What has been transplanted across the ocean was never anything more than a function—namely, sending engineers to the client's site to embed AI into business processes. Transplanted elsewhere, the function grows into different forms: in the US, it grew into a heavy model sustained by big companies; in China, it may skip that step and directly become individuals who travel light, without relying on a platform.

The most valuable knowledge is hidden in frontline business intuition

The FDE function itself is not controversial. 95% of enterprise AI pilots have produced no measurable financial return, even though enterprises have already invested $30 to $40 billion in them. These failures rarely lose on technology.

What truly stands between AI and the business are three walls.

The first wall is language. When technical teams meet a client, their first sentence is often "What data do you have?" while the business leader sitting across the table really wants to ask "How can you help me earn 5 more points?" The traditional solution is to insert a product manager in the middle as a go-between, but each transmission introduces distortion.

The second wall is more hidden. The most valuable knowledge in an industry mostly never gets written into databases; it lives only in the intuition of seasoned practitioners. Under what circumstances a particular client's goods can be released early, which equipment alarms can be ignored—rules like these have no documentation. They are like dark matter in physics: invisible, yet determining how the entire system operates. No matter how powerful the model, it cannot read what has never been written down.

The third wall is people. According to a joint survey of 2,400 employees and executives by enterprise AI company Writer and research firm Workplace Intelligence, 29% of employees admit they are secretly resisting their company's AI strategy—a figure that rises to 44% among Gen Z; 76% of executives list employee resistance as a serious threat to AI adoption. No matter how good the external engineer's technology, what they face is not just a technical problem but also the friction of people within the organization.

The FDE's entire job is to tear down these three walls.

Yawen, who worked as an FDE in Los Angeles for two years, says the job isn't responsible for any specific module of the product, but requires about 70% understanding of the entire product, and you dive into the client's workflow armed with that understanding. "Every day you face vague requirements, like a tangled mess. The client is already anxious, but you have to calm down and untangle the threads of the business one by one."

Clay Bavor is a co-founder of the American AI company Sierra, whose core business is providing customer-interaction AI for large enterprises. Bavor once described it similarly: the team must get close to how the client's business operates, its people, and its business model, achieving "an understanding that, while not daring to claim it's as deep as the client's, is approaching it"—only then can you build a good product.

Without a forward deployed team, the product can still be sold; but to release the technology's value as quickly as possible and at sufficient scale, the FDE is "an important catalyst."

Palantir spent 20 years making this path work—and proved how expensive it is

The FDE was born at Palantir, an American big-data analytics company co-founded with the participation of PayPal founder Peter Thiel, which has long integrated and analyzed confidential data for governments and large enterprises. When it was founded in 2003, its first clients were the CIA, the FBI, and the military. These clients couldn't articulate what they wanted; their data was confidential, their processes were confidential, even the problems they faced were confidential—the standard playbook of sending requirement documents and holding remote meetings completely failed. Palantir's solution was to send engineers directly to the site for long-term deployment, finding bottlenecks and building prototypes on-site, and the FDE title evolved from this.

This model was looked down upon in Silicon Valley at the time: it required a large number of engineers, expanded extremely slowly, and looked just like outsourcing. Palantir's ledger was a cycle: problems solved by FDEs on-site were brought back to headquarters and accumulated into the platform, so the next client needed less customization; front-load the cost of winning lighthouse clients, then scale through the product later. The price was time. Palantir was founded in 2003 and didn't achieve its first full-year GAAP profit until 2023—twenty full years.

In the AI field, Sierra "rediscovered and borrowed" the forward deployed approach from Palantir. To build the first version of its product, the team embedded engineers inside the client's organization; founding engineer Mahi was at one point effectively treated as an employee of the client Weight Watchers, even receiving emails during the client's performance review season.

After enduring that period, the returns began to materialize. In the US, the median annual salary for an FDE is about $210,000, and senior FDEs at frontier AI labs can earn total packages exceeding $780,000. Former Palantir employees have founded more than 100 companies, raising $11.6 billion; among them, defense tech company Anduril is already valued at $61 billion, earning Palantir the title of "the ultimate founder factory."

But this ledger depends heavily on the commercial soil. Decades of inertia in the American consulting industry mean billing by person-day; clients take it for granted that "sending experts to understand my business" must be paid for—Accenture's annual consulting revenue exceeds $30 billion; a mature subscription model lets vendors invest heavily in the first year, confident the money can be recouped through subsequent renewals; and the capital markets can afford twenty years of patience. Palantir's former CFO Colin Anderson put it bluntly: FDE economics only work on large contracts; small deals of $20,000 to $100,000 don't pencil out.

No one disputes the FDE role itself; the real controversy is who pays to sustain the people doing this work: whether anyone is willing to pay for every day of their on-site deployment matters more than how strong the engineer is.

In 2026, from OpenAI and Anthropic to ByteDance, Ant Digital Technologies, and Zhipu, frontier AI companies collectively revived this heavy model, because AI's adoption predicament is exactly the same as what Palantir faced with the CIA back then. The models are ready-made, but every company's data, permissions, workflows, and unwritten rules are black boxes—the only option is to send people in. The difference is that this time, no one has twenty years to endure. According to financial documents obtained by Fortune, OpenAI's 2025 revenue was about $13.1 billion with operating losses exceeding $20 billion, yet its deployment company has already promised a 17.5% annual return within five years. On one side is the financial rhythm of rushing toward an IPO; on the other is the physical rhythm of living at the client's site for months—the two rhythms are at odds.

China's soil can't sustain the heavy model; what grows first is the light model

By Palantir's math, the FDE model genuinely doesn't work in China.

On Liepin and BOSS Zhipin, ByteDance's "Doubao AI Large Model FDE" position is listed at a monthly salary of 35,000 to 70,000 yuan, with 15 months' pay per year, topping out at 1.05 million. But in the same market, fresh graduates in domestic delivery and implementation roles earn about 100,000 to 150,000 yuan a year, and at the veteran ERP vendor Yonyou, implementation and operations staff make 5,000 to 8,000 yuan a month. For the same kind of work—"getting software into the customer's site"—the pay gap spans an order of magnitude. What drives the gap is less about technology than about where the job sits on the pecking order. R&D, algorithms, product, pre-sales, implementation, on-site support—on-site support sits at the very bottom, known in industry jargon as "a vendor's on-site staff are treated worse than dogs."

The root of this pecking order lies in the payment habits of China's enterprise services market over the past few decades. Customers are willing to pay for tangible hardware like servers and server rooms, willing to pay for ROI figures written into contracts, but unwilling to pay for software, and even more unwilling to pay for the process of "understanding the business" itself. Software is often discounted and bundled into hardware integration deals, while consulting is treated as a free giveaway before signing a contract. The institutional system has nailed this habit in place: standard technical service projects are legally required to use the lowest-bid-wins method, where whoever meets the functional requirements and bids lowest wins. Price wars have reached absurd levels—in 2017, Tencent Cloud won the Xiamen municipal government extranet cloud services project with a bid of 0.01 yuan, for a project with a budget of 4.95 million yuan. Under this system, the three months an FDE spends understanding a customer's business never appear on any functional checklist. The "doesn't fit the local soil" argument gets all of this right.

But Li Fei, the man from the beginning who runs all over Shenzhen, does the math differently. "It used to take one or two years and several million yuan to build an enterprise management system. Now we use AI programming and can deliver a custom system in two months."

Palantir needed twenty years of platform accumulation to amortize customization costs; AI has punched straight through that cost. Since customers won't pay for the process, the lightweight model uses AI to push the cost of the process down to nearly zero—exploration becomes so cheap that no one needs to foot the bill. The role of sustaining the FDE has thus shifted from the company to AI itself.

Behind this is not just AI writing code faster; the deeper reason is the explosion of AI agent infrastructure. Coding agents have taken over the entire pipeline from writing code to testing and deployment; standard protocols like MCP let models plug directly into enterprise internal systems; and off-the-shelf orchestration frameworks chain pre-sales demos, requirements gathering, and prototype delivery into a pipeline that one person can orchestrate. In the past, a company needed three separate teams—product, pre-sales, and implementation. Now these stages are strung together by a group of agents. One person plus a group of agents is a complete delivery line.

On delivery speed, a few numbers serve as reference points. With the help of its Forward Deployed Engineer team, Sierra got retailer NEXT live in about six weeks and healthcare company Cigna live in about 58 days.

The reason deep customization can move this fast is tied to coding agents and the overall pace of progress. Chen Mo of an AI company in Shanghai represents a different model. He defines himself as "a general steward who handles everything except writing code and admin," combining pre-sales, product, and project management in one person. When he hires FDEs, he looks at only two things: learning ability, and the ability to grasp the essence of things. "Employees on the team who studied design or foreign languages have all performed outstandingly," one practitioner said. "Sitting in an office in Beijing, you cannot design a solution for a factory in Dongguan."

China didn't wait for credit cards to become widespread and jumped straight into mobile payments; likewise, China may not wait for a Palantir that can afford an FDE team, and jump directly to the next form: individuals who belong to no platform and arm themselves with AI.

A role whose goal is to make itself obsolete

Every time an FDE solves a problem, the solution gets accumulated, and the next customer needs less customization. The better they do their job, the less demand there is for the role across the industry. Its endpoint is eliminating the need for itself.

Ya Wen values this experience more as a foundation for entrepreneurship. FDEs have seen too many successes and failures, and deeply understand user pain points and implementation details. "In the AI era, the barrier to entrepreneurship keeps getting lower. If you've been an FDE, you know exactly what the market lacks." This follows the same logic as the hundred companies founded by Palantir alumni—except that in China, this path doesn't require first passing through a Palantir.

The rise and fall of the FDE is a footnote to the redivision of labor between humans and large models. Models take away everything that can be written down—code, documentation, solutions—generated cheaper by the day; humans hold onto everything that hasn't been written down—the unwritten rules of the shop floor, organizational antibodies, the intuition that "this order must be handled today."

The dividing line of the future may not fall between "technical roles" and "business roles," but more likely between those who can collaborate with AI and those who cannot. The scarce resource is shifting from people who build models to people who bring models into the shop floor. Arguing about how long the FDE title can survive in China isn't very meaningful; what's worth watching is what form the people carrying out this function will take.

Li Fei has already received his Harvard acceptance letter, but he has decided not to go yet, choosing to seize the one-to-two-year window of opportunity in traditional enterprises' AI transformation. Ya Wen says no one can accurately predict the future of any profession. "All we can do is see clearly the direction it's heading, and jump onto the next wave at the right moment."

The FDE is not the last role to be born from the redivision of labor in the AI era—it's just that this time, it was the first to stand on the dividing line.
