FDE vs Consulting vs Outsourcing: Where Exactly Is the Divide Between These Three Roles?

Original title: FDE vs 咨询 vs 外包：三个角色的分水岭到底在哪

53AI · 2026-07-17 · 6 min read

Summary

Using a bank's AI credit-approval project as a running example, this article distinguishes consulting, outsourcing, and the forward deployed engineer (FDE). Consulting sells cognition (insight, frameworks, roadmaps) but doesn't implement; outsourcing sells capacity (code to a spec) but doesn't define requirements; FDEs sell transformation—co-defining ill-defined problems on-site, building solutions, and feeding lessons back into the product. The author frames these as three knowledge models (transfer, execution, emergence) and argues the real dividing line is the feedback loop: an FDE without product feedback is just outsourcing. A closing framework of three questions helps decide which role to deploy, noting AI tools like Claude Code are blurring the boundaries.

Key points

  • Consulting delivers insight and roadmaps but never implements.
  • Outsourcing executes documented requirements without defining them.
  • FDEs deliver code plus cognition plus product insight, at high cost.
  • Without a product feedback loop, an FDE is just outsourcing.
  • AI collapses the exploration-execution cost, but mindset still decides the role.

Full text

After the Forward Deployed Engineer (FDE) role became popular, the most common confusion is—"isn't this just high-end consulting?" or "isn't this just on-site outsourcing with a new label?" On the surface, these three roles do look alike: all are sent to the customer, all help the customer solve problems, and all get paid by the customer. But the underlying logic of how they solve problems is completely different. This article doesn't rank them; it only breaks down their essence.

First, imagine a scenario.

A bank wants to introduce AI to automate credit approval. They reached out to three companies, and each sent someone.

The first company sent a management consultant. The consultant spent two weeks at the bank, interviewing the heads of the risk, technology, and compliance departments, and produced an 80-page deck. The deck included current-state process diagrams, target-state process diagrams, gap analysis, and an implementation roadmap, recommending that the bank "advance AI credit approval transformation in three phases over six months." Then the consultant left.

The second company sent an outsourced engineer. The bank's tech lead wrote a requirements document: "Connect to our credit database, use an AI model to automatically score applications, and return one of three results: approve/reject/manual review." The engineer wrote code according to the requirements, and three months later the system went live and worked. The bank paid the final installment, and the engineer left.

The third company sent an FDE. The engineer sat next to the risk manager and first spent a week observing how they reviewed applications every day—discovering that many rules were "written on paper and taped to workstations," not in the system. He then spent another week building a rough prototype and ran it on the bank's historical data. He found that the model raised the rejection rate for "a certain type of small business loan" from 30% to 70%, but in the bank's actual manual reviews, 60% of those applications were approved—meaning the model was judging them wrong. The FDE adjusted the feature engineering, re-ran it, and the results improved significantly. Then he wrote an email to the product team at headquarters: "Your product needs to add an industry classification feature for the 'small business credit assessment' scenario."

At the end of the first week, the consultant had delivered the deck, the outsourced engineer was waiting for the requirements document, and the FDE was already tweaking the model.

All three companies helped the bank solve a problem. But they solved problems at different levels.

Let's get to the essence.

Consulting, outsourcing, and FDE all solve the same parent problem: "The customer needs something I don't have ready-made on hand."

But they solve it differently, because they make different assumptions about "why there's nothing ready-made."

The customer has a need, but doesn't know what it looks like. I help them think it through.

The core output of consulting is cognition. The consultant's value lies in "I know, I tell you, and now you know too."

Consulting delivers: insights, frameworks, recommendations, roadmaps.

Good consulting is extremely valuable—many strategic decisions are anchored by consulting. But consulting has a natural boundary: the consultant is not responsible for implementing the solution. Whether it gets implemented, and how well, is the customer's own business.

The customer knows what the need looks like, but lacks the time/ability/resources to do it. I help them build it.

The core output of outsourcing is capacity. The engineer's value lies in "you tell me what to do, and I build it."

Outsourcing delivers: features, code, systems.

Good outsourcing is also extremely valuable—China has the world's best delivery-focused engineering workforce. But outsourcing also has a natural boundary: outsourcing is not responsible for defining requirements. You give me the requirements; I only execute. If you gave the wrong requirements and I executed them wrong, that's not my problem.

The customer can't even articulate the need, because the problem itself lies beyond their awareness. I need to be on-site, define the problem together with them, build the solution, and then feed the learnings back.

The core output of the FDE is transformation—turning an undefinable problem into an executable solution, and then distilling the generic parts of that solution into product capabilities.

The FDE delivers: code + cognition + product insight.

The FDE also has a natural boundary: it's very expensive, because it sends the most expensive engineers to the least standardized scenarios.

Looking one level deeper, these three roles reflect three different "ways of generating knowledge."

Consulting uses a "knowledge transfer" model.

Assumption: knowledge is ready-made and can be packaged and transmitted. Consultants have accumulated a wealth of cross-industry frameworks and experience, and can transfer knowledge to clients like "plugging in a USB drive." The core capability is: abstraction—fitting complex problems into a mature framework.

Outsourcing uses a "knowledge execution" model.

Assumption: knowledge can be fully written into a requirements document, and the engineer only needs to read and execute it. The core capability is: engineering—translating deterministic requirements into stable code.

The FDE uses a "knowledge emergence" model.

Assumption: real knowledge isn't in anyone's head; it emerges from "the interaction between people and problems." You can't figure out the requirements sitting in your office; only by sitting next to the customer, building prototypes, trial and error, and observing do the requirements slowly surface. The core capability is: dual translation—being able to translate the customer's vague business perceptions into technical solutions, and also translate on-site experience into product requirements.

To summarize the worldview of the three roles in one sentence:

Consulting believes "knowledge can be taught." Outsourcing believes "knowledge can be written into documents." The FDE believes "knowledge can only emerge through action."

None is superior—these three assumptions each hold in different scenarios. But if you put an FDE on "outsourcing's work," or put consulting on "the FDE's work," problems arise.

The table below is, in my view, the framework that best distinguishes the three roles, across three dimensions: value deliverable, direction of interaction, and compounding effect.

  • ✓ Consulting's feedback goes to the customer—"I've already told you how to do it; whether you do it is your business"
  • ✓ Outsourcing's feedback goes to the customer—"I've built the thing; whether you use it well is your business"
  • ✓ The FDE's feedback goes to their own product team—"this customer's scenario reveals a common need; we need to change the product"

This last one is the core value of the FDE model, and its essential difference from "on-site development."

An FDE without a feedback loop is just outsourcing. An FDE without a commitment to implementation is just consulting. On-site delivery with a feedback loop and a flywheel effect—that's an FDE.

At this point, I have to say honestly: in the AI era, the boundaries between these three roles are blurring.

The reason: AI is reshaping the traditional relationship between "defining requirements" and "executing requirements."

Traditional outsourcing divides the work like this:

客户(需求定义) → 需求文档 → 外包工程师(执行)

Traditional consulting divides the work like this:

咨询顾问(分析) → 建议报告 → 客户(自己决定怎么执行)

But the arrival of AI has changed this chain.

Now, an FDE can:

  • ✓ Use Claude Code to build a prototype in half a day → in the past, this was the "requirements definition phase"
  • ✓ Use the prototype to talk with the customer, who immediately understands "oh, so that's what you meant" → in the past, this took repeated meetings
  • ✓ Write the common patterns discovered on-site into the product's Agent configuration templates → in the past, this was "product planning"

In other words, AI has drastically reduced the switching cost between "exploration" and "execution." What used to require three roles working in relay can now be handled entirely by one FDE.

But this also means:

If you enter the customer's site with an "outsourcing" mindset, AI won't turn you into an FDE. You'll just be an outsourcer who writes code faster than before. The real dividing line was never "whether you can use AI," but "where you bring your on-site experience back to."

Finally, here's a practical framework. The next time someone tells you "we need to send someone to the customer," you can use these three questions to decide:

Question one: Does the customer know what they want?

  • ✓ If yes, and there's documentation → outsourcing is enough
  • ✓ If it's vague but can be clarified through interviews → Consulting
  • ✓ If it's completely undefined and needs to be explored together → FDE

Question 2: After finishing this client, what do you want to happen with the next one?

  • ✓ Don't care, once it's done it's done → Outsourcing
  • ✓ Care, but write the experience up as a report → Consulting
  • ✓ Care, and can turn the experience into the product so all clients benefit → FDE

Question 3: What is your deliverable?

  • ✓ A PPT → Consulting
  • ✓ Code + handover documentation → Outsourcing
  • ✓ Code + product improvement proposals + the client keeps using it → FDE

Once you've asked these three questions, it's basically clear who to send.

Back to the opening scenario.

That bank ultimately chose two of the three firms: the consulting company to help with strategic planning, and the outsourcing team to help with system development—this is the standard approach for most banks.

But the truly interesting story happened with the company that sent an FDE. After the FDE returned, their product team spent three months turning the "small and micro business lending" scenario into a set of configurable product modules. Later, three other banks in the same industry bought this module.

Consulting earns money from insight. Outsourcing earns money from efficiency. FDE earns money from compounding.

None of them is inherently better or worse. But you need to think clearly: what your organization lacks most right now—is it insight, efficiency, or that feedback loop from the client site to the product team?
