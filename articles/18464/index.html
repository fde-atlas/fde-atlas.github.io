Contract Types in the AI Era | The Option of Quasi-Mandate (Completion-Based) Contracts

Original title: AI時代の契約形態｜準委任（成果完成型）という選択

FDX株式会社 · 2026-09-16 · 29 min read

Summary

This FDX article argues that Japan's binary framing of AI project contracts as either contractor (ukeoi) or quasi-mandate (jun'inin) is outdated. Under the 2020 Civil Code revision, quasi-mandate splits into a time-based type and a results-completion type (Article 648-2), where payment is tied to results but the vendor owes only a duty of due care, not completion; partial, divisible results still earn proportional payment via Article 634. METI's February 2025 checklist treats contract type as merely a default rule and says defining deliverable content and quality matters more, noting vendors can reasonably bear some assurance responsibility for AI systems. Examining Palantir's 10-K, the author infers FDE accountability rests on subscription renewals rather than contract clauses, so implementation firms without products should commit to results contractually. FDX recommends the results-completion type, with guidance on drafting outcomes, four required clauses, and phase-by-phase contract selection.

Key points

  • Quasi-mandate now has two types; the results-completion type ties payment to outcomes without a completion obligation.
  • Article 634 allows proportional payment for divisible partial results, so failure does not mean zero payment.
  • METI's 2025 checklist says contract type is a default rule; defining outcomes and quality is the real issue.
  • Palantir's 10-K suggests FDE accountability comes from subscription renewals, not contract clauses.
  • FDX recommends the results-completion type with concrete drafting guidance and phase-based type selection.

In AI project contracts, there is a question that always comes up first: "Is this a contract for work (ukeoi), or a quasi-mandate (juninin)?"

Both procurement departments and legal teams think in terms of this binary choice. And in many cases, the vendor answers "AI cannot guarantee results, so let's make it a quasi-mandate," the client reluctantly accepts, and the result is a contract where compensation is based on hours consumed.

The framing of this binary choice itself is actually already outdated. The April 2020 amendment to the Civil Code split quasi-mandate into two types, one of which is an intermediate form in which compensation is tied to results, but no obligation of completion is imposed. The checklist published by METI in February 2025 also questions the validity of abstractly debating "contract for work vs. quasi-mandate."

This article organizes the three contract types at the level of statutory text, verifies through primary sources how US FDEs (Forward Deployed Engineers) actually operate under what contract structures, and explains why FDX recommends the result-completion type of quasi-mandate as the basic form.

Positioning of this article: This is general information and not legal advice regarding any specific contract. For actual contract execution and negotiation, please consult a lawyer or other professional.

Who this article is for

  • Procurement/purchasing departments that receive AI implementation quotes and need to judge the appropriateness of the contract form
  • Business unit managers who have been told by a vendor "since it's AI, it will be a quasi-mandate" and are uneasy about where responsibility for results lies
  • Legal staff drafting new AI-related service contracts or reviewing existing templates
  • Vendor executives who want to shift their own offering model from hourly billing to billing tied to results

Confirming the three types through statutory text

The biggest reason discussions fail to align is that the single term "quasi-mandate" refers to two different things. First, let's pin it down via the statutory text.

The statutory wording is as follows.

Contract for work (Article 632): "A contract for work comes into effect when one party undertakes to complete certain work and the other party undertakes to pay compensation for the results of that work."

Result-completion type (Article 648-2, Paragraph 1): "Where the parties agree that compensation shall be paid for results obtained through the performance of the mandated tasks, and such results are to be delivered, the compensation must be paid simultaneously with the delivery of those results."

The difference between these two is often overlooked but decisive. A contract for work is a contract promising to "complete" the work, while the result-completion type is a contract promising to "pay compensation for results." Under the latter, the obligation borne by the trustee remains, after all, only the duty of due care as a prudent manager (Article 644).

Note that what Article 648-2, Paragraph 1 directly stipulates is the payment timing (simultaneous performance) for results requiring delivery. Tying compensation to results is itself a matter of the parties' agreement; the provision governs how things are handled when such an agreement exists.

In other words, the result-completion type has a structure in which only the conditions of compensation are tied to results, and no obligation of completion is imposed. Many explanations state that "the result-completion type carries an obligation to complete and deliver deliverables," but the statutory text cannot be read that way. Getting this wrong leads to unnecessary concessions in negotiations.

However, the table above represents the default values the Civil Code fills in when the parties agree on nothing. If delivery obligations for results or quality standards are written into the contract, those obligations arise from the contract. Just because it is a quasi-mandate does not mean one can do substandard work without being held accountable. A breach of the duty of due care gives rise to liability for non-performance. The METI checklist discussed below also makes this point explicit.

Not "all or nothing" | Application of Article 634 mutatis mutandis

Another factor that comes into play in the result-completion type is the application of Article 634 mutatis mutandis via Article 648-2, Paragraph 2. Article 634 provides as follows.

"Where the client derives benefit from the delivery of a divisible portion of the results of work already performed by the contractor, that portion shall be deemed a completed portion of the work. In such a case, the contractor may claim compensation in proportion to the benefit received by the client."

It applies in two cases: (1) when completion becomes impossible for reasons not attributable to the client (the mandator under a quasi-mandate, i.e., the ordering party), and (2) when the contract is terminated before completion. The provision concerns whether there are grounds for attribution on the ordering side; damages for breach of obligation by the trustee remain a separate issue from this proportional compensation.

This matters greatly in practice. The deliverables-completion type is often misunderstood as a contract where "not a single yen is paid unless results are delivered," but for the portion where the client has received benefit even partway through, compensation proportional to that portion can be claimed. In AI development, where premises can collapse mid-project, whether or not this escape hatch of divisibility exists significantly changes the pricing on the vendor side.

Why contracting (ukeoi) works poorly for AI development

METI's "Contract Guidelines on the Utilization of AI and Data" (June 2018, version 1.1 December 2019), taking into account the technical background that AI models are developed using inductive methods based on training datasets, points out that setting completion obligations and performance guarantees for AI models is not necessarily easy. The "exploratory staged" development approach proposed by the guidelines (divided into four stages—assessment, PoC, development, and additional training—with a contract for each stage) is a response to this uncertainty.

What happens if you choose the contracting (ukeoi) model is determined by its structure.

A risk premium gets baked into the price. As long as the vendor bears a completion obligation, it will factor the possibility of non-achievement into its estimate. The higher the accuracy target, the larger the markup.

The scope skews toward the safe side. Because the vendor can only take on work it is certain to complete, areas that are "unknown until you try, but potentially high-impact" drop out of proposals. The real value in AI adoption usually lies in exactly those dropped areas.

Termination by the client is also hard to exercise. Article 641 provides that "until the contractor completes the work, the client may terminate the contract at any time by compensating for damages," but damages compensation is a condition. The cost of stopping once you realize the premises have collapsed is high.

Quasi-mandate (jun'inin), on the other hand, allows either party to terminate at any time under Article 651 (damages may be required for termination at an unfavorable time). Even looking at just this one point, its fit with exploratory work is different.

However, the performance-proportion type creates a structure that "ignores outcomes"

So should you just switch to the performance-proportion type? That type has its own pathology. Under this type, compensation corresponds not to outcomes but to the performance of the mandated tasks themselves (Article 648(2); proportional compensation for mid-course termination is in paragraph 3). In practice this is written as a special agreement of hours worked × unit rate, so the basis for calculating compensation skews toward input labor hours.

The contractor still bears the duty of professional care (Article 644) and can be held liable for non-performance if the work falls below standard. Even so, "whether results were achieved" is not a condition of compensation. This is the structural weakness.

The problem with this structure was articulated first in US government procurement, ahead of Japan. FAR (Federal Acquisition Regulation) 16.601(c) says the following about Time-and-Materials contracts, which correspond to Japan's performance-proportion type:

"A time-and-materials contract provides no positive profit incentive to the contractor for cost control or labor efficiency. Therefore, appropriate Government surveillance of contractor performance is required..." (T&M contracts give the contractor no positive profit incentive for cost control or labor efficiency; therefore, appropriate government surveillance of performance is required)

The same (c) limits T&M use to cases where "it is impossible at the time of contract to estimate accurately the extent or duration of the work or to anticipate costs with any reasonable degree of confidence." Furthermore, (d) imposes restrictions: it cannot be used unless the contracting officer signs a Determination and Findings that no other suitable contract type exists ((d)(1); if the base period plus option periods exceed three years, higher-level approval is required), and the contract must set a ceiling price, with the excess at the contractor's risk ((d)(2)).

In short, in US government procurement, contracts that pay for time and materials are treated as a type that cannot be used without written justification, and must be used with a ceiling. This is precisely the type that, in Japanese AI projects, gets waved through as "it's AI, so quasi-mandate," with neither a ceiling nor justification.

To be clear, FAR is a US government procurement regulation, and Japan's quasi-mandate under the Civil Code is not bound by it. What is being referenced here is not the legal force of the provisions, but the design insight about what incentives fail to operate in contracts that pay for time.

Another side effect: disguised contracting (giso ukeoi) risk

The performance-proportion type also has a labor-management side effect. Since labor hours are the basis of compensation, the client will want to manage how hours are spent, and if the contractor is on-site, involvement close to giving work instructions tends to arise.

The Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare's "Standards Concerning the Distinction Between Worker Dispatch Businesses and Businesses Conducted Under Contracting" (Public Notice No. 37 of 1986) judges whether it is worker dispatch based on the actual substance, not the contract's name. A contract labeled quasi-mandate does not protect a situation where the reality is direction and command.

Under the deliverables-completion type, where results are a condition of compensation, what the client needs to manage shifts from "labor hours" to "outcomes." The structural advantage is that it is easy to design a contract that runs without daily work instructions.

That said, changing the compensation method alone does not eliminate risk. Public Notice No. 37 looks not only at the presence or absence of direction and command, but also at points such as whether the contractor itself manages working hours and service discipline, and whether it independently procures materials, equipment, planning, and technology and operates as an independent business. Changing the contract type only works when the manner of on-site involvement and the contractor's own labor management are designed together.

What to decide before the "type": METI's 2025 checklist

On February 18, 2025, Japan's Ministry of Economy, Trade and Industry published the "Contract Checklist for AI Use and Development." Taking into account the spread of generative AI, it organizes AI-related contracts by broadly dividing them into usage-type contracts (use of general-purpose AI services) and development-type contracts (customization-type and new development-type).

This checklist goes one step deeper than the conventional debate over keiyaku (contract for work) versus jun'inin (quasi-mandate).

"The classification of contract nature premised on these typical contracts under the Civil Code **merely establishes supplementary rules for cases where there is no agreement between the parties. Therefore, the validity of abstractly debating whether a development-type contract should be classified as a quasi-mandate contract or a contract for work needs to be examined."
"It should be noted that whether the development-type contract in question is a quasi-mandate contract or a contract for work is relative, and **in many cases, the key issue is rather how much the user demands from the vendor in terms of the content and level of the deliverables."

The type is not a conclusion; it is a gap-filling rule for when there is no agreement. What should be decided first is the content and level of the deliverables, not the name of the contract type. In practice, a great many negotiations get this order backwards.

The 2025 step forward: separating the model from the system

There is one more change in the checklist worth noting. The AI section states that the framing "setting completion obligations and performance guarantees is difficult" remains valid when the AI model itself is the direct development target, especially in new development-type contracts, and then continues:

"When developing an AI system that includes an AI model, rather than the AI model itself, it can be assumed that system design and development that sufficiently considers the uncertainty of AI model outputs and the like will be required. In such cases, there may be situations where it is reasonable for the vendor to bear a certain degree of warranty responsibility for the realization of the AI system being developed."

"AI means quasi-mandate" is a 2018 framing. In 2026 projects that procure foundation models externally and build systems on top of them, there are more and more cases where even if model accuracy cannot be guaranteed, system behavior can be warranted. The argument of stepping away from deliverable responsibility on grounds of uncertainty no longer carries the weight it once did.

What do FDE contracts in the US look like?

Palantir, the originator of the FDE model, actually operates under what kind of contracts? This is worth checking against primary sources.

Premise: the US has no statutory categories like contract for work / quasi-mandate

US commercial service contracts are generally structured as a combination of an MSA (Master Services Agreement) plus SOW (Statement of Work). The nature of the contract is not determined by the name of a contract type as in a civil code; rather, the acceptance criteria and payment schedule written in the SOW determine the nature of the contract.

The fact that Japanese negotiations tend to spend time on "the name of the type" is largely due to this difference. The METI checklist's positioning of "how much is demanded in terms of the content and level of deliverables" as the core issue is, in substance, close to the US way of thinking.

The actual structure shown by Palantir's 10-K

The revenue recognition disclosures in Palantir's fiscal 2025 Form 10-K (filed February 17, 2026) show a picture of the FDE contract structure that differs considerably from the conventional wisdom.

Professional services are period-of-service type. The 10-K states:

"Professional services are on-demand, whereby the Company performs services throughout the service period; therefore, the revenue is recognized over the related term." (Professional services are on-demand, with services provided throughout the service period; therefore, revenue is recognized over the relevant period)

What can be confirmed here is accounting revenue recognition, not whether the contract contains an acceptance clause. Nevertheless, the fact that completion and acceptance of deliverables are not the starting point for revenue recognition indicates that this service is not deliverable-based but period-of-service type. The inference that, if mapped to Japanese categories, it is close to the percentage-of-completion type, follows from this.

Moreover, at least in US government procurement, the company explicitly states that it does not take on non-commercial development services. In the risk factors of the same 10-K (the item about the government potentially procuring non-commercial development services rather than commercial items), there is the following sentence:

"We sell commercial items and services and do not contract for non-commercial developmental services." (The company sells commercial items and commercial services and does not take on non-commercial development services)

The context is federal procurement. FASA (10 U.S.C. § 2377, 41 U.S.C. § 3307) makes the procurement of commercial items and commercial services the principle, but the government may choose to procure non-commercial development services. The disclosure states that the company's inability to win such orders is a risk. What this sentence negates is the taking on of non-commercial development services, not customization contracts for private customers in general.

Even so, what the revenue composition shows is clear. What is being sold is the software subscription (license plus O&M as a single performance obligation), and professional services are placed as a separate performance obligation, as a period-of-service type service that supports implementation and adoption. FDE work is structured not so much as a product in itself, but as an investment to get the platform into a usable state.

The structure that can be read: deliverable responsibility is guaranteed not by the contract but by "renewal"

The FDE model that can be read from this can be summarized as follows (this is an inference from the 10-K description, not a confirmation of individual contract terms). The FDE's accountability is secured not within the service contract itself, but by whether the subscription is renewed in the next period. Start small, prove value, and expand the use cases and data domains. If results are not delivered, the subscription is not renewed and revenue disappears. Even without a performance-based compensation clause in the contract, the economic consequences are tied to outcomes.

This is not a model Japanese companies can simply imitate. If an implementation support company without its own product that can be recovered through renewals copies the same structure, all that remains is a time-and-materials contract with no accountability anywhere. If there is no guarantee in the form of product renewals, outcome commitments must be written into the contract itself. This is the starting point of FDX.

Outcome-based pricing is already becoming standardized on the product side

As a reference, some AI agent products are priced per outcome. Intercom's "Fin" publicly prices at $0.99 per outcome, defines billable outcomes as "the customer confirmed resolution," "no additional request followed Fin's answer," and "Fin completed the workflow (including handoff)," and explicitly states that billing is limited to once per conversation.

Pricing based on paying for outcomes has actually proven viable, at least in commercial products. However, this cannot be carried over directly to individual implementation support engagements. Conversation-level resolution is easy to measure, has a large sample size, and a low unit price. Implementation support involving business redesign differs in every respect: measurability of outcomes, allocation of responsibility, and profitability. Even so, it is worth referencing because it shows that "defining outcomes and pricing them" is not impossible in itself.

Why FDX recommends quasi-mandate (outcome-completion type) contracts

In light of the above, this article recommends quasi-mandate (outcome-completion type) as the basic form for AX (AI Transformation) implementation contracts. There are four reasons.

1. Risk burden associated with the obligation of completion can be reduced. Under a contract for work (ukeoi), the estimate includes a markup for the possibility of not being able to complete the work. The outcome-completion type can lighten that burden. However, the risk of forfeiting compensation if outcomes are not achieved remains, so the price changes depending on how settlement is designed for non-achievement or termination. The markup does not go to zero.

2. Consuming hours does not become the goal. Since the condition for compensation is the outcome, "using up the planned hours" does not constitute performance of the obligation. The missing incentive that FAR 16.601(c) points out can be closed off through contract structure. However, if the outcome definition is loose, the same problem returns.

3. Partial outcomes can still be settled. Under Article 634, applied mutatis mutandis via Article 648-2, Paragraph 2, if the ordering party receives benefit from a divisible portion, proportional compensation applies. Being able to avoid "all or nothing" is what makes it possible to take on exploratory work.

4. It is easy to design in a way that reduces disguised subcontracting risk. Since what is managed is outcomes rather than hours, the contract can run without daily work instructions. However, as noted above, this only has meaning when accompanied by labor management and ensured independence on the contractor's side.

However, the outcome-completion type does not function unless its formation conditions are met. If you cannot write down the "outcome," you cannot choose this contract type.

Formation conditions | How to write the outcome

Bad ways to write it, and good ways

書き方 何が起きるか
BadMaking "reduce inquiry handling time by 50%" the condition for full compensationThe quality and volume of inquiries fluctuate due to external factors. Judging achievement or non-achievement becomes a source of dispute
Bad"Develop and deliver an AI chatbot"Since only the deliverable is described, there is no definition of what counts as sufficient. At acceptance, the discussion reverts to the obligation of completion
Good"For 8 of the 10 target processes, AI's first-pass answers pass without human correction" plus a written statement of evaluation data, judge, measurement period, and handling if assumptions changeSince the judgment procedure is also defined, acceptance is possible

The principle is this. Write the condition for base compensation as "the state achieved," and do not make the KPI number itself the condition for base compensation. If the number becomes the condition for full payment, the contractor bears external factors beyond its control, and in the end that portion of the markup comes back into the price.

However, this is not a call to abandon KPI linkage. In a structure where outcome-based compensation is stacked on top of base compensation—that is, "base is achievement of a state, the markup is linked to improvement in business KPIs"—the risk from external factors is limited to the markup portion. This two-tier design is exactly the outcome-compensation combination type covered in the FDE Implementation Cost Guide, and does not contradict this article's recommendation. What should be separated is the "condition for full payment" and the "condition for the markup."

And writing it as "the state achieved" is not automatically safe either. Even the good example above—"no human correction for 8 processes"—becomes as disputable as a number unless it is defined which data is used, who judges, and when. Writing it as a state and writing the judgment procedure are separate tasks.

Four items that must be included in the contract

  • Baseline measurement method and timing: without actual pre-engagement measurements, no one can judge whether things improved
  • Divisibility of outcomes: divide into phases and map outcomes to compensation for each phase. Article 634's proportional compensation requires "divisible services" and "benefit to the ordering party," not an explicit written division, but if it is not written down, both divisibility and the scope of benefit will be disputed later
  • Termination criteria: agree before starting on what state triggers a stop
  • Completion conditions for handover: who receives the documentation, code, and operational procedures, and in what form

Matching contract types to each phase

There is no need to sign the entire process under a single contract. Following METI's guideline's exploratory phased model, contract phase by phase.

PhaseRecommended contract typeReason

For the assessment phase, percentage-of-performance type is a strong option. If you try to force a deliverable definition here, the investigation shrinks to the range that can be defined, i.e., the range already understood. You must not demand a definition of the deliverable at the very stage whose purpose is to define the deliverable.

Failure patterns

Changing only the contract type without writing down the deliverable. You agreed to "deliverable-completion type," but the deliverable field still says "AI implementation support package." In this case, if a dispute arises, judgment will be based on the actual substance, so the name of the contract type will not protect you.

Starting without measuring a baseline. If you argue after starting whether things improved, there is no basis for comparison, so it becomes a pointless argument. Spending two weeks on measurement pays off in the judgeability of the entire contract.

Signing a 12-month deliverable-completion contract without dividing it into phases. Because divisibility and the scope of benefit are ambiguous, if the premises collapse midway, a dispute arises over proportional compensation under Article 634. The vendor will ultimately add a premium to the price to cover this, which dilutes the point of choosing deliverable-completion type.

Making a KPI figure a condition for the full compensation. As noted above, this makes the vendor bear fluctuations from external factors. If market conditions have changed by the time of judgment, there is no landing point both parties can accept. If it is placed as a premium success fee on top, the problem is small.

Carving the operations phase into a separate contract and leaving it unattended. The implementation deliverable ends at "a working state," leaving no one responsible if the system fails to take hold.

FAQ

Q1. Even with quasi-mandate (deliverable-completion type), does compensation become zero if no deliverable is produced?

A. Not necessarily zero. Article 648-2, Paragraph 2 applies Article 634 mutatis mutandis, so if completion became impossible due to causes not attributable to the client (the mandator), or if the contract was terminated before completion, and the client has received benefit from divisible parts of the performance, compensation proportional to that portion can be claimed. The legal requirements are that "the performance is divisible" and "the client receives benefit from that portion"; the explicit documentation of phase division is itself not a requirement. However, if you proceed without dividing, both divisibility and the scope of benefit will be disputed later. Writing out the phases is not a legal requirement but a practical safeguard.

Q2. Is deliverable-completion type effectively the same as a contract for work (ukeoi)?

A. No. As the default under the Civil Code, a contractor bears the obligation to complete the work (Article 632) and liability for non-conformity with the contract (Articles 559 and 562-564), whereas under deliverable-completion type the vendor's obligation is limited to the duty of due care as a prudent manager (Article 644). What is tied to the deliverable is the condition of the compensation, not an obligation of completion.

However, this does not mean "no responsibility if the target is not met." If the duty of due care has not been exercised, liability for non-performance arises, and if the contract specifies an obligation to deliver deliverables or quality standards, those obligations arise from the contract. The difference lies in which way things fall when nothing is written down.

Q3. Is the vendor's explanation that "AI cannot guarantee results, so it should be quasi-mandate" correct?

A. Half right, half outdated. As METI's guidelines (AI edition) point out, performance guarantees for the AI model itself are difficult due to the inductive nature of development methods. However, the February 2025 contract checklist states that when developing an AI system that includes an AI model, rather than the AI model alone, there may be reasonable situations where the vendor bears a certain degree of warranty liability. In projects that procure a foundation model and build a business system around it, an explanation that refuses all deliverable responsibility on the grounds of model uncertainty is hard to accept today.

Q4. From the client's perspective, which is cheaper: deliverable-completion type or percentage-of-performance type?

A. Looking only at unit prices, percentage-of-performance type often appears cheaper. However, because its compensation calculation is based on input hours, the total cost until results are produced cannot be predicted. What should be compared is not the monthly unit price, but the total cost to reach the deliverable and the amount payable if it is never reached. From that perspective, deliverable-completion type divided into phases makes the upper limit easier to read.

Q5. Is the US FDE model a success-fee contract?

A. At least from public materials, it does not appear to be secured by success-fee clauses. According to Palantir's fiscal 2025 Form 10-K, professional services are provided on demand and revenue is recognized over time. In other words, completion and acceptance of deliverables are not the trigger for revenue recognition.

What follows is inference from the 10-K, but the natural reading is that deliverable responsibility is secured not by contract clauses but by whether the subscription is renewed. Whether individual contracts contain acceptance clauses cannot be determined from public materials. In any case, if an implementation support company without its own product merely imitates the time-based provision of services, deliverable responsibility ends up nowhere.

Q6. Does choosing deliverable-completion type really reduce the risk of disguised contracting?

A. It makes dismissal easier, but it is not a safe harbor. Public Notice No. 37 judges by the substance, not the name of the contract. Moreover, it looks not only at the presence or absence of direction and supervision, but also at points such as whether the contractor itself manages working hours and service rules, and whether it independently procures materials, equipment, planning, and technology and operates as an independent business. The structural advantage of the completed-deliverable type is that it is easier to design the contract so it can run without daily work instructions—and nothing more than that. Along with changing the contract type, you need to design how the client engages on-site and the contractor's labor management.

Q7. Can an existing quasi-mandate contract template be reused as-is for the completed-deliverable type?

A. No. In many existing templates, the compensation clause is written as "hours worked × unit price," which is a performance-ratio-type design. To convert to the completed-deliverable type, you need to add a definition of the deliverable, the method and timing of acceptance, the handling of separable portions, and termination criteria. It tends to become a revision that only swaps out the compensation clause without defining the deliverable. This is where the bulk of the actual work lies.

Summary

  • "Contract for work or quasi-mandate" is not a binary choice. Under the amended Civil Code effective April 2020, quasi-mandate is divided into the performance-ratio type and the completed-deliverable type (Article 648-2)
  • The completed-deliverable type is an intermediate form in which only the conditions of compensation are tied to the deliverable, with no obligation of completion. What the contractor bears is limited to the duty of good management
  • It is not "zero if no results are produced." Article 648-2, Paragraph 2 applies Article 634 mutatis mutandis: if the client benefits from a separable portion, compensation is proportional
  • In the performance-ratio type, compensation corresponds not to deliverables but to the performance of the delegated tasks, and the basis of calculation leans on input hours. FAR 16.601 explicitly states regarding similar T&M contracts that they "provide no positive profit incentive to the contractor for cost control or labor efficiency," and treats them as unusable without a Determination and Findings that no other type is suitable and a ceiling price
  • METI's February 2025 checklist treats the classification of nominate contracts as "merely a supplementary rule for when there is no agreement," and holds that how far to demand the content and level of deliverables is the real issue
  • The same checklist separates AI model development from AI system development, and states for the latter that there are situations where it is reasonable for the vendor to bear certain warranty liability. "AI means quasi-mandate" is a 2018 framing
  • In Palantir's 10-K, professional services revenue is recognized over time, and for US government procurement it is explicitly stated that non-commercial development services are not accepted. This is inference, but it can be read that FDE accountability for deliverables is secured not by contract clauses but by subscription renewals
  • Implementation support companies without the guarantee of product renewal have no choice but to put deliverable commitments in the contract. This is why this article recommends the completed-deliverable type
  • The condition for validity is how the deliverable is written. Write the conditions of the base compensation in terms of a state reached, and place KPI figures in additional success fees. Writing in terms of state alone is not enough; acceptance becomes possible only once evaluation data, judges, and measurement periods are also determined. Always include the four items: baseline measurement, separability, termination criteria, and handover conditions

FDX's scope of support

As an implementation partner for AX (AI Transformation), FDX covers everything from decomposing business processes to implementation, on-site adoption, and operational handover.

In the context of this article, what FDX handles is not the legal review of contracts, but the design of business requirements and deliverable metrics that precedes it. That means decomposing the target business, measuring the baseline in practice, and writing out "what state counts as a deliverable" down to an acceptance-testable granularity. Drafting contracts, judging the validity of clauses, and assessing legal risks are the domain of lawyers and other specialists, and FDX does not enter that territory.

This division of roles is also a matter of order. A project that starts running without being able to define its deliverables cannot define how it ends either. Conversely, if the deliverable can be written in the language of the business, the work of translating it into contract clauses can be completed on the legal side.

If you want to work out concretely how to write deliverables for your own project, start from Contact Us; if you first want to organize your company's situation, start from AX Diagnosis.
