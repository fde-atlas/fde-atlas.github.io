Where RAG Development Gets Stuck and Key Design Insights

Original title: RAG 構築で業務が詰まる箇所と設計の勘所

日本Fde協会 · 2026-09-22 · 7 min read

Summary

The Japan FDE Association argues that enterprise RAG projects stall not on retrieval accuracy or chunking, but on permission and data-scope design: who may search which documents. A unified index can leak confidential documents to unauthorized users, and access controls from existing systems are not inherited by the RAG layer. The article recommends deciding scope segmentation by user attributes and search-log governance before implementation, adding a final permission check before answers are generated, and documenting boundary decisions to avoid dependence on one person. It positions FDEs, who understand both business sensitivity and search architecture, as the right owners of this design.

Key points

  • RAG rollouts stall on permissions before accuracy tuning.
  • Unified indexes can surface confidential documents to unauthorized users.
  • Segment search scope by user attributes; control who sees query logs.
  • Add a final permission check before answers are generated.
  • Document boundary decisions; FDEs fit this dual business-technical role.

RAG (Retrieval-Augmented Generation, a mechanism that retrieves relevant documents and uses them to generate answers), which connects internal documents to an LLM (large language model) to answer questions, is one of the most frequently discussed topics in business implementation consultations. However, when actually trying to put RAG development into business operations, things stall much earlier than accuracy or chunk splitting. The sticking point is almost always the design of permissions and data access scope: "who should be allowed to see which data." The Japan FDE Association, a neutral organization of Forward Deployed Engineer practitioners (engineers who embed themselves in customers' operations and solve problems with technology; hereinafter FDE), has been confronting this governance-side bottleneck. By reading this article, you will be able to identify in advance the typical sticking points when putting RAG into business use, and determine which points your company should decide first in its implementation plan.

Why RAG Development Stalls on Permissions Before Accuracy

Most articles explaining RAG devote their pages to accuracy improvements such as selecting embedding models or how to split chunks. These are certainly important, but that is not where implementation actually stalls in the field. The moment you show a working prototype, questions inevitably arise: "Doesn't this answer include accounting materials?" or "Should this person really be able to search this document?"

Not Deciding in Advance Who Can Search Which Documents

RAG vectorizes the target documents together (converting them into a form searchable by semantic similarity) and retrieves fragments close to the question. This "together" is the tricky part. If all company documents are included in a single search target, personnel evaluations, contracts, and other departments' confidential information can be retrieved as the basis for answers even to questions from people without permission. The higher the accuracy, the more precisely it retrieves information that should not be shown—a bitter irony.

Access Control Being Left Outside the Search Layer

Existing business systems have accumulated folder-level and role-based access permissions over many years. When RAG is built as a separate layer, these permissions are not inherited, and the search index alone tends to be left defenseless. This creates a loophole: the original files cannot be viewed, but the contents come back as answers via RAG. Closing this loophole at the design stage is the first key point for preventing rework later.

How to Design Access Scope Segmentation and Log Handling

To avoid stalling on permissions, two things must be decided before implementation begins: "scope segmentation" and "log handling." These are governance-side design decisions, separate from accuracy improvement.

Segmenting Search Targets by User Attributes

In practice, a design is required that switches the set of searchable documents according to attributes of the person asking, such as role, department, or project assignment. Rather than having everyone hit the same index, you either apply filters by attribute before searching, or separate search targets by attribute. Which approach to take is determined by the balance between document sensitivity and operational burden. At a financial institution, for example, the retrieval paths for documents containing customer information and those without were completely separated, drawing a boundary so that projects outside one's responsibility would not appear in answers. If this boundary design is postponed, you will face a rebuild after go-live due to an incident where "something that should not have been visible was visible."

Who Can View Search Logs and to What Extent

Often overlooked are the search logs. RAG can record "who asked what and which documents were retrieved," but the question text itself can be confidential. In fields handling confidential documents similar to those in healthcare, it was necessary to apply access restrictions to question logs as well, and to separate duties between log administrators and business users. While logs are valuable as audit evidence, making them viewable by anyone creates a new leakage path. Whether to keep logs, who can view them, and how long to retain them should be decided early in the implementation plan.

A Mechanism to Detect Leakage of Out-of-Scope Information

Even with perfectly drawn boundaries, unexpected questions may retrieve out-of-scope fragments. You can insert a layer that performs a final check on whether retrieved documents are within the user's permissions before the answer is generated, or establish an operation that records and reviews out-of-scope retrievals when they occur. Whether or not you adopt this mindset of "assuming leakage and detecting it" greatly changes the peace of mind after go-live.

Design Decisions That Avoid Personalization and Internal Owners

The permission, scope, and log designs discussed so far are not finished once decided. They need to be reviewed whenever the organization or projects change. That is precisely why it is important not to keep this design in one person's head alone.

Documenting Design Decisions

When we supported a manufacturing client with RAG implementation, the first thing we worked on was not implementation but creating a list of access scope: "which drawings can be searched by whom." The range of drawings that could be shown differed between the design department and the manufacturing department, and unless the rationale for that demarcation was documented, decisions would waver the moment the person in charge changed. We recommend recording even the reasons for the decisions—why this boundary was chosen. RAG permission design is itself a business rule, so if it becomes personalized, maintenance will stall.

Who Within the Company Should Take On This Role

Permission design, access scope segmentation, and log governance cannot be decided by pure engineers alone, nor by business owners alone. The field needs someone who understands the sensitivity of business operations while also being able to design search mechanisms. This is exactly the domain that FDEs handle. We sometimes hear from the field, "It's efficient—one person I can consult with on both technical and business matters," and RAG permission design moves forward precisely by having one person hold both of these aspects. Whether to place this role in-house or partner with external practitioners is a point you will want to decide early. For the approach to this decision, Criteria for Deciding Between In-House Development and External Use for AI Implementation is a helpful reference. For the design of workflows and data integration that precede RAG, see Design Procedure for Business Workflows and Data Integration, and for an overview of the required skills, see Overview of Skills Required for FDEs | Explained in 8 Areas.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q. Shouldn't RAG development start with improving accuracy? Accuracy improvement is important, but what actually stalls at the stage of putting RAG into business use is the permissions and data access scope of "who should be shown which documents." Since drawing boundaries later tends to require a rebuild, we recommend deciding the access scope and log handling before accuracy tuning.

Q. Can existing system access permissions be inherited by RAG? They are not inherited automatically. Since RAG holds the search index in a separate layer, a loophole tends to arise where the original files cannot be viewed but their contents are returned via RAG. A design that segments search targets by user attributes needs to be built in before implementation.

Q. Who within a company should be responsible for this permission design? The ideal person is someone who understands the granularity of business operations while also being able to design the search mechanism, and this is an area that FDEs handle. Since neither pure engineers nor business owners alone can make these decisions, it is advisable to consider early on whether to hire someone in-house who can handle both sides or to partner with an external party.
