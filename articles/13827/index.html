Forward Deployed Engineer Interview: Rounds, Questions and Worked Answers

Prepare.sh · 2026-11-04 · 7 min read

Summary

This guide maps out the forward deployed engineer interview landscape, separating what companies actually publish from community practice material. Ramp names four hiring criteria and treats technical skill as a pass/fail bar; Palantir, Glean, Mistral and Sierra each run different loops, with Sierra replacing coding rounds with an AI-native onsite. Bloomberry's analysis of 1,000 FDE postings (2025 data) shows Python in 66%, AI agents in 35%, and 47% asking for customer-facing ability. The author walks through worked answers for five round types: an ambiguous churn problem, a slipped-deadline customer conversation, exponential backoff with jitter code, an eval harness for a shipment-rerouting agent, and behavioral questions from a former Palantir hiring manager. It closes with preparation steps and a warning against framing FDE work as consulting.

Key points

  • No standard FDE loop exists; each company designs its own and few publish details.
  • Ramp hires for drive, engineering fundamentals, customer empathy and communication.
  • Bloomberry's 1,000-posting analysis: Python 66%, AI agents 35%, customer-facing 47%.
  • Worked examples cover ambiguous scoping, customer talks, backoff code, evals and behavior.
  • Avoid comparing FDE work to consulting; emphasize what you shipped to production.

A forward deployed engineer (FDE) interview tests whether you can write production code and also turn a vague customer problem into something that ships. There's no standard loop. Each company runs its own, and few publish theirs. So this guide separates what companies have actually said from practice material, and it works through the question types you should prepare for.

What companies say they test

Ramp's engineering team is the most direct. Its post on forward deployed engineering names four things it hires for: "Drive and work ethic", "Engineering fundamentals", "Customer empathy" and "Great communication". On the technical side, it says "we see technical performance as a bar to pass but avoid optimizing it further." It checks coding, debugging, data structures and system design, but doesn't over-index on "perfection".

Others add pieces of the picture:

Palantir"Everyone starts with one or two phone interviews", then an onsite. The format is "personalized to your skillset" (Getting Hired)
Glean"you'll complete a brief AI-focused exercise or discussion" (founding FDE posting)

Anjor Kanekar, who hired FDEs at Palantir, lists his essentials as "Outcome Oriented", "Critical Thinker", "High Chaos Ceiling" and "Gritty". That's one person's view, and he now sells hiring advice, but it fits the postings.

Company-specific detail is in our Palantir interview guide.

The skills to prepare

Bloomberry's analysis of 1,000 FDE postings (last updated January 2026, on 2025 data) gives a rough baseline: Python appears in 66%, TypeScript in 35%, AI agents in 35%, LLM experience in 31% and RAG in 12%. About 47% explicitly ask for customer-facing ability.

Those map to four kinds of round. A community-written guide on GitHub suggests a similar split, but the mix varies by company, so ask your recruiter what's in your loop.

Question 1: an ambiguous business problem

"Why are we losing customers?" This line comes from a Palantir posting, which says "Projects often start with a nebulous question like" this one. It describes the job, not a reported interview question, and it makes good practice.

The trap is jumping to a solution. Palantir's advice applies here: "articulate the alternatives and trade-offs, but don't forget to be pragmatic enough to be able to arrive at some concrete approach. Deliver a functioning idea first, then expand it afterwards."

A strong answer goes roughly like this:

Clarify the problem. Who's asking, and what decision will this change? What counts as "losing": cancelled contracts, lower usage, or smaller renewals? Which segment, and since when?

Find the data. Billing for cancellations, product events for usage, CRM for account owner and segment, support tickets for complaints. Who owns each, and how would you get access? On a real customer site, access is often the first blocker.

Propose a first slice. "In week one, I'd join billing and product events for one segment and chart usage in the 90 days before a cancellation. If usage drops ahead of cancellation, we have an early warning signal."

State how you'd know it worked. "If retention staff can act on the list and renewals in that segment improve, we expand. If not, we've learned that churn isn't usage-driven, and we go to pricing and support data."

Interviewers will likely push: "the data's in a system you can't access", or "the VP thinks it's pricing". Say what you'd do next, and don't defend the first plan to the end.

Question 2: a hard customer conversation

"Your deployment slipped 3 weeks. I am the customer's CTO. Tell me." This is an example prompt from a community guide on GitHub, not a reported question. Mistral's careers page lists "A business case or role play" as a possible stage, so the format does show up in real loops.

The test is whether you're direct, and whether you bring a plan.

"We're going to miss the date by three weeks. The cause is that your identity provider's API rate limits are lower than we scoped for, which we found in load testing on Tuesday. I should have tested that earlier. Here's the plan: this Friday you'll have the pilot working for one team, which covers the use case your board cares about. Full rollout follows on the 21st. I need one thing from you, a contact on your identity team by tomorrow. What does this do to your commitments on your side?"

What makes it work: the bad news comes first, you own your part, there's a specific cause and a smaller thing you can still deliver, you make one clear ask, and you end by asking about their problem. What fails: a long technical story before the date, blaming the customer's team, or a new date with no reason to believe it.

Question 3: practical coding

"Implement exponential backoff with jitter for a flaky third-party API, with a retry cap." Also an example prompt from the same community guide.

FDE work happens against other people's unreliable systems, so this kind of problem is realistic. I ran this answer on Python 3.12 in the prepare.sh Ubuntu workspace:

import random
import time


class TransientError(Exception):
    pass


def call_with_backoff(fn, max_attempts=5, base=0.1, cap=2.0):
    for attempt in range(1, max_attempts + 1):
        try:
            return fn()
        except TransientError as err:
            if attempt == max_attempts:
                raise
            delay = random.uniform(0, min(cap, base * 2 ** (attempt - 1)))
            print(f"attempt {attempt} failed ({err}), sleeping {delay:.3f}s")
            time.sleep(delay)

With a fake API that fails three times, then one that never recovers within three attempts:

attempt 1 failed (HTTP 503), sleeping 0.032s
attempt 2 failed (HTTP 503), sleeping 0.030s
attempt 3 failed (HTTP 503), sleeping 0.260s
result: 200 OK
attempt 1 failed (HTTP 503), sleeping 0.007s
attempt 2 failed (HTTP 503), sleeping 0.107s
gave up: HTTP 503

Points worth saying aloud: retry only transient errors, so a 400 shouldn't retry. Jitter stops many clients retrying in step. Retried calls must be idempotent, so ask whether this endpoint is safe to call twice. A library may already do this, so check before writing your own in production.

Question 4: evaluating an AI system

"Design an evaluation harness for an AI agent that reroutes shipments, targeting 99 percent on-time delivery." Also from the community guide.

Anthropic's definition is a good starting point: "An evaluation ('eval') is a test for an AI system: give an AI an input, then apply grading logic to its output to measure success."

Cover these in order:

  • Separate the metrics. On-time delivery is the business outcome, and it arrives late. You also need something faster: was the agent's reroute decision correct?
  • Build the test set. Replay historical disruptions with known good outcomes, and add hard cases such as port closures and conflicting constraints.
  • Grade each layer. Did it call the right tools? Did it respect constraints, such as cold chain? Was the final route acceptable to a human dispatcher?
  • Launch in stages. Shadow mode first, where it recommends and a person decides. Compare, then let it act on low-risk cases.
  • Set guardrails. A cost cap on each reroute, and escalation to a person on low confidence. Keep a record of each decision for audit.

Question 5: behavioral

Kanekar publishes the questions he asked as a Palantir hiring manager:

  • "How did you get from A→B→C and why? How was A→ B different than B→C , what would have happened if you went straight from A→ C?"
  • "What is the hardest thing you have done/worked on/learned so far? Why was this hard for you?"
  • "What was the hardest failure you have dealt with? Did you see it coming? How did you salvage the situation? Who did you ask for help?"
  • "Tell me about a time when you proactively did something outside your day to day responsibility."

Palantir's own interview advice adds: "We want to hear about an actual failure."

Prepare at least one story where you worked directly with a customer, one where you had to learn an unfamiliar system quickly, and one where you changed plan because of what you learned. For an FDE role, "what did the customer do next" is the part of the story to have ready.

A mistake that comes up in reports

Be careful how you talk about consulting. Two commenters on Hacker News, one in 2017 and one in 2026, describe an interview going badly around the comparison. One writes that referring to consulting experience "rubbed the interviewer the wrong way". These are single accounts of old interviews, but Palantir does call this a misconception: "A common misconception is that a Delta is a consultant." Talk about what you built and ran in production.

How to prepare, in order

  • Read the posting closely. Whether it's a coding-heavy builder job or closer to sales changes what you prepare. Bloomberry's data puts about 30% of titles in the sales engineering style.
  • Ask the recruiter which rounds you'll have, and whether AI tools are allowed.
  • Practice two ambiguous cases out loud, timed, with someone interrupting you.
  • Build something with an LLM, including an eval, so you have a real story to tell.
  • Do practical coding: API clients, retries, parsing messy data. Our company question hub has practice by employer.
