FDE Pricing Models for Insurers: T&M, Fixed, Outcome

FWD Deployment · 2026-10-16 · 8 min read

Summary

This guide explains three pricing models for forward deployed engineering work at insurers: time and materials, fixed-scope, and outcome-based fees. Borrowing the structure of US federal contract types, it maps who bears uncertainty risk under each model. T&M suits discovery pilots with unclear scope but needs ceilings, timesheets and weekly approvals; fixed prices require written scope and acceptance tests; outcome fees need a frozen baseline, attribution rules and quality guardrails on metrics like cycle time, leakage and complaint rate. Citing an SEC filing about a disputed Palantir bundled subscription, the author recommends pricing license and services separately, and staging contracts from pilot to production. No verified market rates are offered.

Key points

  • T&M fits discovery pilots but needs a not-to-exceed ceiling and weekly approvals.
  • Fixed-scope pricing only works when scope and acceptance tests can be written down.
  • Outcome-based fees require a signed baseline, attribution rules and guardrail metrics.
  • Price software licenses and engineering services as separate line items with own acceptance tests.
  • Stage contracts: T&M pilot, fixed-scope build, then outcome-linked production terms.

Key points

  • Time and materials pays labor hours at fixed hourly rates per labor category, and it gives the vendor no profit incentive to control cost.
  • A fixed price does not move with the vendor's cost experience, so it only works once you can write the scope down.
  • Outcome-based fees for claims automation depend on a baseline, an attribution rule and defined measures such as cycle time, leakage and complaint rate.
  • Bundled platform-and-services deals have ended in disputes, so price the license and the engineering services as separate line items with their own acceptance tests.
  • Stage the contract: a T&M discovery pilot, a fixed-scope build, then an outcome-linked production phase.

Structure the contract in stages. Use time and materials (T&M) for a short pilot where scope is unclear, a fixed-scope price for a build you can specify, and outcome-based fees for production only after you have a measured baseline. A T&M contract pays for direct labor hours at specified fixed hourly rates and actual material costs, and it does not reward the vendor for controlling cost.

How do T&M, fixed-scope and outcome-based pricing compare?

The three models differ mainly in who carries the risk of uncertainty. The table uses the structure of federal contract types as an analogy, plus what you would have to define yourself for outcome pricing.

Model What you pay for Where the risk sits Best fit
T&M Labor hours at fixed hourly rates, plus actual material costs With you: no positive profit incentive for cost control Work whose extent, duration or cost cannot be estimated with reasonable confidence
Fixed-scope (firm fixed price) A price not adjusted for the contractor's cost experience With the vendor on cost, with you on getting the scope right A defined deliverable with testable acceptance criteria
Outcome-based Fees tied to measures you define in the contract Shared, if baseline and attribution are agreed Production workloads with stable, measurable results

The government rules are structural analogies, not automatic rules for a commercial insurer. Have counsel read your final terms, since this article is general information and not legal advice.

When is T&M the right choice for an FDE pilot?

T&M is intended for situations in which the extent, duration, or cost of the work cannot be estimated with reasonable confidence. A discovery pilot on a legacy policy admin system or an unfamiliar claims data set often fits that description.

Remember that T&M and labor-hour contracts are not fixed-price contracts. Your spend rises with every hour billed.

Because T&M work requires performance surveillance to provide reasonable assurance that efficient methods and cost controls are being used, put the surveillance in the contract:

  • A not-to-exceed ceiling on total fees for the pilot.
  • Weekly timesheets by named engineer and labor category.
  • A named business owner who approves hours and deliverables each week.
  • A fixed pilot end date with a go or no-go review.

What should the T&M rate card contain?

Ask for rates stated separately for each labor category. Under the federal model, T&M hourly rates include wages, overhead, general and administrative expenses, and profit. A private rate card should show the same discipline, with one rate per role, such as engagement lead, senior engineer and data engineer.

Do not anchor on employee pay. The Bureau of Labor Statistics reports 2025 median software developer pay of $135,980 per year, and the same page lists $64.44 per hour. That is what a developer earns, not what a vendor invoices.

A billable rate also covers overhead, management, profit, travel, specialized expertise and any platform charges. I can't give you a verified market-average FDE rate, so require every vendor to quote a rate card and compare those. For posted pay ranges on the employee side, see our page on forward deployed engineer salary.

Should you use fixed-scope pricing for the pilot?

Usually not for the first discovery weeks, and usually yes for the build that follows. A firm-fixed-price arrangement provides a price that is not adjusted based on the contractor's cost experience. Fixed-price types can also provide a firm price or, when appropriate, an adjustable price.

A fixed price only protects you if the scope is precise. Before you sign, write down:

  • The systems and data sources in scope, by name.
  • The claim or submission types included and excluded.
  • The acceptance test for each deliverable, run on your data.
  • The environments the vendor must deliver into.

Which clauses protect you against scope creep?

Scope creep shows up in T&M as extra hours and in fixed-scope as change orders. Ask for these clauses:

  • Written change orders only. No work outside the statement of work is billable without a signed change order stating hours, price and schedule impact.
  • Named assumptions. List every dependency you own, such as data access and subject-matter experts, so the vendor cannot later claim delay from an unlisted one.
  • Acceptance criteria per deliverable. Payment follows passing the test, not delivery of a document.
  • Ceiling and trigger. Notify you at a set percentage of the not-to-exceed amount, and stop work at the ceiling until you approve more.
  • Exclusions list. State what is out of scope in plain terms.

No primary source I can cite gives standard wording for these, so treat them as terms to negotiate and have counsel draft.

How do outcome-based models work for claims automation?

Part of the fee depends on measured business results instead of hours. The cited sources do not supply baseline or attribution rules, so you must define them in the contract. Candidate measures include:

  • Adjusted loss expense
  • Cycle time
  • Straight-through-processing rate
  • Payment accuracy
  • Leakage
  • Complaint rate
  • Regulatory exceptions

Follow these steps:

  • Pick two or three measures, not all seven.
  • Freeze a baseline period from your own claims data and have both parties sign off on it.
  • Define attribution: which claim populations count, and how you exclude changes the vendor did not cause, such as a catastrophe event or a rate change.
  • Pair every efficiency measure with a quality guardrail. A higher straight-through rate should not count if payment accuracy or complaint rate gets worse.
  • Set the measurement window and who runs the report.
  • Give yourself audit rights over the data behind the number.

I have no verified benchmark for gain-share percentages or outcome prices, so derive them from your own baseline and the value of the improvement.

How do FDE firms bill for software licensing versus engineering hours?

It depends on the firm and the deal. A public SEC filing describes a Palantir master subscription agreement that bundled platform access with professional services. The same filing records a dispute alleging that the software did not perform as promised and that required implementation, integration, and enablement services were not provided.

The filing does not disclose how the commitment was divided between license and services, so I can't give you a typical split. The lesson is about structure:

  • Price the subscription and the services as separate line items.
  • List the implementation, integration and enablement services as named deliverables with dates.
  • Tie license payments to access you can use and services payments to accepted work.
  • Define what happens to fees if promised services are not delivered.

What are the steps for a pilot-to-production contract?

  • Sign a master agreement with common terms: data security, IP, audit rights, termination and a change-order process.
  • Add a T&M pilot work order with a not-to-exceed ceiling, a rate card by labor category and a fixed end date.
  • Set exit criteria for the pilot, such as a working prototype on your data and a written scope for the build.
  • Convert to a fixed-scope build work order with milestone payments tied to acceptance tests.
  • Freeze the outcome baseline before go-live, with the measures and attribution rules above.
  • Move to production terms: a base fee for support plus an outcome-linked portion.
  • Schedule reviews each quarter to reset the scope, the measures and the fee.

Which SLA terms tie engineering cost to outcomes?

I can't cite standard thresholds or credit percentages, so leave the numbers to your baseline and negotiation. The terms to include are:

  • Measure definition. The formula, data source and owner for each metric.
  • Target and floor. A target you pay full fees for, and a floor below which fees reduce.
  • Fee at risk. The share of the production fee that moves with results.
  • Guardrails. Payment accuracy, complaint rate and regulatory exceptions must stay within agreed limits.
  • Remediation. A cure period and a plan before any credit applies.
  • Termination right. You can exit if the floor is missed for an agreed number of consecutive periods.

How do you choose how to engage a vendor?

Decide the engagement shape first, then the price. Dplyz places senior engineers inside a business to connect its data, build products and run AI agents and workflows in production. It offers an embedded team, project delivery or technical leadership.

Those map roughly to T&M-style staffing, a defined build and advisory oversight. Dplyz does not publish a price list, so ask any vendor to quote in the structure you want. Common questions

What is the average hourly rate for a forward deployed engineer under a T&M contract?

I can't cite a verified market average. The BLS reports 2025 median software developer pay of $64.44 per hour, but that is employee pay, not an invoice rate. A vendor's rate also covers overhead, management, profit and specialized expertise, so ask for a rate card by labor category.

How do outcome-based pricing models work for insurance claims automation projects?

A portion of the fee depends on measures you define, such as cycle time, straight-through-processing rate, payment accuracy, leakage and complaint rate. You need a frozen baseline, an attribution rule and guardrail metrics, because the outcome only means something against a signed baseline.

Should we use fixed-scope pricing for a forward deployed engineering pilot phase?

Use it only if you can specify the scope and acceptance tests. A fixed price is not adjusted for the vendor's cost experience, so unclear scope leads to disputes or change orders. For discovery work, T&M with a not-to-exceed ceiling is usually the better fit.

How do enterprise FDE firms bill for proprietary software licensing versus engineering hours?

It varies by firm and deal. One public SEC filing describes a master subscription agreement bundling platform access with professional services, and it does not disclose the split. Ask for the license and the services as separate line items with their own deliverables.

What specific contract clauses protect insurers against scope creep in FDE engagements?

Ask for written change orders only, named assumptions, acceptance criteria per deliverable, a not-to-exceed ceiling with a notification trigger, and an exclusions list. Have counsel draft the exact wording for your deal.
