Why Multi-Agent Systems Fail (2025) Explained for FDEs

FDE Interviews · 2026-10-17 · 4 min read

Summary

A Berkeley group including Matei Zaharia, Joseph Gonzalez and Ion Stoica studied why multi-agent LLM systems fail. Using Grounded Theory on over 150 execution traces from five frameworks, they built MAST, a taxonomy of 14 failure modes in three categories: system design, inter-agent misalignment, and task verification. Failure rates across six open-source systems ranged from 41% to 86.7%. A calibrated LLM judge (OpenAI o1) reached 94% accuracy and labeled 1,642 traces. The core claim: failures stem mostly from organizational design, not model weakness, so better models alone won't help. For FDEs, the takeaway is to read traces, name the failure precisely, fix the design, and avoid adding agents when one would suffice.

The paper in one minute

Multi-agent systems split a job across several LLM agents with roles: a planner and a coder, a CEO and a reviewer, a supervisor and a set of tool agents. They are popular, and they often disappoint. The authors measured failure rates between 41% and 86.7% across six open-source systems, each on its own benchmark.

The question the paper asks is why. The team, a Berkeley group including Matei Zaharia, Joseph Gonzalez and Ion Stoica, took over 150 execution traces from five frameworks (traces average over 15,000 lines each) and had six experts read them with Grounded Theory, a qualitative method in which categories come out of the data rather than going in as hypotheses. They kept reading until new traces stopped producing new failure types. Three annotators then labeled traces independently and argued out their disagreements over three rounds until agreement reached Cohen's κ = 0.88.

The result is MAST, the Multi-Agent System Failure Taxonomy: 14 failure modes in three groups. System design issues include ignoring the task's constraints, repeating steps and not knowing when to stop. Inter-agent misalignment covers agents that go off task, withhold what another agent needs, or say one thing and do another. Task verification failures are stopping early, or checking badly or not at all.

To scale up, they calibrated an LLM judge (OpenAI's o1 with few-shot examples) against the human labels, reaching 94% accuracy and κ = 0.77, and used it to label 1,642 traces from seven frameworks. Their central claim is that these failures mostly come from organizational design rather than model weakness, so better models alone will not fix them.

Multi-agent systems often barely beat a single agent or best-of-N sampling, and nobody had a shared vocabulary for why they fail, so every team debugged from scratch.

Read real execution traces until the failure patterns stop changing, define each pattern precisely enough that experts agree, then scale the labeling with a calibrated LLM judge.

MAST: 14 failure modes in three groups, system design, inter-agent misalignment and task verification, with κ = 0.88 human agreement and a public dataset of 1,642 labeled traces.

Why it matters to an FDE

When a customer's multi-agent pilot stalls, the conversation usually turns to swapping the model. This paper gives you a better first move: read the traces and name the failure.

A shared vocabulary speeds up debugging. "The agents get confused" is not actionable. "The reviewer passes code that compiles but never runs it" (no or incomplete verification) or "the tool agent knows the username must be a phone number and never says so" (information withholding) points straight at the fix.

Most of the fixes are design work, which is FDE work. Clear role definitions, explicit stop conditions, who is allowed to end the conversation, and a verifier that checks the actual goal rather than whether the code compiles are all choices you control without retraining anything.

It is also an argument for fewer agents. If a pipeline of fixed steps or a single agent with tools would do the job, the 14 failure modes are 14 reasons not to add coordination you do not need. Several of them, such as conversation reset, information withholding and ignoring another agent's input, cannot happen to one agent.

Use the error bars. The interventions in the paper improved one system by five tasks out of 32. Before you tell a customer a change fixed their system, make sure your own evaluation is big enough to show it.
