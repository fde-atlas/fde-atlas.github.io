On-site Video Recording and AI Analysis for FDEs | How to Obtain Consent, Record, and Analysis Frameworks

Original title: FDEの現場ビデオ撮影とAI分析｜同意の取り方、撮り方、分析の型

TIMEWELL · 2026-09-15 · 12 min read

Summary

In the fifth article of TIMEWELL's FDE practice series, Hamamoto describes how the company's engineers film client work with consent and analyze the footage with AI afterward. Consent is treated as a prerequisite for analysis, not paperwork, with six written commitments covering purpose, scope, retention, viewers, prohibited uses, and masking of faces and name tags. Filming combines a fixed camera, screen recording, and pre-agreed unrecorded periods, with two sessions per task on separate days. AI handles timestamped action transcripts, counting, speech transcription, and flagging contradictions, while humans handle interpretation, ban evaluation-style summaries, and enforce masking. Unlike contracted work analysis ending in reports, FDE video analysis aims to decide what working artifact ships the following week and measures impact by re-filming, e.g., approval wait time dropping from 47 to 12 minutes.

Key points

  • Consent is a prerequisite for analysis, secured in writing from the worker and their manager on six items including masking.
  • Filming uses a fixed camera, screen recording, and agreed unrecorded periods; each task is filmed twice on different days.
  • AI handles transcripts, counts, speech-to-text, and contradiction candidates; humans handle meaning and ban evaluation-style summaries.
  • The goal is deciding next week's working artifact, with impact measured by re-filming, such as approval waits falling from 47 to 12 minutes.
  • Unlike contracted analysis ending in reports, FDE keeps analysis, implementation, and measurement in one engineer's hands.

Hello, this is Hamamoto from TIMEWELL Co., Ltd.

This is the fifth installment in the FDE practice series. So far, I have written about four techniques: listening, observing, being present, and structuring. This time, as an extension of the observation technique, I will describe a method our engineers recently started using: filming work on-site with permission and analyzing it later with AI.

The observation method was about watching on-site in real time. Because of that, things get overlooked, counts are mis-tallied, and nothing is known about the time the observer was away from their seat. Video can be reviewed as many times as needed afterward. And now that machines capable of reading video have reached a practical level, most of the "reviewing" work can be handed over to machines. However, my experience over the past year is that there are more things you need to decide before filming than things you only discover after filming. In this article, I will cover how to obtain consent, how to film, patterns for analysis, and how this differs from contracted work analysis. If you want to first verify how much on-site data your own team can handle, please use the AI literacy diagnostic.

Deciding before you film. Consent is a prerequisite for analysis, not a procedure

First, let me write the most important thing. Before filming on-site, you obtain the consent of the people who will appear in the footage. This is not a procedure; it is a prerequisite for analysis. Footage taken without consent is not only legally problematic, it is also unusable for analysis. That is because the work shown differs between someone who does not know they are being filmed and someone who knows and has accepted it.

I will describe the legal framework to the extent needed for practice. Video in which faces can be identified constitutes personal information under the Act on the Protection of Personal Information. When acquiring it, you are required to specify the purpose of use as much as possible and notify or publicly announce it to the individuals, and you may not use it beyond the scope of that purpose 1. The same applies when filming employees. The "Camera Image Utilization Guidebook" compiled by the Ministry of Internal Affairs and Communications and the Ministry of Economy, Trade and Industry organizes the considerations for using camera images in business by stage: planning, acquisition, storage, and use 2. Although the document was originally written with store and street cameras in mind, its principles—clarifying the purpose, notifying people in advance that they will be filmed, limiting the retention period, and handling third-party disclosure—apply directly to filming work on-site.

Before filming, our company explains the following six items in writing to the individuals being filmed and their supervisors, and obtains consent. Purpose (that it is work analysis for building something that moves). Scope (whose work, which tasks, and at what times of day will be filmed). Retention period (that the footage will be deleted once analysis is complete). Viewers (which of our engineers will see it). Non-use (that it will not be used for individual evaluation, attendance, or HR purposes). And masking faces and name badges before feeding the footage to AI. The sixth item is not so much a legal requirement as our own commitment. We remove personally identifiable information from footage before machines read it.

One more thing that always happens on-site is worth noting. When you bring up filming, the person in charge feels they are "being monitored." Naturally. That is why our company explains the purpose of filming as "to build something that moves that will reduce your work," and shows the person the first analysis results first. For example, one sentence like: "You spend a total of 47 minutes a day waiting for approvals. Next week we will build a screen that shortens this." With that one sentence, monitoring turns into analysis. Filming without returning the footage to the person is surveillance.

How to film. Fixed cameras, screen recording, and the times you do not film

We combine three filming methods.

The first is a fixed camera. A small camera on a tripod is placed diagonally behind the person in charge, at the position where the observer would sit in the observation method. The framing includes the screen, the paper at hand, and the motion of leaving the seat. Faces are not included. Framing design reduces the effort of masking. The advantage of a fixed camera is that it records even when no observer is present. It captures the time the observer was away from their seat, the rush before lunch break, and the wrap-up before the end of the workday.

The second is screen recording. The person in charge records the screen of the PC they use, operating it themselves. With a fixed camera, the text on the screen cannot be read, but with screen recording, which system and which screens were opened, and in what order, is captured accurately. Since customer data appears here, the scope of recording and how it is stored are set more strictly than for the fixed camera. Our company's basic policy is that screen recordings never leave the customer's terminal, and analysis is performed within the customer's environment.

The third is the times you do not film. Phone calls, visitors, breaks, and any task the person says "please don't film this" are not filmed. If the non-filmed times are decided in advance, the person in charge can relax and do their work as usual during the filmed times. If you try to film everything, everything becomes "work performed for the camera."

The length of filming is, for a single task, two sessions on different days. As I wrote last time, people's movements change when they are being watched. The second session is closer to their usual behavior than the first. We almost never film a third time. If the differences are visible across two sessions, that is enough; beyond that, the burden on the person in charge outweighs the benefit.

Analysis delegated to AI, and analysis left to humans

How do we analyze the footage we film? This is what has changed over the past year. Major multimodal models can now accept video as input, query content at specified timestamps, and produce transcripts of events 3. We use this capability to have machines fill in the five columns of the observation record.

There are four analyses we delegate to AI. First, timestamped transcripts of actions. "10:03:12 opens the approval screen," "10:03:40 picks up a paper form," "10:04:05 speaks to the person at the next desk." Of the five columns a person used to write in the observation method, machines fill in three: time, action, and screen. Second, counting. The total time waiting for approvals, the number of screen switches, the number of returns to the same screen, the number of times hands moved back and forth between paper and keyboard. Things that were hard to count by human eyes come out accurately. Third, transcription of speech. From the fixed camera's audio, we transcribe the words the person in charge spoke. Fourth, surfacing candidate discrepancies. We have the machine read the interview transcript and the video transcript side by side and surface candidate contradictions such as "the explanation says section-chief approval, but the video shows an assistant manager pressing the button."

There are three analyses left to humans. First, assigning meaning. A machine can count "switched screens 3 times," but whether those 3 switches reflect hesitation, habit, or something unavoidable due to the system's design is something only someone who was on-site can know. The fifth column, the sense of something off, is written by a person. Second, prohibiting summaries that read as evaluations. When you have a machine summarize footage, sentences like "Person A's work efficiency is..." appear. We do not use these. The unit of analysis is the task, not the person, and outputs begin with "For this task, the approval wait time is..." Third, never feeding unmasked footage to the machine. Faces and name badges are removed, and customer names are removed from screen recordings, before the footage is read.

This division of labor makes the difference from the observation method clear. In the observation method, a person writes all five columns, and because they write while watching, things get missed. With video, the machine writes three columns, so the person can focus on the "sense of incongruity" column. Last time, I wrote that the eye for noticing discrepancies is trained by writing the incongruity column every day; video is a method that lets you spend human time only on that column.

Turning what was recorded into something that moves the following week

Here, let me clarify the purpose of the FDE recording video. It is not to create a work analysis report. It is to decide what moving thing to put in place the following week.

The numbers that come out of the footage become material for the Friday work I described in the KJ method article. For example, numbers like "total time waiting for approval: 47 minutes," "screen switches: 212 times per day," and "back-and-forth between paper and keyboard: 18 times per hour." On their own, these do not become a moving thing. On Friday, when they are bundled alongside interview transcripts and observation records and given names, a structure becomes visible—"approvals take long because approvers don't look at the screen, so things circulate on paper"—and what to build the following week is decided. Video is a method of adding numbers to the bundle's material.

And the effect of what was built is measured the same way. The week after the moving thing is put in place, the same work is recorded again and the same counts are taken. Differences emerge, such as "total approval wait time went from 47 minutes to 12 minutes" or "screen switches went from 212 to 96." This difference is the result for the person in charge, and the basis for our company's judgment of "is this pattern worth returning to the product." It is measured not by technical metrics like 95% accuracy, but by metrics of the person's day.

Differences from contracted work analysis

Recording and analyzing work on video is a method that has long existed in the field of industrial engineering. It was systematized as motion study and has been used in manufacturing as a tool for improvement activities. In contracted development and consulting as well, filming is sometimes done as part of business analysis. Looking only at the motion, they appear the same, so I will lay out the differences in a table.

PerspectiveContracted work analysisFDE video analysis
PurposeVisualization of the current state and a report of improvement proposalsDeciding the moving thing to put in place the following week, and measuring its effect afterward
Unit of analysisProcesses, standard timesWork, and within it, waiting, switching, and back-and-forth
ExitReports, improvement proposalsA moving thing, and a pattern to return to the product
How effect is measuredEstimates at the time of the proposalRe-recording the same work the week after deployment and computing the difference
Handling of footageOften retained by the analystNever leaves the customer environment and is deleted after analysis

The biggest difference is the exit. Contracted work analysis ends with a report and improvement proposals. Whether the proposals get implemented becomes a matter of a separate contract. In FDE video analysis, one cycle extends to putting a moving thing in place the following week and re-recording the week after that to compute the difference. Analysis, implementation, and effect measurement all run through the same engineer's hands.

Another difference is how effect is measured. Contracted proposals include estimates like "expected reduction of X hours per year." The FDE does not use estimates; they produce actual measurements from the week after deployment. Whether 47 minutes became 12 minutes can be known simply by recording. It is because of these actual measurements that the judgment "return this pattern to the product" gains a basis. The question a16z raised as a test for distinguishing the FDE pattern—whether work hours are decreasing at mature customers 4—can also only be answered through an accumulation of actual measurements.

Our practice and its limits

I will write honestly. We have only just started using this method ourselves, and we need to assume situations where it does not go well. Three failures are likely to occur. The fixed camera's angle of view is wrong, and the crucial paper forms are not captured. The person in charge forgets to stop the screen recording, and it keeps recording past the promised time (in that case, we delete that portion in front of the person). The machine's transcription counts screen switches more times than actually occurred. All of these can be prevented by deciding the camera angle, the stop procedure, and who reviews the footage before recording.

Let me also write the limits. Video can only analyze what is captured. The judgments in the person's head, what the person on the phone said, what the person at the next desk whispered—none of that is captured. So video is no substitute for the observation method or interviews. You write the incongruity column with the observation method, add numbers with video, and ask about what's in the head through interviews. Only when all three come together do they become material for the bundle.

And then there is the weight of consent. Recording goes deeper into the person's work than observation. We do not treat consent to record as a one-time thing; we confirm verbally each time we record, show the analysis results to the person first, and tell them when the footage has been deleted after analysis. If you skip this back-and-forth, you will not get consent for the next recording. Consent determines not only the quality of the analysis, but whether you can enter the next site at all.

Summary

If the observation method is "watching on the spot," video is "watching as many times as you want afterward." Before recording, put six things in writing: purpose, scope, retention period, who can view, what it will not be used for, and masking, and obtain consent from the person captured and their supervisor. For the recording method, decide in advance: fixed cameras for the screen and hands, screen recording for system operations, and the times when you will not record. In analysis, the machine handles timestamped transcription of actions, counting, speech-to-text, and surfacing candidate discrepancies, while the human handles meaning-making and evaluation, with a ban on readable summaries and masking.

The purpose of the FDE recording video is not a report, but deciding the moving thing to put in place the following week and re-recording the week after to compute the difference. Whether 47 minutes became 12 minutes can be known simply by recording. This actual measurement becomes the basis for the judgment to return the pattern to the product.
