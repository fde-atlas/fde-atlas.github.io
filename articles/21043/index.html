The FDE model is not the exclusive domain of giant companies like Palantir or OpenAI. In fact, in recent years, there has been a growing number of cases where SaaS startups aiming for enterprise adoption have used "small FDE teams" as a weapon to win large customers.

As the final installment of the FDE case study series, this article explains how startups made the FDE model work, drawing on real examples from Serval, Ironclad, Looker, and Promise featured in First Round Review, along with three criteria for deciding whether to adopt it in your own company.

Key Takeaways from This Article

  • The FDE model is not just for giant companies; SaaS startups like Serval, Ironclad, and Looker have also won large customers with small FDE teams.
  • At Serval, an AI platform for IT operations, FDEs shipped over 60 third-party app integrations based on insights gained in the field with customers.
  • The conditions for the FDE model to work are threefold: (1) targeting a large-customer segment, (2) a product that can be applied to diverse use cases, and (3) workflows and requirements that vary significantly from customer to customer.
  • In startup FDE hiring, it is considered important not to lower the technical bar below that of a regular software engineer.

Case 1: Serval — Shipping Over 60 Integrations from FDE Field Insights

At Serval, an AI platform for IT operations, FDEs spend about 20% of their working hours directly with customers, and the insights gained there are reflected directly in the product. Items shipped from FDE initiatives include over 60 third-party app integrations, a feedback system that lets customers evaluate agent performance, and SLA management features. All of these are features that "would never have been prioritized without being on-site with customers."

"The normal feedback loop goes solution engineer → PM → EM → sprint planning and takes months. The FDE model removes the middlemen; engineers hear the feedback directly and build right away. FDEs are a way to scale the early-startup energy of a CTO hearing customer feedback directly and fixing things immediately."

What is interesting about the company's operations is its approach to prioritization. "P2 (medium-priority) requests keep getting left behind, and leaving them behind makes for an inferior product. With the FDE model, P2s actually get worked on." — They have built a structure where improvements that would be perpetually postponed under roadmap-driven development are continuously shipped by FDEs. Notably, the company initially deployed FDEs to all customers, but has since shifted to an approach focused on large customers with over 1,000 employees.

Case 2: Ironclad — How "Legal Engineers" Won Fortune 100 Companies

Ironclad, a contract management SaaS, formed an FDE-style team called "Legal Engineering" from its early days when ARR was under $1 million. Its early customers were extremely diverse—publicly traded tech companies, YC startups, global beauty brands, and professional sports teams—and the article introduces cases where very different workflows, such as Japanese influencer contracts and MLB season ticket contracts, were supported by individual implementations on the same platform.

After maturing, the company shifted to a policy of limiting FDE deployment to high-ACV (Fortune 100-level) customers. One Fortune 100 general counsel reportedly affectionately called the ever-present Ironclad team "The Backpacks." Regarding scope creep, they adopted a clear decision criterion: "Accept it if it leads to reusable software value; decline it if there is no path to that." Requests for new workflow types were charged per workflow, converting them into expanded product coverage.

Case 3: Looker — Increasing Win Rates with "Embedded Implementation" During Free Trials

Looker, a BI tool, was practicing a prototype of this model even before the term FDE became widespread. During the free trial period, engineers built a PoC using the customer's real data rather than a demo with dummy data—a strategy of "selling it as a product while making the experience feel like a customized service." By showing value working on real data first, they were able to confidently pitch at an annual price of $25,000 or more. It is also important that CEO Frank Bien validated this model through financial modeling and made the investment decision after confirming unit economics showing they "could reach $100 million ARR at 2,000 customers."

Does Your Company Need an FDE? Three Decision Criteria

Synthesizing the insights of the practitioners featured in First Round Review (Ironclad, Serval, Promise, and Looker), the conditions for the FDE model to work come down to the following three.

CriterionQuestionIf Not Met
(1) Large-customer segmentAre you targeting Fortune 500-level, high-ACV customers? By definition, FDEs are an upmarket playIf PLG or freemium is your end state, the investment in FDEs cannot be recouped
(2) Product plasticityCan the product be applied to diverse use cases by "changing shape" (the opposite of Apple-style strong prescriptiveness)?If your product vision is strongly prescriptive, there is little room for FDEs to create value
(3) Non-uniform customer baseDo workflows and requirements differ significantly from customer to customer (like the diversity of Ironclad's first 50 customers)?If your ICP is extremely uniform, standard features and onboarding are enough

Practical Insights for Startups Hiring FDEs

  • Don't lower the technical bar: Ironclad's best FDEs were "people who could have become staff engineers at top companies," and they went through the same coding assessments as regular engineers
  • Five qualities to look for: (1) junior-level flexibility (independence over length of experience), (2) tolerance for pain (grit), (3) strong technical ability, (4) an inability not to build, and (5) curiosity about business
  • Interviews based on real problems: An open-ended question like "Pick one problem from your career and tell us how you solved it with technology" draws out practical thinking skills
  • Send them to the field: "The most impactful work came during a few weeks spent in a small German town" (Balaji from Promise). Design on-site engagement as a source of results
  • The final judgment criterion is "Would I want to be in the trenches with this person?"

FAQ

Frequently Asked Questions

Q: Is there any point in having an FDE at a small startup?

It depends on the conditions. If you target large customers (high ACV), your product can be applied to diverse use cases, and your customer base is non-uniform—these three conditions are met, there are real examples of it working, like Ironclad, even at a stage with less than $1 million in ARR. Conversely, it is a poor fit for PLG-type products with a uniform ICP.

Q: I'm worried that FDE requests will grow endlessly (scope creep).

Ironclad's decision criteria are a useful reference: "Accept requests that lead to reusable software value, and decline those with no path to it." Also, the better the FDE, the less likely they are to stop working on their own, so it is important for managers to rebalance as a healthy tension built into the design.

Q: How should the results of an FDE team be measured?

At the case-study companies, contribution to deal closure (Looker's trial conversion), feedback into the product (Serval's 60+ integrations), and retention and expansion of large customers (Ironclad's Fortune 100) serve as the de facto performance metrics. The principle is to measure by customer outcomes and contribution to productizing, not by the number of features shipped.
