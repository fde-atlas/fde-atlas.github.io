How to Implement AI in Your Company | 6 Decisions to Make in the First 3 Months

Original title: 社内AI導入の進め方｜最初の3か月で決める6つのこと

FDE Consulting · 2026-10-28 · 8 min read

Summary

Citing Japan's 2026 (Reiwa 8) Information and Communications White Paper, the article notes 86.4% of Japanese companies use generative AI, yet 27.0% report no organized initiatives—noticeably higher than the US, Germany, or China. The author argues that internal AI adoption means making company-level decisions, and that the key decisions emerge within the first three months: appointing a responsible executive, defining permitted information and tools, building a system to track usage, selecting the first business processes to change, deciding how to measure effects, and planning next steps and budget. A month-by-month schedule is provided. The author advises reusing existing governance structures rather than creating new ones, keeping decisions like leadership and process selection in-house, and bringing in outside help—such as FDEs—for rebuilding workflows and connecting AI to internal systems.

Key points

  • 86.4% of Japanese firms use generative AI, but 27.0% lack organized initiatives
  • Six decisions should be made in the first three months of adoption
  • First processes should be frequent, text-based, and human-verifiable
  • Reuse existing IT and risk governance structures instead of new organizations
  • Outsource workflow rebuilding and system integration, possibly via FDEs, but keep decisions in-house

It is no longer rare to find people using AI at work. According to the Ministry of Internal Affairs and Communications' 2026 Information and Communications White Paper, 86.4% of Japanese companies reported using generative AI for some kind of work. On the other hand, 27.0% of Japanese companies answered that they have "no organizational initiatives" for business transformation through generative AI, notably higher than the US, Germany, and China.

People are using it, but that does not necessarily mean the company is driving it forward. In my view, internal AI adoption is the work of making the decisions a company needs to make in order to close this gap. And those decisions mostly become clear within the first three months. This article summarizes those six decisions and how to proceed month by month.

Detailed guidance on how to choose which tasks to start with is covered in Steps for Improving Business Efficiency with AI, and the reasons why adopted AI stops being used are covered in Why AI Adoption Fails. Here, I deal with the step before that: "what to decide internally."

Where Do the Benefits of AI Adoption Appear?

First, clarify what you are adopting AI for. In the white paper's question about the possible effects of using generative AI, Japanese companies ranked "improving business efficiency through shorter work time, etc." highest at 61.6%, far above "improving the quality of products and services" at 32.2% and "resolving staff shortages" at 26.8%. The white paper states that while generative AI in Japan is viewed in the context of business efficiency, the other three countries may be taking a step further in how they view it.

I think of the benefits of AI adoption in the following three stages.

StageBenefitWhat It Takes to Realize It
Individual workLess time spent drafting documents, summarizing, and researchingA safe environment for use and usage rules
Business processesFewer manual steps in departmental work such as applications, inquiries, and reportsDesign of role division between people and AI, integration with internal data
Products and servicesChanges in the quality and speed delivered to customersChanges to business processes and someone accountable for the results

This table is my own organization of the topic. The benefits at the individual work level appear relatively quickly once tools are distributed. From business processes onward, the number of company-level decisions increases. I consider the six decisions to make in the first three months to be the foundation for advancing to the second and third stages.

Six Decisions to Make in the First Three Months

#DecisionWhat Happens If You Don't Decide
1Person in chargeEach department proceeds on its own, and no one stops or advances anything
2Permitted information and toolsEmployees use AI at their own discretion, and handling of confidential information varies from person to person
3A system for tracking usageNo one knows where or for what AI is being used, so neither risks nor effects can be measured
4The first processes to changeProgress stalls at "what should we use it for?"
5How to measure effectsNo way to judge whether to continue or stop

1. Decide on a Person in Charge

The AI Business Guidelines from the Ministry of Internal Affairs and Communications and the Ministry of Economy, Trade and Industry list, as a common principle, designating a person responsible for ensuring accountability within each organization. Regarding management, they state that management's responsibility is significant and that exercising leadership is important.

In my view, the person in charge should be an executive or someone who can speak directly with executives. This is because both setting usage rules and changing business processes require decisions that cross departmental boundaries. For what title to use, see What Is a CAIO (Chief AI Officer)?

2. Decide on Permitted Information and Tools

When employees use AI at their own discretion, the handling of the information they input varies from person to person. Decide which tools the company permits, and spell out what information may and may not be entered. How to create the regulations is summarized in How to Create Internal Usage Regulations for Generative AI

3. Build a System for Tracking Usage

The Guide to Utilizing the AI Business Guidelines (Draft) lists, as preparatory items for establishing AI governance, four things: a system for understanding the status of AI development, provision, and use within the organization; building a risk assessment framework; building an incident reporting framework; and establishing and operating rules suited to the characteristics of the AI the organization handles.

The items the guide says to track include information such as for what purposes and uses, which departments are using which AI, and what data they are using. As collection methods, it cites applications from users and regular surveys and interviews. The procedure for identifying uses of AI the company has not approved is covered in Steps for Auditing Shadow AI

Regarding risk assessment, the guide states that rather than routing every use case through a consultation desk, you should set criteria to separate cases requiring consultation from those that can be cleared by self-checking. In my view, if you try to review everything in the first three months, the desk gets bottlenecked and people on the ground stop submitting applications. Deciding the criteria first ultimately broadens the range you can actually track.

4. Choose the First Processes to Change

Have each department propose candidates, and have the person in charge narrow them down to one to three. In my view, it is best to start with tasks that are performed frequently, involve text input and output, and whose mistakes a person can verify. A detailed perspective on how to choose is in Steps for Improving Business Efficiency with AI

What matters here is deciding together with the person in charge of the processes on the ground. If the AI team alone chooses the processes, they will not fit the actual workflows, and even a trial will go unused.

5. Decide How to Measure Effects

Before starting, measure the time and volume involved in the target processes. Unless you compare using the same measurements after adoption, the effect will never rise above "it somehow feels easier." Measuring not only time but also the number of rework cycles and time spent on verification makes it easier to spot efficiency gains that are only superficial. How to compare costs and effects is covered in How to Calculate the ROI of AI Investment

6. Decide How to Proceed to the Next Stage and the Budget

At the end of the third month, decide which processes to continue, which to expand, and which to stop. Among the processes to continue, those that need to connect with internal systems or data require a development budget for the first time at this point. In my view, rather than securing a large development budget from the start, deciding after seeing the results of the three months makes clearer what you are paying for. The breakdown of development costs is summarized in Breakdown of AI Development Costs

How to Proceed Month by Month

Allocating the six decisions across three months looks like this. The table is a rough guide based on my view and will shift depending on the company's size and industry. The guide also states that the order of tackling the preparatory items can vary by business, so keep parallel handling in mind.

MonthMain DecisionsWhat Exists When Done
Month 1Person in charge, permitted information and tools, tracking usageAppointment of the person in charge, first version of usage rules, a list of AI in use
Month 2The first processes to change and how to measure effectsTarget processes, on-site managers, and pre-introduction time and volume
Month 3Evaluating trial results, how to proceed to the next stage and budgetDeciding whether to continue, expand, or stop; the plan for the next three months

Month 1: Set the foundation

Appoint a manager, create the first version of usage rules, and compile a list of AI tools currently in use. The rules do not need to be perfect from the start. Of the four preparation items in the handbook, for incident reporting, at minimum decide on a contact point first.

Month 2: Choose processes and measure

Gather candidates from departments and select the first process. Together with the on-site manager, measure the current procedure and time. The numbers measured here will serve as the basis for decisions in Month 3.

Month 3: Trial and decide

Trial the selected process for a few weeks and compare using the same measurement method as in Month 2. Based on the results, decide which processes to continue, expand, or stop. Deciding to stop is also an achievement. Knowing which processes AI does not suit will speed up selecting the next candidates.

You do not need to create a new structure

When it comes to introducing AI in-house, the discussion tends to turn toward creating a dedicated organization. However, the draft handbook states that if an existing structure is in place, there is no need to create a new one solely for AI utilization. The AI guidelines set by the Cabinet Office's AI Strategy Headquarters also state that it is useful to use existing governance mechanisms applied to IT systems.

In my view, adding AI roles to existing mechanisms—such as the information systems department, risk management meetings, and personal information officers—is more realistic for getting started within three months. Examples of how to build such a structure are collected in How to Build an AI Governance Structure.

What tends to run short is people. According to key points from IPA's "DX Trends 2026," companies that answered that the volume of personnel promoting DX was "somewhat insufficient" or "significantly insufficient" totaled 85.5%. When the six items cannot be handled by in-house staff alone, you need to decide in advance which parts to outsource.

How to think about outsourcing

In my view, the manager, usage rules, and selection of the first process should be decided in-house. If an external company decides them instead, the reasons behind the decisions will not remain within the company after three months.

External help is most valuable at the stage of redesigning workflows and connecting AI with internal systems and data. When choosing a partner, rather than judging by the quality of proposals, check whether they will get involved on-site, redesign the process, and see it through to production use. Such roles overlap with the job type known as FDE (Forward Deployed Engineer). The FDE role is described in What is an FDE? The Meaning and Work of Forward Deployed Engineers, and how to choose a partner is summarized in How to Choose an FDE Support Company.
