How FDEs Handle the "We Can Do It Ourselves" Objection

Original title: FDE 如何应对客户“我们自己能做”的异议

FDE Times · 2026-11-05 · 5 min read

Summary

This guide addresses the common objection "we could build this ourselves" that forward deployed engineers (FDEs) face. The author argues that arguing back or citing data loses the project's key ally—usually the person who will own the system after the FDE leaves. Instead, FDEs should genuinely acknowledge the client's ability, then reframe the question from capability to long-term ownership, since a weekend prototype's real cost lies in running and maintaining it. The article offers a three-step conversation script and a responsibility map: clients keep differentiating components while shared infrastructure is built on the vendor's platform. It cites an MIT report that 95% of enterprise generative AI pilots fail, with internal builds succeeding at one-third the rate of purchased tools, and Menlo Ventures data showing 76% of AI use cases are bought rather than built. It closes with advice on turning such experiences into interview stories.

Key points

  • Acknowledge the client's capability first, then shift to ownership questions.
  • The prototype is cheap; running and maintaining it is the real cost.
  • Clients keep differentiating parts; shared infrastructure goes on the platform.
  • MIT report: 95% of GenAI pilots fail; internal builds succeed at one-third the rate of buying.
  • Repeated build-it-themselves requests across clients are a product signal.

This objection is usually about who will own the system after the Forward Deployed Engineer (FDE) leaves, and the person asking is often that future owner.

Key takeaways

  • Sincerely acknowledge that the customer's team is capable of building it, then ask whether they want to own that part long-term.
  • The first build isn't the real cost; the real cost is running and maintaining the system yourself over its entire lifecycle.
  • Parts that provide differentiation are kept by the customer's team; shared infrastructure is built on the platform for speed.

Picture the first week of a deployment. In a meeting, the customer's head of data engineering opens his laptop and shows everyone a demo he built over the weekend: calling the LLM API directly, summarizing support tickets quite neatly. Then he asks: "My team could build this ourselves. Why should we buy?"

This is where many Forward Deployed Engineers (FDEs) get it wrong. Some rush to defend the product's features point by point; others pull out data to prove the customer is oversimplifying. Either approach might win the argument, at the cost of losing the most important person in the project.

Ironically, it's the FDE's way of working that invites this question. PostHog describes the FDE as someone who sits inside the customer's team, builds prototypes quickly, and accepts that prototypes are rough.

Seeing something running within days, the customer naturally thinks "we could do this too." And the person asking you this question is very likely the one who will run the system after you leave.

Why say "yes" first?

"We could build this ourselves" is rarely just a technical opinion. It contains professional pride, worry about vendor lock-in, and sometimes pressure from above for the internal team to take on more. Arguing back directly only touches the outermost layer.

So the best response usually starts with sincere agreement: yes, your team could absolutely build this version.

A Gong article cites a group of experts: build-vs-buy decisions often go wrong in exactly one place—people assume a quickly built prototype is already a complete capability.

What you're acknowledging is the prototype, not the idea that the prototype is the end point.

The next step is to reframe the question. According to Gong, instead of asking "can it be done," ask where the real differentiation lies. The second question is: once the excitement of the prototype fades, does the team still want to own that layer?

MIT Sloan researchers offer a principle along the same lines: when you need speed, buy off the shelf, or buy and build on top; only build yourself where it can create a unique competitive advantage.

The real cost arrives in year two

The weekend demo has no running cost yet, because nobody is running it. MIT Sloan defines "build" as: the organization develops, runs, and maintains its own models. The demo only accomplishes the first; the effort lies in the latter two.

Applied to the ticket-summarization scenario, these become concrete questions. Running: the tickets contain customers' personal data—who decides what data can be sent externally? When the system breaks at 2 a.m., who's on call?

Maintaining: when the model provider ships a new version and summary quality drops, who notices, who updates the prompts, who reruns the evals?

You don't need to answer these questions for the customer. The goal is to let that engineering lead see for themselves the queue of work behind the demo, then decide which parts the team will take on. Gong's conclusion: the key is whether the team is honest with itself about "who owns this."

A conversation script you can use right now

Below is a sample script you can adapt to your own speaking style.

Customer: My team could build this ourselves. Why should we buy? FDE: You're right—your demo works well, and your team is fully capable of building it. Let me ask from a different angle: six months from now, which parts do you want your team to own? Customer: What do you mean? FDE: For example, who reruns the evals when the model changes, who approves what data can be sent externally, who's on call when something breaks. What's unique to your company should be owned by your team; the parts that are just shared infrastructure, we build on the platform, so your team doesn't have to carry that burden. Customer: What's unique to us is how tickets are categorized and routed to the right people. FDE: Then your team builds that part, and I'll work alongside you. Let's also decide now: after I leave the project, who on your side will own this.

The script has three steps: agree, shift to the question of ownership, then hand over a piece of real work to the customer. The last sentence isn't a pleasantry—it's a handover plan set from week one.

Mapping out who builds what

After the conversation, turn the consensus into a table both sides can see, like the comparison table at the start of this article. For each component, spell out: who builds it, who operates it after handover, and what the risk is if it breaks. The risk column matters most—it shows which parts must have a clearly named owner.

The division follows MIT Sloan's principle. Ticket categorization and routing are the customer's differentiator, so the customer's team builds and keeps them. Data connectors, evals, and access control are shared infrastructure, built on the platform for speed.

Low-risk work like internal weekly reports is a safe zone where the customer's team can build it themselves and learn by doing. If it breaks, things are a few days late—no one is truly hurt.

Don't expect every customer's table to look the same. Rocketlane points out that FDEs have no one-size-fits-all playbook, and neither do ownership maps. a16z reminds us that customers don't want to feel trapped in a lengthy consulting project. A table that already names the operator tells them exactly when you'll step away.

Common mistakes

The most common mistake is using data as a weapon. According to an MIT report covered by Fortune, 95% of enterprise generative AI pilots fail. Buying tools from a professional vendor and building a partnership yields a success rate of about 67%, while building in-house succeeds at only a third of that rate.

Menlo Ventures also found that today 76% of AI use cases are bought rather than built, because off-the-shelf solutions reach production faster and show value sooner. These numbers are worth knowing.

But throwing them at an engineer who just finished showing a demo will only make him more defensive. Better to keep them for yourself, as a reminder of why it's worth asking the ownership question.

The opposite mistake is also common: to please the customer, the FDE promises everything will be custom-built. The result is a system no one can maintain and a project with no end—exactly the situation a16z says customers don't want. Another one: reaching consensus without naming an owner, so once you leave, everything slowly drifts.

There's a rarely noticed mistake: treating "we'll build it ourselves" purely as an objection to defuse. If several customers want to build the same layer themselves, that's a signal worth bringing back to the product team.

Turning this skill into a job-hunting advantage

Don't write "strong communication skills" on your resume. Write specifically how you turned a "we'll build it ourselves" objection into a shared ownership map: which parts the customer's team keeps, which are built on the platform, and who operates them after handover.

In interviews, prepare this story in a situation-action-result structure. A story with a named owner and a clear division of labor is more persuasive than any adjective you could apply to yourself.

The next time someone opens their laptop and says "my team could build this," you don't need to win the argument. What you're after is an ally: the person who will still be running the system long after you've left the project.
