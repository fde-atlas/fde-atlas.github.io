How to become an excellent FDE?

Original title: 如何成为一个优秀的FDE？

53AI · 2026-09-10 · 12 min read

Summary

Kevin Bai, Rippling's founding FDE and a former Palantir engineer, explains what makes an effective FDE. He argues FDEs fit best when selling technically complex products to non-technical buyers, combining consultant, product manager, and engineer roles. Engagements must start with listening, deliver a minimal first version quickly, and always end in software. He also covers hiring criteria, career growth, and warns against joining for the hype.

Key points

  • FDEs suit technically complex products sold to non-technical buyers.
  • The role combines consultant, product manager, and engineer responsibilities.
  • FDE differs from sales engineers, regular engineers, and consulting.
  • Start with listening, ship a minimal version, then iterate.
  • At Palantir, FDE is a terminal title; growth means broader scope.

Full text

01 PART

What exactly is an FDE?

WHAT IS A FORWARD DEPLOYED ENGINEER

Kevin Bai is Rippling's founding FDE—he joined over a year ago as the first person to build out this function at the company; before that, he spent several years at Palantir, which is often credited as the "inventor" of the FDE role. His core conviction: FDE is the best way to win in the enterprise market. But first, you have to answer two questions correctly.

Q Tell us about yourself. How did you become Rippling's "founding FDE"?

My name is Kevin, and I joined Rippling a little over a year ago as the first person helping to build out the FDE function. Before that, I spent several years at Palantir, which I joined in 2021—given how much this industry has changed, that really was a long time ago. I genuinely believe FDE is the best way to win in the enterprise market. So beyond my day job, I also give talks at various conferences and help founders and different types of companies build out their FDE functions.

Q When a founder comes to you asking "should we adopt FDE," what's your framework for deciding?

I always start by clarifying one thing: do you actually need an FDE function? It's easy to get swept up in the hype—thinking you have to do it to raise funding, or doing it just because it's accepted conventional wisdom. I think that kind of thinking is really foolish. My framework comes down to just two questions: Who are you selling to? And what are you selling?

If you're selling something highly technical and complex—like the Anthropic API, or GitHub as a product—it's certainly technical, but your buyers are software engineers; the profile is the CIO or CTO, who can understand and get hands-on with it themselves. Conversely, if you're selling products like Slack or Jira, the tools aren't all that technical, and most end users don't have technical backgrounds either—that's also a good match.

The situation that truly calls for FDE is: you have a highly technical product, but your ideal customer profile isn't technical. Take Palantir's Foundry—we sold to Fortune 500 companies—but they aren't tech companies; they're in consumer goods, manufacturing, aviation. They have enormous, thorny problems worth solving with great solutions, yet not a single person on the customer side can truly connect the software to their business problems. That's where FDE comes in.

In today's era, generative AI makes it easier than ever to build powerful solutions, but "powerful" also means "extremely complex." If your customers come from traditional industries, most of them may have no idea what an agent even is. So it's wise to make sure that "their team has no technical talent" doesn't become an obstacle to your startup's success.

Providing technical solutions to non-technical customers—FDEs are the ones who bridge that gap.

Q So what exactly counts as an FDE? Can you sum it up in one sentence?

Simply put, an FDE is a customer facing software engineer. There's a history to why the role exists, but first let's break the role down: in my experience, this role carries three jobs at once—consultant, product manager, and software engineer. Some companies split these across two or three people, but as a function, FDE is the combination of all three capabilities.

02 What it is not: the triple misreading

WHAT AN FDE IS NOT

When a role gets hot, misconceptions follow. The most common quip is: "It's just a sales engineer with a new name." Kevin says: please show some respect for sales engineers—they do excellent work—but an FDE is genuinely not that, nor is it merely "a developer pushed to the front lines," and it's certainly not "consulting with a new name."

Q An FDE isn't a sales engineer? What's the difference?

Sales engineering is a pre-sales function, mainly building demos to win deals, and the collaboration ends at signing. FDE is not that—it owns the entire process end to end, from discovery to delivery.

Q Then how does it differ from a regular software engineer?

The purpose of a software team, in spirit, is to build "one-to-many" products—make something cool and scale it to 100, 1,000, even 10,000 users. FDE is exactly the opposite: it's "one-to-one"—building something that solves a specific problem for a specific customer. If your thinking goes even one step beyond that scope, you've already gone off track.

Q Some people also lump FDE into the "professional services / consulting" category.

FDE does draw some elements from those practices, but at its core it's a deeply engineering-focused role. If your organization doesn't operate that way, that's fine—you can call it FDE, but in the form that drove enormous commercial success at Palantir, those things are not FDE.

So always remember this standard: the final output of an FDE engagement should be software.

An FDE engagement should produce software as its final output—think of it as inventing new SKUs one by one.

03 Three hats: from "listening" to end-to-end delivery

CONSULTANT · PM · ENGINEER

Consultant, product manager, software engineer—this role is most effective when all three capabilities live in the same person. And in every engagement Kevin has been part of, the first step is never talking—it's listening.

Q Where does an engagement usually begin?

The first step isn't talking—it's listening. Your job is to earn the customer's trust—from the executive sponsor, to VPs, to the CEO—so they see you as a trusted partner. What you want them to tell you isn't just the problem that closed the original deal, but the things that truly keep them up at night—the problems that, if they were a public company, might even drag down the stock price—because those are the ones worth solving.

The most important communication skill for an FDE is listening.

Q What does "listening" look like in a real conversation? Can you give a concrete example?

Generally, what customers tell you is just the symptom. Say they come to you complaining "the dashboard loads too slowly, it's really hurting us"—I wouldn't start by telling them they're wrong; that would be flippant and dishonest. But I would try to dig out the underlying root problem together: why does this affect your business? You didn't choose to work with us because our dashboard loads the fastest. What business problem are we actually here to solve?

If the conversation reveals that their real goal is "to sell more stuff" and they're worried slow dashboards hurt conversion—then the problem isn't how fast it loads, but how well it converts. Latency itself is a quagmire: fix it, and next week you'll want it faster, and the week after that still faster; even a tenfold speedup might produce no real impact at all. So my goal isn't just to figure out what you need, but also to persuade you, as much as possible, to agree on the problem that's truly worth solving.

Q Once the direction is aligned, how do you decide "what exactly to build"?

The ideas never stop coming: making the dashboard faster is one way; checking this person's search history and recommending the most relevant courses is another; making the call-to-action button bigger; or even making it super annoying to leave the page until they buy something—but you can't build everything. Even if the customer had unlimited money, their time is finite. Your job is to find the one thing most likely to achieve the goal and completable within the given time.

Suppose the pilot is only four weeks. What I'm looking for isn't the perfect solution that delivers the biggest improvement, but "the best thing I can get done in two and a half weeks"—because I'm certain you'll hate the first version, and then we'll have to keep iterating. It's a bit counterintuitive: some people would want to pick an optimal solution that just barely fits into four weeks, but then you'll lose the deal unless you can deliver flawless code on the first try, and that's never going to happen. The key principle is iterative development, while focusing your energy on high-priority wins.

Kevin's trade-off criteria

Ship a minimal-scope first version, then iterate from there.

Q Does this ability come from product management experience?

What makes someone good at this isn't necessarily product management experience, but the ability to truly "own" the problem—not merely "own" the project; there's a big difference between the two. Someone with a strong sense of ownership over a project will be willing to do grueling work and put in long hours, which is all well and good; but at the end of the day, the customer doesn't care whether you're happy or how fast or slow you work—they care about whether their problem gets solved.

So a truly excellent candidate is someone who can clearly articulate the framework in their head—why they made a certain decision, and how they justify it along the dimension of "the problem being solved." There's no standard answer here, but there is a right way to answer the question.

Unlike a regular engineer, you can't close a PR and call it a day. You're fully responsible for end-to-end delivery: how it's built, how it's demoed to the customer, how you elicit feedback, and how you get them to ultimately accept the solution. Most people from a development background have been "protected"—there's no DevOps team to hand off to, no QA team; you are everything, from start to finish.

Finally, I want to distinguish a pair of terms: "quality" and "completeness." Quality means the code itself is well written, with no bugs within reasonable bounds; but that doesn't mean it's complete—because the customer probably won't read that code at all. Completeness means: does it run? Does the customer understand that it runs? Does it produce the business outcome the customer is paying to get?

04 Who fits the FDE role? What do interviews actually test?

WHO FITS · HOW WE HIRE

FDE is a hot title these days, but Kevin puts it bluntly: reasons driven by the hype are all bad motivations—this job is essentially three careers stacked into one, with triple the surface area for failure. The real question is: what kind of person is suited for it?

Q Based on background, who is best suited for this role?

This role is a great fit for people who were founders in the past or want to be founders in the future. The FDE role looks a lot like a design-partner style collaboration: you don't know what you're going to build, and the customer doesn't know what they're going to buy. You show up, talk with them, and then build the thing—even if at first nobody's sure whether it's actually needed. Institutions like YC and a16z will teach you the same thing over and over: go talk to customers, then build the thing.

But not everyone is a founder. This role truly sits at the intersection of three disciplines: consulting, product, and engineering: a software engineer who has always carried a product perspective, or a product manager who has always been deeply technical, is a great profile—you're basically already doing this job, just perhaps not under the title of FDE. At the end of the day, this job is for people who genuinely crave "three jobs in one."

A word of advice

Reasons driven by the hype are all bad motivations—your surface area for failure is three times that of a regular job.

Q When interviews assess the "engineering" side, what specifically do you look at?

I assess ownership and completeness. Just writing the code isn't enough: where are the test cases? Do you yourself know where things might go wrong? Which edge cases could cause failures? How would you design around them? And what usage instructions would you give the customer so they don't fall into pitfalls?

Take the simplest example: I ask you to write a function that adds two numbers. If you just write it without telling me "you can't pass in strings—this function only accepts integers," then the moment a customer passes in a string, they'll think your app is broken and your platform is no good. Completeness means truly understanding the full scope of the problem: how it can be used, and how it might be misused. The good news is these fundamentals also help in regular software engineering interviews—far too many people write code without ever considering test cases.

Q What about the behavioral and communication side?

It's hard to demonstrate in an interview that you're a good listener, but it's not hard to demonstrate how you show up and how you collaborate with customers—your interviewer may well be your customer, and you're selling yourself to them. The consulting industry has a term for it: "executive presence"—how well you can perform in front of senior leadership. Maybe you've never been an FDE, but have you reported to a VP of Engineering at a startup? Have you presented to a CEO? How did you do compared to your peers? These are things you should be practicing repeatedly before you even enter the interview process.

You can't grind LeetCode for two weeks and master every problem, and you can't become a compelling presenter just by watching YouTube videos or reading a book about speaking—you have to actually speak and practice. The real way to improve communication clarity is to do it with intention and preparation. My mentor used to say: "fail to prepare, prepare to fail." You can't vaguely make yourself a better speaker, but you can think about every word you say, how those words will be received, and how to prepare to deliver them.

Also, everyone has to work with people at their job—ask your colleagues how your communication is: would they say you speak clearly and can make complex things simple? If they don't say that, then ask how you could do better. Actively seeking feedback is an underrated skill too.

Q If you only have a week or two left, is there still time to prepare for the product side?

Product sense and clear writing actually have a lot in common. Start with habits: make some decisions, then write them down, explaining clearly why you did this rather than that; then find someone with zero context on your situation and no knowledge of your decisions, and see whether they can understand you purely from what you've written. Judgment can't be trained in a week, but you can spend time studying truly excellent products and understanding how they were made. The very best people—in tech, art, or sales—all spend time watching others do something exceptionally well, then learn from them. You have to know what "excellent" looks like before you can apply it to your own problems.

LAST

The future, the ladder, and a last word

THE FUTURE, THE LADDER & A LAST WORD

Will FDE "self-cannibalize within two to three years" as some predict, or is it here to stay? Drawing on more than twenty years of practice at Palantir, Kevin talks about the future, the promotion ladder, and leaves that "last word" for everyone entering the field.

Q How do you think the FDE role will evolve in the future?

Palantir has been around for more than 20 years, and the FDE role has existed since the company's founding—because we realized that in environments that are extremely complex and where neither the ultimate buyer nor the users have a technical background, this is the best way to deliver software that actually works. As long as this kind of problem exists, the FDE role will exist.

Certain FDE positions being absorbed, certain companies using FDEs failing—these things will definitely happen, because there are plenty of companies doing all sorts of things and calling it FDE, and I can't judge an entire function based on a name alone. But if what you're doing is: working directly with customers, listening to what they want, producing a plan they sign off on, and then building the thing and delivering it to them—I don't see how that can fail.

Every great product is made this way—first understand one person, then build the thing and hand it to them.

Q So what does the growth ladder for an FDE look like?

At Palantir, FDE is a terminal title: you join as an FDE on day one, and in year ten you're still an FDE. The business lead who runs the entire commercial business and manages a book worth nine figures (hundreds of millions of dollars) holds the title of FDE—which fits Palantir's spirit perfectly: there should be nothing above this title. Of course, some companies now have Senior FDE or Staff FDE levels, and that's fine too—people are free to take the title and use it however they like.

But in this customer-facing, outcome-driven role, the growth path looks like this: you gradually take on larger scopes. At first, you may not be expected to own a customer account independently; instead, you work alongside a more senior colleague, handling a small piece of that customer's problems. Over time, you can own one customer on your own, then all the customers—then an industry, a domain, a region. The model Palantir taught us is: we give you a company, and you are its CEO. You have a single customer, and the only way to generate revenue is to make that customer as successful as possible. If you do well, maybe we'll give you a few more customers.

Q Finally, any parting advice for people who want to get into this field?

At the end of the day, you have to figure out what you truly want to do—you only get one career. Do you want to be an FDE because that's genuinely what you want to do, the role you've been waiting for? Or is it because someone told you it's what you should do, or because it's trendy and in the news? The people who perform best in interviews are those who have a genuine reason for the role and truly want to succeed in it. If you don't have those reasons yet, that's perfectly fine—take the time to figure out what you really want to do, then go there and do it to the absolute best of your ability.

I also want to offer a caveat: FDE is a very special job, but it's still just a job—not a panacea that will solve all your problems. I can assure you that at many great companies, including Palantir, plenty of FDEs are deeply miserable. It's a career, just like software, product, or recruiting are careers. In the end, choose something you believe you're good at, and then find a role that brings it all together.

The skills themselves haven't changed—only the form, the titles, and the roles have. FDE isn't just another trendy title; it's a combination of capabilities for "truly solving problems all the way to the customer," requiring someone who is both willing to listen and able to deliver. Kevin has also brought his years of practice into his own podcast. If this "one job, three roles" position appeals to you, start by doing what he suggests: write down your first decision—what is the problem I truly want to solve?
