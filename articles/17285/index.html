Startup Roll-Up Strategy | Why It's Trending and Why Two-Thirds Fail

Original title: スタートアップのロールアップ戦略｜流行る理由と3分の2が失敗する理由

TIMEWELL · 2026-09-13 · 21 min read

Summary

TIMEWELL's Hamamoto examines the startup roll-up wave: why it is surging and why most fail. He cites US examples like General Catalyst-backed Long Lake taking Amex GBT private for $6.3B and OpenAI-backed Thrive Holdings raising $2B at a $12B valuation, plus Japan's Hinoki (¥3.1B raised) and FUNDiT (100+ acquisitions). Against this, an HBR-cited study found over two-thirds of roll-ups destroyed investor value. The author identifies four structural failure causes, then offers practical guidance: a 100-day people-first PMI playbook, sequencing AI adoption after codifying tacit shop-floor knowledge, debt-versus-equity funding rules, five acquisition criteria, and a 20-item pre-signing checklist. He argues Japan's version should be framed as business succession, with permanent-holding designs that survive multiple-compression.

Key points

  • The boom rests on three factors: 50.1% successor-less SMEs, an unproven AI margin hypothesis, and equity-first, debt-layered capital structures.
  • Over two-thirds of roll-ups historically fail due to multiple-arbitrage dependence, integration costs scaling with deal count, unproven AI gains, and acquisition becoming the goal.
  • The first 100 days should focus on people and trust; AI should only be introduced after converting tacit frontline knowledge into data.
  • Fund stable-cashflow-coverable portions with debt; pay for integration and AI work with equity, and delay deal two until deal one stabilizes past 100 days.
  • Japan's roll-ups should adopt permanent-holding, succession-oriented designs that work even when exit multiples disappear.

Hello, this is Hamamoto from TIMEWELL Inc.

Since the start of 2026, I have clearly heard the word "roll-up" more often from entrepreneurs and investors. In the US, an acquisition company nurtured by General Catalyst announced it would take a public company private for $6.3 billion, and Thrive Holdings, backed by OpenAI, raised $2 billion at a $12 billion valuation. In Japan, Hinoki, which successively inherits small and midsize businesses lacking successors, raised ¥3.1 billion, and FUNDiT has executed more than 100 M&A deals in its five years since founding. I would say an era has arrived in which startups buy and bundle existing businesses rather than building from zero.

However, let me first put some old numbers on the table. According to research cited by Harvard Business Review, more than two-thirds of roll-ups failed to create value for investors 1. In a survey by the Small and Medium Enterprise Agency, 24% of SMEs that conducted M&A answered that results "fell short of expectations" 2. This article gives equal weight to the reasons for the trend and the reasons for failure, and brings together in one place how to carry out PMI in traditional industries, the meaning as business succession, how to allocate debt and equity, how to raise funds and the decision criteria for acquisitions, troubles that commonly arise after acquisition, and a pre-implementation checklist. If you want to check your own team's AI readiness before introducing AI into acquired companies, please first use the AI Literacy Diagnostic.

Why roll-ups are trending now

There are three reasons: sellers have increased, a hypothesis has emerged that AI can raise profit margins, and the structure of capital has changed.

The first is supply. According to a 2025 Teikoku Databank survey, the successor-absence rate among roughly 270,000 companies nationwide was 50.1%. Although it has improved for seven consecutive years, half of companies still have no successor. Looking at the breakdown of succession, internal promotion was 36.1%, surpassing family succession at 32.3% for the first time, with M&A and other methods accounting for 20.6% 3. According to Tokyo Shoko Research, 67,210 companies suspended operations or dissolved in 2025, a record high for the third consecutive year; of these, 52.8% were profitable companies and 34.0% had representatives aged 80 or older 4. This means that more than 30,000 companies a year disappear while still profitable, simply because they have no successor. According to RECOF's tally, M&A involving Japanese companies reached 5,115 deals in 2025, exceeding 5,000 for the first time, with 80% being domestic-to-domestic deals 5.

The second is the demand-side hypothesis. In an August 2025 paper, General Catalyst argued that the US service industry, despite being a market of over $6 trillion annually, has been left behind in softwareization, and, taking the stance that "customers buy outcomes, not products," announced a strategy of providing capital to founding teams that build vertically specialized AI to acquire and operate service companies in that industry. The target it sets is "Rule of 60," meaning simultaneously achieving 10-20% growth and 30-40% profit margins 6. Long Lake, supported by the firm, claims a 25-30% productivity improvement in condominium association management, and after acquiring more than 30 companies in three years, announced in May 2026 that it would take corporate travel management company Amex GBT private for $6.3 billion 7. Thrive Holdings has bundled more than 50 accounting firms into "Current" and roughly 20 IT maintenance companies into "Shield," and explains that its tax filing AI processed more than 7,000 filings with 98% accuracy, reducing preparation time by 30% 8.

The third is the structure of capital. Roll-ups of the past often relied on repeated acquisitions using stock as currency with a listing in mind, and stalled when share prices fell. Today, the mainstream approach is to first accumulate substantial equity and then layer on borrowings to create investment capacity. Hinoki has announced that it will combine ¥3.1 billion of equity with borrowings to create investment capacity on the scale of ¥50-100 billion, and will inherit 20 companies with EBITDA of ¥100 million to ¥1 billion over ten years. It is also distinctive in adopting "permanent ownership" that does not presuppose a sale 9. FUNDiT has raised a cumulative ¥6.2 billion including debt from financial institutions, and has acquired more than 100 small and midsize IT businesses in the advertising and sales promotion space 10. Prosel Holdings, born out of a technical college, has taken over machine shops with the stated goal of using AI to cut design and estimation processes from one week to about one hour, and in 2024 made a Niigata ironworks a subsidiary 11.

Half of companies lack successors, a hypothesis that AI can raise margins, and the stacking of equity and debt. With these three in place, roll-ups became a "trend."

Still, why roll-ups don't succeed that well

Having all the reasons for a trend in place is a different matter from succeeding.

HBR's 2008 article "Seven Ways to Fail Big" analyzed 750 major business failures over the past 25 years and counted roll-ups as one of the seven failure strategies. In the research cited, more than two-thirds of roll-ups did not create value for investors 1. In a survey presented in the SME Agency's SME PMI Guidelines, 24% of companies said post-M&A satisfaction fell short of expectations, with reasons including "synergies did not materialize" at 44.7%, "the target's management and organizational structure was weak" at 36.8%, "the target's employees were dissatisfied" at 28.9%, "the acquisition price was too high" at 23.7%, and "integrating corporate culture and organizational climate was difficult" at 22.8% 2. The same is true of US search funds. According to Stanford University's 2026 study, the aggregate IRR of 862 funds was 33.9% and ROI was 4.75x, both high, but only 58% completed an acquisition, and returns are concentrated in the top funds 12. Even when the average looks good, the median picture is different.

Why does this happen? I believe there are four structural causes.

The first is relying solely on the multiple differential. If you buy a small company at 3x EBITDA and the bundled company is valued at 8x, value is created without doing anything. This is the primal appeal of roll-ups, but the exit multiple is not something you can decide yourself. It disappears with changes such as rising interest rates, a cooling public market, or a lack of buyers. The multiple differential is a design in which, the moment it vanishes, only the debt remains.

The second is that integration costs grow in proportion to the number of deals. What the HBR article points out is simple arithmetic: integrating five companies takes nearly five times as much work as integrating a single company five times the size 1. Accounting systems, payroll structures, and customer contracts all differ from company to company. By the time you buy the tenth company, the integration of the first may still not be finished.

The third issue is that AI-driven margin improvement is still a hypothesis. The figures from General Catalyst and Thrive Holdings are the numbers they themselves have published as their own success stories. Whether those results can be replicated across industries has not yet been proven by anyone. If you pay a high acquisition price by baking in the expected AI effects, the buyer bears the entire loss when those effects fail to materialize.

The fourth issue is that acquisitions become the goal. From the moment funds are raised, finding "the next deal" becomes the top priority. A growing number of deals looks like a track record and makes reporting to investors easier. Integration, by contrast, is unglamorous and hard to report on. This asymmetry causes integration to be postponed. The fact that the SME PMI Guidelines open by stating that "the completion of an M&A deal is merely the starting line" shows that the government is aware of this asymmetry too 2.

The companies that lose at roll-ups are not the ones that are bad at acquiring. They are the ones that are too good at acquiring, so that integration cannot keep up.

How to actually execute PMI in old industries

So, after acquiring a company in an old industry, what should you do, and in what order? The SME Agency's SME PMI Guidelines are Japan's most systematic answer to this question. I will lay out the key points in my own words 2.

The timeline has three stages: "pre-PMI," where you gather information with PMI in mind from the M&A consideration stage onward; the "intensive implementation period" of roughly one year after the deal closes; and "post-PMI" thereafter. In particular, establishing a promotion structure, building trust with stakeholders, and assessing the current state are supposed to be carried out intensively, targeting the first 100 days after closing.

In the first 100 days, the most important thing is people. The Guidelines require that M&A information be communicated "promptly, to all employees of the transferor side, simultaneously, equally, and accurately," and that beforehand, key persons with significant influence over operations and other employees be informed ahead of time and consulted throughout the process. It also states that even when changes or improvements are needed, one should "not deny the transferor-side employees' existing work or methods, and respect the counterpart." Among the failure cases cited is one where, immediately after the M&A, the acquirer introduced its own "common sense" practices one after another, and as a result failed to win cooperation and even had difficulty operating the business.

What I would add is the order in which to introduce AI. Most startups pursuing roll-ups assume they will raise margins by bringing AI into their acquisitions. However, the profits of companies in old industries usually depend on one or two master craftsmen and a handful of long-standing customers. The judgment in those craftsmen's heads is written down nowhere. If you suddenly impose your own AI tools here, you will reproduce the Guidelines' failure case exactly. The right order is, first, to externalize the tacit knowledge on the shop floor and turn it into data: how drawings are read, how the basis for estimates is set, how exceptions are judged. Only after making that readable by AI can you talk about automation. I believe Prossell Holdings' choice of "design and estimating" as its first target is the correct order 11. This "AI-Ready-ification of shop-floor knowledge" is the same story as Japan's winning strategy that I wrote about in my article on physical AI [2].

For that, the people responsible for integration need to sit next to the shop floor. Not people who write integration plans in conference rooms, but people who visit the acquired company's office several days a week, watch craftsmen prepare estimates at their side, and feed the material to AI on the spot to gauge its reactions. This is the working style we call the FDE style; I wrote about it in detail in "What is an FDE?" [1]. Roll-up PMI is decided not by the quality of the integration plan, but by the number of people sitting on the shop floor.

The meaning of pursuing this as business succession

If a roll-up is treated purely as "buy cheap, sell high," it falls straight into the four structural causes of failure I described. I believe the real meaning of doing this in Japan lies on the business succession side.

Let me line up those numbers again. Of the 67,210 companies that suspended operations or dissolved in 2025, 52.8% were profitable, and 34.0% had representatives aged 80 or older 4. They have profits, technology, and customers, yet they disappear because no one is there to take over. What disappears is not just the company. The supply networks of the customers who ordered parts from it, the local employment, and the craftsmen's skills all disappear together. Roll-ups are, for now, one of the few mechanisms that can stop this "profitable closure" phenomenon.

The meaning for the selling owner is different, too. A fund's holding period is typically five to seven years, after which the company will be sold again. Hinoki's explicit commitment to "permanent ownership," with no fund-style exit assumed, is an answer to the question sellers care about most: "what will happen to my company and employees afterward?" 9. I believe this is precisely where Japanese succession-type roll-ups should differ from American AI roll-ups. If you design the structure so that it works even if the multiple gap disappears, meaning that repayments and growth are driven solely by the acquired company's cash flow and integration-driven improvements, you no longer depend on an exit.

Policy is also providing support. The SME Agency continues to run public applications for the "Business Succession and M&A Subsidy," and in September 2026 the fifteenth round of adoptions and the sixteenth round of application guidelines were released 13. The SME M&A Guidelines reached their third edition in August 2024, and the M&A support organization registration system is taking shape 14. If you design a roll-up in the context of business succession, you can use both the funding and the institutional frameworks.

How to divide debt and equity, and how to raise funds

Now for the money. Roll-up funding comes in two types, equity and debt, and the principle for dividing them is simple: whatever can be repaid from the stable cash flow the acquired company generates is debt; investments that do not yet generate cash are equity.

Debt should cover the portion of the purchase price that the target company's operating cash flow over the past several years can repay even in the worst case. In Japan, the Japan Finance Corporation's "Business Succession, Consolidation, and Revitalization Support Fund" is available. Direct loans for SME businesses go up to 1.44 billion yen, with repayment periods of up to 20 years for equipment funding and up to 10 years for working capital, each with a grace period of up to 5 years. If the funds are used for shares and the like (including goodwill) and no collateral is taken, there is an interest rate reduction measure, and the scheme can be combined with the special exemption from personal guarantees by business owners 15. For the small business category, there is a separate limit of 72 million yen. It is common to combine this with syndicated loans from private financial institutions, seller notes where the seller receives part of the consideration deferred, and earn-outs where additional payments are made based on performance.

Equity should cover integration, AI adoption, and people. The work of turning the acquired company's shop-floor knowledge into data, shared accounting and order-processing infrastructure, and hiring the people who will drive integration. None of these generate cash for one to two years. If you fund them with debt, repayments come first and integration stalls. I read Hinoki's decision to first raise 3.1 billion yen in equity before layering on borrowings as an effort to preserve this order 9.

How funds are raised splits into two approaches: raising money deal by deal, or raising it upfront as a platform. Raising deal by deal makes the first company easy to fund, but you have to re-persuade investors for every subsequent acquisition, and deals fall through in the meantime. Raising upfront means you must explain to investors "by what criteria you will buy how many companies of what kind" before any deal exists. Whether you have criteria you can articulate at this stage, in fact, largely determines whether a roll-up succeeds or fails.

Let me write down my benchmarks. Keep debt as a share of the purchase price at a level where repayment still works even if the target's operating cash flow drops 30%. Secure integration costs separately from the purchase price, in equity, at 10-20% of the price, in advance. And do not sign the final agreement for the second company until the first integration has stabilized beyond 100 days. These may look like unattainable benchmarks, but most roll-ups that failed collapsed precisely when they crossed one of these three lines.

Acquisition decision criteria and troubles that commonly arise after acquisition

I believe five acquisition criteria that can be explained to investors are sufficient.

CriterionQuestionExample that fails
PurposeCan you state in one sentence what changes in the group through this acquisition?"It was cheap" or "The deal came along"
DependenceWhich customers, and whose skills, do the profits depend on?The top three customers account for 70% of revenue, and a single craftsman prepares all the estimates
ReplicabilityIs there evidence, already replicated at other companies, that your own AI or systems can raise this company's profit margin?Applying the numbers from the first company to a second company in a different industry
RepaymentDoes debt repayment work under worst-case cash flow?The structure assumes acquisition-time profits continue as they are
Integration peopleIs a specific person, by name, assigned to sit on-site at this company several days a week?The plan can only say "handled by the integration team"

I will not proceed with any deal that fails even one of the five, even at a lower price. The SME Agency survey found that 23.7% cited "the acquisition price was too high," which I read as the result of deciding on price rather than on criteria 2.

Post-acquisition troubles follow set patterns. The third edition of the SME M&A Guidelines explicitly notes that troubles involving breach of matters stipulated in the final agreement are occurring, and in particular points to the existence of buyers who fail to take on the seller's owner guarantee (keiei hosho) as assumed, asking intermediaries to take action to exclude inappropriate buyers from the market 14. In other words, a startup acting as the buyer itself risks being seen as one of these "inappropriate buyers" without intending to be. The handling of the owner guarantee must be agreed with the financial institution before the final agreement and put in writing.

Other typical patterns are as follows. Skipping due diligence and assuming off-balance-sheet and contingent liabilities. Share transfers are procedurally simple, but since the legal entity carries over as is, this kind of risk is higher 2. Key people learning the information late and quitting the month after closing. Paying the seller's owner a retirement allowance upfront with the intention of keeping them on as an advisor, only for the retirement to be denied for tax purposes. Imposing all of the buyer's accounting and approval rules from day one, bringing operations to a halt. Greeting major clients too late, and losing them to competitors. All of these are failure patterns described in the Guidelines, and all can be prevented through the design of the "first 100 days."

Pre-execution checklist

Finally, here is a checklist to fill in yourselves before signing the final acquisition agreement. If three or more of the 20 items cannot be filled in, delaying the deal by one month is the cheaper option.

AreaItem
Purpose1. Written in one sentence what changes in the group through this acquisition
Purpose2. Defined numerically what "success looks like one year from now"
Target3. Confirmed the top five customers by revenue and the renewal conditions of their contracts
Target4. Identified by name the craftspeople and staff who drive profits, and discussed retention conditions with them directly
Target5. Conducted financial and legal due diligence and confirmed the presence or absence of off-balance-sheet and contingent liabilities
Target6. Agreed with the financial institution on the handling of the seller's owner guarantee and put it in writing
Target7. Written the reasons for choosing a share transfer or a business transfer from the perspectives of risk isolation and licenses/permits
Price8. Built the price rationale on a base case that excludes any AI effects
Price9. Confirmed that debt repayment works in a scenario where operating cash flow drops 30%
Price10. Estimated integration costs separately from the purchase price and secured them in equity
Funding11. Can explain to investors the debt-to-equity ratio and the reasons for it
Funding12. Confirmed that seller note and earn-out terms do not conflict with the seller's retention
Integration13. Named, by name, the person responsible for integration who will sit on-site several days a week
Integration14. Set the schedule for advance disclosure to key people and simultaneous disclosure to all employees
Integration15. Written down what will and will not be done in the first 100 days
Integration16. Separated the buyer's rules into those to impose on day one and those to hold off on for a year
Integration17. Decided the order of greetings to major clients and who will handle them

Conclusion

The reasons roll-ups are trending are that three things have come together: a supply side marked by a 50.1% lack-of-successor rate and profitable business closures, the hypothesis that AI can raise profit margins, and a capital structure that layers debt on top of equity. Yet more than two-thirds still fail to create value because four factors actually intensify amid the trend: reliance on multiple arbitrage, integration costs that grow in proportion to deal count, unverified AI effects, and the asymmetry whereby acquisition itself becomes the goal.

The answer to pulling it off is unglamorous. Define the closing of the M&A as the starting line, spend the first 100 days on people and trust, do not import the buyer's assumptions as given, and introduce AI only after turning the field's tacit knowledge into data. Keep debt within what worst-case cash flow can repay, cover integration and people with equity, and do not sign the second company until the first has stabilized. The acquisition criteria are five: purpose, dependence, replicability, repayment, and integration people.

And the point of doing this in Japan is to preserve the more than 30,000 companies that vanish each year while still in the black, along with their skills and supply chains. If the design works even when the multiple gap disappears, you avoid depending on an exit and can promise the selling owner an "after."

Entrepreneurs planning a roll-up, or those at large companies considering M&A in existing industries as a new business, let's start together from the integration design of the first company. The WARP program covers turning on-site knowledge at acquired companies into a form AI can read, and developing the people who sit on the ground.
