Decomposition interview rubric: what employers describe

FDE Interview Prep · 2026-09-25 · 15 min read

Summary

This post investigates whether employers publish a scoring rubric for the decomposition interview. The answer is no: no company releases a scoring sheet, weights, or pass rates. What exists instead is Palantir's published advice on open-ended questions (deliver a working idea first, state trade-offs, think aloud), a Blind post by a self-described former employee describing what failure looks like, and prep-site checklists with no cited employer source. The author also traces an unsourced pass-rate figure circulating among prep sites and builds an eight-item practice checklist, demonstrated on a factory-downtime prompt.

Key points

  • No employer publishes a decomposition rubric, weights, or pass rates.
  • Palantir advises delivering a working idea first, then expanding it.
  • A Blind ex-employee lists failures: no questions, big assumptions, impractical solutions.
  • Prep-site scoring lists cite no employer rubric behind them.
  • The author offers an eight-item checklist with a worked example.

You have a on the calendar and you want the scoring sheet: the list the interviewer ticks, so you can practice against it instead of guessing. There isn’t a public one. This post belongs to our decomposition interview guide, and it answers the narrower question you searched: is there a rubric, and if not, what can you prepare against?

The short answer: no public rubric exists

No employer in our research publishes a decomposition interview rubric, a pass rate for the round or a weight for it. What does exist is thinner, but more useful than it looks:

  • Palantir publishes the behaviors it wants when you face an open-ended problem, in its own words.
  • A Blind user who says they used to work at Palantir describes what a bad performance looks like, which is a rubric turned inside out.
  • Prep sites publish lists of what interviewers score. They cite no employer rubric behind them.
  • A pass-rate figure circulates between prep pages, and none of them shows data behind it.

So the honest plan: turn the published behaviors into a checklist you own, and practice until each item shows up in what you say out loud.

What Palantir publishes about open-ended questions

Palantir’s careers site states that open-ended technical questions are a key part of its interview process. The page’s only interview-specific advice fits in two sentences: “When discussing an open-ended problem in an interview, articulate the alternatives and trade-offs, but don’t forget to be pragmatic enough to be able to arrive at some concrete approach. None of this is a scoring sheet: no levels, no points, no pass line. But it names behaviors you can rehearse, turned below into words you can say.

What former employees and candidates describe

Reports are weaker evidence than a published page: each is one person’s account. They still show the round from the other side of the table.

The clearest description is of failure. A Blind user with a Palantir tag, who says they formerly worked there, wrote in March 2025 that a bad decomp performance looks like this: not asking questions, making large assumptions without clarifying with the interviewer, misunderstanding the problem before jumping in, giving impractical solutions, and not expanding on or optimizing the original v0 solution. What prep sites say interviewers score, and what that rests on

One prep site, Aced (formerly Exponent), lists in its FDE guide what it says interviewers score: clarifying before solving; identifying missing data, stakeholders and success metrics; decomposing and sequencing by risk and value; a thin walking-skeleton MVP; surfacing failure modes; and continuous communication. Source 14Forward Deployed Engineer Interview: The Definitive 2026 Guide (FDE)PublisherAced (formerly Exponent)Source typeinterview prep site The prep site cites no employer rubric for that list. Source 14Forward Deployed Engineer Interview: The Definitive 2026 Guide (FDE)PublisherAced (formerly Exponent)Source typeinterview prep site

It is a sensible practice list, and much of it lines up with Palantir’s published advice. But no employer wrote it, and nothing in it says how any company weighs one item against another.

Here is a quick test for any “what interviewers score” page:

  • Does it link to the employer’s own page? If it does, read that page and use its words.
  • Does it say who wrote the scoring list? “Interviewers look for” with no source is the site’s opinion.
  • Does it give numbers: points, weights, a pass line? If no employer publishes them, someone made them up.

Our checklist, and why it is built this way

With no public rubric, you need your own. This is ours, not what interviewers score. We built it so each published behavior shows up in words an interviewer can hear, and added a few items that make those behaviors easier to show.

Our decomposition checklist

  • Ask what the problem costs. Before proposing anything, ask what “better” means, for whom, and by when.
  • Find the decider, the user and the number. Who signs off, who opens the thing every day, and which number moves if it works.
  • Look at the inputs before trusting them. What data exists, who owns it, how fresh it is, and what is wrong with it.
  • Say two ways in, and pick one. Name the option you rejected and why.
  • Build a functioning first version. The thinnest thing one real user could use soon.
  • Say what comes next. What the next version adds, and what would trigger it.
  • Say how it could mislead. How the first version could give its user a wrong answer, and how you would find out in week one.
  • Narrate the handoffs. Say which step you are on, and move between steps in one sentence.

Why this shape? “Deliver a functioning idea first” is easy to agree with and easy to forget when you are nervous. “The first version is one report, for one person” is a sentence you can practice until it comes out on its own.

Using the checklist on a practice prompt

Take the factory downtime question: a manufacturer says unplanned downtime is killing its margins, and it has sensor data nobody uses. The tempting answer is a model on the sensor data; the checklist stops you taking it.

The weak opening. “I’d build a predictive maintenance model on the sensor data. Let me sketch the pipeline.” That skips the first three items at once. It is also the shape of the bad performance described on Blind: a large assumption, made before clarifying.

The checklist opening, in words you could say:

“Before the sensors: which line loses the most margin to unplanned stops, and is that from frequent stops or long repairs? Sensors mostly attack the first. If it’s long repairs, spares and technician response time usually matter more.”“The plant manager owns the margin target. The maintenance planner is my user, because an alert only helps if it becomes a scheduled job. The controls engineer owns the plant network, so I’d read from the historian, not the machines.”“Inputs: historian tags, downtime events and work orders. My first question is whether tags map to physical assets. ‘Nobody uses it’ often means that map is missing.”“Two ways in: a model across the plant, or one rule on one line. I’d start with the rule. We don’t yet know whether the work orders record failures well enough to label them, and a rule is something the planner can argue with.”“The first version is a morning report for the planner: assets on one line whose readings crossed a threshold, with their recent work orders.”“Once the planner acts on it, the next version adds a second failure mode, or a model once we have enough labels to test it.”“It fails if the threshold fires just as often on healthy machines as it does before a breakdown. I’d replay last year’s readings against the work orders to check, before the planner sees a single alert.”

Each quote maps to one item, in order. Then run the same checklist on the city bus reliability question, where the data is unknown, and on the fitness-app feature question, where you get a table and must scope to something you could ship.

Mistakes the checklist catches

  • Solving the stated ask. The customer names a tool; you build it. Fix: ask what the tool is for before you agree to it.
  • Endless clarifying. You ask questions until time runs out. Fix: after a few answers, say your assumption out loud and commit.

Score your own practice run (our scale, not an interviewer’s)

To grade a practice run, you need to hear the difference between missing an item and nailing it. Here is our scale, drawn from the factory example; no employer publishes one.

Missing sounds like Strong sounds like
Cost: “I’d build a model on the sensors.” “Which line loses the most margin, and is it frequent stops or long repairs?”
People: “The business wants less downtime.” “The planner is my user; the plant manager owns the target.”
Inputs: “We’ll use the sensor data.” “Do the tags map to physical assets?”
Two ways in: One plan, no alternative. “Plant-wide model or one rule on one line. I’d start with the rule.”
First version: “Phase one is the data platform.” “A morning report for one planner on one line.”
Next: Silence after v0. “Once the planner acts on it, we add a second failure mode.”
Mislead: “Then we deploy it.” “I’d replay last year’s readings against the work orders first.”
Narrate: Long silences, then a finished diagram. “That’s the user settled. Now the data.”

After each practice run, mark every item missing, partial or strong, and work on your weakest items first.
