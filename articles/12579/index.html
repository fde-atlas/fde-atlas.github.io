Can Chinese enterprise software still be saved?

Original title: 中国企业软件，还有救吗？

53AI · 2026-11-03 · 14 min read

Summary

The author, founder of the Cuiniuhui (崔牛会) community, reviews how AI disrupted China's enterprise software and SaaS sector in 2025. Wall Street's early-year software selloff was dubbed the “SaaSpocalypse,” and domestically vendors face a “death cross”: new AI revenue is not yet at scale while legacy per-seat revenue declines rapidly, as one top vendor learned after clients began demanding only AI. The article argues customer demand is shifting from tools to outcomes, software is becoming capabilities invoked through AI entry points, and the FDE model in China risks sliding back into low-margin project work unlike Palantir's deep, profitable client ties. His conclusion: enterprise software has a future, but vendors must transform into AI-native companies early and re-prove their value; over 80% of surveyed founders plan to keep old business as cash cows while pivoting fast.

Key points

  • SaaS vendors face a “death cross” as seat-based revenue falls faster than AI revenue grows.
  • Customers now buy outcomes and completed work, not tools and features.
  • AI entry points turn software into pluggable capabilities competing on quality and cost.
  • China's FDE hype risks repeating project-based traps due to thin margins and weak client commitment.
  • Most founders keep legacy business for cash flow while racing toward AI-native transformation.

Full text

Earlier this year, Wall Street coined a rather scary name for the software industry: SaaSpocalypse, the end of SaaS.

Why am I writing such an article only now, near the end of the year? First, I wanted to "let the bullet fly a little longer"; the other reason was to see whether SaaS vendors had come up with any solutions.

In March this year, I renamed our original "China SaaS" conference to the "China Enterprise AI Conference," which caused quite a stir in the industry. Some said that CuiNiuHui, which had always been a staunch supporter of SaaS, was now talking about AI, while others saw this change as "the end of the SaaS era and the beginning of the enterprise AI era." Later, I made many videos about this as well. In the face of the wheels of the times, AI is rolling over everything at an unprecedented speed.

In early April, CuiNiuHui launched a series of national enterprise AI commercialization conferences—eight in total—hoping to find some ToB vendors already ahead in AI commercialization and hear how they were actually doing it. Looking back now, although their AI commercialization strategies were transitional, they still offered considerable inspiration to everyone. One point I repeatedly made at that series of events was: SaaS vendors are facing the challenge of a "death cross," and many vendors may be eliminated because they cannot make it through this cycle.

SaaS companies face the challenge of the "death cross"

Under this view, I received quite a few "challenges" from practitioners, because they believed it wouldn't happen that fast. But in fact, revenue from the new AI offerings has not yet scaled, while the old seat-based revenue business is declining rapidly. By the second half of this year, when looking at this "death cross" again, more than one founder had already felt it deeply.

Among them, a founder of a leading company in our industry shared a story with me. He said: "At the beginning of this year, I made a wrong judgment. I thought AI wouldn't come fast, so I figured I could use this window to sell SaaS products aggressively—sell as long as I could and make this wave of money first. So we added some sales staff at the start of the year. In terms of results, the first quarter looked good, but then problems emerged. Because in the second quarter WorkBuddy came out, and in the third quarter WorkBuddy Enterprise came out. Under the massive investment of the big tech companies, customers' mindsets were changing rapidly. Now, they only want AI."

This founder's helplessness, I believe, is just a microcosm of most entrepreneurs. Yet the "death cross" phenomenon has only just begun, which is the most despairing part...

Half a month ago, I recorded a video about the extent of SaaS vendors' revenue decline this year, hoping to remind everyone to quickly make adjustments and countermeasures. This drew attacks from a group of people saying I was selling anxiety, and later I recorded another video explaining the matter.

Back to the "end of SaaS" narrative at the beginning: it spread at the time along with the selloff of "software stocks." Throughout this year, China's enterprise software industry has grown increasingly anxious with every advance and iteration of AI. I have interviewed so many enterprise software founders this year that I know their predicament well.

With AI now able to write code and handle work in legal, finance, and sales, who will software that charges annually or per seat be sold to in the future? Cailianshe even published an article, "Is SaaS Dead? Wall Street Sees a Massive Software Stock Crash as Fears of AI 'Stealing Jobs' Reach Their Peak," documenting this round of anxiety. Connecting the AI hot topics of this year—OpenClaw, FDE, Harness, REA (AI binary reverse engineering)—there has been a new hot topic almost every two months, and behind each hot topic comes a new wave of anxiety. For SaaS and enterprise software, they are like one "death warrant" after another.

It's as if you can always hear cries and echoes: "SaaS, are you still there? Are you still there?..."

"An egg broken from the outside is surely people's food; if broken from the inside, it is surely new life. For Haier, what is needed is to break from within—that is, self-breakthrough, self-disruption, self-challenge." Zhang Ruimin said this at the China Innovation Development Forum in 2014, right at the time when the internet era was transitioning to the mobile internet era. Since the transition was inevitable, nostalgia lost its meaning.

Development is getting easier, and customers can do it themselves; AI can do work in place of people, so fewer software accounts need to be sold; the entry point for work has fallen into AI's hands, and application vendors' bargaining power is rapidly weakening. At the beginning of the year, some financial institutions' analyses did indeed list weakened moats, pressured seat growth, and declining pricing power as the market's main concerns.

Yes, all of these issues deserve serious discussion.

But extrapolating from here all the way to "enterprise software and SaaS have no future" still seems somewhat hasty to me. Perhaps someone will step up again and mock me as a "royalist."

Having tracked enterprise services for so many years, I know very well how hard this business is. Everyone says enterprise services are a slow business, because the old problems of Chinese enterprise software (heavy customization, heavy delivery, hard to make money) have not disappeared with the changing times. Some companies have struggled for many years: revenue grew, but headcount grew along with it, and in the final tally, per-capita efficiency didn't improve at all. One project gets done, and the next one has to start all over again.

With AI here, will these problems disappear automatically? Of course, we can't just tell ourselves "enterprises will always need software" for comfort. Once I saw a comment on WeChat Moments that read: "SaaS telling the AI story now is like drinking to embolden yourself while walking a dark road at night." Is this view wrong? Not entirely. But do I agree with it? Something still feels off.

We need to look one level deeper: the work that enterprises need—who will do it in the future, agents or humans? Why would customers be willing to pay? If this company takes that money, can it both get the job done well and retain a reasonable profit?

This is the keyword I want to discuss today—position. Will enterprise software and SaaS still have a place in the future? And where?

01

What impressed me most about that founder's story earlier was the final line: "Now they only want AI." Compared with the SaaS era, one of the biggest advantages of the AI era is that customers have been fully educated—in other words, the bosses' minds have been "washed." But the negative effects follow immediately: a boss's understanding of AI is like an Agent with severely missing context. The AI in the boss's mind is omnipotent, while the AI in implementation appears clumsy and inadequate. Customers' enthusiasm for AI has been fully stirred up, but their understanding of the conditions needed for implementation may not keep up.

As the wave of aggressive advertising from the "WorkBuddies" crashes down, it is foreseeable that enterprise bosses' nerves will be stirred up again. On one side, customers are enthusiastic about AI; on the other, SaaS is lost and melancholy. Clearly, the phrase "now they only want AI" makes salespeople uncomfortable: the product still works fine, yet customers no longer want to learn about it. But ask further: exactly which task do customers want to hand over to AI? What do they want it to do?

Take sales management as an example. In the past, customers bought CRM to have salespeople fill in records and follow up on opportunities, and to let managers view reports. If customers now want AI to organize communication records, assess opportunities, remind about follow-ups, and even complete part of the customer communication, then what customers compare when purchasing products has changed. Previously they looked at features, usability, and reports; now they also look at how much work the AI can take over, how well it does it, and how much human effort is still required.

If a software vendor's product still mainly asks customers to enter and query data, with a chat box added on top, it will be hard to meet customers' expectations for AI. In the past, customers bought a tool; in the future, they will be willing to buy the capability to complete part of the work, and may even pay based on results.

Who provides this capability needs to be re-competed for—it doesn't just disappear. The original CRM vendors can do it, as can model companies, platforms, and new startups; the digital technology subsidiaries of central state-owned enterprises can do it, and even customers with R&D capabilities may do it themselves.

So the question is not whether the CRM category will disappear, but rather when customers' needs have shifted from a tool mindset to a results mindset, who can meet those needs, deliver better services, and earn revenue from it.

Therefore, the position of enterprise software must be rediscovered along the lines of customer needs, not fantasized about amid anxiety.

02

I'm writing this article right after the 2026 Tencent Ecosystem Conference ended. Although it was called the Tencent Ecosystem Conference, it felt more like a WorkBuddy ecosystem conference.

Going back to the story I told earlier: customers are being influenced and penetrated by the "WorkBuddies," so why do they say they "no longer need SaaS"? It's not really that they don't need SaaS—it's that bosses have never had a real concept of software, yet they've been wowed by the "WorkBuddies" writing copy, querying data, and a whole series of impressive feats.

Another change that deserves attention: people may increasingly stop opening a piece of software directly, and instead invoke its capabilities through AI. Put bluntly, "software does the hardest work, but gets no credit for it." Software is like an honest, hardworking kid—it does the work, but the AI platform steals the spotlight. There's no way around it; the platform is the entry point.

A while ago I recorded a long video that breaks down in detail how the entry point of enterprise software has changed. Chinese enterprise software has gone through the feature entry point, the portal entry point, and the platform entry point, and is now entering the AI entry point stage. During the platform entry point era of "Ding-Fei-Qi" (DingTalk, Feishu, WeCom), there was prolonged tug-of-war between the platforms and SaaS vendors, affecting procurement and pricing, but the overall impact was limited. In the AI entry point era, however, SaaS and software become capabilities and plugins. The earlier you connect your capabilities into the new workflows, the better your chances of being part of customers' re-selection. But whether the platform will recommend you, and whether customers will keep using you, still has to be proven through capability and operations.

Let's look at an example: contract review. AI can suggest revisions, but to actually deploy it inside an enterprise, it must incorporate the company's contract templates, risk preferences, historical agreements, and approval rules. Some suggestions can be adopted automatically, some need to go to legal for confirmation, and after revision they still have to connect with downstream processes.

General-purpose models will keep getting stronger, and these steps will keep getting automated. The position software vendors should fight for is whether they can organize this entire stretch of work more reliably and more economically.

In one of our closed-door discussions, the head of a contract services company mentioned a direction: deepen professional capabilities so that other general-purpose or enterprise-built agents can call on them, while continuing to explore combining their existing products with AI. I think this exploration is quite representative. Customers can access your capabilities through someone else's entry point, and you may still be valuable in that flow.

It's worth noting that being called upon doesn't mean your position is secure. Do you have advantages in quality and cost? How high is the switching cost for others to replace you? Is the platform willing to pay for this capability, and do customers know the difference? All of this still needs to be proven. I've made this point before: in the AI era, software vendors need to re-prove their value.

There's another reality that can't be ignored: after customers build it themselves, who is responsible for maintenance, upgrades, permissions, and exceptions? From this perspective, if professional vendors can spread common work across more customers and lower customers' total cost of long-term use, there is still a reason for them to be bought.

03

Some might say: since features are easily replaced, go deeper into the business and build your position on services and outcomes.

In September we organized an FDE practitioners' conference, inviting seven FDE practitioners to review their cases and playbooks. The conclusion: this direction is worth pursuing, but you must avoid falling into the next trap: the position gets deeper and deeper, people get more and more exhausted, and in the end you're back on the old project-services road.

Take Rolling AI's scheduling case, which frames the problem very concretely. A scheduling plan affects employee hours, income, and store managers' execution burden. After computing a plan, you still have to handle performance, compensation, and collaboration before it can truly be adopted. There's real value here, and real investment too. Which part of it customers are willing to pay for, and how much of it vendors can shoulder, needs to be spelled out together.

Here's another FDE example: Juzi Interactive. Their pricing experiments have also been about constantly adjusting this boundary. In founder Li Jiarui's retrospective, revenue sharing based on GMV runs into variables they can't control—traffic, products, pricing; sharing based on labor savings involves the customer's operating data and calculations. Later, in applicable scenarios, they switched to charging by lead volume plus a base fee.

So being accountable for outcomes sounds great, but in practice you still have to untangle authority, attribution, acceptance criteria, and each side's investment.

And then we return to the old question we keep discussing: after finishing this one customer, what about the next? If every engagement requires people to re-understand, re-model, and re-coordinate, then AI may have only made this one customization a bit cheaper. Revenue goes up, but the repeated investment of manpower doesn't go down, and the scaling problem remains.

This is what I care about most when looking at FDE. It's also the so-called "aftereffect" of the market's overheated enthusiasm for FDE—right now everyone claims to be doing FDE. One person, one gun: fast to start, but what about afterward? A while ago, a vendor friend told me a story:

I'll leave this question to you too: if you were the customer, how would you choose? I think don't rush to look at the price—look at what each of the two quotes actually promises, and what work and responsibility each leaves to the customer.

Going deeper into the business is definitely the right path, but is FDE the way to solve it? Not necessarily.

Moreover, the FDE discussed in China is far from Palantir's. Palantir's customers provide sufficient profit margins, and Palantir likewise embeds itself in their core business, forming a mutually binding relationship. Ask yourself: which customer in China is willing to offer you a high enough profit margin while also forming a deeply binding relationship with you? Customers want to spend the least money and make vendors do the most work, ultimately ending in a lose-lose outcome.

This is something I've never been able to understand: if enterprises don't adjust this mindset, the problems unsolved in the traditional software era won't be solved in the SaaS era, nor in the AI era.

In this era, what capabilities software vendors need to accumulate is worth thinking about. Can rules discovered on-site make it into the product? Can recurring problems become evals and exception-handling methods? Can work that once required senior experts be handed next time to tools, systems, or trained teams? These accumulations also need to be actually invoked, validated, and maintained—storing them in a knowledge base is only the beginning.

In other words, the position a software vendor should strive for is one where capabilities keep getting stronger and organizational capability keeps growing, rather than relying solely on the founder and a few core employees to hold things up.

04

Having said all this, I want to return to the beginning. "Enterprise software has a future" and "whether a given company can survive long enough to reach that future" are two different things.

That's why I keep warning about the "death cross." The new business may have a direction, but the old business's cash flow is already being affected; new products, sales, and delivery capabilities all require investment, and companies may not have unlimited time to wait for them to mature.

At this point, every company's customers, products, and cash situation are different, so it's hard to offer a single unified transformation timetable.

Starting in April this year, I've been running an interesting closed-door group called "AI Decision·30," a year-long ongoing discussion that continuously tracks 20 SaaS companies, discussing their decision-making thinking and processes in the SaaS-to-AI transformation, as well as the changes happening inside the companies and among their external customers.

A couple of days ago, the founders in our group, together with their core executives, visited one of the companies "sprinting" through its AI transformation to learn from it. In that session, we felt even more deeply: at moments like this, the organization still comes first. Whether a company can quickly transform into an AI-native company—the founder must be the one who "takes the fall" for it; there's no one else to blame. The founder of that company said this: "I know that if we don't change, we will surely 'die.' So there's really only one path before us: change."

In a recent session of "AI Decision · 30," I ran a survey: in 2027, will you increase or decrease your investment? Over 80% of the bosses gave this answer: "Keep the old business as the cash cow to guarantee cash flow, and rapidly transform toward AI-native." This offers everyone a perspective on how to treat new versus old businesses.

"Customers are changing too fast, and AI is changing too fast—both are extremely uncertain for us. By comparison, I have control over my own company, so driving internal transformation offers more certainty." That's what one founder said. Of course, this doesn't mean he has given up on customers; rather, in terms of priorities and certainty, he chose to "secure the inside" before "conquering the outside."

Also, be wary of using the old high-cost sales and delivery system to support new products that are priced lower and justified by different purchasing logic. When the product changes but the operating methods don't, the new revenue may not fill the big hole left by declining old revenue.

Even a transitional state has its value. As long as it generates real revenue and leaves behind customer understanding and product capabilities that will be useful in the next stage, it's worth doing seriously.

Last week, I studied Professor Zeng Ming's latest "Looking at the Next Decade" in full. He also gave a fairly clear judgment on this round of AI development: he believes the first stage—when model capabilities become commercially usable—has passed; the next stage, where AI agents flourish in all directions, is coming; and only at the end will come the stage of the AI OS ecosystem.

At the same time, he noted that between the first and second stages there will be a transition period, in which everyone will keep experimenting and iterating to find new solutions. The accumulation during this stage is equally important.

Why do I run group discussions like "AI Decision · 30"? Because I want to accompany this group of software company founders in finding a path through the cycle. What I'd rather see is vendors delivering answers like these: why did this customer stay, why is this new piece of work worth buying, what repetition was avoided in the next delivery, and which line item of cost did the company improve as a result.

This helps us get through the period ahead far better than reciting AI mega-trends once again.

05

Writing up to this point and looking back at the "SaaS doomsday" talk from the start of the year, my position is actually clearer now.

Seats will come under pressure, some features will lose their pricing advantage, and some software vendors will fall behind—that's an inevitable outcome. As Professor Zeng Ming said, people go through birth, aging, sickness, and death, and companies have no reason to be immortal. But whether the industry has a future still depends on looking at the new supply, the new purchasing behavior, and software vendors' ability to adapt together.

Between "an old pricing model is under attack" and "professional software vendors can no longer sustainably create value and earn revenue," there is still a long stretch of reasoning that needs to be proven.

The jobs customers need to get done won't automatically preserve any company's business. Model companies, big tech firms, new startups, and customers themselves may all take a share of it. For the original software companies, experience and customer relationships can provide a starting point, but their future position still has to be fought for through product and operations—that is, as mentioned earlier, they need to prove themselves all over again.

Some will organize work at the front line, some will provide professional capabilities that can be invoked, some will go deep into industry sites, and others will serve a broader customer base through products and partners. Since the division of labor has already happened, which path can actually succeed depends on real choices and sustained willingness to pay.

Since customers' ways of working, reasons for buying, and modes of supply are all changing, we should of course move with the times, push the discussion forward, and look at how software vendors can turn these changes into business.

At the same time, the "death cross" I've warned about is meant to help everyone see earlier: what is changing in the old position, how to gain a foothold in the new one, and how much time and resources remain to complete this transition.

So, do Chinese enterprise software and SaaS still have a place? I believe they do. Companies that can project professional capabilities into the business, keep customers continuously gaining value, and at the same time build their own accumulation and retain profits, still have a chance to find their place. I don't agree with pronouncing "doomsday" on the entire industry. But the place won't come back on its own just because we disagree with the doomsday verdict.

I hope that the next time we hear the question "SaaS, are you still there?", what we respond with is growth rather than anxiety...
