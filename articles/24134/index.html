FDE Interview Question: How to Answer the Distyl Customer Scenario Round

Original title: FDE 面试题：Distyl 客户场景轮怎么答

FDE Times · 2026-11-09 · 5 min read

Summary

This guide explains the customer scenario round of Distyl AI's FDE interview process, which candidates most often underestimate despite it carrying the highest weight. Using a mock scenario where a compliance team halts a launch, the article identifies two common failure modes: pushing responsibility back to the customer or jumping straight into architecture. The recommended approach is to ask questions first—who made the decision, which data is blocked, what deadlines exist—then restate the problem, take ownership of concrete tasks like drafting documentation and scheduling reviews, and proactively raise risks and alternatives. A five-step practice framework is provided, along with common mistakes such as overpromising or blaming the customer's systems.

Key points

  • The customer scenario round carries the highest weight at Distyl but is often underestimated.
  • Scores depend on which questions you ask and in what order, since interviewers reveal nothing unprompted.
  • Taking ownership means owning the process, not immediately offering an architecture solution.
  • Five steps: define via questions, restate, own a concrete task, raise risks unprompted, agree on next steps.
  • Common mistakes: overpromising outcomes, blaming customer systems, and asking unordered questions.

Key Takeaways

  • Candidates often underestimate the customer scenario round, yet Distyl AI weights it more heavily than any other round.
  • Strong answers proactively take on the work of establishing a compliance review process, don't push the work back to the customer, and never blame the customer's systems.
  • Taking ownership doesn't mean giving an answer immediately: first define the problem through questions, then propose solutions.

Halfway through the onsite interview, the interviewer's tone changes. Now he's the customer's head of operations: "Our compliance team just halted sending data to the model. We're launching next week. What do you recommend?"

Many candidates fall back into one of two instinctive reactions. The first is pushing the work back to the customer: "This needs internal approval on your side; once it's approved we'll deploy right away." The second is diving straight into architecture: masking the data, running the model internally, adding a filtering layer. Either one can cost you the offer, for different reasons.

This article is about the third kind of answer: take ownership, but don't rush to a solution. After you join, you'll use this skill in every customer meeting; passing the interview is just its first application.

The Most Overlooked Round, Weighted the Highest

An article on techinterview.org analyzing Distyl AI's Forward Deployed Engineer (FDE) hiring process points out that the customer scenario round is the one candidates most often underestimate, and the one Distyl values most.

The same article also mentions something that should directly shape how you prepare: the interviewer won't volunteer details, and your score depends on which questions you ask and in what order.

FDE Academy's interview guide distills what the evaluator wants to learn during the simulated customer segment into one question: do you take ownership, or do you deflect it? The Distyl analysis adds that ownership is assessed in every round, including following problems all the way to production rather than stopping at "it works on my machine."

So don't prepare for this round as a system design question with a side of role-play. What it tests is: when a project stalls, will you step up and drive it forward.

Taking Ownership Doesn't Mean Having the Answer

Two pieces of advice seem to contradict each other. FDE Academy writes that for compliance-conflict questions, a good answer is to proactively take on the work of establishing a compliance review process rather than pushing responsibility back. The same source also warns: jumping straight to a solution is a guaranteed fail.

In fact the two don't conflict, because what you take on is the process, not yet the solution. You commit to leading the search for an approvable path, but you don't claim to know how to get there before you've clarified who halted it, which data was halted, and why.

The Distyl analysis says the same thing: strong candidates define the problem clearly before discussing solutions.

There's also a line you must not cross. FDE Academy advises never to blame the customer's systems, even if the problem really is there. "Your process is too slow" may be true, but it will alienate the person across the table and will likely cost you points with the evaluator.

A Script, Broken Down Line by Line

Back to the opening scenario. Below is a hypothetical practice script, not a real interview transcript. Note the order of the questions: first ask about people, then data, then timing.

Candidate: "Thank you for telling me early. Let me confirm: who at compliance made this decision? Did they give a clear reason?"

Customer: "Lan from Legal. She's worried about customer data leaving our systems."

Candidate: "Is it all data, or only certain fields? For example, phone numbers, account numbers?"

Customer: "Mainly personally identifiable information."

Candidate: "Who was next week's launch date promised to? Could we launch a portion first, or does it all have to go live?"

Only then does the candidate restate the problem and take on the work: "As I understand it, what's blocked is the identifying fields, not the whole project.

Here's my suggestion: today I'll write a data flow document explaining where each field goes, then set up 30 minutes with Lan to define the approval criteria together.

I'll own both the document and the meeting."

The last few sentences are where the real points are: "There are two risks I want to flag upfront. If we mask the identifying data, quality on some tasks may drop and will need re-running through evals. If Lan needs more time, I suggest launching with non-sensitive data first, so at least part of it is actually running next week."

Compared with the answer that pushes the work back, the difference isn't just in attitude—it shows in every sentence.

Pushing the Work Back to the Customer

  • "Tell me when it's approved"
  • "Your process is too slow"
  • Leading with an architecture proposal in the first sentence
  • Never clarifying who owns the next step

Taking Ownership

  • Asking who makes the decision, which data is blocked, and who the deadline was promised to
  • Restating the problem and asking the customer to confirm
  • Taking on drafting the document and scheduling the review meeting
  • Proactively raising risks, trade-offs, and alternatives

Practice the Five Steps

The first step is asking questions to define the problem. The interviewer playing the customer won't volunteer information, so prepare three sets of questions in advance: who makes the decision, what exactly is the scope, and what are the time constraints. Aced's guide for the ElevenLabs case study round likewise notes that evaluators want to see you do customer discovery before proposing any solution.

The second step is restating the problem in your own words, so the customer can correct you if you've misunderstood. The third step is taking on a specific piece of work with a clear owner and timeline, rather than a vague promise like "I'll help."

The fourth step is pointing out failure modes and trade-offs without being asked. According to Aced, in Anthropic's solution design round, candidates must define the problem themselves, propose failure modes themselves, and articulate trade-offs; the "problem ownership" score specifically measures whether you do this proactively.

The final step is agreeing on the next step toward production, because that's where ownership gets proven.

Throughout these five steps, keep your tone steady. According to Aced, Cognition's simulated customer calls are scored separately on whether your answers during the call are clear and calm.

Mistakes That Eliminate Candidates

The most common mistake is treating "taking ownership" as "having the answer." To seem proactive, candidates lead with an architecture proposal in their first sentence, falling right into the trap FDE Academy warned about. The correct order is: ask questions first, then discuss process, then give a solution.

The second mistake is over-promising: guaranteeing you'll convince Legal, or committing to launch on time at any cost. Take on what you can control—documents, meetings, and alternatives—not decisions that belong to others.

The third mistake is asking many questions with no order. Ten unrelated questions still don't show that you're defining the problem; the Distyl analysis explicitly notes that the order of your questions is scored too.

This skill should also show up on your resume. When a job description mentions "ownership" or "customer-facing," go through your experience and find a time a project stalled and you led the effort to build a process that moved it forward. That bullet point should state clearly: what you took on, what obstacles you cleared, and how the system reached production.

In this round, the offer usually doesn't go to the person with the best answer, but to the one who, fifteen minutes in, has convinced the customer they will see things through to the end.
