Summary

Written by Shohei Tokumitsu, CEO of DeFactory, this article explains "FDE Lab Development," a monthly-fee lab-style consulting model built on forward deployed engineer principles. Senior talent (senior SEs, PdMs, CTOs, tech leads) embeds at client sites and owns the full cycle from problem discovery through implementation to production launch, unlike SES (staffing), custom development (spec delivery), or IT consulting (proposals only). It maps 20 problem areas across 8 domains including product development, DX, tech organizations, EC, startups, security, and hiring. The model targets firms stuck at "PoC death" or over-dependent on vendors. Reported results: 11 deals in six months, average monthly rate of ¥1.35M (¥1.1M–1.8M), ~80% direct client transactions.

Key points

  • FDE Lab Development commits to business outcomes, not billable hours or deliverables.
  • It differs from SES, custom development, and IT consulting by covering discovery through production.
  • It covers 20 problem areas across 8 domains, including AI adoption and EC.
  • Best fit: companies with stalled PoCs or vendor over-dependence; poor fit for fixed-spec work.
  • DeFactory reports 11 deals averaging ¥1.35M/month over six months (n=11).

"We built a PoC but can't get it into production" or "We've left everything to vendors, but we don't know if the real problems are actually being solved"—companies facing these challenges are rapidly increasing in DX promotion and AI adoption. The root cause is that traditional "staffing-based SES" or "proposal-only consulting" cannot fully solve on-the-ground problems from start to finish.

In this article, drawing on my experience supporting the launch of multiple FDE organizations as a technical advisor, I explain the full picture of "FDE Lab Development," a new support model that solves these challenges. FDE Lab Development is a lab-style development and consulting service that embodies the philosophy of the FDE (Forward Deployed Engineer), embedding itself in the client's site and taking end-to-end responsibility from problem discovery through implementation and production deployment.

Its greatest feature is that instead of "providing man-hours" or "delivering proposals," it "creates working solutions together with the client."

Now that DX and generative AI initiatives frequently stall at the "PoC stage," attention is growing toward this model, which bridges the gap between proposal and implementation. Here, I explain its essence from three perspectives.

In conclusion, FDE Lab Development is a support model whose goal is not to provide labor (man-hours) but to solve the client's business problems themselves with technology (creating solutions).

Specifically, "creating solutions" in FDE Lab Development means the following.

  • Involvement from problem discovery: Rather than executing given specifications, defining the true problems together with the client
  • Responsibility through implementation: Not just proposals and design, but hands-on building of working solutions
  • Support through production deployment and adoption: Not stopping at PoC, but supporting until solutions are integrated into operations and deliver results
  • Commitment to outcomes: Measuring value not by hours worked, but by whether the client's business has moved forward

The biggest problem with traditional IT support is that "the person who proposes," "the person who builds," and "the person who uses" are separated. A consultant's vision is built by a different vendor, and the field can't use it, so it's abandoned—this structure was a breeding ground for DX failures. FDE Lab Development resolves this separation by having one person (or one team) carry it through end to end. For a detailed explanation of the definition and role of the FDE profession itself, see What is an FDE (Forward Deployed Engineer)? In conclusion, FDE Lab Development stands apart in that, unlike SES (man-hour provision), outsourced development (delivery per specifications), or IT consulting (proposals and management), it takes responsibility for "problem discovery through implementation and production deployment" on an outcome basis.

As a common on-the-ground failure example, in outsourced development, teams can be bound by "specifications fixed at the requirements definition stage," unable to pivot flexibly even when circumstances change during development, resulting in a system no one uses upon completion. FDE Lab Development's strength is that, like a laboratory, it repeats hypothesis testing and can flexibly reshape solutions in response to changing circumstances.

In conclusion, DeFactory's FDE Lab Development is a lab-style business model in which highly skilled professionals—senior SEs, PdMs, CTOs, and tech leads—work alongside clients on-site, supporting from problem discovery through implementation and production deployment under monthly contracts.

The features of DeFactory's lab-style consulting are as follows.

  • Support from highly skilled professionals: Participation by professionals who combine implementation skills with upstream design capabilities, such as senior SEs, PdMs, CTOs, and tech leads
  • Direct-transaction-centered structure: Based on direct transactions with end clients, with a transparent structure free of intermediary markups
  • Outcome-based support: Not merely providing man-hours, but committing to solving the client's business problems

Behind the viability of this model lies the scarcity of FDE talent. Professionals who "understand technology, understand business, and can get hands-on in the field" are extremely rare in the hiring market. By providing such highly skilled talent on a lab-type basis, DeFactory enables companies to leverage transformation-driving capabilities that they could not fully secure through full-time hiring, for exactly the period they need. In conclusion, FDE Lab Development is most effective for companies struggling with the disconnect between proposal and implementation, such as "we can build a PoC but can't take it to production" or "we can't break free from fully outsourcing to vendors." Conversely, it is not necessarily the best fit for simple development with fully finalized specifications. Here, we explain the criteria for determining which companies this model suits.

In conclusion, the biggest trap many companies fall into is "PoC death," the pattern where a proof of concept succeeds but the project stalls without ever reaching production operation. This is caused by a major gap between the PoC and production.

Typical patterns of companies that fall into "PoC death" are as follows.

  • Ambiguous objectives: Starting with "let's just try AI for now," with no criteria for production
  • Lack of implementation capability: Even if a PoC is built externally, the company lacks the technical capability to run it in production
  • Data preparation barriers: Production requires preparing large volumes of diverse data, but they can't keep up
  • Failure to integrate into operations: Even if the technology works, it cannot be embedded into frontline business workflows

As a common on-the-ground failure pattern, demos run impressively, but when it comes time to move to production, projects hit wall after wall—"the data is messy," "the field can't use it," "there's no operational framework"—and frequently fizzle out. FDE Lab Development bridges this gap by designing with production in mind from the PoC stage and accompanying clients all the way through implementation.

In conclusion, organizations unable to escape a state of dependency where everything is outsourced to vendors and no technology or know-how accumulates in-house are also a typical fit for FDE Lab Development. Full outsourcing may seem easy, but in the long run it erodes a company's competitiveness.

The biggest problem with full outsourcing is "losing the ability to make decisions in-house." FDE Lab Development emphasizes not just building and delivering, but also transferring know-how to clients through in-house development support (Area ⑧). It is this kind of accompaniment—"teaching how to fish rather than giving fish"—that leads to organizational independence.

In conclusion, FDE Lab Development suits companies that say "the problem is clear but we don't know how to solve it" or "we want flexibility to respond to change," while it does not suit companies seeking "large-volume orders of simple work with fully finalized specifications."

The value of FDE Lab development is maximized precisely for "highly uncertain challenges." For template-based development with fixed specifications, traditional contracted development may be more cost-efficient. Conversely, the more exploratory the challenge—such as "we don't know what to build" or "we won't know until we try"—the more powerful FDE Lab development becomes, as it can handle everything from problem discovery to implementation.
