What Is Shadow AI? Risks That Bans Can't Prevent and Practical Countermeasures That Work on the Ground

Original title: シャドーAIとは？禁止では防げないリスクと、現場で効く対策

FDE Consulting · 2026-10-06 · 10 min read

Summary

This article explains shadow AI—employees using AI tools without IT approval—and argues that bans alone fail because official tools are less convenient. It cites 2026 data: IBM reports shadow AI appeared in 43% of security incidents (up from 20%), with average breach costs of $5.39M; Verizon found 67% of AI users on company devices use personal accounts. Using Samsung's 2023 ChatGPT source-code leak as a case study, it outlines five risks including data leakage, browser extensions, and untraceable usage. The proposed remedy is a four-step approach: visualize usage, classify data into three tiers, build an official AI environment more convenient than personal accounts, and review monthly. The author positions FDE work as the way to build official AI tooling that field teams actually adopt.

Key points

  • Shadow AI incidents doubled from 20% to 43% in IBM's 2026 breach report.
  • Verizon found 67% of employees using AI on work devices use personal accounts.
  • Samsung banned generative AI after engineers leaked source code via ChatGPT in 2023.
  • Surveys show rules alone don't reduce unofficial AI use; convenience drives it.
  • The four steps are visualization, data classification, official environment, and monthly review.

Shadow AI is the use of AI tools by employees for work without formal approval or oversight from the IT department. Pasting internal documents into a personal-account generative AI, installing AI browser extensions the company doesn't know about, recording and summarizing meetings with AI. In every case, the person just wants to finish work faster and has no ill intent.

Even so, in 2026, shadow AI began to show up clearly in data breach statistics. IBM's "Cost of a Data Breach Report 2026" showed that the share of security incidents involving shadow AI doubled from 20% the previous year to 43%. In IPA (Information-technology Promotion Agency)'s "Information Security 10 Major Threats 2026", "cyber risks surrounding AI use" ranked third in the organizational category for the first time.

Here, we organize the definition of shadow AI, the 2026 figures, the risks and real-world examples, and then present countermeasures that don't rely on "banning" in four steps.

What is shadow AI?

IBM defines shadow AI as "the use of AI tools and applications by employees or end users without formal approval or oversight from the IT department."

Typical examples include the following uses.

  • Pasting internal emails, documents, and source code into a personal-account generative AI
  • Installing AI browser extensions (summarization, translation, writing) the company doesn't know about
  • Recording and summarizing meetings with AI meeting-notes services
  • Using AI features added later to SaaS already in use at work without checking the settings
  • Giving AI agents access rights to internal systems or email

How it differs from shadow IT

IBM explains the difference as follows: shadow IT focuses on unauthorized applications and services in general, while shadow AI focuses on AI-specific tools, platforms, and use cases. When thinking about countermeasures in the field, the following differences matter.

シャドーIT シャドーAI
ScopeUnauthorized applications and services in generalAI tools and AI features
Main riskData storage locations fall outside management controlIn addition to storage and processing of input data, errors in output creep into business decisions
DetectabilityFound through new service contracts or communicationsHard to find, hidden among AI features of existing SaaS, browser extensions, and personal accounts
Ease of startingDeployment takes effortFree and can be started in minutes

The "detectability" and "ease of starting" rows in the table are our own summary. Shadow AI spreads faster and more quietly than shadow IT.

Five risks of shadow AI

1. Leakage of input information

The biggest risk is confidential information being sent to services outside the company's control. According to Verizon's 2026 report, the most common type of data sent to generative AI was source code, and in 3.2% of DLP (data loss prevention) violations, research and technical documents were sent to unauthorized AI.

Once sent, the information cannot be retrieved by the company. As we discuss later, Samsung too, in a notice to employees, expressed concern that data sent to AI services is stored on external servers and is difficult to retrieve or delete.

2. Reading by browser extensions and AI agents

Some AI browser extensions collect and retain the content of pages being viewed in order to grasp context. Verizon's report points out that if such an extension is installed in a browser viewing internal sites, even non-public data could be siphoned off, and reports that at the average company, more than 15% of users have unauthorized AI extensions installed. When AI agents are given access to email or internal systems, the scope of impact expands even further.

3. No records are left

If AI is used in the company environment, records of who input what can be kept. With personal accounts, they are not. Even when a problem occurs, it is impossible to later investigate what was sent where.

4. Errors in output enter business processes

IPA's 10 Major Threats 2026 also includes, among the risks surrounding AI use, problems arising from uncritically accepting AI-processed or AI-generated results without sufficient verification. With unmanaged AI, it is not even known which business decisions AI output was used in.

5. Fines and reputation

In IBM's 2026 survey, about one in five breaches involving shadow AI led to regulatory fines. Entering personal information or information entrusted by business partners into external AI can violate laws, regulations, or contracts.

What actually happened: the Samsung case

In April 2023, engineers at Samsung Electronics uploaded internal source code to ChatGPT, sending it outside the company. The following month, the company banned the use of generative AI on company PCs, tablets, smartphones, and its internal network (Bloomberg, May 2, 2023).

The entry point for this leak was not an attacker, but an engineer who simply wanted to get work done faster. The risk of shadow AI lies not in being breached from the outside, but in data being carried out from the inside with good intentions.

Why bans and rules alone don't reduce it

Stopping use entirely, as Samsung did, is an easy-to-understand response. However, judging from surveys in Japan, rules alone do not reduce shadow AI.

  • According to a survey by Kadokawa ASCII Research Laboratories, even among companies with company-wide guidelines in place, 46.9% of people had experience using unofficial AI for work—barely different from the 50.0% at companies with only partial guidelines. Even at companies with full company-wide guidelines, 45.1% answered that "rules have not caught up with the reality on the ground."
  • In a survey by Ertes (survey of 300 company employees and civil servants), of the 78 people who said they had "reservations" about uploading non-public information, 64.1% had actually uploaded it.
  • In a survey by SIGNATE Lab, shadow AI users cited dissatisfaction with the insufficient functionality of the company's standard AI and the hassle of applying for approval.

People use it despite their concerns. They use it despite the rules. Because it is more convenient than the official route. We believe shadow AI should be treated not as "something to crack down on," but as "a signal showing which tasks on the ground need AI."

Four steps for shadow AI countermeasures: Make the official route the easiest one

StepWhat to doSpecificallyWhat you end up with
1. VisualizationGrasp who is using which AI and inputting whatAnonymous surveys, communication logs, inventory of browser extensions, checking AI feature settings in SaaS in useA list of AI usage and a diagram of data flows
2. ClassificationDecide what information may be inputThree categories—"public information," "internal information," and "confidential/personal information"—and which AI may be used for eachA one-page rule table
3. Official environmentProvide an official AI that the field will want to switch toCorporate contracts and API configuration checks, login with company accounts, logging, templates for frequently used tasksAn official AI environment and usage patterns for each task
4. OperationContinuously review rules and the environmentOnce a month, review usage logs and surveys to identify missing official features and new shadow AIMonthly review records

The order matters. If you start with step 2's rule-making and skip steps 1 and 3, then as the Kadokawa ASCII Research Laboratories figures show, unofficial AI will continue to be used even with rules in place.

1. Visualization: First ask, without blaming

Make the survey anonymous and design it so that using AI itself is not blamed. Ask four things: which AI is being used, for which tasks, what types of information are being input, and why the official AI is not being used. The answers to the last question will tell you what to build in step 3.

Communication logs and browser extension inventories are conducted to catch usage that doesn't surface in surveys. Announce to the whole company upfront that the goal is not to punish individuals but to grasp the company's overall data flows.

2. Classification: Show what may be used, not a list of prohibitions

A rule that only says "don't input confidential information" leaves the judgment of what is confidential to the field. List concrete examples for each of the three categories (customer names, quote amounts, source code, HR information, etc.) and write "for this category, you may input it into this AI." Rules that don't fit on one page won't be read on the ground.

3. Official environment: Make it more convenient than personal accounts

This is where you get the most impact. If the official AI is slower than personal accounts, has fewer features, or requires a hassle to get approved, the field won't come back. The reason 67% of users in Verizon's survey used personal accounts is probably because it was the easiest option.

The official environment should cover the following four points.

  • Confirm through contracts and settings that inputs are not used for AI training
  • Require login with company accounts so access can be cut off upon resignation or transfer
  • Keep records of inputs and outputs
  • Prepare templates for frequently used tasks such as meeting minutes, emails, document creation, and code reviews

4. Operation: Rules become outdated the day they are made

AI tools and features change every few months. Once a month, review usage logs and survey results to identify missing features in the official environment and newly emerging shadow AI. Update the rules at the same time.

Shadow AI countermeasures checklist

#Item to check
1There is a list of AI tools employees use for work
2Categories of information that may be input are defined with concrete examples
3There is an official AI environment (corporate contract/API)
4The official AI is configured not to use inputs for training
5The official AI requires login with company accounts and can be cut off upon resignation
6Usage records of the official AI are kept
7AI features added to SaaS in use have had their settings checked
8There are rules on whether AI browser extensions may be installed
9Permissions granted to AI agents are kept to the minimum necessary for each task
10Usage status and rules are reviewed at least once a month

If three or more items don't apply, start with step 1: visualization.

Building an "official environment that gets used" by embedding into the field

The success or failure of shadow AI countermeasures is decided in step 3. You can buy rules and monitoring tools, but an official AI environment that fits the field's actual work cannot be built without observing that work.

FDE (Forward Deployed Engineer), which we practice, is a way of working where engineers go into the field, break down the work, build AI mechanisms that are actually used, and keep improving until they take root. The "tasks where the field needs AI" identified through shadow AI visualization become, as they are, the candidates for what to build first in the official environment.
