KPMG Forward Deployed Engineer (FDE): In the AI Era, the Bottleneck Is Context

Original title: KPMG 前线部署工程师 FDE：AI 时代瓶颈在上下文

FDE Times · 2026-10-20 · 3 min read

Key Takeaways

  • Justin Johnsen, an FDE and Chief Technology Architect at KPMG, says the bottleneck today lies in gathering context and clarifying intent before development begins.
  • In his view, AI's performance depends on the context it is given. That's why FDEs must stay close to the customer's problems and guide customer teams in using AI.
  • KPMG's job posting still requires at least seven years of experience delivering production software end to end—the technical bar for this role has not been lowered.

When Justin Johnsen first joined KPMG as a software engineer, every line of code had to be written by hand. Today he is a Forward Deployed Engineer (FDE) and Chief Technology Architect at KPMG. In his view, the hardest part of the job is no longer at the keyboard.

In a first-person article written for Business Insider, Johnsen says the bottleneck used to be writing code; now it is gathering context and clarifying intent before development begins. Business Insider's Japanese edition, based on an interview with him, recorded the same view.

If you are an FDE, or aiming to become one, this shift changes how others evaluate you. On-site with a customer, your value no longer depends on how fast you write code, but on how deeply you understand the problem—so that the requirements you hand to AI contain no ambiguity it has to guess about.

AI's ceiling depends on the context you give it

Johnsen believes AI is extremely capable, but its performance depends entirely on the context it receives. That sounds obvious, yet its implications for FDEs are less intuitive. A model can write a module in minutes, but if it misreads the requirements, bad code arrives just as fast.

Suppose a customer asks you to "automate invoice reconciliation." Hand that sentence unchanged to a coding agent, and you'll get code that runs—but it may solve the wrong problem: What discrepancy threshold is acceptable? How are foreign-currency invoices handled? Who approves exceptions?

Those answers live in the heads of the customer's finance team, not in the code repository.

That's why, when Johnsen defines the FDE role, he emphasizes "seeing it through": FDEs work directly with customers and follow the problem all the way through. Business Insider's Japanese edition puts it more concretely: the journey starts with understanding business requirements and continues until a truly usable solution is delivered.

But don't read this as "lower technical requirements"

The easiest mistake to make is treating the AI-era FDE as a consultant with a laptop. KPMG US's job posting for Technical Lead, Forward Deployed Engineering says the opposite: candidates need at least seven years of recent experience delivering production software systems end to end.

According to the posting, new hires will join a small team of "AI-native" full-stack engineers that rapidly builds and deploys end-to-end AI systems. The team works in two-to-six-week cycles, taking direct feedback from users and stakeholders. Cycles that short only work if the person writing the specs also knows where the system is likely to break.

Gathering context doesn't replace engineering skill—it builds on it. Only if you already know how foreign-currency invoices can break a reconciliation data pipeline do you know the right questions to ask about foreign-currency invoices.

What does an intent spec look like?

Return to the hypothetical invoice reconciliation case. The spec handed to the agent is no longer a single sentence; it can start by stating the business goal: reduce the number of invoices the finance team has to match manually, rather than "match every invoice."

The constraints section spells out what must be asked of the customer first: who sets the auto-matching threshold, and which day's exchange rate applies to foreign-currency invoices. The edge cases section lists installment invoices and voided invoices, and specifies that they go into a manual review queue.

Finally, the acceptance criteria: rerun the process on a month of historical data and compare the results with what accountants produced by hand. Every line in this document is a question you must ask the customer—and that is exactly the bottleneck Johnsen describes.

This spec is also worth bringing to an FDE interview. Explaining such a spec clearly and pointing out which single question saved the project from building the wrong thing is usually more persuasive than listing a string of frameworks.

Johnsen's career began with writing every line of code by hand. The seven-year production system delivery requirement in KPMG's job posting shows that this kind of experience is still the ticket in; what has changed is that today it must be used to ask the right questions before writing the first line of code.
