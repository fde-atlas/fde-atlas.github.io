When sales promised what you cannot deliver

FDE Interview Prep · 2026 · 2 min read

Summary

A practice interview prompt for FDE candidates: how to handle a sales promise the engineering side cannot deliver. The author outlines five moves—pin down the exact promise and how you learned of it, identify a concrete technical or contractual reason it fails, align privately with the salesperson first, correct the record with the customer speaking as one company, and set up safeguards for future deals. It lists traps such as villainizing sales, absorbing the promise through weekend heroics, or correcting it unilaterally, plus likely follow-up questions and a two-minute sample answer set in a fictional hotel-group pricing project.

Key points

  • The scenario tests correcting an overpromise while keeping customer and account team aligned.
  • Five moves: verify the promise, find the real blocker, align with sales privately, correct jointly, prevent recurrence.
  • The person who made the promise should deliver the correction, with the engineer beside them.
  • Traps include blaming sales, silently building the impossible feature, or going over the salesperson's head.
  • Includes likely interviewer follow-ups and a sample answer about a 40-property hotel pricing project.

How to answer

Rippling’s Enterprise Account Executive, AI & Data Cloud posting, as of September 2026, tells the seller to collaborate with Solutions Engineers, Professional Services or Forward Deployed Engineers to bridge the gap between “vision” and “execution”. Source 1Enterprise Account Executive, AI & Data CloudPublisherRippling (ats.rippling.com)Source typecompany job posting This question is about the day that gap opens: the interviewer wants to see you correct a promise and keep both the customer and the account team on your side.

Five moves:

  • The promise, exactly. What was said, to whom, in writing or not, and how you found out. Quote it if you can. If you heard it in front of the customer, say how you flagged it there without contradicting the seller: a promise left unanswered on a call reads as agreed.
  • Why it couldn’t be delivered. A concrete reason, technical or contractual, not “it was too hard”.
  • The salesperson first. A private conversation before anything reaches the customer: what the customer needed underneath the promise, and a joint plan for the correction.
  • The customer, with one voice. Who delivered the correction (ideally the person who made the promise, with you beside them), the need you met instead, and what changed in writing.
  • Upstream. What you set up so it didn’t happen again, and what the next deal with that salesperson looked like.

Say the sentence you used with the customer, and make it sound like one company: “We described this as real time, and I want to correct that and show you what solves the problem behind it.” No “they”, no “sales”.

The traps: a villain story about the salesperson, a weekend of heroics that absorbed the promise without correcting it, and correcting it alone so the account team hears it from the customer.

Follow-ups

What the interviewer may ask next, once your first answer is on the table.

  • Why do you think the salesperson made the promise, and what did the customer need underneath it?
  • What would you have done if the account executive had refused to correct it with the customer?
  • How did you work with that salesperson on the next deal?

Where answers go wrong

  • Makes the salesperson the villain, or tells the customer “sales oversold this”, which shows the interviewer how you will talk about their account team.
  • Quietly builds the impossible thing over nights and weekends, so the promise is never corrected and the next one is bigger.
  • Corrects the promise alone, over the salesperson’s head, and leaves the account team to find out from the customer.

Answer this in two minutes

Write the answer you would say out loud. The clock starts with your first word.
