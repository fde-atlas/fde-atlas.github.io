FDE Must-Read: Thinking in Systems and Feedback Loops

Original title: FDE 必读：Thinking in Systems 与反馈回路

FDE Times · 2026-11-03 · 4 min read

Summary

This article argues that Donella Meadows' Thinking in Systems is a practical discovery tool for forward deployed engineers facing vague customer complaints. Using a support-team backlog example, it explains three concepts: reinforcing loops (why things get progressively worse), delays (which dictate how value can be proven within pilot windows), and Meadows' leverage-point hierarchy, where adjusting numbers ranks weakest. An AI agent that merely speeds up replies tweaks a number; one that feeds reopen signals back to writers changes the loop's structure. The author suggests reading Leverage Points first, then the book, then Dancing With Systems, and shows how to frame this work on a resume.

Key points

  • The book circulated from 1993; Meadows died in 2001, and Diana Wright's edited Chelsea Green edition appeared in 2008.
  • Reinforcing loops explain escalating problems; reopen rates can test the hypothesis with data.
  • Delays matter: a change needing six weeks cannot be judged in a four-week pilot.
  • Meadows ranks adjusting parameters 12th of 12 leverage points—numbers rarely change behavior.
  • Recommended reading order: Leverage Points essay, the full book, then Dancing With Systems.

Thinking in Systems: A Primer · Donella H. Meadows ·

Cover image: Open Library

Key Takeaways

  • The manuscript circulated from 1993 onward; Meadows died in 2001, and the Chelsea Green edition edited by Diana Wright was published in 2008
  • Three concepts worth remembering: reinforcing loops, delays, and the hierarchy of leverage points with adjusting numbers at the bottom
  • For FDEs, it is a tool for customer discovery, not a checklist

Customers rarely hand a Forward Deployed Engineer (FDE) a well-defined problem. Most customers describe a feeling: the ticket backlog keeps growing, their own customers are complaining more and more, and the team is exhausted.

To break down problems like this, read Donella H. Meadows's Thinking in Systems: A Primer. Meadows was the lead author of the 1972 report The Limits to Growth.

This book will not teach you any APIs or frameworks. It teaches you how to ask questions so that your next discovery session doesn't stop at surface symptoms.

A book the author never lived to publish

The manuscript circulated from 1993 onward. Meadows died in 2001, and the book was completed after her death: edited by Diana Wright and published in 2008 by Chelsea Green Publishing in White River Junction, Vermont.

The Donella Meadows Project, which manages her legacy, calls the book a primer, meaning an introductory text. Meadows's most memorable advice is equally plain: before disturbing a system in any way, first watch how it behaves.

For an FDE, this changes the direction of customer interviews. Instead of rushing to ask "What do you want the tool to do?", ask "What in this process is feeding the problem?"

Concept one: find the self-reinforcing loop

Meadows writes that positive feedback loops are the source of a system's growth, explosions, erosion, and collapse. These reinforcing loops grow exponentially, and if left unchecked, can cause severe and irreversible damage.

Take the customer support team from earlier, and suppose the team rewards employees based on the number of tickets closed each week. A large backlog forces employees to rush their replies, rushed replies are low quality, customers reopen tickets, and the backlog grows further.

This is a reinforcing loop, and it explains why things are "getting worse and worse" rather than just "bad." Once you name the loop, you have a hypothesis you can test with data: does the reopen rate rise along with the backlog?

Concept two: probe the delays

Meadows writes that when a system has long delays, it cannot respond to short-term changes. On a customer support team, reopened tickets usually come back late, often days after the rushed reply.

Suppose the same team only reviews quality reports once a quarter. By the time the data arrives, the situation has already changed, and the team often reacts too late, or overcorrects.

In deployment projects, delays also determine how you prove value. A change that takes six weeks to show results cannot be judged within a four-week pilot. So ask the customer up front: after they change something, how long does it take before they know whether it worked?

Concept three: adjusting numbers is the weakest lever

This concept comes from Meadows's article Leverage Points: Places to Intervene in a System, in which she ranked the places where one can intervene in a system. Ranked twelfth, the lowest and weakest, are constants, parameters, and numbers, such as subsidies, taxes, and standards.

She explicitly notes that parameters rank "at the bottom" among effective interventions, and that numbers rarely change behavior. The higher up the list, the more powerful the intervention point.

Meadows never wrote about customer support teams; the table below is a tentative application. The first row maps directly to her twelfth place; the other two rows go beyond numbers and do not correspond to any specific level on her list.

Attempted interventionJust adjusting numbers?What might happen to the loop
Adding headcount, raising SLAsYes, exactly the twelfth-place categoryThe loop slows down, but persists
Showing employees which tickets get reopenedNo, it changes the information repliers receiveRepliers immediately know whether their answer failed
No longer rewarding by tickets closedNo, it changes how the team is measuredRemoves the incentive to rush replies

Now suppose you are building an agent to draft replies for this customer support team. If it merely helps people reply faster, it is essentially adjusting a number: increasing speed within a loop that is still flawed.

The same agent could do more, such as feeding the reopen signal back to the person who wrote the reply. That touches the structure of the loop, rather than just making it spin faster.

One caveat: the ordering of this list should be treated as a suggestion, and critics argue it oversimplifies the complexity of social systems. Use it to broaden the range of options you consider; don't turn it into a checklist.

In what order should you read?

Read the Leverage Points article first; it is published on the Donella Meadows Project website and was also reprinted by Nonprofit Quarterly. The article is short, and once you finish it you can put the ladder to use. Then read the full book to build a solid grasp of feedback loops and delays.

Finally, read Dancing With Systems, published on the Donella Meadows Project website in 2012, which contains the advice to "observe the system before acting." Any FDE who has watched a deployment plan that looked perfect on paper get warped by a customer's real processes will understand why it's worth rereading before every customer visit.

How do you put feedback loops on your resume?

The customer support team example above can also be turned into a strong resume bullet. Instead of writing "deployed an agent for a customer support team," write: "Identified a reinforcing loop between ticket backlog and reopen rate; fed the reopen signal back to repliers; set the reopen-rate checkpoint at six weeks to account for the loop's delay."

That one sentence covers all three concepts from the book: the feedback loop you discovered, the intervention point you chose, and the delay you accounted for when setting the checkpoint. In case interviews, tell the story in those same three steps.

The next time a customer says "everything is getting worse," treat it as your first clue: somewhere, a reinforcing loop is very likely running, and your job is to find it.
