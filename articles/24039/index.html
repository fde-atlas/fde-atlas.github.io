What Are LLM Evaluation Methods? A Guide to Metrics, Procedures, and Tools That FDEs Use in the Field

Original title: LLMの評価方法とは？FDEが現場で使う指標・手順・ツールを解説

FDE総研 · 2026-10-05 · 10 min read

Summary

This Japanese article explains how forward deployed engineers (FDEs) evaluate LLM applications in customer environments. It distinguishes model evaluation from system evaluation, covers three evaluation types (automated, LLM-as-a-Judge, human), metric selection per use case (RAG, classification, summarization), a step-by-step evaluation workflow, and tool choices like Ragas, promptfoo, LangSmith, and Langfuse, with emphasis on agreeing pass criteria with customers and handling data security.

Key points

  • Evaluation splits into automated checks, LLM-as-a-Judge, and human review, combined by purpose.
  • FDEs focus on system-level evaluation; good benchmark scores don't guarantee performance on customer data.
  • Metrics should be derived from use-case failure definitions, plus latency, cost, and safety.
  • Workflow: define failures, build golden datasets, baseline, iterate one change at a time, monitor in production.
  • Tool selection prioritizes data residency constraints and whether customer engineers can maintain the setup.

What types of LLM evaluation methods are there?

LLM evaluation methods fall broadly into three categories: "automated evaluation," "LLM-as-a-Judge," and "human evaluation." Rather than using just one, you combine them according to your purpose.

It helps to think of LLM evaluation in two parts: "model evaluation," which measures the performance of the model itself, and "system evaluation," which measures the quality of the entire application, including prompts, retrieval, and post-processing. What FDEs (Forward Deployed Engineers) mainly handle in the field is the latter, system evaluation. Even a high ranking on public benchmarks like MMLU does not guarantee the model will work as expected on the customer's business data.

How does automated evaluation differ from LLM-as-a-Judge?

Automated evaluation measures agreement with correct answers using rules or statistics, while LLM-as-a-Judge has a separate LLM do the scoring.

Automated evaluation produces scores mechanically using exact match, regex-based format checks, string overlap measures like BLEU or ROUGE, and embedding similarity measures like BERTScore. It is cheap and fully reproducible, but it tends to score low answers that "mean the same thing but are worded differently."

LLM-as-a-Judge writes scoring criteria (rubrics) into a prompt and has an evaluation LLM judge things like "accuracy" and "faithfulness to sources." It can also evaluate free-form answers. On the other hand, Zheng et al. (2023, "Judging LLM-as-a-Judge with MT-Bench and Chatbot Arena", arXiv:2306.05685) report issues such as position bias, where judgments change based on presentation order; verbosity bias, a preference for longer answers; and self-preference bias, where the LLM rates its own outputs highly.

When is human evaluation necessary?

Human evaluation is indispensable when creating scoring criteria and when verifying the reliability of automated evaluation.

How well LLM-as-a-Judge scores align with human judgment cannot be known without comparing them against samples actually scored by humans. In practice, there are many situations where scoring the first few dozen to around a hundred samples together with the business stakeholders and articulating "what makes an answer good" determines the quality of the entire evaluation process going forward. Rather than scoring everything by hand forever, the realistic approach is to focus human effort on criteria creation and periodic spot checks.

How do you choose LLM evaluation metrics?

Metrics are chosen by working backward from "what counts as failure in the use case." In addition to quality metrics, always measure cost and latency together.

If you decide on metrics first, you tend to end up measuring only what is easy to measure. FDEs should first work with the customer to enumerate failure cases—"answers that would cause problems for the business"—and then choose metrics that can detect those failures.

What are the representative metrics by use case?

For RAG, faithfulness and retrieval accuracy are central; for classification and extraction, accuracy and F1; for summarization and dialogue, coverage and helpfulness.

For RAG metrics, the definitions in the Ragas documentation are widely referenced. Measuring "whether the answer is supported by the retrieved documents (faithfulness)" and "whether the necessary documents were retrieved (Context Recall)" separately lets you isolate whether the problem lies on the retrieval side or the generation side.

What metrics besides quality should be measured?

In production operation, you need to measure latency, cost per request, and safety alongside quality.

Even with high accuracy, adoption stalls if responses take tens of seconds or the per-request API cost doesn't make business sense. HELM from Stanford CRFM (Liang et al., 2022) also proposes multifaceted evaluation covering not just accuracy but robustness, fairness, bias, toxicity, and efficiency. In the field, looking at at least the following four axes together makes discussions easier.

  • Quality: accuracy, faithfulness, format compliance
  • Speed: p50 / p95 latency
  • Cost: input/output token volume and per-request cost
  • Safety: leakage of personal information, inappropriate output, resistance to prompt injection

What is the procedure for LLM evaluation?

LLM evaluation proceeds in the order: "define failures → create evaluation data → measure baselines → improve and re-evaluate → monitor in production."

There is a reason for this order. If you start improving the prompt before creating evaluation data, no one will be able to judge whether the improvements actually worked.

How do you build an evaluation dataset?

An evaluation dataset is built from pairs of inputs close to the customer's real data and expected outputs, mixing typical examples with difficult ones.

Here is how to create evaluation data (a golden dataset).

  • Collect inputs from actual inquiries and logs: mask personal information before use
  • Separate typical examples from boundary cases: mix common questions, questions the documents cannot answer, ambiguous questions, and deliberately adversarial inputs
  • Attach expected outputs or scoring criteria: for tasks with no single correct answer, describe "elements that must be included" and "elements that must not be included"
  • Have business owners review it: don't let the FDE decide the correct answers alone
  • Version-control it: keep a change history of the data so scores can be compared

More is not necessarily better. Even a small dataset that covers failure patterns tends to yield more hints for improvement. You can also synthesize evaluation data with an LLM, but synthetic data alone tends to miss the quirks of real inputs, so combining it with real data is the safe choice.

How do you run the improvement cycle?

Make only one change at a time, re-evaluate with the same evaluation data, and compare the scores and failure cases before and after.

If you change the prompt, model, chunking, and retrieval count all at once, you won't know which change had an effect. Beyond average scores, always visually inspect "cases that were correct before the change but failed after it (regressions)." Build pre-release evaluation into CI and stop merging when scores fall below the threshold; this way, quality standards are passed on to members who join later.

What tools are available for LLM evaluation?

LLM evaluation tools fall into three categories: evaluation metric libraries, test runners, and tracing/monitoring platforms.

Rather than covering everything with a single tool, it is often easier to operate by splitting roles between testing during development and monitoring in production.

Ragas, DeepEval, promptfoo, LangSmith, Langfuse, and Arize Phoenix are commonly used.

How should you choose a tool?

Prioritize where the customer's data can be stored and whether the tool fits into the existing development and operations setup.

Before comparing features, check how data is handled. For customers in finance, healthcare, and the public sector, prompts and logs sometimes cannot be sent to external SaaS, making self-hostable OSS (Langfuse, Arize Phoenix, etc.) the candidates. Next, consider whether the customer's engineers can take it over. An evaluation platform no one can operate after the FDE leaves will eventually go unused. Choose based on the customer team's tech stack: Ragas or DeepEval for Python-centric organizations, promptfoo for CI-centric workflows.

What should FDEs watch out for when doing LLM evaluation on-site with customers?

FDEs need to build evaluation criteria together with the customer and pay particular attention to data handling and accountability for explaining evaluation results.

The FDE role, popularized by Palantir, involves embedding in the customer's site and making the product usable in real business operations. The same applies to LLM evaluation: being technically correct alone is meaningless unless it can support the customer's decision-making.

How do you agree on evaluation criteria with the customer?

Agree in writing, before evaluation, on a passing threshold ("what percentage is acceptable for production") and on failures that are absolutely unacceptable.

Since LLM outputs are probabilistic, 100% accuracy cannot be promised. Deciding the following items in advance makes later discussions much easier.

  • Passing threshold: target values for key metrics and their rationale (e.g., comparing against error rates when handled manually)
  • Critical failures: failures that stop the system even with a single occurrence, such as presenting incorrect amounts or outputting personal information
  • Scope requiring human review: where to draw the line between automated processing and final human judgment
  • Conditions for re-evaluation: timing of model updates, document additions, and business rule changes

Since numeric targets vary greatly depending on the customer's business risk, it is best to assume there is no universal "correct number."

What should you be careful about regarding data and security?

Because evaluation data contains real business information, first confirm masking, storage location, and whether sending to external APIs is permitted.

With LLM-as-a-Judge, the inputs and outputs being evaluated are sent to the evaluation LLM. Even if the production system runs in a closed network environment, it is a common oversight to have only the evaluation pipeline send data to external APIs. Confirm with the customer's information security department, including the evaluation dataset, logs, and the destinations of judge calls.

One more point worth noting is how evaluation results are communicated. If you report only average scores, decision makers will simply hear that it "works more or less." By presenting representative success and failure examples side by side and explaining "which types of questions fail, and how" in business terms, you provide material for decisions on additional investment and operational design. My experience as a practitioner in the field tells me that evaluation is not just a way to measure quality; it is also a shared language that lets the customer and the FDE see the same reality.
