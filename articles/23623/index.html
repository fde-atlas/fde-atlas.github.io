ElevenLabs FDE Role Requirements and Interview Process

Original title: ElevenLabs FDE 岗位要求与面试流程

FDE Times · 2026-10-23 · 5 min read

Summary

Requirements shift between hiring rounds—a European Deployment Strategist posting demanded 3+ years of client-facing experience plus familiarity with LLM and agent frameworks. ElevenLabs has posted two job listings with the same title: Forward Deployed Engineer (FDE), but with very different technical bars. The first required proficiency in Python and an understanding of software architecture and API integrations; the second only required basic Python, enough to write scripts and build demos.

Neither listing explained the difference. But viewed together, it can be inferred that this voice AI company hires FDEs at two or more levels, each with different technical depth and focus.

If you're targeting FDE roles at AI companies, both listings are worth a close read. They can help you pick the right role to apply for and prepare for what's actually being tested. Both listings have since been removed from Built In: the software engineer version was taken down on September 5, 2025, and the strategist version on February 24, 2026.

Same Job Title, Two Technical Levels

The software engineer version describes an engineer who owns large projects end-to-end for ElevenLabs' most strategic partners. This person works daily with both the client's engineering teams and management.

Anyone who has read backend job ads will find these requirements familiar: proficiency in Python, solid software development skills. The version posted on Working Nomads goes further, explicitly calling for software architecture and API integration skills.

One detail is easy to miss: both versions accept small-scale client communication experience, such as that gained in student clubs or personal projects.

The strategist version shifts the focus toward the client. This role involves meeting with strategic customers, understanding their most important needs in audio and voice AI, identifying the thorniest pain points, and then building demos. Python is only needed at a basic level, plus enough API integration knowledge to write scripts and build prototypes.

The "Basic" Bar Isn't Fixed

The last row of the table deserves a closer look. A Deployment Strategist listing on Working Nomads was marked as Mid Level, requiring enough technical foundation to quickly understand the company's voice and audio models.

Another listing for Europe, posted on Built In Dublin and taken down on July 21, 2026, required 3+ years of customer-facing experience in technical roles and familiarity with LLM and agent frameworks. That's a significant step up from the "basic Python" in the earlier strategist listing.

The lesson: don't treat a job ad as a fixed description of the role. On the same track, requirements can rise between hiring rounds. If you're aiming for the strategist track, prepare for the hardest version: be able to build a small AI agent yourself with a mainstream LLM framework, not just write scripts that call APIs.

Location Is Also a Requirement

The FDE listing on Working Nomads stated that the company strongly prefers candidates on the US East Coast or in Europe, naming New York, London, Warsaw, and Berlin.

Given the job responsibilities, the reason isn't hard to guess. Collaborating daily with client engineers requires substantial overlapping working hours. Imagine an integration breaks at 10 a.m. in London: the client needs someone responding that same morning, not after you wake up.

For candidates in Asia or other distant time zones, this is worth weighing before applying. The official job page says visas are considered case by case, so if you're willing to relocate near these cities, make that clear from the first round.

The Case Interview: Ask First, Draw Later

aced.io, an interview prep site unaffiliated with ElevenLabs, describes a five-round process. One round is a 60-minute auto-timed coding test on CodeSignal, with standard question types.

Also according to aced.io, there's a case interview requiring diagramming in Excalidraw, where the interviewer wants to see that you understand the client's needs before proposing solutions. Another independent guide, HCL GUVI, offers similar advice: don't jump straight into system design; ask clarifying questions first.

These are all third-party guides, so treat them as prep maps rather than a company-confirmed process. The official job page doesn't specifically mention the FDE role, only saying the process typically ends with a final round involving a founder or a Bar Raiser.

Imagining a Case Interview

Suppose the interviewer plays an insurance company that wants to use a voice AI agent to handle customer calls. Strong technical candidates often immediately start drawing: speech recognition, then a language model, then voice playback.

That diagram may not be wrong. But according to both guides, this candidate has skipped exactly the part being tested.

A better approach is to spend the first few minutes asking questions. How many calls come in daily, and which types are most common? Which metric does management use to measure success: wait time, first-call resolution rate, or cost per call? Which system does the AI agent need to read data from, and does that system have an API?

Once you've heard enough answers and then draw the diagram, every module on it has its own reason to exist. You can explain why you automate certain call types first and why the rest still go to humans.

This is exactly the day-to-day work described in the job listing: facing both the client's engineers and managers, who care about different things.

The Final Round Asks What You Personally Did

In the founder or Bar Raiser round, "my team did X" won't help you much. This role requires owning projects end-to-end, so the interviewer will likely want to know which parts you actually did yourself.

Prepare two or three stories. Each should include a specific technical decision, an alternative you rejected, and why you rejected it.

Apply the same treatment to your resume. An entry like "participated in payment system integration" carries little information. Rewrite it as "designed the API integration layer for the payment gateway, choosing asynchronous processing to avoid timeouts under high load," and the reader immediately sees your capabilities and decisions.

Which One Should You Apply To?

If you write Python every day and live near one of the company's preferred cities, the software engineer version is a reasonable target. The coding test described by aced.io is auto-graded and time-limited, so practice for speed: no one will hear your explanations in that round.

If you don't have much customer-facing experience, the note about student clubs and personal projects is a useful hint. Pick a personal project with real users, and write down what you asked them and how you improved the product after listening to their feedback.

If your communication skills are stronger than your coding skills, the strategist version gives you an entry point. A lower technical bar doesn't mean you can skip the technical work: you still need to write your own scripts and build your own demos, rather than waiting for engineers to do it for you.

The two job postings differ in code depth, but both put the customer at the center of the role. Whichever one you apply to, be prepared to be asked how deep your understanding of the customer is, not just how well you write code.
