What is an FDE? The difference from a consultant is whether you're still around after the report is delivered.

An FDE (Forward Deployed Engineer) is an engineer who is stationed long-term at the client's site, gets systems into production, and keeps running them. Behind the hype there is also skepticism that it is just "consulting in a new skin." This article breaks the controversy into three layers and then proposes three verifiable mechanisms to help you judge whether a partner will stay with you to the end, or leave once the report is delivered.

FDE, short for Forward Deployed Engineer, is one of the fastest-rising job titles in the current AI boom. The job involves being stationed long-term at the client's site, getting systems into production, and keeping them running. Ted Mabrey, Palantir's global head of business, recently wrote something quite unflattering to his industry peers on his own Substack: every "FDE imitation" he has seen is just a "half measure." LeadDev's report quotes another industry figure even more bluntly: "Right now, many organizations call anyone who can face customers and knows a bit of technology an FDE."

What makes this statement so damaging is that the speaker is not an outside critic, but someone from the very company that invented the title. TechCrunch's July 2026 report cites research from the staffing firm Christian & Timbers: there are roughly 17,000 people in the US market carrying the FDE title, but the top talent who truly have industry know-how and can reliably calculate ROI for enterprises is estimated at only 2,000 people, and the original text specifically stresses that this is "a total count, not the number of recruitable openings." Both numbers come from the same study, but the study does not clarify whether the 2,000 are a subset of the 17,000, so we will not convert this into a precise ratio and will only draw one directional conclusion: there is a substantial gap between people carrying the FDE title and people who can actually live up to the definition.

This is the most awkward, and most worth seriously discussing, controversy in the FDE hype: is this really a new division of labor, or is the consulting industry wearing a new coat? We do not intend to dodge the question. This article lays the skepticism out clearly, and then explains what verifiable differences exist between "staying with you to the end" and "delivering the report and leaving."

When we were preparing this series recently, we argued about it internally first. Someone threw out a very direct question: "This title sounds trendy, but what exactly is the difference between what we do and implementation consultants from ten years ago?" It is not an easy question to answer, because on the surface the two really do look alike. But precisely because it is hard to answer, it is worth writing out clearly rather than glossing over.

The controversy can actually be broken into three layers, each with its own reasons

The skepticism about FDEs cannot be dismissed with a single line like "it's just marketing talk." Laid out, there are at least three independent layers, each backed by concrete numbers or statements, and each worth responding to separately.

Layer of controversySpecific content
Title rebrandingEven Palantir's own executives admit that most people claiming to be FDEs are just "half measures," and the phenomenon of slapping a new label on client-facing staff does exist in the market
Concentrated supplyThere are about 17,000 FDEs in the US, and a "significant portion" are already employed by Palantir itself, the company that invented the concept; only an estimated 2,000 people can reliably deliver results
Macro contradictionThe software industry as a whole is still laying people off, yet FDE job postings surged in the same year; LeadDev states outright that this investment "runs counter to the broader trend of layoffs"

The first layer, title rebranding, is the easiest to understand and the easiest to rebut: by changing a title and adding a keyword to a resume, any customer success manager can call themselves an FDE. Judging whether a role is a real FDE cannot rely on the title alone; you have to look at the harder evidence in the next two layers.

The second layer, concentrated supply, is actually an honest warning sign. TechCrunch notes that a "significant portion" of those 17,000 FDEs are already employed by Palantir, the company that invented the concept, meaning the talent pool that has genuinely been trained in this way of working is concentrated in the hands of a very small number of companies. Meanwhile, a report by BusinessNext points out that FDE job postings surged from 643 to 5,330 within one year (April 2025 to April 2026), a 729% year-over-year increase. Demand is growing far faster than talent can be trained, so the market will naturally fill up with people who "carry the label but not the substance." This is a normal phenomenon in any market with a supply-demand imbalance; cloud architects three years ago and data scientists five years ago went through the same stage.

The third layer, the macro contradiction, is the most interesting. At the same time, the software industry is still laying people off, yet companies are willing to pay higher salaries than for ordinary engineers to grab FDEs. These two things seem contradictory. Part of it reflects the broader trend of companies reallocating their overall AI investment, but part of it does come back to the same core: companies do not lack people who can write code; they lack people who can actually wedge a technical solution into a client's system that has not been touched in ten years, and take responsibility for the outcome. This role is scarce, which is one of the reasons—though not the only one—why layoffs and hiring sprees are happening at the same time.

From the candor of Palantir's own people to the identical skepticism in the Chinese market

If only Western tech media were talking about this, one might still call it topic manufacturing. But widening the lens, you find that the "consulting in a new skin" skepticism is appearing almost simultaneously across languages and markets.

The first case comes from inside the company that invented the title. Ted Mabrey is Palantir's global head of business; he has no motive to belittle a title his own company invented, yet he still stated plainly on Substack that every FDE imitation he has seen on the market is just a half measure. This statement matters because it draws a line: a title does not equal capability. Even if you write FDE on your resume, it does not mean you truly possess the working style of "going deep into the client's site and taking responsibility for outcomes" as originally defined by Palantir. Wikipedia's "Forward Deployed Engineer" entry defines the role as "a customer-facing engineer who develops and deploys software at the client," spanning the work of solutions architects, sales engineers, and other functions. The fact that the role spans so many functions itself shows that the boundaries of this title have always been blurry, which is what leaves room for "rebranding."

The second case comes from a completely different market. A commentary piece in the mainland Chinese tech media Huxi is even less forgiving: the author asks directly, from ERP system implementations in the early days to long-term on-site consulting services, "which of these tasks isn't FDE work?" He argues this has been done in mainland China for thirty years, and "the only difference is that we don't call it FDE; we call it outsourcing." The article also points out the most practical question from the client's side: "Why is the quote so much more expensive than outsourcing?" These questions are almost identical to the doubts that arise in the minds of Taiwanese brand owners when evaluating any kind of "accompaniment-style service."

Looking at these two cases together reveals a common conclusion: the skepticism about consulting in a new skin appears simultaneously across languages and markets, and it is a test that any new job title will face in the early stage of its hype. The question that really should be asked is never "does this title sound new," but "once you strip away the title, what remains of this person's relationship with the client."

The third point of comparison is the scale of the hype itself. TechCrunch's report mentions that quite a few large consulting firms plan to expand their FDE headcount tenfold, from teams of twenty to over a hundred people. Expansion at that speed can hardly be achieved by "carefully selecting top talent," and dilution is almost inevitable. The same report even quotes industry insiders' pessimistic speculation that this role could be automated away within two to five years, and might even disappear entirely in five to ten years. When even the people who spawned the hype doubt how long the job will last, outside skepticism is not exaggerated at all.

Consulting versus staying with you to the end: three verifiable mechanisms

Putting the three layers of dispute above together, we believe the real question to ask is whether this way of working has any substantive mechanical difference from the traditional consulting model—the novelty of the job title is not the point at all. We believe the key differences lie in three verifiable places, things you can directly use to examine a contract or a partnership, not just abstract adjectives:

  • Where does accountability end: In the traditional consulting model, accountability ends with the report itself. Once the consultant finishes the analysis, writes clear recommendations, and delivers the presentation, the contract is over. Whether the brand follows the advice, and whether it works after they do, is outside the scope of the contract. In the hands-on accompaniment model, accountability ends with results. Both parties have to look at the numbers together, and if the numbers are wrong, they have to figure it out together until the problem is truly solved. This is also why one article points out directly: when brands choose a system or a partner, what ultimately matters is who can actually connect data into revenue, not how pleasant the interface is to chat with.
  • Density of on-site time: The consulting model usually involves a one-time deep interview plus a few weeks of data analysis, producing a thick recommendation report. In the accompaniment model, on-site time is much longer and recurring—this month it's member data integration, next month it's figuring out where a campaign script is getting stuck, and six months later, going back to check whether the original assumptions have been adjusted along with changes in the brand's business. Only with enough time can you see the brand's real business rhythm; what you hear in a single interview is usually just one side of the story.
  • Does what is learned on-site feed back into the product: This is what we consider the most critical point—whether the next customer has to step in the same potholes all over again. In the consulting model, knowledge usually stays in the consultant's head; when that consultant leaves or moves to another project, the experience disappears with them. The accompaniment model requires turning every pitfall encountered during implementation into a feature or rule in the next version of the product, turning experience into an asset that can be replicated and inherited. We ourselves have written before that when brands choose a CDP system or partner, what ultimately matters is who can connect data, execution, results, and learning into a cycle that actually turns, not whose model is bigger. The same logic applies to judging "whether this person is really accompanying you": if nothing they leave behind becomes part of the system after they're gone, then no matter what the job title says, they are essentially still a one-time consultant.

These three mechanisms together answer the awkward question raised at the beginning. Whether the term FDE will fade, we don't know, and we won't pretend to. But "accountable for results, enough on-site time, experience feeding back into the product"—these three things won't disappear just because a buzzword fades, whether the name changes or not.

91APP's standard of judgment is how long the work has been done, not whether the name has changed

The point here is not to slap a trendy label on our own services, but to honestly lay things out for comparison: in the process of brands implementing 91APP CDMP or personalized marketing systems, what problems the consulting team has to handle, and where that resembles—or may fall short of—the three mechanisms of "accompaniment-style implementation" discussed above, letting readers judge for themselves rather than us making one-sided claims.

Specifically, this overlap shows up in three places:

  • After implementation is complete, there is ongoing work adjusting to business changes—member data consolidation, channel integration, script optimization—not just delivering the system and calling it done.
  • The consulting team stays embedded with the same brand over the long term, accumulating an understanding of the brand's business rhythm, without a whole new batch of people starting from scratch each time.
  • The most easily overlooked point: problems the CS team encounters on-site don't just stay in conversation logs, but are systematically organized into reusable knowledge, evolving the CS role into a growth advisor capable of proactively identifying risks and opportunities.

This is also why we care so much about the phenomenon of "CDP implementation failure." Past analysis has pointed out that one of the most common causes of CDP implementation failure is insufficient service depth: the vendor hands over the system, leaves the rest for the brand to figure out on its own, and when problems arise, no one actually rolls up their sleeves to solve them together. This is exactly the scenario to guard against in the "rebranded consulting" controversy—no matter how new the job title, if on-site time is insufficient and experience doesn't feed back, the probability of implementation failure is just as high.

The value of this is not in being first to slap a trendy label on ourselves, but in giving brands three mechanisms they can use to examine any partner claiming to help implement systems, optimize marketing, or drive data transformation—whether the person's business card says consultant, project manager, or FDE.

Before choosing a partner, ask these questions before signing

Rather than teaching brands how to distinguish "real vs. fake FDEs," it's better to turn the criteria into a few concrete questions you can directly ask a partner. These questions are far more useful than the job title itself.

  • Does accountability end when the contract ends: Ask clearly whether "delivery" and "post-launch continuous optimization" are in the same contract, or whether a new quote is required after launch. The answer will tell you whether their business model is designed as a one-time service.
  • How is on-site time arranged: Ask how much time they expect to spend on your data and processes—whether they write a report after a few interviews, or return on a fixed schedule to review numbers and adjust scripts. Time density doesn't lie.
  • Where did the previous customer's lessons go: Directly ask what became of the pitfalls they've encountered before—did they stay in someone's head, or become rules or features in the system? If they can't give a concrete example, it usually means the experience wasn't retained. If they're confident, ask directly whether you can verify with a previous customer.
  • Who is accountable for the numbers: Confirm who has to sit down and work through problems when they arise—is it the original point of contact, or a new batch of people who have to get to know your brand all over again? The cost of starting over with new people is often higher than paying a bit more in consulting fees.
  • Are requirements translated into technical specs, or taken at face value: Observe whether, after receiving requirements, they just execute them as-is, or ask back "what problem are you really trying to solve?" People willing to ask back are usually the ones genuinely doing implementation.

These five questions don't require the other party to know the term FDE—only an honest answer. Hype always fades; what remains does not

The term FDE may still exist in five years, or it may slowly fade out like other once-popular job titles—we don't get to decide that; the market will give the answer. But eliminating a job title is easy; eliminating "accountable for results, staying long enough, experience feeding back" is far harder, because these three things have never been packaging rhetoric—they are fundamentals that no genuinely useful service can bypass.

For retail brands in Taiwan, rather than agonizing over whether the partner in front of you should be called an FDE, put your energy into asking the right questions and seeing clearly how their business model is designed. Names will change; whether someone stays until the end is the real dividing line.

Is FDE just rebranded consulting? FAQ

Q1: What exactly is an FDE, and how does it differ from a traditional consultant? A1: FDE (Forward Deployed Engineer) was originally a role created inside Palantir by merging solutions engineering and integration engineering; the job is to be stationed long-term at the customer's site, implement systems, and keep them running. The biggest difference from a traditional consultant is not the title but where accountability ends: consultants are usually accountable for "report quality," while FDEs or accompaniment-style services are accountable for "post-launch results."

Q2: Outsiders say FDEs are just rebranded consultants—is this criticism fair? A2: Yes. Even Palantir's own executives have admitted that most people calling themselves FDEs on the market are only "half-baked." Fair criticism doesn't mean the role has no value; it only means the title is easily abused. When judging, look at the collaboration mechanism, not just the business card.

Q3: Can small and mid-sized brands without big-tech resources also demand this kind of accompaniment-style service? A3: Yes. Accompaniment depends on how the collaboration is designed, not on brand size. Before signing, a brand can directly ask the partner: how is post-launch accountability divided, how is on-site time arranged, and what became of past pitfalls? These questions can be answered clearly regardless of how big the partner's team is.

Q4: For this kind of long-term, hands-on implementation, how long does it take to see results? A4: Because it involves data integration, cross-department communication, and system adjustments, implementation is inherently slower than a one-off consulting report—but that also makes it less likely to produce prototypes that are "fast but inaccurate." The actual timeline varies with the complexity of the brand's systems; we recommend first confirming whether the partner has clear milestone checkpoints, rather than relying on a vague "launch date."

Q5: How does 91APP concretely "run alongside" customers when implementing CDMP or personalized marketing? A5: The core is that the consulting team continuously handles work that changes with the business—member data consolidation, cross-channel integration, and marketing script optimization—and distills problems encountered in the field into reusable decision logic, rather than leaving them locked in a single consultant's experience.

Q6: How can you avoid picking a partner that has merely changed its job title without any real change? A6: Rather than looking at titles, ask a few concrete questions: Does responsibility end when the contract ends? How is on-site time allocated? What became of past clients' outcomes? Those who can't give concrete examples are usually just a rebrand.
