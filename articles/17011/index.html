When a VC Goes Forward-Deployed: What It Means and How Founders Should Vet It

TIMEWELL · 2026-10-21 · 6 min read

Its Intelligence Team has five functions, of which GTMINT and TALINT involve going into companies. Hello, this is Ryuta Hamamoto from TIMEWELL.

"VCs are becoming FDEs" is something I have been seeing a lot recently. One trigger is that on 16 October 2026, US venture capital firm Theory Ventures described an approach in which it embeds people in portfolio companies to help build sales and hiring together.1 From a founder's point of view, an investor who rolls up their sleeves with you, beyond money and advice, is a reassuring presence.

This article separates what has been published from what rests on the firm's own account. It then sets out what it means for a VC to move from advice to execution, and what founders should check beforehand. The basis is a single firm's disclosure, so I will not say this is a trend across VCs. What I cover is one published initiative, and a way of thinking about it for your own company.

What has been published

On 16 October 2026, Theory Ventures published a post titled "Forward Deployment." The author is Lauren DeMeuse of the firm. The firm says it built an "Intelligence Team" to make a partner's investment judgment repeatable as an organization. It has five functions: SIGINT, building data systems; HUMINT, growing a network of experts; GTMINT, speeding up portfolio companies' go-to-market through direct collaboration; MINT, building a marketing playbook; and TALINT, connecting talent and companies.2

Of these, the ones that go into portfolio companies are GTMINT and TALINT. The Forward Deployment post describes an FDE as someone who sits beside the customer, understands the work, and fills the last gap through customization. It then applies the same idea to VC. Capital and advice take a company most of the way. But the rest, such as the first sales hire, the first executive search and discussions at board meetings, happens inside the company. So Theory goes in.1

It gives portfolio company Ollama, a platform for running open-weight models, as an example. Here is what the post says.1

  • The engagement began in summer 2026, at one day a week
  • The person leading go-to-market sat in on dozens of interviews
  • Hiring and a GTM plan were put in place within 90 days
  • A working, scalable GTM technology stack was built
  • A customer pipeline was built for the new product, Ollama Enterprise
  • A sales organization was launched for the founding team

In a comment from Ollama's CEO, the GTM stack is said to serve millions of Ollama developers.

What has not been published

There are also many things the post does not say.

  • Cost. What the portfolio company pays for the engagement is not stated.
  • Team size and the standard duration and frequency. "One day a week" and "within 90 days" are for Ollama. They are not presented as a standard template.
  • Verification of results. The results come from the firm's own account and a quote from Ollama's CEO. No independent verification is given.
  • Scope. The post says the practices offered now are two, GTMINT and TALINT. It says nothing about extending to other areas.

The post also states the premise that makes this possible. The firm invests in a concentrated set of companies, so it can go into them when needed.1 I take that to mean it would be hard for a VC with a large number of portfolio companies to do the same.

How I read this

The move from advice to execution overlaps with the spread of the FDE. Send people to the customer and fill the last gap: that model spread through AI implementation work from 2025 to 2026. I set out the figures in Why the FDE Market Grew So Fast. Theory's approach reads as that idea reaching the world of investing.

A founder's bottleneck really is the first sales hire and the first hires. What money and advice do not reach is the part where someone has to do the work. How do you choose the first salesperson, what do you ask in interviews, how do you build a sales playbook? If an experienced person comes in even one day a week, I think the speed of a launch can change.

But how big the effect is, we do not yet know. Because the results are the firm's own account, it is too early to take "launched within 90 days" as a general expectation. Ollama is already used by many developers, and conditions may have been favorable. The post does not let us tell.

Five things for a founder to check

From here on this is my own arrangement, not published fact. When a VC raises hands-on support, these are points worth confirming so that expectations do not diverge later.

1. What will be done, by whom, and how much.

"Going into the company" means different things to different people. Will they advise on sales, join sales calls, sit in on hiring interviews, or make documents? Write down the names of the people, how many days a week, and how many months. The "one day a week" and "within 90 days" in Theory's case can serve as a reference point.

2. How the portfolio company's information will be handled.

Getting into sales work means touching customer names, the state of deals and information on hiring candidates. Confirm the scope of confidentiality and the line on what the VC shares with other portfolio companies. It matters most if the same VC has invested in a competitor in the same market.

3. Who holds decisions.

The final call on hiring, sales policy and pricing is the founder's. Agree that the VC's people are in a supporting role and do not hold decision rights. Between "advice" and "execution" lies the question of who leads decisions.

4. Decide how it ends.

How long will support continue, and how will it move to self-sufficiency? In the Anthropic and FIS partnership covered in the FDE market article above, it was made explicit that the aim of embedding an FDE is not to stay on site but to hand over knowledge so the other side can run on its own. I think the same applies to VC support. Hand the sales playbook over to people inside the company. Leave the hiring process in writing. Decide what will remain after it ends.

5. How costs and consideration are set.

Theory's post says nothing about cost. If your company is offered this, confirm in writing whether the support is part of the investment terms or costs extra, and how it affects equity and the contract.

How this differs from bringing in an FDE yourself

VC support rests on a relationship in which the portfolio company's growth is also the VC's gain. When a business brings in an FDE itself, or uses an outside one, cost and results are set out in a contract. Both share the need to decide who does what, how far, and how it ends.

Summary

Theory Ventures has described its Forward Deployment approach, in which it embeds people in portfolio companies to help build sales and hiring. In the Ollama case, it says a one-day-a-week engagement put hiring and a GTM plan in place within 90 days. But that is the firm's own account, and neither cost nor independent verification is given. The post also says the approach assumes concentrated investment in a small number of companies.

From a founder's point of view, an investor who works with you beyond money and advice has value. That is exactly why it matters most to be clear beforehand about what will be done, by whom, how much, how far, and how it ends.
