FDE Interview Techniques | Drawing Out Customers' Real Challenges

Original title: FDEのヒアリング術｜顧客の本当の課題を引き出す

日本Fde協会 · 2026-07-05 · 8 min read

Summary

The author, head of the Japan FDE Association, argues that FDE interviewing differs from sales or requirements gathering: its goal is discovering what problem to solve, not what to sell or build. Customers usually state requests rather than underlying problems, and habit makes them unable to articulate their own inefficiencies, so FDEs must observe before asking. He proposes a three-step method—pre-research on the industry, on-site observation, then deep probing questions—illustrated by cases: a manufacturing client's inventory discrepancies fell 80% after building a shared information process instead of the requested system, and a ¥100M project narrowed scope to 40% by asking which features the business could not survive without, hitting a 180% profit target. He lists five question patterns and stresses that trust, built through honesty (admitting what you don't know), matters more than technique.

Key points

  • FDE interviews aim to discover the real problem, unlike sales or requirements interviews.
  • Customers express requests, not problems, and habit hides inefficiencies from them.
  • Three steps: pre-research, on-site observation, then deep probing questions.
  • Cases: inventory discrepancies cut 80%; project scope narrowed to 40% with 180% profit target.
  • Trust built through honesty outweighs questioning technique.

FDE interviews differ from both general sales interviews and requirements-definition interviews. Salespeople ask to determine "what to sell," and SEs ask to decide "what to build." FDEs ask to discover "what should be solved." This difference fundamentally changes the quality of the output. As the Representative Director of the Japan FDE Association, I have repeatedly experienced through extracting requirements from more than 20 stakeholders on projects worth 100 million yen that "the skill of asking correctly" determines a project's success or failure.

By reading this article, you will be able to put into practice interview techniques for drawing out essential challenges rather than customers' surface-level requests. If you want to learn FDE basics first, please also see "What is FDE? A Practitioner Explains the Differences from SEs and SIers".

Customers Cannot Articulate Their "Real Challenges"

Requests and Challenges Are Different

What customers first say is a "request," not a "challenge." "We want to revamp our inventory management system" is a request. The underlying "departments cannot share information in real time, so decision-making is delayed" is the challenge. If you build a system by taking the request at face value, the challenge will not be solved.

I experienced exactly this with a manufacturing client. They said they wanted "a new inventory management system," but when I walked the floor and observed the work, the existing Excel was handling inventory management just fine. The real problem was that the warehouse, sales, and accounting were each looking at separate files, resulting in three different inventory figures. Instead of building the system as requested, we created an information-sharing mechanism, which reduced inventory discrepancies by 80% per month.

Why Customers Cannot Articulate Their Challenges

Customers' inability to articulate their challenges accurately is not a matter of ability. People who do the work every day have become accustomed to the inefficiency. Because "this is how we always do it" has become the norm, they cannot notice that there is a challenge there. That is why FDEs combine "observing" with "asking," not just asking alone.

The 3 Steps of FDE Interviews

FDE interviews proceed in three steps. What matters here is following this order.

StepWhat to DoPurposeTime Required
1. PreparationResearch industry structure, company information, and competitive landscapeBuild a foundation so you don't ask "amateur questions"Half a day to 1 day
2. On-site ObservationWatch the workflow with your own eyes beside the person in chargeDiscover inefficiencies that go unspoken1 to 3 days
3. In-depth QuestionsVerify what you noticed during observation through questionsIdentify the root cause of the challenge1 to 2 hours

Step 1: Preparation — Overcoming "Do You Actually Understand Our Industry?"

When an FDE enters a customer's workplace, the first hurdle is earning trust. The moment people think you don't understand industry terminology or the basics of the business, they stop speaking candidly. That is why, before the first visit, we thoroughly research the customer's industry structure, competitors, and recent news.

When I joined a financial institution project, my experience managing funds worth 5 billion yen as a trader instantly helped build trust. Of course, not everyone has that kind of background, but simply acquiring industry knowledge through preparation is enough to create the impression that "this person knows what they're doing."

Step 2: On-site Observation — "Look Before You Ask" Is the Golden Rule

Before the interview, first observe the workplace with your own eyes. Sit next to the person in charge and observe the actual work. The information gained in this "observing" phase is of a completely different quality from what you get in interviews. That's because people cannot explain their own inefficiencies.

At one small and medium-sized enterprise, the accounting staff spent two hours every morning manually transcribing data from three systems into Excel. When asked, they said "it's my usual work." But from the outside, it was clearly a task that should be automated. Without this observation, this challenge would never have come up in an interview. This observation phase is also the starting point for AI-driven business improvement. For details, see "How to Approach AI Business Improvement | Steps from an FDE Practitioner".

Step 3: In-depth Questions — The Courage to Ask "Do You Really Need That?"

You verify what you noticed during observation through questions. The question I use most often here is "Do you really need that?" It may sound rude, but once a relationship of trust has been established, it is surprising how often the answer is "Actually, it's a rule my predecessor started, and I don't know why we do it."

In a system project worth 100 million yen, more than 20 stakeholders each raised different requests. Implementing everything would have exceeded both budget and timeline. I went around asking each department, "If this feature didn't exist, would your work come to a halt?" Keeping only what they answered "yes" to as mandatory requirements narrowed the development scope to 40% of the original, and we achieved 180% of our profit target.

5 Question Patterns You Can Use in Interviews

Here are five questions FDEs frequently use in the field.

First: "Who is this work for, and why is it being done?" Work with an unclear purpose may be unnecessary in the first place. Second: "If this work disappeared, what problems would it cause?" Thinking in reverse reveals its essential value. Third: "Around when did this process start?" This can uncover old rules that have become mere formalities.

Fourth: "Tell me your ideal state. If there were no constraints, what would you want to do?" Removing constraints brings out what people truly wanted to do. Fifth: "Do you really need that?" As mentioned above, it is the simplest and most powerful question.

Without Trust, Interviews Don't Work

Until You're Told "You Know Our Business Better Than Our Own Employees"

The quality of an interview depends more on the relationship of trust than on questioning technique. Without trust, no matter how good your questions are, you won't get honest answers. A client once told me, "Maekawa, you know our business better than our own employees." This was the result of thorough preparation and on-site observation.

What I do to earn trust is simple: keep promises, meet deadlines, and say "I don't know" when I don't know something. The third is especially important. The moment you pretend to know something, trust collapses. From my experience building an organization of 60 people at a startup, I have also come to realize that people trust not "perfect people" but "honest people."

An engineer's discovery process is different from sales

Sales discovery is done "to make the sale." That's why it requires techniques to emphasize the customer's pain and steer them toward your company's solutions. FDE discovery is different. It aims to accurately understand the customer's challenges, and sometimes even to say, "That's not a problem technology can solve." This sincerity builds long-term trust.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q. Do I need special qualifications or frameworks for discovery?

A. No special qualifications are required. Knowing frameworks like SPIN Selling can be helpful, but the most important thing in FDE discovery is the mindset of "observe before you ask." Rather than technique, the willingness to immerse yourself in the field is what determines results.

Q. What should I do if the customer is uncooperative and won't show me their operations?

A. You may face initial wariness. In such cases, it's effective to first demonstrate one small improvement. Often, attitudes change the moment you show results, saying, "We can automate this task." Trust is built through results, not words.

Q. Can you do something close to field observation even in online discovery sessions?

A. Some degree of observation is possible by having the customer share their screen and show you their actual work. However, non-verbal information such as the atmosphere of the workplace, the expressions of the people involved, and their interactions with colleagues can only be obtained in person. For important projects, we strongly recommend visiting the field at least for the first session.
