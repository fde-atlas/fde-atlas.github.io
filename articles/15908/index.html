I Was Reborn in an AI Isekai as an FDE: A First-Hand Account from the Front Lines of a North American AI Agent Company

Original title: 我在 AI 异世界重生为 FDE，来自北美AI Agent 公司的一线实录

Superlinear Academy · 2017 · 8 min read

Summary

The author, Head of FDE at Cresta, recounts switching from startup cofounder to Forward Deployed Engineer at an AI agent company serving contact centers. He explains that enterprise AI products rarely work out of the box: FDEs align generic platforms with each customer's data, systems, and KPIs, writing production code and owning outcomes end to end. Unlike Sales Engineers or Solutions Architects, FDEs span pre-sales through production. He estimates the role splits roughly into thirds: coding (mostly Python), prompt writing, and testing/evals, plus about one-third travel. He argues FDEs matter because model differences are now marginal and implementation determines value, noting OpenAI, Anthropic, Databricks, and Snowflake are hiring aggressively. The piece closes with a profile of suitable candidates: versatile engineers comfortable with clients, speed, and travel.

Key points

  • FDEs make AI products actually work at customer sites.
  • Enterprise AI rarely works out of the box; integration is the hard part.
  • FDEs differ from SEs/SAs by writing production code and owning results.
  • Roughly one-third each: coding, prompt writing, and evals.
  • OpenAI, Anthropic, Databricks, Snowflake are hiring FDEs aggressively.

Hello everyone. At the "invitation" of Mts Shu, who claims to "never assign topics," I've just come home from two weeks of intense business travel, and I'm here to share about the FDE (Forward Deployed Engineer), a role that's currently trending in the AI isekai. Honestly, I hate reading articles heavy on "AI flavor," so let me state upfront: this piece is not necessarily objective, and it's completely non-authoritative. If you want a well-rounded, comprehensive summary, feel free to search on your own. If this piece has any value at all, it's probably in offering some data points—explaining why, after four years of entrepreneurship, I switched companies and was "reborn" as an FDE. Is this a new wave, or just old wine in new bottles, a piece of fleeting jargon?

I previously wrote two English blog posts on LinkedIn (1, 2) about joining a new company and trying a new role. Some of the content below comes from those two posts—call it "recycling waste" 🤣. But after a cup of coffee this morning, I suddenly thought: since this is for Chinese readers, there's no need to be so measured—I can write something more interesting. I've switched both tracks and careers—isn't that exactly an "isekai rebirth plus class change"?

So, dear readers, ready to like, follow, and hit the notification bell? Next, from my personal perspective, I'll talk about why 2B AI companies are all frantically hiring FDEs.

What is an FDE? Why do AI companies need them?

I'm the Head of FDE at Cresta, an AI company. Let me first answer a question: what is an FDE?

FDE, Forward Deployed Engineer. Of course, that says nothing at all..

In one sentence: an FDE is the engineer who actually gets the AI product "running" at the customer's site.

As we all know, AI demos are cool—you can produce a wow effect in a short time (I won't bother with screenshots). But when it comes to real deployment, many enterprises' own data and processes are either very "grassroots" or siloed from each other. To put it politely: business processes, legacy systems, and data stacks all differ. If you buy HubSpot or Stripe, a few days of training to get familiar with the interface and you're up and running; whereas an AI product is often just an input box or a phone number: simple enough to answer questions, complex enough to issue refunds, change itineraries, hire and fire people—to do everything a web app, mobile app, or API/SDK can do. It's a bit like the "box computing" some people used to hype, but AI really is a redefined user experience.

The FDE's job is to stand on the customer's front line and align the general-purpose platform with specific scenarios: understand the business, stitch capabilities together, polish the experience, launch, and scale stably. An FDE doesn't just deliver the product to the "last mile"—it's more like turning IKEA furniture into the rocking chair your home needs most, tuning a Yamaha piano to the tone best suited to your study, or being the tailor who hand-crafts John Wick's suits.

The FDE role was first introduced by Palantir when closing big defense deals—it seems to have over a decade of history now, and back then engineers really were deployed to the front line (maybe they still are).

"Fine, so that's just implementation, onsite engineer, solution architect, professional services—selling headcount and time?" Uh 😅, if you insist on thinking that way, go ahead. But why is this term so hot right now, even hailed as the hottest job of the AI era?

It's simple: we're still in the wild west of the AI era 🍖. The old SaaS/standardized-product playbook doesn't always work in the face of AI. Enterprise AI products can't be expected to work out of the box, can't count on customers knowing how to "tune" the AI themselves, often can't achieve PLG, and are hard to make self-service. If you use old-world methods in this AI isekai, real deployment is very hard.

In the still rapidly evolving AI agent space, the diversity and uncertainty of requirements are especially pronounced. You'll encounter all kinds of "seemingly unreplicable" customization work: corpus governance, refining conversation flows, integrating with the customer's APIs and data gateways, adapting to compliance and security requirements, post-launch monitoring and rollback strategies... These tasks seem scattered, but they eventually crystallize into the product roadmap and a library of best practices. That's the value of the FDE: using each real delivery to force the product to evolve—seizing the high ground and locking in major customers without waiting for the product to be complete in every respect.

Take Cresta as an example (fine, the ad had to come sooner or later). Essentially, we're an AI agent company, mainly serving contact centers (text, voice, email). The company was founded in 2017; by the 1B "unicorn" standard, we hit that last year. Many large banks and airlines have used Cresta for years to assist human customer service agents (a bit like an AI interview helper such as CheatingDaddy: real-time hints on what to say, evaluating communication effectiveness, quick debriefs). Now it's used more for cost reduction and efficiency: as long as a case isn't too "outrageous," many issues can be handled by an AI agent—for example, completing bookings, rebooking, refunds, and other complex processes—and if it can't be handled, it's escalated to a human agent, or one human agent handles multiple cases simultaneously, providing special approvals and support behind the AI agent.

To truly run such a system in production, you need to connect to the customer's VPN, CRM, ticketing system, knowledge base, and telephony system, handle authentication, rate limits, and data permissions; and you need to align conversation strategy with business KPIs (such as first response rate, conversion rate, average handle time, etc.). Without an FDE on the front line tracing leads, threading needles, and owning things end to end, stable deployment is very difficult.

A traditional Sales Engineer does demos before the deal is signed; traditional PS does tuning and secondary development after signing. The FDE spans the whole journey: getting the AI truly running through engineering and experience, while pushing the product team to make adjustments and improvements, and continuously supporting the customer.

You can't expect the product team to ship a perfect product that customers can use on their own. In the AI era, certainty has been redefined. Writing prompts is a blend of technology and art, and it requires simulation and backtesting against large amounts of historical conversation data. Even customers with plenty of engineers often can't handle such complex and delicate AI projects. What customers pay for is no longer a product—it's an AI solution, a brand-new user experience. Dirty work, tedious work, brain work—doesn't matter. As long as we get paid, right?

While you're still doubting whether FDEs are necessary, AI giants like OpenAI, Anthropic, Databricks, and Snowflake are frantically recruiting and cultivating FDEs. The differences between models have become very subtle, and the value of an application often comes down to how well it's implemented. Only when customers are happy using it will they buy more tokens.

How is it different from a Solution Engineer / Solutions Architect?

Many people compare FDEs with Sales Engineers (SEs) and Solutions Architects (SAs). It's true that we all face customers, but the FDE's way of working is more "engineering-oriented."

First, FDEs write production code directly. SEs focus more on technical communication and prototype demos in pre-sales scenarios, while SAs focus more on architecture design and technology selection; the FDE both designs and implements, connecting the solution into the customer's real systems and being accountable for launch quality.

Second, FDEs are accountable for results all the way to the "last mile." We don't just deliver a solution—we get it running, keep it stable, and produce measurable business value. Whether the launch meets its targets, whether it can scale stably under SLOs, and how to diagnose and roll back when problems occur are all part of an FDE's daily work.

Third, the FDE is a "hybrid player." They can discuss business closed loops with client executives, and also debug issues with the client's engineers while staring at logs; they handle requirements clarification and scope control, and also personally write code on the critical path. This kind of switching is the norm for an FDE.

Why I was reborn as an FDE

In my LinkedIn article, I used Steve Jobs' "connect the dots"—that old Western cliché—to describe how I got to where I am as an FDE.

By this article's framing, I'm a case of "rebirth plus class change."

I started out as a full-stack engineer, working with JavaScript, Flash, EJB, and various other languages and platforms; later I moved into management, leading teams in Canada, the US, India, and China. From 2021 until last month, I was a cofounder at a streaming database startup. I've dabbled in PM, UX, Doc, DevRel, SDR, SA, PS, CloudOps... I only first heard the term FDE last month, and my reaction was: wow, this is so friendly to people like me who've touched a bit of everything and have startup experience. To put it more formally: these "dots" were connected by a single line—engineering implementation skills, product abstraction skills, and customer-facing collaboration and delivery skills—all demanded simultaneously in the FDE role, forming a combined force.

This is also why I especially love being an FDE: every day I get to map abstract, complex technology onto concrete business problems; I keep the engineer's obsession with quality and efficiency, the PM's stubbornness about product experience, and a sense of responsibility for business outcomes; plus I get to travel.

An FDE probably spends 1/3 of their time aligning with clients on vision goals and concrete processes, 1/3 building the AI solution, and 1/3 coordinating feedback with internal teams. On the implementation side, roughly 1/3 writing code (mostly Python, with some JS/Go/Java), 1/3 writing prompts (some best practices, plus plenty of unspeakable art and taste), and 1/3 writing tests and doing evals.

So, what kind of person is suited to be an FDE?

In "AI boilerplate" style, the list could be very long. In plain language, I think an FDE needs to be "fast, sharp, and relentless" (of course that's one-sided, mainly for memorability—they say rhymes are easier to remember).

  • Fast: fast on multiple levels: quickly grasping the client's pain points, requirements, and constraints, delivering quickly, learning new things quickly. An FDE must first and foremost be a solid software engineer. Full-stack is best. Unlike a typical senior engineer, an FDE doesn't necessarily pursue the most elegant or maximally performant solution; shipping a workable Python solution in 2 days is often more practical than polishing a Rust solution for 20 days. Much of Cresta's backend is Go or AWS-based services, which are fast enough; what FDEs often write is integrations with the client's various systems (mostly REST or GraphQL, occasionally private SDKs), or integrations with SaaS like Salesforce/HubSpot, where Python is usually sufficient. Using a coding assistant or Vibe Coding is totally OK. Over the past year I've mainly used Claude Code, and my first PRs after joining were mostly written with Copilot/Codex assistance. You can think of the FDE as the best way to "monetize" vibe coding: employers and clients pay you to vibe coding 😁
  • Sharp: be incisive, focus on the big and let go of the small, adjust priorities dynamically. Quickly earn the client's trust, accurately identify what's critical to the project, and find the right internal resources for support.
  • Relentless: being an FDE is not an easy job. Many big names associate the FDE role with entrepreneurship, because anyone who's founded a company knows that nine-to-five rarely gets things done. Changing requirements and personnel changes are the norm; travel when travel is needed, work overtime when overtime is needed; ask when you don't understand, learn when you don't know how.

Soon Cresta's annual revenue will surpass $100 million, and we plan to step up hiring in Vancouver, with all kinds of roles open. As for the FDE, the ideal profile 👤 is roughly:

  • Experience at a consulting firm or startup, strong technical skills, hard-working and resilient, with extensive client collaboration experience, articulate and adaptable (no drinking capacity required, and no golf needed either—after all, we have big sales to handle 🤝 client relationships)
  • Broad technical range and strong learning ability, proficient in all kinds of Vibe Coding (like "proficient in Word and other office software" back in the day, but more fitting now); fast output and flexible
  • OK with or even enjoying travel, since about 1/3 of the time will be on the road 🤷 (On my first day, my boss said there was a project where I could observe how an FDE works, and the next day I drove to Seattle. The second week, I went to the Silicon Valley HQ to get familiar with the product team. If it's not too far, talking with the client onsite early in a project is more efficient and builds better rapport; afterwards there are lots of Zoom/Teams online meetings, often with VPN access to the client's staging env. After all, we're not resident engineers)
