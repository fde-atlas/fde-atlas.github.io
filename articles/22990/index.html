For freelance engineers, remote work greatly expands your options for how to work. The freedom to work from anywhere with zero commute time, and the chance to take on high-rate urban projects while living in a rural area. Many people are drawn to these benefits and are searching for fully remote projects.

However, even projects listed as "remote allowed" sometimes turn out to require 3 days in the office per week. Such pitfalls are not uncommon. To achieve fully remote work, you need to know how to evaluate projects properly and how to prepare yourself to be chosen by clients.

This article comprehensively covers the differences between remote and fully remote projects, the reality of market rates, the pros and cons, and concrete strategies for continuously securing projects. Let's clarify the definition and types of remote work. "Remote allowed," "partially remote," and "fully remote" may sound similar, but the actual working styles differ greatly. Here, we will organize the differences in terminology and then introduce market trends for remote projects for freelance engineers with concrete figures. We will also touch on how easily different job types can work remotely, so use this to judge whether your specialty is suited to remote work.

First, let's clarify the terminology.

"Remote work" is a general term for working outside the employer's office. It refers to any style of working away from the office, such as working from home or at a cafe or coworking space. In other words, hybrid arrangements like "going to the office a few days a week and working from home the rest" are also included in remote work.

On the other hand, "fully remote" is a working style where all work is done outside the office. You never go into the office and complete all your work from home or a coworking space. Visits to client sites are generally not required, and all meetings are held online.

What requires caution is the label "remote allowed." Even with this wording, it is not rare for the job to actually require several days in the office per week. You often hear stories like "I applied because it said remote allowed, but it turned out 3 days a week in the office was mandatory."

Before applying, always confirm "how often is office attendance required" and "can the work be completed fully remotely." If it is not stated in the project details, we recommend asking the agent or contact person directly.

Remote work spread rapidly after COVID-19, but since then there has also been a movement back toward the office. Across all industries, the rate of remote work implementation has been declining from its 2020 peak.

However, in IT freelance settings, remote projects are still abundant. This is because many clients' development environments support remote work, and more companies have systems in place where "work runs smoothly without coming to the office."

According to Relance's "Freelance Engineer White Paper 2024," about 50% of freelance engineers work primarily remotely. Breaking it down, 29.5% work "basically remotely" and 21.0% work "mostly remotely." In other words, one in two freelance engineers works mainly remotely.

At major freelance agencies as well, remote-compatible projects account for roughly half of all listings. Even those living in rural areas can join projects in the Tokyo metropolitan area as long as they have an internet connection, so job options have expanded more than ever. The era of "you can't take Tokyo projects unless you live in Tokyo" is coming to an end.

Although we simply say "freelance engineer," there are many different job types, and how easily remote work can be achieved varies greatly by role. It is important to know whether your specialty is suited to fully remote work.

Job types with many fully remote projects

Fully remote projects are easy to find in the following job types.

  • Web engineers (frontend/backend)
  • Mobile app engineers (iOS/Android)
  • Cloud infrastructure engineers (AWS, GCP, etc.)
  • Web designers

What these job types have in common is that work can be completed with just a PC and an internet connection. Writing code, creating designs, building cloud environments—these tasks can proceed without any problem even away from the office. In particular, for web and app projects, projects that assume fully remote work are not uncommon.

Job types with few fully remote projects

On the other hand, some job types find fully remote work difficult.

  • Embedded/control systems engineers
  • Network engineers (working with physical equipment)
  • PMs and IT consultants

In embedded development, there are many cases where actual hardware such as sensors and devices cannot be taken home, so office attendance may be required for testing and debugging. For projects involving hardware in the manufacturing industry, complete remote work is difficult in reality.

PMs and IT consultants are required to communicate closely with stakeholders, so many of their projects require on-site work or regular office attendance. However, recently the use of online meeting tools has advanced remote work considerably, and cases where work can be completed from home except at critical junctures are also increasing.

According to Relance's "Freelance Engineer White Paper 2024," the average annual revenue of freelance engineers is about 6.32 million yen. Compared with the average annual salary of salaried engineers, this can be considered a somewhat higher level.

Particularly noteworthy is that about 60% achieve an income increase after switching to freelancing. The fact that more than half earn more than they did as salaried employees is encouraging data for those considering freelancing.

Let's look at average annual revenue by age group.

  • 20s: about 5.2 million yen
  • 30s: about 6.3 million yen
  • 40s: about 6.7 million yen
  • 50s: about 6.3 million yen

There is a tendency for income to rise with experience. However, the slight drop in the 50s is thought to be because more people choose work styles suited to their lifestyles, such as how they select projects and adjusting the number of working days.

Of course, these are only averages, and income can vary greatly depending on skills, working hours, and how projects are chosen. If you can continuously win high-rate projects, an annual income exceeding 10 million yen is well within reach; conversely, if projects dry up, there is a risk of earning nothing.

2-2. Rate Benchmarks by Job Type | Guidelines for Web Development, Mobile, and Infrastructure

Freelance engineer income varies by specialty and skill set. Let's look at rate benchmarks for representative job types. Use this as a reference for understanding your own market value.

Web Application Development

600,000 yen or more per month is one guideline. Experience with modern frameworks (React, Vue, Next.js, etc.) and TypeScript makes it easier to aim for high rates. Full-stack engineers who can handle both frontend and backend tend to command even higher rates.

Mobile App Development

The market rate is 500,000 to 800,000 yen per month. Experience with native development in Swift or Kotlin is valued. Demand is also growing for cross-platform development experience with Flutter and React Native. With a track record of developing popular apps, you could aim for 800,000 yen or more per month.

Cloud Infrastructure

You can aim for 700,000 yen or more per month. Experience designing and building on AWS, GCP, or Azure makes it easier to match with high-rate projects. In particular, AWS certifications and experience with IaC tools such as Terraform are strengths. Infrastructure engineers with DevOps expertise have very high market value.

These are only guidelines; rates fluctuate depending on years of experience, which phases you can handle, and communication skills.

Freelance engineers who achieve high rates share common traits. Beyond technical skill, what matters is whether you have qualities that make clients think "I want to work with this person."

① Possessing In-Demand Technology Stacks

Mastering technologies with high market demand is the first step toward high rates. Fields currently likely to lead to high rates include cloud platforms such as AWS and GCP, TypeScript, Go, Python, and AI/machine learning.

It is important to keep up with new technologies without neglecting them. The moment you think "my current skills are enough," your market value starts to decline. Maintain a mindset of continuous learning.

② Being Able to Handle Upstream Processes

Engineers who can join projects from upstream processes such as requirements definition and basic design, not just coding, are highly valued. Rather than someone who "only does what they're told," talent that can "think together about what should be built" is in demand on any project.

To earn trust as a freelancer, it is said that at least three years of practical experience is desirable. Becoming a freelancer at an early stage of your career may make it difficult to access high-rate projects.

③ Building Trusting Relationships with Clients

Technical skill alone will not win high rates. Personal qualities that make clients think "I want to work with this person" are also important.

Especially in remote projects, where faces are unseen, communication skills are highly evaluated. Response speed, thoroughness in reporting, and how you handle problems—these "ease of working together" factors lead to repeat orders and rate increases.

Full remote work has many attractions, but it also comes with challenges. Here we objectively organize the advantages and disadvantages to help you judge whether it is the right work style for you. We also clarify the characteristics of "people suited and not suited to full remote," so read while considering which category you fall into.

Full remote work offers many attractions unavailable in office work. We explain the main advantages in four categories.

The flexibility to work wherever and whenever you like is the greatest advantage of full remote.

With no commute, many people can spend an extra one to two hours or more a day on other things. Simply being freed from the stress of crowded trains greatly improves quality of life.

It also becomes easier to adjust working hours. Flexible work styles are possible, such as "starting work at 10 a.m. because mornings aren't my strength" or "taking a break in the evening and resuming at night." Many people also say it makes balancing housework and childcare easier.

Being able to join high-rate projects in Tokyo regardless of where you live is a major advantage.

This makes possible a way of working that combines living in a rural area with urban-level rates: "rural location × high rates." If you live in an area where rent is less than half of Tokyo's, the money left over from the same income increases significantly.

Even if you want to return to the area where your family home is, or need to accommodate a partner's job transfer, as long as you can secure a fully remote project, you can balance work and life in the place of your choice.

For those struggling with workplace relationships, full remote work can reduce mental strain.

Since you don't need to see colleagues and bosses face-to-face every day, stress from social interactions decreases dramatically. You can keep your distance from office small talk, cliques, and drinking-party obligations.

Because only the minimum necessary communication is required, you're less likely to have your time consumed by sudden interruptions or long meetings. Being able to create an environment where you can purely focus on work is a major advantage.

With full remote work, where your home can be your workplace, you can set up the optimal work environment for yourself.

Desk, chair, monitor, keyboard—you can choose all of them to your liking. A height-adjustable standing desk, an ergonomic chair that's easy on your back, a 4K monitor that's easy on your eyes. In an office you have to use whatever is provided, but at home you can invest in your own setup.

Being able to control an environment that maximizes your concentration is a strength unique to full remote work.

While full remote work offers many conveniences, there are also drawbacks and challenges to be aware of. Understanding these and taking countermeasures is the key to thriving long-term in full remote work.

In an environment with no watchful eyes, self-management is everything.

When no one is watching, people tend to slack off, or conversely, work too much without taking breaks—mismanaging their pace. If you can't manage your schedule, daily rhythm, and health, your productivity drops and your reputation with clients suffers as well.

"I got lazy working remotely and ended up scrambling right before the deadline," "It was hard to switch between work and personal time, and I was exhausted from always being in work mode"—these are common complaints. Without self-discipline, full remote work can backfire.

Unlike face-to-face interaction, facial expressions and atmosphere don't come across, so building trust takes time.

When communication centers on text and video calls, nuance can be hard to convey in writing alone. A light joke may unintentionally offend someone, or instructions may be misinterpreted, causing rework.

That's precisely why it's important to build trust through quick responses, thorough reporting, and the quality of your deliverables. You need to be even more conscious of "how you communicate" than in face-to-face settings.

Working alone for long hours makes it easy to feel lonely.

In an office, you can refresh yourself through small talk or lunch with colleagues, but at home there's no such casual interaction. You can't easily ask the senior colleague next to you when you're stuck, which makes psychological isolation more likely.

As countermeasures, joining communities of fellow freelancers or using coworking spaces a few times a week are effective. It's important to consciously create opportunities to interact with people.

Information leaks carry the risk of immediate contract termination.

Preventing shoulder surfing when working in cafes, using a VPN, strict password management, installing antivirus software—these basic security measures are essential. Freelancers with low security awareness are avoided for that reason alone.

Demonstrating that you are "someone who can work safely even from home" leads to earning clients' trust.

Full remote projects are highly popular, so competition to win them is fierce.

Since engineers can apply from anywhere in the country without location constraints, many engineers flock to a single project. In particular, high-rate remote projects with good conditions often fill up soon after posting.

With so many applicants, clients also tend to be more selective. Hurdles can be raised, such as "two years of experience is fine for on-site work, but three or more years is required for remote work." Since it's hard to win contracts without high-level skills that make you immediately effective, a solid strategy is to start with on-site projects and gradually transition to full remote work.

Considering the pros and cons, the characteristics of people suited and not suited to full remote work become clear.

Characteristics of people who are suited to it

  • Good at self-management and able to concentrate even when no one is watching
  • Able to think and act on their own rather than waiting for instructions (self-driven)
  • Comfortable with text-based communication
  • Has a clear purpose for wanting to work fully remotely, such as "I want to move to the countryside" or "I want to balance work with childcare"

If your motivation is merely something like "I just don't like commuting," you're likely to give up when facing the challenges of full remote work. People with a clear purpose can overcome even considerable difficulties.

Characteristics of people who are not suited to it

  • Can't concentrate unless someone is watching
  • Performs better with direction and oversight from bosses and colleagues
  • Prefers face-to-face communication
  • Still inexperienced and needs support from senior colleagues

If someone not suited to it aims for full remote work, we recommend first gaining experience in on-site projects and honing self-management skills and online communication skills before taking on the challenge.

To continuously win full remote projects and earn a stable income, you need a strategy. Beyond technical skill, building trust that makes clients think "I want to work with this person" is the most important thing. Here, we explain concrete strategies from winning projects to building trust and raising your rates.

To reliably secure full remote projects, speed and thorough information gathering are key.

As mentioned earlier, full remote projects are highly competitive and often fill up soon after being posted. The ironclad rule is to apply immediately when you think "this project looks great." Many people have experienced thinking they'd apply later, only to find the deadline had already passed.

Use multiple platforms in parallel to broaden your information gathering. By registering with freelance agents, you may also be introduced to non-public projects. Regularly check new listings on project sites, and be ready to act immediately when an interesting project appears.

Also, getting projects introduced through engineer communities and personal networks is an effective approach. You may be approached with "I want to entrust this remote project to you because you're trustworthy," so building connections daily expands your opportunities.

What clients look for in a remote engineer is not just technical skill. Since they are entrusting work to someone they never see face-to-face, the sense of trust that "I can rely on this person" matters above all else.

Traits of a Trusted Engineer

  • Accurate reporting, communication, and consultation, sharing necessary information at the right time
  • Quick responses and no communication gaps
  • Meeting deadlines (or consulting early if a deadline cannot be met)
  • Reporting problems promptly instead of hiding them
  • Aiming to deliver results that exceed expectations

Because progress is harder to see in remote work, proactively reporting regularly gives clients peace of mind that things are moving forward properly. A freelancer who reports, communicates, and consults without being asked is a hugely appreciated presence for clients.

Another effective strategy is to work on-site at first to build trust, then switch to remote. Rather than insisting from the start that "it has to be fully remote or nothing," being flexible can actually be the faster path. Once trust is established, it becomes easier to get approval with "If it's Mr./Ms. XX, working from home should be fine."

To land high-rate projects, it is important to effectively showcase your skills and track record.

Publishing code on GitHub, sharing knowledge on a technical blog, and maintaining a portfolio site—this kind of "visualization" sets you apart in the competition for projects. Make sure anyone can tell at a glance, "This person can do this kind of work."

It is also important to clearly communicate your strengths to your agent's coordinator. Since coordinators also act as sales representatives, share plenty of material they can use to promote you. Concrete results like "I doubled processing speed in the development of the XX system" add persuasiveness.

Rate negotiations are most effective after you have delivered results under your initial contract. Simply saying "please raise my rate" right away will not work. Show that your skill improvements have increased your contribution to the project, and propose compensation that matches it. Concrete grounds such as "I obtained the XX certification" or "I learned a new technology and my productivity improved" make negotiations go smoothly.

Remote projects are more plentiful than you might imagine, and with the right skills and preparation, high-rate projects are entirely attainable. As the data shows—about 50% of freelance engineers work primarily remotely—remote work is no longer a special way of working.

However, engineers who can thrive long-term in fully remote roles possess, in addition to technical skill, "the ability to earn clients' trust."

Thorough reporting and communication, quick responses, quality deliverables, and honest handling when problems arise—these accumulate into trust, leading to repeat orders and higher rates. Precisely because remote work lacks face-to-face contact, whether clients feel "I can trust this person" is the deciding factor for success.
