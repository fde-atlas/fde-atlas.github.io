Why AI Pilots Fail: How FDEs Resolve Frontline Employee Resistance

Original title: AI 试点为什么失败：FDE 如何化解一线员工抵触

FDE Times · 2026-10-09 · 5 min read

Summary

This guide argues that AI pilots often fail not because of the model but because frontline workers distrust it, citing a PwC and Manufacturing Institute survey where 62% of frontline employees are skeptical. Using a factory quality-inspection example, it shows how FDEs should treat resistance as data, translate objections into concrete concerns (surveillance, opaque decisions, accountability, job loss), and fix them through transparent interfaces, human approval, and written commitments. It also recommends outcome-based metrics and a named owner for frontline feedback in the first 90 days.

Key points

  • 62% of frontline workers distrust AI; objections hide specific concerns.
  • FDEs should shadow shifts and convert resistance into design requirements.
  • Five fixes include showing model reasoning, human approval, and no performance tracking.
  • Measure pilot success by inspection time and missed defects, not usage counts.
  • Assign a named owner for feedback in the first 90 days; scale via peer champions.

62% of frontline workers are skeptical of AI, and behind every objection lies a specific concern—such as fear of being monitored. The FDE's job is to uncover these concerns and design them away.

Key Takeaways

  • According to a 2026 survey by PwC and the Manufacturing Institute, 62% of frontline workers are skeptical of AI, and only 24% are excited about it.
  • They are skeptical because they can't see how AI makes decisions and worry about being monitored; the solution is to show the machine's reasoning and have humans approve the results.
  • Don't measure a pilot by usage counts; in the first 90 days, designate a specific person to listen to feedback and act on it.

Three weeks into the pilot, the usage curve started to decline. Team leads still logged in, but mostly ignored the model's recommendations. At a pre-shift meeting, a quality inspector asked point-blank: "Is this thing here to score us?"

This scenario is common. A 2026 survey by PwC and the Manufacturing Institute found that 62% of frontline workers are skeptical of AI, and only 24% say they are excited about it. Even after management has signed the contract, the actual users may still harbor doubts at the start.

For the Forward Deployed Engineer (FDE), this is core work. Salesforce describes the FDE as someone who works closely with customers to remove bottlenecks and accelerate AI adoption. If the people actually using the tool don't trust it, that too is a bottleneck—and removing it falls to the FDE.

Every Objection Hides a Specific Concern

The first mistake engineers most often make is treating resistance as noise, as a psychological issue that will pass if you wait it out. Resistance is actually data. Prosci, a firm specializing in change management, found that among frontline workers and managers, skepticism deepens when they can't see how AI arrives at its decisions.

There is also a harder-to-voice concern. According to ITPro, once employees see AI as a tool for monitoring them, they respond passively and find ways to work around it. The improvement loop then breaks down: if no one corrects bad recommendations, the model learns nothing from the field.

So when you hear an objection, the first step is to translate it into the real concern. The table below is a "reference manual" you can bring to the shop floor.

What the user saysThe real concernWhat the FDE should do
"Is this machine here to score us?"Being monitoredKeep correction records separate from individual performance reports, and say so explicitly
"It's just guessing"Can't understand how the machine decidesShow the reasoning and confidence level for every recommendation
"If the machine is wrong, who takes the blame?"Taking the fall for the machineMake a human the final approver
"How many of us will still be needed?"Losing their jobAddress this question first, not last

The last row matters most. MindStudio recommends handling the fear of job loss first. Avoid it, and every subsequent training session becomes a box-checking exercise.

How a Pilot Was Rescued

Suppose an electronics components factory has a 12-person quality inspection team trial a model that reads images and suggests defect types. In the first week, everyone is curious; by the third week, the proportion of recommendations marked "accepted" has dropped sharply.

That timing is no coincidence. Worklytics, a company that sells analytics tools, has behavioral data showing that AI tool usage tends to drop noticeably after the third week: the novelty wears off and real friction begins.

This is the vendor's own data, so treat it as a reminder: that week, be on the shop floor, not in the office staring at dashboards.

The first step is to shadow a team lead through a full shift. In PwC's survey, 45% of respondents said their company's AI projects failed partly because of insufficient involvement from frontline leaders. The conversation might go like this:

FDE: Which defects does the machine get wrong most often? Team lead: Scratches and dust. It mistakes dust for scratches, and scratches mean the part gets scrapped. FDE: If the machine could tell you which part of the image it was looking at, would the judgment be easier to evaluate? Team lead: Yes. But when people correct it, is anyone keeping track? Does it count against each person's score? FDE: Every correction is recorded and used to retrain the model, but it is never linked to anyone's name in performance reports. This will be written into the project documentation—you can check it yourself.

That conversation produced five changes, the first three coming directly from the team lead's words. The first was the interface: showing the image regions the model relied on, its confidence level, and a few similar historical cases.

The second was decision authority: the quality inspector confirms or corrects, and every correction becomes training data. The third was a written commitment: correction records do not go into individual performance reports.

These three changes also form the basis for reporting to management: as Prosci puts it, trust comes from transparency and human oversight; when humans and AI review together, accountability is clear.

The fourth change was how success is measured. People using the tool doesn't mean the pilot succeeded. For a quality inspection team, better metrics are inspection time per batch, the number of missed defects, and what proportion of inspectors' corrections make it into the new model.

The fifth change was designating a specific person who, for the first 90 days, is responsible for listening to frontline feedback and actually acting on it. Put that person's name in the plan, plus a weekly meeting with the team leads.

Users must see their feedback turn into updates, or they will stop offering it.

To Scale, Start with Peers

Once the first team is stable, don't rush to roll out plant-wide. Worklytics recommends building a network of peer "champions": identify naturally influential people in each group and give them deeper training before the broad rollout.

The night shift would rather hear about a fellow quality inspector's experience than that of an outside engineer.

The remaining work is inside your own company. Salesforce says its FDE team feeds everything learned in the field back to the product and engineering teams. Dust being misclassified as scratches is a model defect; the concern about "will this count against our score" is a product design requirement, deserving its own ticket.

Mistakes That Slowly Kill a Pilot

The most common mistake is demoing only to the boss and assuming users are already on board. The second is hiding how the model works on the grounds that users "don't need to know." The third is letting usage logs quietly become an employee leaderboard—which is exactly the surveillance concern ITPro describes.

Two more mistakes are harder to spot. One is reporting usage counts as value created; the other is collecting feedback and letting it sit with no one accountable for acting on it. All five mistakes share the same root: treating frontline users as recipients of the product when they are actually its co-designers.

If you're preparing to move into an FDE role, watch for phrases like "user adoption," "change management," and "working with frontline teams" in job descriptions.

On your resume, a line like "After interviewing end users, added explanations to every model recommendation; adoption rate improved from X to Y" carries more weight than three lines of framework listings.

In interviews, be ready to describe a time a user resisted you and what you changed afterward.

Salesforce's FDE director once warned that without FDEs, thousands of customers could remain stuck in the pilot stage forever. For the quality inspection team in our example, what pulled the project out of the mud wasn't a better model—it was a person willing to walk onto the shop floor and ask the team lead what they were worried about.
