Evals Baseline Building Playbook: Turning "Feels Usable" into Regression-Testable Numbers

Original title: 评测基线建设 Playbook：把「感觉可用」变成可回归的数字

FDE中国 · 2026-09-04 · 7 min read

Summary

This article gives a seven-step playbook for building evaluation baselines for Agent projects, aimed at teams with limited resources, sensitive client data, and no dedicated eval engineers. The steps cover collecting use cases, layering them by difficulty, annotation with agreed judgment criteria, metric selection, writing a baseline report, setting regression cadence, and alerting. Key arguments: use cases must come from real traffic and client acceptance scenarios rather than engineer imagination; each layer should track only two or three clearly defined metrics; and the biggest mistake is treating baseline building as a months-long perfectionist project—start with 30–50 rough samples and a minimal regression, then thicken the set over time. Regressions should finish within 15–20 minutes, and in China, data-egress boundaries must be confirmed upfront.

Key points

  • Collect use cases from real traffic and client acceptance scenarios, not engineer imagination.
  • Layer use cases into at least three tiers; mixing pools hides regressions on core paths.
  • Pick two or three clearly defined metrics per layer; the metric definition table matters most.
  • Start with 30–50 rough samples and a minimal regression rather than a perfect annotation set.
  • Keep regressions under 15–20 minutes and run them on every prompt, model, or tool change.

The most dangerous moment for an Agent project is not launch day, but the first major revision: swapping models, restructuring prompts, adjusting tool-calling logic. After the change, the team gathers around a screen clicking through a dozen cases; some say it's better, some say it's worse, and in the end the loudest voice decides. The problem isn't the people—it's the lack of a baseline: no fixed set of use cases, no set of clearly defined metrics, no report recording the current level. This article lays out a complete approach to baseline building in seven numbered steps, each covering what to do, the deliverables, and common pitfalls, designed from an architectural perspective and assuming limited resources, sensitive customer data, and no dedicated eval engineer on the team. It continues the validation phase in the project lifecycle and is a prerequisite for the "regression is executable" item in the PoC acceptance checklist.

Step 1: Use case collection—come from real traffic, not from imagination

What to do: Collect use cases from three sources: real traffic (sampling by frequency and failure rate from desensitized request logs), customer acceptance scenarios (operations explicitly named in contracts or acceptance documents), and expert experience (the "situations newcomers most easily get wrong" as described by the customer's veteran practitioners). For each use case, record the input, expected output or expected behavior, source, and an estimate of occurrence frequency. Aim to accumulate thirty to fifty cases first; don't get greedy.

Deliverable: A use case registry with fields including at least: ID, input, expected behavior, source, frequency, and difficulty tag.

Common pitfalls: Use cases entirely hand-crafted by engineers—they look clean and tidy, all "standard happy paths," and precisely fail to cover where things break in production. Another pitfall is collecting only failure cases, so the baseline is all hard problems and the quality of normal paths becomes invisible.

Step 2: Layering—use cases of different difficulty must not be mixed into one pool for scoring

What to do: Divide use cases into at least three layers. Layer 1, core paths: high-frequency, highly deterministic operations with high failure costs, such as a standard query or a standard document generation; Layer 2, edge scenarios: non-standard inputs, multi-turn follow-ups, situations requiring intent clarification; Layer 3, known difficulties: scenarios the current solution clearly handles poorly but the customer has agreed to cover manually for now. The layering criteria are jointly confirmed by the team and the customer and written into documentation.

Deliverable: A use case set annotated with layers, plus a one-page layering definition document.

Common pitfalls: Scoring a single total without layering—after a change the total goes up, which may mean the core paths improved, or that a hard problem was luckily guessed right while core paths quietly got worse. Mixed-pool scoring is the most insidious pitfall in Agent evals. Layer 3 should have a cap: too high a proportion of hard problems gives the team a pessimistic illusion about overall quality. Layering is also not fixed once and for all: at monthly reviews, use cases may migrate between layers, but each migration must be recorded, otherwise historical numbers lose comparability.

Step 3: Annotation—first define "what counts as correct," then label

What to do: For each use case, first confirm with the customer or business experts "what counts as correct": for cases with a unique standard answer, record the answer; for open-ended outputs, write three to five acceptable judgment points, such as "must include conclusion X, must not contain fact Y, tone must meet requirement Z." Annotation is done independently by two people; inconsistent items are discussed and adjudicated. For confidential customer data, the desensitization plan must be determined at this stage, confirming what can enter the eval set and what can only be used onsite.

Deliverable: A one-page annotation guideline (judgment criteria, examples, dispute adjudication records) + the use case set after dual annotation.

Common pitfalls: Skipping the judgment criteria and labeling directly—two weeks later no one remembers why a given case was judged correct; using automatic metrics (such as string similarity) to replace human judgment of open-ended outputs—the numbers look good but have nothing to do with what the business considers "correct."

Step 4: Metric selection—pick only two or three clearly explainable metrics per layer

What to do: For core paths, track three items: task success rate (proportion completed end-to-end with results judged correct), average latency, and per-run cost; for edge scenarios, track clarification rate (whether clarification is proactively offered when needed) and hallucination rate (proportion of fabricated non-existent facts); for known difficulties, only record pass rate for trend observation, with no target line. All metrics must have their calculation definitions written clearly in documentation: what the denominator is, and whether edge cases count as correct or incorrect.

Deliverable: A metric definition table (metric name, definition, denominator, applicable layer, collection method)—this table matters more than the metrics themselves.

Common pitfalls: "More metrics is better" is an illusion—nobody looks at a ten-metric dashboard, and in retrospectives everyone just argues about definitions. Better five metrics with clear definitions than twenty vague ones. Also beware of treating LLM-as-judge scores as objective truth; the judge itself needs spot-check calibration.

Step 5: Baseline report—pin "the current level" on paper

What to do: Run a full round of evals before the system enters frequent changes, and write the results into a baseline report containing: the use case set version number and count distribution, numbers for each metric in each layer, about ten representative pass and failure cases (with input/output excerpts), and a list of known weaknesses. Keep the report within three pages so someone not involved can read it in fifteen minutes. The baseline report should be sent to key people on the customer side for confirmation, to avoid later disputes over "what counts as a normal level."

Deliverable: A baseline report within three pages + a frozen eval set v1 + a one-click executable eval script.

Common pitfalls: Running the baseline with the strongest model and the most finely tuned prompts—the baseline is inflated, and every subsequent change looks like a regression. The baseline should record "the real level of the current delivered version"; being mediocre is fine—it's for comparison, not for showing off.

Step 6: Regression cadence—which changes must trigger a run, and what to compare against afterward

What to do: Set three iron rules: if any of the following occurs—changing prompts, swapping model versions, adjusting tool or retrieval logic—a full regression must be run; for daily iteration, run the core path layer daily or every two days for fast feedback; run once before each iteration branch is merged, with results posted in the merge request. All regression results are compared against the baseline report and written into a timestamped results archive, forming a traceable historical series. Keep regression runtime within a cup of coffee (fifteen to twenty minutes): regressions over half an hour are almost inevitably skipped, so plan eval set size together with runtime—prefer a small, precise core layer over a large, slow full set.

Deliverable: Written regression rules + a time-ordered series of regression results.

Common pitfalls: Not running regression for small changes—in an Agent system there is no such thing as "just changed one word"; a single adjective can drop edge-scenario success rate by a dozen points. Another pitfall is only running regression after something breaks; numbers produced afterward cannot answer "did this change break it."

Step 7: Alerting—detect degradation before the customer does

What to do: Set a floor for task success rate on the core path (e.g., trigger an alert if it drops below the baseline report number minus five percentage points), combined with online spot checks: sample a fixed number of items from real traffic each day and automatically run them through the eval set for judgment. The actions to take after an alert fires should be written down in advance: who is responsible, which changes to check first, and how long until a conclusion is due. When online metrics and offline eval numbers diverge directionally (offline improves, online worsens), always defer to the online numbers and go back to check whether the eval set is outdated.

Deliverables: An alert rule document (thresholds, owners, response actions) + routine results from daily online spot checks.

Common pitfalls: Setting the alert threshold too sensitively, so it's crying wolf every day and the team learns to ignore alerts; or alerting without defining response actions, so alert emails sit in the inbox as if there were no alerts at all. The eval set itself also needs maintenance: review new failure modes appearing online once a month, incorporate the ones that belong in the eval set, and bump the version number.

Summary and next steps

The essence of baseline building is spending effort once to move the team from "arguing over every change" to "looking at the numbers for every change." Of the seven steps, the ones you should least skip are step two, layering, and step four's metric definition table—they determine whether the numbers are trustworthy; the most commonly overvalued is the size of the labeled set: thirty to fifty clearly layered test cases beat three hundred samples from a mixed pool. The baseline report serves both as the basis for internal changes and as an anchor for aligning with customers on what "normal" looks like, directly supporting the task success rate and cost metrics in delivery quality measurement. To understand from the design principles of agent systems why these steps break down, read further in the Agent topic; for how practice-level documentation is organized, see the FDE knowledge guide.
