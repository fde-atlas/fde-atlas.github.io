Sharpening Tax (2026) Explained for FDEs

FDE Interviews · 2026-10-26 · 3 min read

Summary

The Sharpening Tax paper examines how post-training reshapes an agent's success distribution: a tuned model may solve more tasks on the first attempt while losing coverage that a base policy recovers through repeated independent rollouts. Comparing 14 base/post-trained model pairs across three interactive benchmarks, the authors find frequent tradeoffs between single-attempt accuracy and pass@k-style coverage, summarized by a normalized 'tax' statistic on test-time scalability. They also propose Posterior-Tempered Group Sampling (PTGS), which adapts RL rollout temperature to prompt difficulty—more exploration on hard prompts, more concentration on easy ones—improving both accuracy and coverage over fixed-temperature baselines in controlled experiments. The metric needs careful reading: a positive tax can mean lost recoverable tasks or simply faster success that benefits less from retries. For FDEs, the takeaway is that one-shot assistants and retry-capable offline systems need different success profiles, and retries cost tokens, latency, evaluator capacity, and can be unsafe for state-mutating agents.

Key points

  • Post-training can raise first-attempt accuracy while reducing coverage from repeated rollouts.
  • The study compares 14 model pairs across three interactive benchmarks.
  • PTGS adapts RL sampling temperature to prompt difficulty, improving accuracy and coverage.
  • A positive tax may reflect lost tasks or faster success—interpret it with absolute metrics.
  • FDEs should define the operating point and budget before choosing coverage or consistency.

The paper in one minute

Sharpening Tax studies how post-training changes an agent's success distribution. A tuned model may solve more tasks on its first attempt while repeatedly failing a subset that a less concentrated base policy occasionally solves. That distinction matters when a system can generate several candidates and reliably recognize a successful one.

The authors compare base and post-trained model pairs on interactive benchmarks. Base models receive a lightweight harness that helps them use the required interfaces. The study reports frequent tradeoffs between single-attempt accuracy and success after many independent rollouts, then introduces a statistic summarizing the change in test-time scalability.

The paper also proposes posterior-tempered group sampling, or PTGS. During RL training, it estimates prompt difficulty and varies sampling temperature: harder prompts receive more exploration, while easier prompts receive more concentrated sampling. The reported controlled experiments improve both accuracy and coverage relative to their fixed-temperature baselines.

The metric needs careful interpretation. A positive tax can arise because a policy loses recoverable tasks, but it can also arise because it succeeds sooner and therefore benefits less from retries. Read the tax beside absolute success, consistency and cost. The official project and code provide the study's implementation and experimental context.

Why it matters to an FDE

A one-shot support assistant and an offline coding search system need different success profiles. The assistant may value a strong first answer and predictable behavior. The coding system may afford several isolated attempts, provided tests can identify a useful result. A single leaderboard score cannot represent both requirements.

The paper provides vocabulary for that decision, but repeated attempts are not free. They consume tokens, latency and evaluator capacity. For agents that mutate state, retries may also require a clean environment or explicit recovery semantics; blindly repeating an irreversible action is not equivalent to independent sampling.

Use inference-time compute to define the real budget and trajectory evaluation to define success. If the system cannot distinguish a good candidate from a bad one, oracle pass@k coverage overstates the result a user will receive.
