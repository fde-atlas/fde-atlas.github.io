FDE Project Scoping: The Why, What, How Three-Layer Approach

Original title: FDE 项目范围界定：why、what、how 三层方法

FDE Times · 2026-10-08 · 5 min read

Summary

This guide argues that FDEs should scope projects across three layers—why (business reason), what (measurable outcome), and how (the system built)—before writing any code. It uses Databricks' Forward Deployed Engineering team, launched June 2026, as an example: projects are anchored on OKRs shared with customers, replacing rigid statements of work. The why and what are typically settled jointly with Account Executives and Engagement Managers, while the how belongs to the FDE. The article offers a five-step method, a one-page briefing template, common mistakes like writing deliverables instead of outcomes, and advice for showcasing this skill in resumes and interviews.

Key points

  • Customer requests like "we need an agent" are usually just the how.
  • Databricks anchors projects on OKRs shared with customers.
  • Why and what are confirmed jointly with AEs and Engagement Managers.
  • Writing the what as a deliverable rather than an outcome is a common mistake.
  • Resumes and interviews should present projects in why-what-how order.

Key Takeaways

  • "We need an agent" is just one possible answer to the "how." Before opening the IDE, an FDE must work backward to the "why" and the "what."
  • Databricks anchors every project with OKRs shared with the customer. OKRs are how the "what" gets locked in before work begins.
  • The "how" is built directly by the FDE, while the "why" and the "what" are usually settled together with the Account Executive (AE) and the Engagement Manager. Shared ownership does not mean the FDE can skip these two layers.

Monday morning, customer kickoff meeting. The VP of Operations opens with a familiar line: "We need an AI agent to answer customer questions." As the Forward Deployed Engineer (FDE) in the room, your first instinct is probably to think about which model to use, where the data will come from, and what infrastructure to deploy on.

That instinct points in the right direction—it just arrives too early. The customer's statement is only one possible answer to the "how," and before it come two questions that no one in the room has answered: why the change is needed, and what counts as success.

The skill to practice here is breaking every project into three questions: why, what, and how. You need to know which question you answer and which ones you answer with others; and you should not write the first line of code while the first two questions are still blank.

What makes the three questions different?

The "why" is the business reason: what the customer is losing, or what opportunity they are missing. The "what" is a measurable outcome—the number both sides agree will judge the project. The "how" is the system you build: data pipelines, models, agents, system integrations, and how the whole thing runs once it goes live.

The three layers are kept separate because each fails differently. Get the "why" wrong, and you will polish something nobody needs. Get the "what" wrong, and the project never ends, because no one knows what "done" means. Get the "how" wrong, and at least you can still see the errors in the logs.

Why/what/how is not any company's official terminology—it is simply a convenient way to divide up the thinking. That said, Databricks' description of its forward deployed engineering team, established in June 2026, reflects this logic quite clearly.

Databricks locks in the "what" with OKRs

In its announcement, Databricks said customer demand has shifted from data pipelines and migration toward business problems. Its stated mission is to accelerate customers' business outcomes with AI.

Pulse 2.0 describes the same trend: customers are moving away from the "build and hand off" consulting model toward engineering teams that work inside their organizations and are accountable for measurable outcomes. That is the "why" of the whole model.

The "what" is stated directly: projects are anchored to the customer's business outcomes through shared OKRs. Even before this, Databricks' professional services organization had replaced rigid statements of work (SOWs) with OKR-based ways of working and continuous collaboration with customers.

Jason Martin, Databricks' VP responsible for FDEs, said at an Insight Partners event that this work cannot revolve around hours and headcount—it must revolve around outcomes.

The "how" belongs to the FDE. According to Databricks, the FDE's job is to take the platform in its raw form and turn it into a business solution that actually runs in the customer's environment. When the platform cannot yet do what the customer needs, the FDE works directly with the R&D team to extend it.

So who answers the "why" and the "what"? Not the FDE alone. Insight Partners' record of the event notes that Databricks' pre-sales and post-sales have now merged into a single workflow. The job posting for the company's Manager, FDE role requires the hire to work with Account Executives, Engagement Managers, and Field Engineering leads to plan and deliver projects.

Partners can bring broader expertise, but Databricks does not say they own the "why" or the "what."

Example: "We need a chatbot"

Back to that kickoff meeting, this time set at a hypothetical consumer electronics retailer in Vietnam. Instead of pushing back on the agent idea, you keep asking your way up the chain.

"What's the biggest headache in customer service right now?" Suppose the answer is: every promotion season, the call center gets overwhelmed, customers wait too long, and simply abandon their orders. That is the "why." Next question: "If this project succeeds six months from now, which number on your dashboard will you look at?" That answer becomes the "what."

After the meeting, you write a one-page brief like this and send it to both the customer and the Account Executive:

LayerContent (hypothetical)Who signs off
WhyCall center overwhelmed during promotions; customers abandon orders due to long waitsVP of Operations
What (Objective)Customers get fast answers without adding call center headcountCustomer + AE/Engagement Manager
What (Key Results)Median wait time drops by X%; Y% of order issues resolved automatically; share of customers escalated to humans does not increaseSigned off by both sides
HowAn agent that reads the order system, escalates to humans when unsure, and uses call center logs to build an evals setFDE

Note three things. X and Y are deliberately left blank, because those numbers should be filled in by the customer, not you. The "How" row mentions an evals set, because a Key Result only matters if it can be measured. And once the "what" is written down, the "how" may no longer be a chatbot.

The most effective approach might simply be automatically pushing order status updates.

Five steps to do it yourself

Step one: write down the customer's request verbatim and label it. It is almost always a "how." Step two: use questions about pain points and the cost of the status quo to work your way up to the "why"—don't ask technical questions.

Step three: turn the "why" into an Objective with two or three Key Results that have metrics and deadlines. Step four: determine who signs off on each layer. If your organization has an AE or an Engagement Manager, bring them in now—don't wait for a disagreement.

Step five: only after the "what" is signed off do you start designing the "how." If during the build you find the platform lacks a needed capability, record it as a requirement for the product team—don't quietly patch around it.

Common Mistakes

The most common mistake is writing the "what" as a deliverable: "deploy an agent," "complete the migration." That is the work you will do, not the outcome the customer gets. A simple test: if the project can be "completed" while the customer's situation has not improved, you have written a deliverable.

The second mistake is letting the "why" live only in one person's head—usually whoever signed the contract. Once that person moves on, the team no longer knows what it is optimizing for. The third mistake is treating collaboration with the sales team as someone else's job.

As the CTO of JPMorgan Chase's Consumer & Community Banking put it, they don't need more consultants—they need engineers who can build what doesn't yet exist. But to build the right thing, engineers still need to understand why people need it.

Does your resume speak to the "what"?

For developers looking to transition into an FDE role, this ability shows up most clearly in your resume and interviews. When reading job descriptions, watch for phrases like "business outcomes," "OKRs," and "partner with Account Executives"—they signal that the company expects you to hold conversations on three levels.

For every project on your resume, "built..." should be followed by "to achieve...". When asked about past projects in interviews, walk through them in the order of why, what, and how, rather than jumping straight into the tech stack.

This week's exercise: take the biggest ticket on your plate and try writing three lines for it: why, what, and how. If you've spent ten minutes on the "what" line without a single number, your next step should be to go ask the person who assigned you that ticket, not to write code.
