Palantir & OpenAI FDEs vs Independent Teams

Ryz Labs · 2026-10-03 · 5 min read

Summary

This Ryz Labs piece contrasts vendor forward deployed engineers with independent FDE teams. Palantir (which popularized the role, internally called Deltas), OpenAI, and Anthropic all staff FDEs whose job is making the vendor's own product succeed, often bundled with large platform commitments. Independent teams have no product to sell, build on the client's existing stack, and own the result end to end. The author recommends vendor FDEs for rolling out an already-chosen platform, independent teams for systems spanning multiple vendors, and describes a common hybrid setup plus questions buyers should ask before committing.

Key points

  • Palantir FDEs (Deltas) focus on one customer, deploying its own platforms like Foundry and AIP.
  • OpenAI and Anthropic FDEs own deployments from scoping through production rollout.
  • Vendor FDE strengths: deep product knowledge, proven patterns, bundled cost, early access.
  • Limitations: product-first design, scope stops at product boundary, rotation, quiet lock-in.
  • A hybrid model works: vendor FDEs handle their product; an independent team owns the whole system.

Full text

Forward deployed engineers from Palantir, OpenAI or Anthropic work for the vendor, and their job is to make that vendor's product succeed in your organization. They are excellent at that, and often included with a large platform or model commitment. An independent forward-deployed team has no product to sell, so it builds on the stack you already run and can choose, or mix, models and clouds on the merits. Use vendor FDEs to roll out a platform you have already bought. Use an independent team when the system is yours to design and own.

Palantir made "forward deployed software engineer" a well-known title. Its careers pages describe the role as engineers working directly with customers on their hardest problems. In its engineering blog, Palantir explains the internal nicknames: product engineers are "Devs," and forward deployed engineers are "Deltas," a leftover from early business-development teams named after letters of the NATO alphabet. A Dev focuses on one capability for many customers; a Delta focuses on one customer and many capabilities. Palantir also has a related role, the deployment strategist (internally "Echo"), which sits closer to product management.

The important point for buyers: Palantir's FDEs deploy Palantir's platforms, such as Foundry and AIP. The configuration, data models and applications they build are built on those platforms.

OpenAI has built a forward deployed engineering team and posts FDE roles in cities including San Francisco, Seattle, New York, Munich and Tokyo. Its postings describe FDEs owning a customer deployment from discovery and technical scoping through system design, build and production rollout, working directly with the customer's engineering and domain teams, and feeding what they learn back into OpenAI's products.

Anthropic hires forward deployed engineers in its Applied AI organization. Its postings describe hands-on deployment work with large customers, building production systems on Claude, including integrations such as MCP servers and agent workflows, and documenting repeatable deployment patterns for Anthropic's own product and engineering teams.

Cloud providers and enterprise software companies have followed, with FDE postings of their own. The pattern is the same: a vendor puts engineers next to its largest customers to turn a purchase into daily use, and to learn what the product should do next.

  • Deep product knowledge. They know the platform's internals, its limits and its roadmap, and can reach the vendor's product team directly.
  • Proven deployment patterns. They have seen the same product rolled out at many companies and know where it usually breaks.
  • Often bundled. FDE time is frequently included with a large commitment, so the incremental cost to you can be low.
  • Early access. Vendor FDEs may bring features and model versions before they are generally available.

None of this is a criticism of the people. It follows from who pays them.

  • The product is the starting point. A vendor FDE will design around their product. If a different model, database or open-source tool fits part of the problem better, that is not their call to make.
  • Scope stops at the product boundary. The integration with your ten-year-old case-management system, the data cleanup in your warehouse, the evaluation harness your risk team wants: vendor FDEs help where it serves adoption, but they are not your engineering team.
  • They rotate. Vendor FDEs move to the next strategic account. The knowledge leaves with them unless your team captured it.
  • Lock-in grows quietly. Each workflow built on a proprietary platform raises the cost of changing it later. That is a fine trade if you chose the platform deliberately; it is a problem if it happened by default.
  • Multi-vendor problems have no owner. Most enterprise AI systems touch a model provider, a cloud, a data platform and internal systems. Each vendor's FDEs own their piece. Someone still has to own the whole.

These models are not exclusive, and many enterprises use both. A common setup for enterprise AI looks like this:

  • You choose a model provider and a cloud, and the vendor's solutions engineers and FDEs help with initial setup and best practices.
  • An independent forward-deployed team builds the actual system: the data pipelines, the retrieval layer, the integrations with your internal platforms, the evaluation suite and the monitoring.
  • Vendor FDEs stay available for product questions, quota issues and early-access features.
  • Your own engineers pair with the independent team and take over the system, or the team stays on for the next workflow.

The independent team is the one accountable for the result working end to end. The vendors are accountable for their products working.

  • What will be built on your platform, and what would it take to move it later?
  • Who owns custom code, prompts and evaluation sets?
  • How long will the same engineers be assigned to us?
  • Who integrates the parts your product doesn't cover, and who is on call for them?
  • If another model performs better on our evaluations for one task, can we use it?

If the answers are clear and you are comfortable with them, vendor FDEs may be all you need. If not, you need an engineering team of your own for that work, either hired or brought in.
