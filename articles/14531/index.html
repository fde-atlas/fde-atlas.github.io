Pre-sales FDE work: when the engineer helps win the deal

FDE Interview Prep · 2026-09-25 · 14 min read

Summary

The article examines FDE roles that include pre-sales work: some forward deployed engineers join deals before signing, doing discovery, demos and proofs of concept. The dividing line is whether the same engineer then builds and runs what was sold; if someone else implements the demo, it is sales engineering. Evidence comes from job postings at OpenAI, LangChain, Baseten, Deloitte and Anthropic, plus reporting from The Pragmatic Engineer. The piece also covers interview preparation: scoping a small first version, demoing without overpromising, avoiding commercial commitments, and a worked six-step answer for a skeptical hospital customer.

Key points

  • OpenAI and LangChain postings span pre-sales through post-production ownership.
  • Anthropic lists a Pre-Sales Program Lead for Forward Deployed Engineering.
  • The test: if someone else builds what you demoed, it is sales engineering.
  • Interviews probe scoping with sales present and naming stubbed demo parts.
  • A worked example shows proposing a pass-bar POC on the customer's own data.

You found an posting that says “pre-sales” and now wonder if it is a sales job with an engineer’s title. It isn’t, if you also build what gets sold: some forward deployed engineer (FDE) roles join after the contract is signed, and some help get it signed, like answering an account executive’s “can you join Thursday’s call and show them it works on their data?” For how the whole FDE job compares with sales engineering, read the FDE vs sales engineer guide.

The short answer: yes, some FDE roles include pre-sales work. OpenAI’s financial services FDE posting in New York asks the FDE to lead discovery and scoping “from pre-sales through post-production”. Source 1Forward Deployed Engineer (FDE), Financial Services- NYCPublisherOpenAI (Ashby)Source typecompany job posting LangChain’s Deployed Engineer posting asks the role to own the technical win in pre-sales by designing proofs of concept. Source 2Deployed Engineer (Bay Area) @ LangChainPublisherLangChain (Ashby job board)Source typecompany job posting What keeps it FDE work is that the same engineer then builds and runs what was sold. To prepare for these interviews, we train one skill above the rest: scoping a first version and demoing it without promising more than you can ship.

Pre-sales or post-sale FDE: what changes

Think of the sale as a line: discovery, demos and proofs of concept to its left; the build, the rollout and the on-call to its right.

A post-sale FDE meets the customer to the right of the line. The promises are already made, and part of the job is finding out which ones hold. A pre-sales FDE starts to the left of it and helps decide what gets promised in the first place.

That difference changes your interview prep. A post-sale FDE needs stories about inheriting a messy scope. A pre-sales FDE needs stories about shaping one, often with a salesperson in the room who wants the answer to be yes.

Where it stops being FDE work

Past a certain edge, pre-sales work is sales engineering with a different title.

Our test, for any posting: does the role end at the demo, or at the running system? If someone else builds what you demoed, it is sales engineering. From the postings above, these phrases signal a real pre-sales FDE role when they sit next to “on-call” or “ship production”:

  • “own the technical win”
  • “designing POCs” or proofs of concept
  • “from pre-sales through post-production”

Coming from sales engineering? Our post on moving from sales engineer to FDE covers the interview gaps.

Is a pre-sales FDE just a sales engineer?

Both roles can scope a proof of concept for the same prospect. What differs is what the proof of concept is for. This table is our framing, not a rule any employer publishes:

Question Sales engineer Pre-sales FDE
After signature Hands off to a delivery team Builds and runs it
A shortcut becomes Someone else’s backlog Your own backlog
Success means The deal closes The system runs in production

The middle row is the one interviews probe. A pre-sales FDE’s shortcut becomes their own backlog, so a good one says out loud which parts of the demo are real.

Across all three, our method trains three skills (how we teach, not an employer’s rubric):

  • Scoping a first version the prospect can judge. Small, on their problem, with a pass bar agreed before you build.
  • Demoing without overpromising. Say which parts are live, which are stubbed, and what would change in production.
  • Holding the line on commitments. Commercial terms belong to the account owner. Our free question on procurement asking you for a staffing promise drills exactly this.

A worked scoping answer for an undecided prospect

Here is a prompt of our own. You are the FDE on a call with the director of revenue cycle at a regional hospital network, who is evaluating whether your AI platform can check insurance claims for errors before they are submitted. The account executive is on the call. The director opens: “Your sales team says this works. I’ve heard that before. Convince me.”

Step 1: Refuse the pitch, ask about the decision

Don’t open with features. Find out what she has to decide.

“Fair. Before I show you anything, can I ask what ‘works’ has to mean for you to recommend this? Who else signs off, and what did you try before that didn’t work?”

Suppose she answers: denied claims are the pain, her billing team fixes them by hand, and a previous vendor’s tool flagged so many claims that the team stopped reading the flags. The CFO signs.

Step 2: Play it back

“So the real risk isn’t that we miss errors. It’s that we flag so much your team ignores us, like last time. And your CFO needs to see fewer denials, not a clever model. Is that right?”

Wait for the correction: the playback is where she tells you what you got wrong.

Step 3: Propose a proof of concept small enough to judge

“Here’s what I’d suggest instead of a general demo. Pick one claim type your team corrects most often. Give us last month’s claims of that type, both the ones that were denied and the ones that paid, de-identified. We run our checks and show you two numbers: of the denials caused by errors a check could catch, how many we’d have flagged, and of the claims that paid, how many we’d have flagged by mistake. You set the bar for both before we start.”

This is the core of the answer: it runs on her data, measures her fear (too many flags), and ends in a decision she can take to her CFO.

Step 4: Say what the demo will not show

“To be clear about what this proves: it runs on a copy of your data, not inside your billing system. Connecting to your system, handling access and running it every day is the real project, and I’d be the one building it. I’ll write down what we’d need from your IT team so you can check it before you sign. If de-identifying strips fields our checks need, like dates of service, we’d need a business associate agreement before we touch the real claims, and that goes on the list for your compliance team.”

That is not a formality: the Safe Harbor method in the HIPAA Privacy Rule removes every element of a date tied to a patient except the year, so a date of service does not survive de-identification.

Step 5: Handle the promise question

She will test you: “Can you promise it will catch every error?”

“No, and you shouldn’t trust anyone who does. What I can promise is that you’ll see the misses and the false flags on your own claims before you decide. If the numbers don’t clear your bar, you should walk away, and I’ll tell the account team the same.”

That last sentence costs you something in front of the account executive, which is exactly why the director believes it. Agree it with the AE before the call. It also fits the Anthropic line quoted earlier: a team with more demand than it can serve has to choose well before it closes fast.

Step 6: Close with owners and dates

“So: you send the de-identified extract and the bar. I send the data request and a one-page plan by Friday. We review results together. Pricing and contract terms are for your account executive and you.”

The story to have ready

The behavioral half asks for a time you shaped a scope with sales. A fictional skeleton to swap for your own:

  • Situation. An account executive told a prospect the proof of concept would include live sync with their CRM.
  • Before the call. You told the AE that sync was a month of work and offered a smaller test you could stand behind.
  • The smaller proof of concept. A nightly export of one team’s records, run through the model.
  • The pass bar. The prospect’s ops lead set it in her own words: fewer misrouted records than her team’s manual process, checked by her team.
  • What shipped. The test cleared the bar, the deal signed with sync in phase two, and you built it.

The question when sales promised what you cannot deliver is the full drill.

Mistakes that sink pre-sales rounds

Pitching before diagnosing. You hear “claims” and describe your model. Fix: ask about the decision and the last failed attempt first. Our lesson diagnose before you prescribe is built around this habit.

A happy-path demo. You show the one claim that works. Fix: show a miss on purpose, and say what you would do about it. In our method, that minute is where a skeptic starts to believe the rest.

Promising production results from a proof of concept. “It’ll be the same accuracy in production” is a promise you can’t keep. Fix: name what changes between the demo and production, and who does that work.

Making commercial commitments. Staffing, price and contract timelines are not yours to sign. Fix: acknowledge the ask, name the owner, and offer the assumptions you can write down.

Treating the account executive as the enemy, or as the boss. Both lose the room. Fix: agree the plan with them before the call, and correct a promise together, not in front of the customer.

A proof of concept with no pass bar. Without an agreed bar, a good result closes nothing. Fix: get the bar in the prospect’s words before you build.

Forgetting you will own it. A demo nobody could run in production only moves the problem to your future self. Fix: for every shortcut, say how the real version would work. Sometimes the right answer is that the prospect doesn’t need an embedded engineer at all; our question on when a customer does not need an FDE is that judgment call.

Before a pre-sales round

  • I can say, in one sentence, whether the role ends at the demo or at the running system.
  • I have a story about scoping a first version with a salesperson in the room.
  • I can say “no, and here’s what I can promise” without sounding defensive.
  • I name the stubbed parts of any demo I show.
  • I close with owners, dates and the decision the proof of concept enables.
