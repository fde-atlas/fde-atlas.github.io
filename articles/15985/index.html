FDE job ads up 1,165%: titles counted, not jobs

FDE Times · 2026-10-09 · 6 min read

Summary

The article argues that headline growth figures for forward deployed engineer jobs—800% (Financial Times), 1,165% (Bloomberry) and 5,230% (Indeed)—all count job ads or index values, not actual hires. A curated index counted only 224 open roles at 39 companies as of 30 May 2026. Bloomberry's reading of 1,000 ads found roughly 60% are builder FDE roles, 30% resemble sales engineering and 10% internal tooling. The piece traces the title's origin to Palantir's 2011 rename of solutions engineers, and offers two tests for evaluating ads: whether the work is mostly configuration, and whether customer-side work flows back to change the product.

Key points

  • Growth percentages count job ads, not people hired or real FDE work.
  • A curated index found only 224 open roles at 39 companies.
  • About 60% of 1,000 ads analyzed were builder FDE roles.
  • The FDE title began as Palantir's 2011 rename of solutions engineers.
  • Two tests: is it mostly configuration, and does customer work flow back into the product?

Three sources, three growth rates: more than 800%, 1,165% and 5,230%. Yet on 30 May 2026 a curated index counted only 224 open forward deployed engineer roles, across 39 companies.

The gap does not mean anyone is wrong. It shows that the percentages measure something quite different from what matters to you. They count how often the words “Forward Deployed Engineer” appear on job ads, not the work you would actually do.

For engineers who are FDEs or want to become one, the question worth asking is not how big the market is. It is what the job ad on your screen actually describes. Answering that starts with understanding what each number counts.

Three numbers measure three different things

Bloomberry compared the number of job ads carrying the FDE title from January to October 2025 with the same period in 2024 and arrived at an increase of 1,165%. That method counts titles. A company that renames its solutions engineer role as FDE is added to the total, even if not one more line of code gets written at a customer.

The figure of more than 800% comes from the Financial Times, cited by Fast Company, and covers January to September 2025. It too counts ads, not hires. Fast Company’s own article notes that an FDE’s responsibilities can differ from company to company.

The most startling figure, 5,230%, is cited by KDnuggets from Indeed and compares April 2026 with January 2025. KDnuggets itself warns that it is an index calculated on a small January 2025 base, drawn from a single job site, and does not reflect headcount.

A simple hypothetical shows the problem: a market that goes from 10 ads to 100 has grown 900%, yet 100 ads is still a very small market.

Taken together, the three percentages describe the momentum of a label. Only the absolute figure in the last row hints at scale, and that scale is far smaller than the headlines lead readers to imagine.

One title is being used for three jobs

Counting alone cannot show what lies inside. That is why Bloomberry’s most valuable finding is not the 1,165% figure but the result of reading 1,000 ads closely. The author found that companies are using the same FDE title for three different kinds of work.

About 60% are builder FDE roles, the group Bloomberry describes as the majority. About 30% are close to sales engineering, and about 10% are internal tooling roles. This is one person’s manual classification of a sample, not an official statistic, but it is enough to change how you read job ads.

Imagine you have saved 10 FDE ads to apply for. If Bloomberry’s ratios hold for your list, only about six of them involve building software at the customer.

All 10 carry the same name, but a year in they would give you three very different sets of skills.

Renaming titles is nothing new

According to Tom Hollands of a16z, the FDE title itself was born from a rename. In 2011 Palantir took its solutions engineers and integration engineers and gave them a new name. Hollands calls this title arbitrage: creating a new title to signal new authority taking shape inside an organisation.

The definition rewards a close reading: it is about signalling authority, and says nothing about whether the work changes. Hollands also sees the downside of the mechanism: titles can enter a Red Queen race, in which prestige inflates without substance to match.

By the time a title becomes a hot signal in the market, renaming a role costs almost nothing and still makes the ad more attractive.

Tandem, a company with its own interest in the subject, argues that once the FDE title took off, companies began putting the FDE label on adjacent roles.

According to Tandem, a significant share of the growth in ads comes from renaming. You need not take that claim entirely on trust. It is enough to note that it fits the roughly 40% of ads Bloomberry placed outside the builder group.

Two tests for reading a job ad

Because the aggregate numbers do not help, the sorting has to be done ad by ad. Tandem offers an output-based test: if the ad describes configuration but the title is FDE, the role is an implementation engineer.

KDnuggets offers a second, sharper test: does the work at the customer flow back to change the product? If not, according to KDnuggets, it is consulting with a nicer title.

Apply both tests to two hypothetical ads. Ad A reads: “configure workflows on our platform for enterprise customers, train users, support product demos”.

Ad B reads: “write production code to integrate with customer systems, identify recurring patterns across deployments and work with the product team to build them into the platform”.

Ad A fails both tests. Its main verbs are configure and demonstrate, and there is no path for results to flow back into the product. Ad B passes both: you write software, and what you learn at the customer has somewhere to go in the product.

Ad A is not a bad job. Implementation engineering and sales engineering are respectable professions. The problem is that if you take it believing you are doing FDE work, two years later your CV will tell a different story from the one you meant to tell.

What to do about the hype

When filtering ads, read the verbs first and the title second. Words such as “build”, “ship”, “write production code” or “work with product” usually belong to the builder group. “Configure”, “demo” and “support the sales cycle” lean towards the other two. The job description tells the truth more than the title does.

In interviews, turn the KDnuggets test into a question: when was the last time code or a finding from a deployment made it into the core product, and by what route? A real FDE team can usually answer straight away, with a concrete example. If the interviewer struggles, you have your answer.

When writing your CV, apply the same test to yourself. Do not just write “deployed for 5 customers”. Show the feedback loop, for example an integration pattern you wrote for one customer that later became a shared feature.

A line like that shows you have done FDE work, not merely held the FDE title.

The 1,165% figure will cool, like every growth curve measured on a new label. What will still count then is whether you have actually carried findings from customers into the product.
