As models get stronger, where has engineering work moved to?

Original title: 模型更强以后，工程工作移到了哪里

pafa.github.io · 2026-02-23 · 15 min read

Summary

Drawing on Het Trivedi's account of his first six months as a forward deployed engineer at Baseten, this chapter asks where engineering work moves as models improve. Customers still need models to run reliably within latency, throughput, and cost constraints. The author reviews 2026 announcements from OpenAI, Anthropic, AWS, and Microsoft, arguing that FDE demand depends on what work enterprises take on, not model strength alone: the same model requires very different engineering when it merely assists users, retrieves company data, or takes actions. Product changes like Salesforce Headless 360 and Mistral's open weights can reduce customization, so vendor announcements cannot substitute for assessing whether an FDE team beats well-designed alternatives.

Key points

  • Model progress both creates and eliminates engineering work; net effect is unproven.
  • Same model, three setups (tool, retrieval, actions) demand different engineering rigor.
  • 2026 vendor announcements mix investments, alliances, and goals—not evidence of universal demand.
  • Baseten serves technical clients; Anthropic targets firms lacking internal resources.
  • Compare FDE proposals against internal teams plus mature products before investing.

Looking back on his first six months after joining Baseten in January 2024, Het Trivedi wrote about a set of questions closer to daily work than "is the model smart enough": customers need to get models running, meeting requirements for response speed, throughput, and cost, and letting real requests pass reliably through the system. After model optimization, the team also has to test together with the customer, and continue supporting the handoff after delivery. 1

Response speed is how long users have to wait. Throughput is how many requests can be handled over the same period. Cost determines whether a feature can be offered sustainably. An impressive demo may not yet answer these three questions at the same time.

This personal account comes from a platform company that helps customers run AI models. It cannot represent every FDE's day, but it works well as an entry point to the AI era: new model capabilities offer more things to try, and companies still need to turn one of them into a sustainable business. Engineers work between the two.

Subsequent public materials placed similar demands at a larger organizational scale. By 2026, companies such as OpenAI, Anthropic, AWS, and Microsoft announced or described engineering and delivery arrangements aimed at enterprises. Reading these announcements, it is easy to reach a tidy conclusion: the stronger the models, the more FDEs are needed. That claim is still too quick. Model progress can eliminate some work, and can also make other work worth investing in for the first time; job demand depends on what companies do next, and who is capable of doing it well.

To understand this shift, technical questions, organizational capabilities, and vendors' business choices need to be viewed together.

One model, entering three different kinds of work

Start with a teaching comparison. A company wants to use the same model to help employees process text materials. The following three arrangements are not a record of any real customer's project.

The first arrangement gives employees a general-purpose tool, where people select materials, ask questions, and check the output. Here, people take on much of the work the system does not: judging whether materials are suitable to upload, discerning whether answers are usable, and deciding what to do next. If the task's risk and scope of use allow, a mature tool plus concise training may already be enough.

The second arrangement connects the model to the company's own document repository. Users want to ask questions directly instead of manually hunting for each document. The team therefore needs to know what the system can retrieve, what different employees can see, whether materials are outdated, and whether answers can be traced back to their sources. The model can help understand questions, but the actual distribution of materials and access permissions still has to be provided by the system.

The third arrangement further allows the system to act, for example updating a business record based on an approved request. At this point, an error is no longer just an inaccurate sentence on a screen; it may also change subsequent work. The team needs to define which changes can be executed directly, which require human confirmation, how to retry after failures, and whether repeated execution would cause a second modification.

All three arrangements can use the same model, yet the engineering work differs greatly. Their differences come from what data the system obtains, which processes it connects to, and what action permissions it receives. You cannot infer from a single model test that the later arrangement is already equally reliable.

This comparison also explains why some companies increase field-facing engineering investment. Not every technical difficulty has become harder; rather, companies have pushed their goal from "helping one person write something" to "taking on a continuously running workload." The judgments previously patched in ad hoc by people need to be made visible; some can be turned into software, and some should remain with humans.

Conversely, if the goal is only the first arrangement, putting an expensive FDE team into it may not pay off. A job title cannot decide the level of investment for a company. Only by first identifying the work in question can you know whether what's missing is usage training, mature implementation, or new capabilities to be explored jointly with the vendor.

Baseten: customers are already technically savvy, yet still need to build together

Vlad Shulman, head of FDEs at Baseten, explained in an article last updated June 11, 2025 that the team sits within the engineering organization, works directly with customer engineers, and also participates in building the core product. The article also notes that the self-serve platform can support some customers deploying on their own; those needing close collaboration are customers facing frontier models, strict performance requirements, and rapidly changing needs. 2

This arrangement is notable because it does not describe customers as people waiting for technology to spread. Customers may have strong engineering teams and still need vendor help. The difficulty may lie where the product meets new uses: customers know how fast their users need responses, while the vendor better understands what improvement opportunities exist inside the platform; both sides need each other to make good choices.

"Inference" is the common technical term here, meaning letting an already-trained model process inputs and produce results. For operators, it can first be understood as the process of a model serving requests. It does not happen just once; it must remain available as real requests keep arriving. How much compute a run needs, whether it can respond in time, and what to do when resources are short all enter engineering decisions.

Imagine a teaching comparison: one text-processing task can tolerate a longer wait, while another real-time interaction cannot keep users waiting. Even if both have similar requirements for answer quality, they may not warrant the same serving setup. Pursuing the shortest wait time may increase resource investment, while squeezing costs too hard may worsen the experience at peak times. Engineers must first figure out which constraint matters most, rather than chasing a single leaderboard number.

Trivedi described practices including caching model weights to reduce the wait when a service first starts; weights are the parameters a model must load to run, and "cold start" is the moment when the service is not yet ready and must first complete these loads. After optimization, engineers test together with the customer, then move into handoff support. 1 This material does not publish complete metrics for a named customer, but it has grounded the labor in the concrete steps of running models.

It also offers an explanation that can be checked: if user waiting is mostly spent on model preparation, changing loading and caching arrangements may help; if the waiting comes from queuing in the customer's own systems, even faster model loading will not eliminate that queue. The FDE's value must come from identifying these two segments of waiting and being able to mobilize the corresponding engineering capabilities. Merely knowing the latest model names cannot accomplish this judgment.

This also explains why Baseten places FDEs within the engineering organization. But that is its choice based on its product and customer conditions, and cannot directly become an organizational rule for other companies. The same management retrospective also acknowledges that some needs are better suited to technical account managers or implementation teams. 2 Needing periodic consulting advice and needing to jointly advance product capabilities are two kinds of work that can be arranged separately.

Vendors are buying different delivery capabilities

As of this book's source cutoff date, customer-facing engineering is no longer just a job design. The following public arrangements are concentrated in 2026. AWS's original announcement page does not show a publication date, but its follow-up document of October 1 explicitly states that the FDE organization and the $1 billion support were announced on June 30. 3 Model companies, cloud platforms, and service partners have chosen different organizational approaches.

Systems integration is making previously separate software and data work together. Separating it from model development makes it clear why these arrangements require partners. On March 18, 2026, Accenture and Microsoft announced an FDE business practice, emphasizing respectively the alignment of platform technology with process, industry, and delivery at scale.9 The same partner can participate in multiple arrangements, so announcement headcounts cannot be added up as a single global independent workforce.

These investments first reflect how vendors are organizing their supply: some are deepening technical collaboration, some are adding day-to-day implementation resources, and some are strengthening sales channels. To explain this round of changes, each announcement must be placed back into the gap it was meant to address, rather than ranked by dollar amount.

When capabilities become callable, which part of implementation work will shrink?

Another change is happening in the products themselves. On April 15, 2026, Salesforce introduced Headless 360, proposing to let agents call platform capabilities through APIs and tools. An agent here means an AI program that can invoke software tools and advance tasks. "Headless" means not having to go through the original operational interface first; it does not mean business rules and permissions disappear. The same announcement still lists approval chains, sharing rules, and permissions as underlying prerequisites.10

The scope of capabilities needs further verification. Its July developer article lists the Headless 360 MCP Server as beta, noting that the first batch of roughly one hundred skills is mostly aimed at system administration by administrators; calls execute under the identity of an authenticated user, and existing access restrictions continue to apply. MCP can first be understood as a set of connection conventions for agents to discover and invoke external tools. The April platform direction and the July beta status of this specific service cannot be combined into "all business processes now run automatically."11

The September 15 AIforce announcement pushed one step further toward business entry points: Salesforce in Claude was offered in Beta at the time, still relying on the enterprise's existing data, business rules, and permissions.12 Platform direction, administrator tools, and business entry points usable by employees are three different levels of progress. When purchasing, verify at which level the current task is actually available; a single platform release cannot be equated with your enterprise's processes already being connected.

For field engineering, this means some work that previously required operating interfaces one by one or writing custom connection code may decrease. But teams still must judge: which identity should be used, which records may be changed, and whether out-of-the-box operations cover the enterprise's exceptions. If the product already satisfies all of these, the scope of customization should shrink; if ordinary employees' tasks can only be done through an administrator's broad permissions, the interface being available does not solve the delivery problem. Product progress changes how work is distributed; it does not automatically grant business legitimacy to every call.

Models themselves also have different delivery stages. On October 6, 2026, Mistral listed Large 4 as public preview, while noting that open weights were still to come.13 On October 29, the company announced the release of improved open weights, with download and self-deployment entry points.14 Being able to try a model through a hosted entry point is a different condition from being able to bring the weights into the customer's own environment. With a hosted entry point, the delivery team must verify the service scope, data processing terms, and whether the interface suits the task; with self-deployment, they must also agree with the customer on who provides compute, installation and operation, maintenance, and license verification. In neither case can the release itself be treated as the customer having completed deployment.

Equally close to the customer, but addressing opposite shortages

Baseten's materials describe one scenario: highly technical customers who need the vendor to work with them on frontier performance and product issues. Anthropic's new business announcement instead puts another type of customer at the center: the company believes many mid-market enterprises lack the internal resources needed to build and run deployments.6 The former requires deep collaboration between technical capabilities; the latter also requires filling execution gaps. Both needs can drive customer-proximate engineering work, but they do not correspond to exactly the same teams.

For highly capable customers, the most valuable part of external engineers may be whether they can go deep into the vendor's product, find the cause of a performance issue, and feed improvements back into core engineering. If they can only forward documentation or hold coordination meetings, the incremental value to the customer may be limited.

For customers lacking internal resources, the difficulty may come earlier: who will break the work down, sort out connections, and arrange operation and maintenance? Sending an engineer willing only to tackle the hardest technical problems may still leave a large amount of ordinary work unclaimed. In this case, teams with industry and implementation capabilities matter as much as knowledge of cutting-edge models.

Three organizational arrangements can be further distinguished here. Vendor-owned teams find it easier to search for answers along their own product lines; professional services partners may be more familiar with cross-department transformation and heterogeneous systems; internal customer teams hold long-term responsibility and knowledge of day-to-day changes. All of these are potential advantages that need to be verified against specific people and projects; they are not capabilities automatically delivered by company type.

The ideal division of labor is not necessarily making any one of them infinitely large. Having a product issue solved by vendor engineers, data and process transformation done jointly by partners and the customer, and daily operations gradually handed back to the internal team may fit better than letting any single party do everything. The corresponding cost is more handoffs: it must be clearly documented who is responsible for which segment, so that "everyone is involved" does not end up meaning "no one is accountable."

This comparison also corrects a common dichotomy in earlier discussions: product vendors handle technology, and consultancies only give advice. What emerged in the 2026 announcements is precisely a recombination of model technology, engineering, process, and business capabilities. How they combine still depends on delivery and cannot be judged by alliance names alone.

The deeper the engineering involvement, what does the customer ultimately get?

Cohere's engineers have distinguished two kinds of dependence: the switching cost of choosing a platform, and the day-to-day dependence caused by customers not owning operational knowledge. Their argument is co-building, training, and testing, rather than leaving all problems permanently to external staff.15 This offers another check on the organizational expansion described above: after vendor investment increases, can customers better change their own systems?

For the technical customers in Baseten's materials, the answer may show up as customers being able to deploy and observe models themselves and engage in joint engineering when new platform limits appear; for enterprises lacking internal staff, it may require explicitly purchasing long-term operations services. Both relationships can be valid, but pricing and responsibilities should not be conflated. Describing the second type of contract as "the customer is already independent" leaves unclaimed work when support ends.

Concrete verification of handoffs is left to Chapter 18 Adoption and Takeover. Here it is incorporated into demand judgment: product gaps needing one-time fixes, services requiring long-term purchase, and capabilities the customer intends to internalize are three different kinds of procurement. FDE teams with the same name are not necessarily able to cover all three requirements.

Real demand cannot be proven by the seller alone

The preceding explanation focused on genuine engineering needs. But another explanation is equally compelling: as enterprises begin allocating budgets for AI, vendors want to expand customer relationships, drive adoption of their own platforms, and enter more lines of business through service capabilities. Setting up teams, alliances, and new companies may also be a change in distribution and business arrangements.

This need not conflict with solving problems. Helping customers get systems running can indeed increase platform revenue; the customer gains value, and the vendor gains returns. What needs to be tested is whether the two are aligned over the long term. For example, does a sound technical recommendation allow the customer to shrink the project, choose a simpler product, or even decide not to proceed for now? If a team is only considered successful when usage expands, these choices may be undervalued.

Therefore, you cannot directly infer from "multiple companies have announced FDEs" that "all enterprises need FDEs." These materials come from vendors; they show how those vendors organize resources and what judgments they make about the market; they are not a survey of overall enterprise demand. The different announcements also mix existing capabilities, new organizations, future investments, and partnership goals, and each must be read carefully on its own terms.

Facts pointing in the opposite direction are also worth keeping. In 2025, the UK government decided not to continue developing Redbox, citing in part the fact that commercial tools had entered existing systems, while internal accumulation was redirected to other work. UK: public accountability will examine this public-sector record in detail. 16 It is not a case of FDE failure, but it is enough to refute the one-way explanation that "the stronger model capabilities become, the more custom engineering there will be."

Therefore, this round of change has at least two opposing directions: new capabilities open up tasks that were previously impossible, increasing exploratory engineering; mature features cover existing tasks, reducing the need for customization. How jobs will change depends on whether the former kind of new work can offset the latter kind of disappearing work. The existing announcements are not enough to tally up these two quantities.

A practical way to compare is to place the FDE option alongside an equally seriously designed alternative: if this work were done by the customer's internal teams, mature products, and implementation partners, what would still remain unsolved? If the answer is only "a more fashionable job title is missing," there is no need to change the organization; if the answer is that product gaps go unaddressed, key technical decisions lack information, or cross-organizational issues stall indefinitely, then there is a more concrete reason to invest.

From job-market buzz, back to the work itself

What this round of public materials supports is a conditional answer: when enterprises apply AI to more complex, continuously running work, there is still data, systems, permissions, performance, and organizational adoption to build beyond the models; some problems are best solved by teams close to the customer that can also draw on the vendor's engineering capacity.

It does not require every enterprise to first set up an FDE, nor does it mean customers can hand over all internal responsibility. For those preparing to purchase, what matters is which segment of capability they lack; for those preparing to set up a team, what matters is giving engineers authority over what they do and ensuring experience flows back into the product; for those preparing to enter this profession, what matters is whether the job's promises match the actual work.

Returning to the task Trivedi described, what the customer wants is not a prettier job description, but a model that works reliably at the required speed, capacity, and cost. Only by scaling that result to the whole enterprise can we see the necessity and cost of the various organizational choices. After completing the organizational and business judgment, the engineering chapters will follow the working sequence of requirements, data, operations, evals, and handover, using different real cases and clearly marked teaching scenarios, to see how these promises become actions that can be checked every day.
