Summary

This article by Japan's DeFactory addresses freelance engineers worried about AI replacement and career stagnation. It argues that coders who merely implement specs are losing market value, while those who manage AI and handle requirements and architecture gain value. It outlines three routes to exceeding ¥15 million annual income: deepening technical expertise (architecture, cloud native, data engineering), shifting to management (PM, consulting, VPoE), or building a business (micro-SaaS, incorporation). It also promotes a strategic return to full-time employment as a startup CTO, where stock options can outweigh freelance cash flow in lifetime wealth. A phased roadmap with recommended stacks and certifications is included.

Key points

  • An AI-replacement risk table rates coding and testing as high risk, requirements and team building as low.
  • Skill combinations raise rates: data engineering plus bizdev can reach ¥1.3–2.0 million monthly.
  • Freelancers risk selling only existing skills, staying stuck in AI-replaceable work.
  • Returning to employment as CTO/VPoE offers stock options and asset-based wealth beyond hourly billing.
  • A three-phase roadmap covers skills, certifications (AWS, CKA), and career branching after year five.

\n\n\n\n

\n\n\n\n

However, do you ever, in a fleeting moment, feel a chilling "anxiety about the future"?

\n\n\n\n

"We live in an era where generative AI can write the code. Will my skills still be relevant in five years?"

\n\n\n\n

"Will I be able to keep taking on projects at this pace into my 40s and 50s?"

"While engineers around me are becoming CTOs or starting businesses one after another, am I being left behind?"

1-1. In the Age of Generative AI, "Doers" Will Be Weeded Out (AI Replacement Risk Assessment Table)

\n\n\n\n

The advent of ChatGPT and GitHub Copilot has dramatically improved coding productivity. This is wonderful, but it also means that the value of "engineers who merely write code according to specifications" is plummeting.

\n\n\n\n

From now on, engineers' market value will polarize as follows.

\n\n\n\n

    \n
  • Engineers whose market value will fall: "Doers" who insist on manually writing code at a level AI can produce.
    • \n\n\n\n
  • Engineers whose market value will rise: People who can use AI like a subordinate and answer "questions without a single correct answer" — such as requirements definition and architecture design — that AI still finds difficult.
    • \n

\n\n\n\n

"People who code fast" will lose to AI, but "people who can use AI to build products 10 times faster" will be valued more than ever.

1-2. The "Career Discontinuity" Risk Unique to Freelancers

\n\n\n\n

Because freelancers are expected to be "immediately productive," there is a trap they can easily fall into: their work tends to become selling off "what they are already good at and can already do" in pieces.

\n\n\n\n

Clients want results without paying education costs, so they ask you to develop with "the familiar PHP" rather than "the unfamiliar Go language." Continuing to accept such work provides short-term income stability, but in the medium to long term it leads to a situation where "you never acquire new skills."

\n\n\n\n

As a result, no matter how many years pass, taking on upstream processes or modern technology stacks keeps getting postponed, and you can never escape the "high AI-replacement-risk areas" shown in the table above. This is the biggest "career discontinuity" risk for freelancers.

2. Three Specific Strategic Routes

\n\n\n\n

2-1. [Route I] The "Specialist" Career Strategy of Mastering Technology

\n\n\n\n

The first path is the route of pursuing "technical skill," the core duty of an engineer. However, merely being able to write code is not enough.

\n\n\n\n

① Evolving into a Full-Stack Engineer

\n\n\n\n

The term "full-stack" tends to be overused, but true full-stack engineers remain highly scarce and valuable.

\n\n\n\n

In particular, the ability to view everything from frontend to backend and infrastructure end-to-end, "understand the overall data flow and bottlenecks of a service, and build a prototype entirely on your own" is powerful. In the startup phase of new businesses (0→1), there is no budget to hire multiple specialists, so this type is welcomed at the highest rates.

\n\n\n\n

② Working as a Tech Lead or Architect

\n\n\n\n

To raise your rate as a specialist, shifting from "implementer" to "designer (architect)" is essential.

\n\n\n\n

A tech lead takes responsibility for "technical decisions" such as technology selection, database design, and whether or not to adopt microservices. Even as a freelancer, with sufficient trust you can join as a "technical advisor." Once you are compensated not for "time spent working with your hands" but for "the expertise to make judgments," you can maintain a high income while reducing your working hours.

\n\n\n\n

③ Pivot to Trending Technologies: Cloud Native and Data Engineering

\n\n\n\n

Web3 and similar fields used to be the trend, but today the enterprise space offers more solid, higher-rate demand for both "Cloud Native" and "Data Engineering."

\n\n\n\n

    \n
  • Cloud Native (Kubernetes, Terraform): Technologies that underpin large-scale services. The learning curve is steep, but engineers who can handle them are in drastically short supply, and rates have remained extremely high.
    • \n\n\n\n
  • Data Engineering (Snowflake, BigQuery, dbt): Demand is surging for building "clean data foundations" as a precursor to AI adoption. It's an excellent career change for backend engineers, with outstanding future prospects.
    • \n

\n\n\n\n

[Pro Tip] Advice from DeFactory

\n\n\n\n

Choosing a technology just because "it's popular" is risky. The key to landing high-rate projects is identifying areas where "demand (business needs) is large, but supply (available engineers) is scarce." Right now, Kubernetes and Snowflake are unquestionably in the middle of that very "imbalance."

\n\n\n\n

2-2. 【Route 2】 The "Generalist" Career: Shifting to Management and Upstream Processes

\n\n\n\n

The second path is a route that leverages your technical background to shift toward moving "people" and "projects." Instead of "building," you commit to "making the business succeed."

\n\n\n\n

① Transitioning to Project Manager (PM/PdM)

\n\n\n\n

PMs (project managers) with engineering backgrounds are fiercely competed for in the market.

\n\n\n\n

That's because they intuitively understand things that PMs without engineering backgrounds find hard to judge, such as "accuracy of effort estimation" and "technical feasibility." They also earn deep trust from development members and are highly valued as firefighters for troubled projects.

\n\n\n\n

To land high-rate freelance PM projects paying over 1.2 million yen per month, you need more than simple progress management—you need "the ability to translate business requirements into system requirements."

\n\n\n\n

② IT Consultant and Technical Advisor Options

\n\n\n\n

Taking an even broader view, consultants "solve business problems with technology."

\n\n\n\n

Clients don't want "an app built"—they want "higher revenue." The key is whether you can make proposals not to build, such as "App development is one option, but let's first cut costs by implementing existing SaaS." By shifting your perspective from "someone who builds" to "someone who solves problems," your market value transcends the concept of "monthly per-person rates."

\n\n\n\n

③ Organization Building and VPoE Proxy Services

\n\n\n\n

At rapidly growing startups, there are many cases where the product is thriving but the engineering organization is on the verge of collapse.

\n\n\n\n

This creates demand for an "external VPoE (Vice President of Engineering)"-type role, handling things like conducting interviews on the company's behalf, designing engineer evaluation systems, and systematizing onboarding. It's an ideal career for those who find more fulfillment in building organizations than in writing code.

\n\n\n\n

[Pro Tip] Advice from DeFactory

\n\n\n\n

What freelance PMs and consultants need most is "the ability to say NO." Accepting every client request isn't kindness. For the project's success, having the courage at times to cut scope and offer alternatives is precisely what builds trust and commands high rates.

\n\n\n\n

2-3. 【Route 3】 The "Business Owner" Career: Own Products and Entrepreneurship

\n\n\n\n

The third path is a route of breaking free from client work and becoming a "business owner" with your own product.

\n\n\n\n

① Risks and Winning Opportunities in Micro SaaS Development

\n\n\n\n

An engineer's greatest privilege is "the ability to create assets (products) with your own hands." There's active movement toward building small businesses (micro SaaS), such as management tools for specific niche industries or API integration plugins.

\n\n\n\n

However, "diving straight into development with zero income" is dangerous. The success rate is by no means high.

\n\n\n\n

The recommended approach is to earn a solid living through client development while developing on the side, using "spare time" on weekday evenings and weekends as a form of risk hedging. Once your own product generates 200,000-300,000 yen in monthly profit, reducing client work and gradually shifting your focus is the strategy with the highest survival rate.

\n\n\n\n

② Incorporating Client Development and Scaling the Organization

\n\n\n\n

When you feel the limits of working solo (revenue ceiling = your own available hours), incorporating and building a team structure is the classic path.

\n\n\n\n

You move into a direction role and delegate hands-on work to partners to gain leverage. Here, you're tested not so much as an "engineer" but as a "business owner"—on sales ability and cash flow management.

\n\n\n\n

[Pro Tip] Advice from DeFactory

\n\n\n\n

Beware of the "client work trap." Even after incorporating, if it's still "client work performed by just you," nothing changes from being a freelancer. Among those we've supported successfully, the only ones are people who reinvested client revenue into "development staffing for their own products" or "team building" and created a system that runs without them.

Is "Don't Become a Freelance Engineer" Really True? A Survival Strategy Guide to Boosting Your Market Value

\n\n\n\n

3. The Secret Trick of "Returning to Full-Time Employment" Without Lowering Your Income = "High-Class Career Change"

\n\n\n\n

This is the core of the "survival strategy" that we most strongly want to convey to all of you thriving as freelancers in this article.

\n\n\n\n

It is the choice to deliberately "return to full-time employment," using your track record as a freelancer as a weapon.

\n\n\n\n

Until recently, "returning from freelancing to being a company employee" tended to be discussed in negative terms, such as "couldn't land projects anymore" or "their skills weren't good enough." Some people may have seen it as a kind of "falling from grace."

\n\n\n\n

However, times have changed. We now live in an era where excellent freelance engineers are welcomed by companies with excellent compensation as "CTOs (Chief Technology Officers)," "VPoEs (VP of Engineering)," or "Tech Leads." This is not a "defeat" that abandons freelance freedom, but a "strategic re-employment (high-class career change)" to gain greater discretion and assets.

\n\n\n\n

Why are top-tier engineers now choosing to "return to full-time employment" one after another? We will unravel the little-known benefits and the path to success.

\n\n\n\n

3-1. The "True Benefits" of Freelancers Returning to Full-Time Employment

\n\n\n\n

By the time you went independent as a freelancer, you had already proven your "self-sufficiency" and "technical skills." Exercising those abilities within an organization creates value beyond simply providing labor.

\n\n\n\n

■ "Attachment to the Product" That Allows Commitment to 1→100, Not Just 0→1

\n\n\n\n

In a freelance (quasi-delegation contract) position, it is common to only handle a portion of a project or to leave the product when the contract period ends. Even if you think "it would be better if we did this," proposals outside the scope of the contract may not be accepted. Also, due to security reasons, there are many cases where you are not allowed to touch core technical areas or customer data.

\n\n\n\n

By returning as a full-time employee, you can take responsibility, over a span of several years, for the phases after "0→1" following the service release: the growth hacking of "1→10" and "10→100." The joy of nurturing a product with your own hands and continuously improving it based on user feedback is a fundamental joy of being an engineer that is hard to obtain in contract development.

\n\n\n\n

■ Access to "Executive Management" and Discretionary Authority

\n\n\n\n

Rather than a typical career-change route, when you are hired based on an evaluation of your track record as a freelancer, an increasing number of cases offer you the position of "executive management (CTO/VPoE/Head of Development)" from the start.

\n\n\n\n

This means your role changes from "someone who writes code" to "the person responsible for growing the business using technology." You have hiring authority, make final decisions on technology selection, and build organizational culture. There you will find the "dynamism of moving an organization" and "intense discretionary authority" that you could never obtain as a freelancer.

\n\n\n\n

■ Freedom from Back-Office Work and "True Productivity Improvement"

\n\n\n\n

Common freelance worries: sales activities, contract negotiations, invoice issuance, expense reimbursement, tax filing... The benefit of being freed from these back-office tasks is immeasurable.

\n\n\n\n

An environment where all the time previously stolen by administrative work can be 100% fully committed to "technical development," "product development," and "business strategy formulation." This is arguably the ultimate "productivity improvement" measure for engineers, and an environment free of stress.

\n\n\n\n

It is natural to have these concerns. However, judging based only on immediate "monthly income (flow income)" is dangerous. When compared in terms of "lifetime income," including benefits and potential future "assets (stock income)," the picture changes completely.

\n\n\n\n

Even if a freelancer keeps working at high rates, that is merely an accumulation of "selling time by the hour (labor income)." On the other hand, holding stock options as a high-class full-time employee is an act of "turning your labor into an asset."

\n\n\n\n

By adopting not only the yardstick of "annual income" but also the perspective of "asset building," the true value of returning to full-time employment becomes clear.

\n\n\n\n

3-3. Conditions for a "Return to Full-Time Employment" That Secures Favorable Terms

\n\n\n\n

Of course, you will not succeed by simply job-hunting aimlessly. If you apply through ordinary job sites, you risk being filtered out at the document screening stage or being slotted into a standard salary table.

\n\n\n\n

The key to success is leveraging "being headhunted by clients" or "referrals," or using an agent's "high-class scouting" services.

\n\n\n\n

By negotiating through a client who already knows your abilities on the ground, or through an agent who can properly evaluate your track record, you can use "your freelance rate" as the baseline (anchor) for salary negotiations.

\n\n\n\n

【Pro Tip】Advice from DeFactory: The Killer Phrase for Negotiations

\n\n\n\n

Let me share a "magic phrase" you can use when negotiating salary during an interview.

\n\n\n\n

"I currently earn ¥XX million per month as a freelancer (annual revenue of ¥XX million). While maintaining my standard of living is important, I find your company's vision and the future potential of stock options even more appealing. Therefore, I would be open to considering a base salary of ¥XX million per year (slightly lower than my freelance income)."

\n\n\n\n

This phrase has two key points.

\n\n\n\n

    \n
  • Demonstrating market value: Nail down with numbers that "this is what I am worth right now."
    • \n\n\n\n
  • Expressing commitment: Show enthusiasm with "I want to bet on your company's future more than money," and win them over.
    • \n

\n\n\n\n

As a result, the company feels that "an excellent person who would normally command high compensation is willing to take a discount out of sympathy with our vision." A sense of "getting a bargain" and "trust in their enthusiasm" are created simultaneously, making it easier to receive an offer and tending to improve your treatment after joining.

5. Frequently Asked Questions About the "Future" and "Shelf Life" of Freelance Engineers

\n\n\n\n

Q1. Will generative AI (ChatGPT and GitHub Copilot) take over coding jobs?

\n\n\n\n

【Conclusion】The jobs of "mere coders" who simply write code according to specifications are highly likely to be replaced by AI within a few years.

\n\n\n\n

However, engineering jobs themselves will not disappear.

\n\n\n\n

Demand is actually surging for professionals with skills in upstream processes such as requirements definition and architecture design, as well as engineers with the management ability to skillfully operate AI tools through prompts and dramatically boost development efficiency.

\n\n\n\n

In the coming era, the decisive factor in the survival competition will be whether you can master AI as "a powerful assistant to boost your own productivity" rather than fearing it as "a threat that steals jobs."

\n\n\n\n

【Reference】What is an FDE? The Role and Essential Skills of Forward Deployed Engineers in the AI Era

\n\n\n\n

Q2. Until what age can you survive as a freelance engineer (shelf life)?

\n\n\n\n

【Conclusion】There is no clear "shelf life" or "age limit" for freelance engineers. As long as you can shift your skills, it is entirely possible to continue working at high rates even in your 40s and 50s.

\n\n\n\n

The IT industry once whispered about the "engineers hit their limit at 35" theory, but with the chronic shortage of IT talent today, that theory no longer applies at all. That said, the roles clients expect from you certainly change as you age.

\n\n\n\n

Building on the programming skills you developed in your 20s and 30s, from your 40s onward you will be expected to provide advanced, experience-backed added value such as "project management (PM)," "negotiation with business sides (BizDev)," and "mentoring junior staff and technology selection." If you can update your role in line with your age, working actively for life is by no means a dream.

Q3. What is the difference between engineers whose demand disappears and engineers who are in high demand?

\n\n\n\n

【Conclusion】The biggest difference is whether or not you have "the ability to solve business problems." People who only build what they are told see their demand decline, while people who can commit to business growth are always in high demand.

\n\n\n\n

The reason companies are willing to pay high rates to freelance engineers is not simply "because they want a system built." It is "because they want to increase revenue or reduce operational costs through the system."

\n\n\n\n

In other words, engineers with a "BizDev (business development) perspective" who deeply understand their clients' business challenges and can propose optimal solutions through technical approaches are highly valued regardless of economic conditions or trends. Honing not only skills in specific programming languages but also industry knowledge and business literacy is the greatest defense for maintaining your market value.

There is no single correct answer for a freelance engineer's career path. However, one thing is certain: there is no bright future at the end of mindlessly completing project after project.
