Why 95% of Enterprise AI Pilots Fail, and How Forward Deployed Engineers Get Them to Production

Scaler Topics · 2026-10-06 · 15 min read

Summary

This Scaler Topics guide unpacks MIT NANDA's 2025 finding that 95% of enterprises saw no measurable P&L impact from GenAI despite $30-40 billion invested, clarifying it measures profit impact, not pilot failure. It lists seven reasons pilots stall—demo-first use cases, unready data, missing system integration, unmapped exceptions, no learning loop, undefined success metrics, and late compliance work—and explains how Forward Deployed Engineers close the gap through outcome-first scoping, workflow mapping, integration, evals, and production hardening. It also cites RAND, Gartner, and BCG findings and includes an illustrative KYC rescue example.

Key points

  • MIT NANDA 2025: 95% of firms saw no measurable GenAI P&L impact.
  • Only ~5% of custom enterprise GenAI tools reached production.
  • External partnerships deployed 67% of the time vs 33% for internal builds.
  • Seven failure modes: unclear problems, unready data, no integration, no learning loop, and more.
  • FDEs own integration, evals, and hardening to move pilots into production.

The GenAI demo works. The executive is impressed. Six months later, it is still a slide, a notebook or a chat window no team depends on. That is the gap behind searches for why AI projects fail. The widely shared “95%” headline is narrower than it sounds, but it points to a real delivery problem: getting an AI pilot into daily work. This guide explains what MIT’s finding actually measured, the seven reasons enterprise pilots stall and the engineering work that moves them into production.

Why do AI projects fail?

Most AI projects fail not because the model is weak but because the pilot never becomes part of how the business works. Pilots stall when they solve an unclear problem, run on data that is not production-ready, are not connected to real systems and workflows, cannot learn from feedback, and have no owner or success metric.

Pilot purgatory is the state where an AI proof of concept works in a demo but never reaches production use or measurable business impact. The demo is usually the easy part; the hard part is surviving real data, exceptions, approvals and user trust.
That is the gap a customer-embedded engineer is built to close. If the title is new to you, start with what a Forward Deployed Engineer is, then return here for the production problem.

What did the MIT “95% of AI pilots fail” study actually find?

MIT NANDA’s July 2025 report found that 95% of organisations in its research reported no measurable P&L impact from GenAI. It did not show that 95% of pilots crashed, were cancelled or produced no useful individual productivity at all.

What the 95% actually measures

The GenAI Divide: State of AI in Business 2025 says that, despite an estimated $30–40 billion in enterprise GenAI investment, 95% of organisations were getting zero return in the form of measurable profit-and-loss impact. The report calls the separation between high adoption and low transformation the “GenAI Divide.”

Its core explanation is a learning gap: enterprise tools often do not retain feedback, adapt to local context or fit the workflow around them. That is an integration and operating-model problem, not a claim that every underlying model is poor.

How the researchers gathered the evidence

The research period was January to June 2025. The report describes a systematic review of more than 300 publicly disclosed AI initiatives, 52 structured interviews with organisations and 153 survey responses from senior leaders across four conferences.
In the appendix, “success” means deployment beyond the pilot phase with measurable KPIs, with ROI assessed six months after the pilot. The authors also list material limits: a non-representative sample is possible, success measures vary by organisation and a six-month window can understate long-term deployments. Those caveats matter when reading the headline.

Which pilot-to-production numbers matter most?

For custom or vendor-sold enterprise GenAI tools, roughly 60% of organisations evaluated a tool, about 20% reached a pilot and about 5% reached production. Generic tools were explored or piloted by more than 80%, and nearly 40% were reportedly deployed, but deployment did not necessarily create P&L impact.
External partnerships reached deployment about 67% of the time versus 33% for internal builds in the interview sample. The report warns that this is correlation, not proof that buying is inherently better.

Is the 95% figure accurate?

The 95% figure is a useful warning about measurable GenAI impact in a specific 2025 sample, not a universal failure rate for all AI. The responsible reading is to use it alongside its methodology, limitations and other studies—not as a slogan that “AI does not work.”

Marketing AI Institute’s critique makes the strongest fair objection. It notes the small interview base, the six-month P&L lens and the unclear method for synthesising 300 public initiatives. It also argues that efficiency, retention, conversion and pipeline effects can be real before they appear as a clean P&L result.

The counterpoint does not erase the delivery problem. RAND’s 2024 research identifies repeated causes across AI work: misunderstood business problems, inadequate data, technology-first thinking, weak deployment infrastructure and tasks beyond what AI can reliably solve. Gartner’s forecast names poor data quality, insufficient risk controls, rising costs and unclear business value. BCG finds the same distinction in a different form: activity such as hours saved is not automatically financial value.

Whichever study you trust, the pattern is consistent: pilots rarely fail at the model alone. They fail at the handover to real work. That handover problem also explains why companies are suddenly hiring Forward Deployed Engineers; this page stays focused on the failures themselves.

Why do enterprise AI pilots stall before production?

Enterprise pilots usually stall because the surrounding system was never designed for production. The recurring causes are visible before launch: weak ownership, unready data, disconnected workflows, no learning loop and late governance.

1. The problem was chosen for the demo, not the business

A fluent chatbot or dashboard can impress a steering committee without fixing a costly workflow. The warning sign is a use case with no accountable business owner, no baseline and no decision about what changes if the pilot succeeds. RAND similarly identifies misunderstood purpose and technology-first choices as root causes.

Fix: name the workflow owner, write one outcome metric and kill a technically impressive use case if its economic value is too small to matter.

2. The data is not production-ready

A pilot may use 500 clean files, while live use involves scans, missing fields, access controls, stale records and conflicting identifiers. The model appears capable until it touches the organisation’s actual systems. Data readiness is not a preprocessing task to postpone until after the demo.

Fix: inventory sources, permissions, refresh frequency and quality checks before scaling. The practical work includes cleaning messy real-world data, but it also includes agreeing who can access it and why.

3. It never connects to systems of record

A standalone chat interface creates an answer; the real work may still happen in a core-banking system, CRM, ticketing tool, ERP or document platform. If users must copy, paste and re-enter everything, the pilot adds work instead of removing it.

Fix: design an early read-only integration to the system where the decision happens, then add actions only with clear approval and audit controls.

4. Nobody mapped the work as people actually do it

Standard operating procedures describe the happy path; real work includes exceptions, handoffs and undocumented fields. An AI system that automates only the neat version earns distrust on the first difficult case.

Fix: shadow users, capture exception types and test real historical cases. In Indian IT-services and GCC work, learn both the documented process and the workaround used to meet the service level.

5. The system cannot learn from feedback

A retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) system can look convincing until retrieval misses a policy, the answer is wrong and there is no way to capture the correction. MIT NANDA’s “learning gap” is this missing ability to retain context, evaluate output and improve through use.

Fix: create an evaluation set from real cases, show users an accept/correct path and review errors on a fixed cadence. Many GenAI pilots are RAG systems; this walkthrough explains why retrieval, embeddings and retrievers can break even when the prompt looks fine.

6. Success was never defined, so it cannot be proved

“Hours saved” is not automatically business value. If nobody decides where saved time goes—higher throughput, lower outsourcing spend, fewer errors or a faster customer decision—the benefit can disappear back into the system. BCG calls this the productivity trap: activity improves while the cost structure does not.

Fix: agree the baseline, target, measurement window and decision owner before the pilot starts. Measure the customer or operational outcome, not merely model usage.

7. Production risk, cost and compliance arrive late

Security review, personally identifiable information, auditability, latency, model costs, monitoring and rollback paths are easy to skip in a proof of concept. They become unavoidable when the system touches customers or regulated data. Gartner’s 2024 forecast specifically names poor data, risk controls, cost and unclear value as abandonment drivers.

Fix: bring security, legal and operations into week one. For BFSI work in India, treat the RBI FREE-AI Committee report and applicable data-protection obligations as design inputs, not final-stage paperwork. Plan the monitoring, versioning and rollback that production-grade ML pipelines need.

How do Forward Deployed Engineers get AI pilots into production?

A Forward Deployed Engineer (FDE) is a software engineer who works embedded with a customer to take an AI system from prototype to production in the customer’s real systems, data and workflows. FDEs close the pilot gap by owning the messy middle: integration, workflow fit, evaluation, production hardening and stakeholder alignment.
An FDE bridges product, security, client engineering and the business owner. For the wider lifecycle, use the five-phase FDE deployment playbook; this section maps FDE work to failure modes.

They start from a business outcome, not a model

An FDE turns “we need an AI assistant” into a bounded workflow, owner, baseline and decision threshold. They surface weak use cases early, before the team spends months hardening something that cannot move a material metric. The outcome-first discipline is explained further in how FDEs connect AI strategy to business outcomes.

They sit with users and map the real workflow

Instead of accepting a requirements document as complete, an FDE watches how a claim, invoice, support ticket or KYC file moves through the organisation. They identify exceptions, permissions and the moment where a human must retain judgment. This creates a system users can recognise and safely adopt.

They wire AI into real data and systems

An FDE works through APIs, identity, data boundaries, observability and the client’s unfamiliar architecture. They may start read-only, prove usefulness in one workflow and introduce approved actions later. This is why production AI delivery requires more than a strong model demo.

They build the learning loop

An FDE helps create evaluation cases from production-like history, makes feedback cheap for users and turns recurring errors into an engineering backlog. The goal is not to pretend the AI is perfect. It is to make quality visible, improve the system and keep users from silently abandoning it.

They harden the deployment and transfer capability

The work includes guardrails, monitoring, cost controls, incident paths, documentation and training for the client team. That is how the system becomes operable after the launch team steps back. As KPMG argues, the organisations best positioned for durable advantage deploy FDE talent against the right problems and alongside complementary capabilities.
The MIT sample’s 67% versus 33% partner/build observation is not proof every company should buy. It suggests that someone must be accountable for adapting technology to the customer workflow.
The role can resemble consulting because it is customer-facing, but the distinction is production accountability: the FDE ships and owns working code. For the full comparison, see isn't this just consulting?.

What does a pilot-to-production rescue look like?

An effective rescue narrows the problem, connects to production conditions early and earns trust through evidence. The following is an illustrative composite, not a client case study or a claim about any specific Indian bank.
An Indian private bank pilots a GenAI assistant for KYC document review. On 500 clean sample files, it looks promising. Production stalls because scanned documents arrive in more formats, the document-management system cannot expose data through the prototype, compliance will not allow unrestricted external API calls and reviewers have no reason to trust a response they cannot correct.
An FDE begins by sitting with reviewers and listing exception types. With client IT, they set up a read-only document connection and an approved path for sensitive data. They build an evaluation set from historical cases, add accept/correct actions in the reviewer interface and agree to compare handling time and error rate against a baseline. The first rollout is to one review team; only then does the team expand access.

What should an AI team check before moving a pilot to production?

A pilot is ready to progress only when the team can describe its business owner, workflow, data, evaluation and operating controls. If these are unknown, more model tuning is unlikely to help.

  • Name one business owner and one baseline metric.
  • Map the real workflow, including exceptions and handoffs.
  • Agree production data access, not only a sample extract.
  • Define the integration path to systems of record.
  • Build an evaluation set from real historical cases.
  • Capture user feedback in the product and review it weekly.
  • Get security, privacy and sector-specific compliance input early.
  • Set cost, latency, logging and monitoring budgets.
  • Roll out to one team before expanding.
  • Write runbooks and train the people who will own the system.

If the team cannot tick at least seven items, it is still a pilot. The aim is not to delay deployment; it is to avoid treating unfinished operational work as an AI-model problem.

What does this mean for engineers in India?

Indian engineers already work inside the pilot-to-production gap. IT-services teams build pilots for global clients, GCC teams inherit promising prototypes from overseas and startups need to make AI work in enterprises with old systems, complex approval chains and regulated data.

The work is learnable: production coding, APIs, data basics, LLM or RAG evaluation, cloud operations and clear communication with non-engineers. These are skills a Forward Deployed Engineer needs, but they matter just as much to an AI engineer who wants their pilot to survive contact with production.
The trade-off is honest: this work is ambiguous, client-facing and occasionally on-site. It suits engineers who enjoy discovering the real problem, not only implementing a predefined ticket. If that is the direction you want to build toward, start with a step-by-step FDE roadmap.

Estimates vary because studies define failure differently. RAND’s 2024 report cites estimates above 80%, while Gartner predicted at least 30% of GenAI projects would be abandoned after proof of concept by end-2025. MIT’s 95% figure is specifically about organisations reporting no measurable GenAI P&L impact.

It is best treated as a directional signal, not a universal failure rate. Critics reasonably question the small sample and narrow P&L lens, but other research also finds recurring delivery problems.

What is the main reason AI projects fail?

Usually, it is not the model by itself. Projects fail when the business problem is unclear, production data is unavailable, the AI sits outside the real workflow, users cannot correct poor outputs or nobody owns the measure of success. These are engineering and organisational design problems together.

How do you move an AI pilot to production?

Choose one owned business metric, map the real workflow and exceptions, secure production data access, connect to the system of record and create an evaluation set. Add user feedback, security controls, monitoring and a phased rollout. Production starts when an operating team can safely depend on the system.

What is pilot purgatory in AI?

Pilot purgatory is when an AI proof of concept works in a demo but never reaches everyday production use or measurable business impact. Teams keep launching new experiments rather than scaling one system, usually because integration, data, feedback, ownership or governance were never resolved early enough.

What does a Forward Deployed Engineer do in AI projects?

A Forward Deployed Engineer works with a customer to take an AI system from prototype to production. The role combines software engineering with workflow discovery: integrating real systems and data, building evaluation and feedback loops, handling production constraints and helping users adopt a system that is safe to operate.

Is AI pilot-to-production work a good career in India?

It can suit engineers who enjoy coding alongside customer problem-solving. Indian IT services, GCCs and startups all face the challenge of turning global or enterprise pilots into usable systems. Expect ambiguity, stakeholder communication and real production constraints; the reward is work with visible business outcomes.
