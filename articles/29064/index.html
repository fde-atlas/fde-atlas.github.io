FDE Essential Reading: Weinberg's Three Laws of Consulting

Original title: FDE 必读书：Weinberg 咨询三定律

FDE Times · 2026-11-17 · 4 min read

Summary

This article applies Gerald Weinberg's 1985 book The Secrets of Consulting to the daily work of forward deployed engineers. Its three laws — problems always exist, problems are ultimately people problems, and you're paid by the hour, not by the solution — still describe what FDEs face in a client's first week. The author also covers the Raspberry Jam Rule on spreading yourself thin, reserving a quarter of your time, and how to use the laws in interviews and resumes.

Key points

  • Weinberg's first law: no matter how it looks, the problem is real.
  • Second law: no matter what it looks like, it's always a people problem.
  • Third law: remember how you're paid, not just the solution.
  • Raspberry Jam Rule: spreading across too many clients thins your impact.
  • Read the 1985 original first; the 2002 sequel is weaker.

Key Takeaways

  • The book is 248 pages, published by Dorset House in 1985 with a foreword by Virginia Satir, and is now available as PDF and EPUB on Leanpub
  • Three laws worth remembering: there's always a problem; the problem is always people; never forget what you're being paid for
  • The Raspberry Jam Rule reminds FDEs: spread across too many clients at once, each client only gets a thin layer

"No matter how it looks at first, it's always a people problem." Put more bluntly: no matter what the client says, the problem is there. This is the First Law from The Secrets of Consulting, written by Gerald M. Weinberg in 1985. Forty-one years later, it still describes exactly what a Forward Deployed Engineer (FDE) encounters in their first week on-site with a client.

The book was published by Dorset House, runs 248 pages, and has a foreword by Virginia Satir. The publisher positions it for three audiences: consultants, people who hire consultants, and people who want to become consultants. Leanpub now sells PDF and EPUB editions, with the subtitle A Guide to Giving and Getting Advice Successfully.

For FDEs, this is not a book about "making slides and delivering reports" style consulting. It teaches how to work inside someone else's organization: writing code while also getting people to listen to you. If you're technically strong but just starting out with client requirements discovery, this book is a good starting point.

Three Laws, Read as a Scoping Checklist

Weinberg doesn't preach grand principles. According to a review in The Personal MBA, he teaches through short anecdotes and named rules, such as the Raspberry Jam Rule and Rudy's Rutabaga Rule. The most valuable to carry with you are the three laws of consulting.

The First Law says clients always have a problem. Imagine you're sent to integrate an AI agent into a client's customer service workflow, and the person in charge tells you: the data is clean, the API is ready, you just need to plug it in.

Through Weinberg's lens, this statement isn't an answer—it's where you should start asking questions: Why was this project approved? Who is hurting the most right now?

The Second Law is where this book truly earns its price: no matter how it looks at first, it's always a people problem in the end. Suppose the client's data pipeline breaks every Monday morning. You can patch the schema three times, but if the root cause is that neither the data team nor the ops team owns that export file, the fourth patch will break too.

The Third Law is even less pleasant to hear: never forget, they're paying you by the hour, not by the solution. Alik Levin once summarized these three laws on Microsoft's blog, contrasting this one with packaged service models as a counterexample.

For FDEs, this contrast is even sharper, because the standard for judging your work is usually whether the system actually runs at the client. So when reading this law, treat it as a reminder to ask yourself what you're being measured by, not as advice to stretch out your hours.

Raspberry Jam, and Keeping a Quarter of Your Time Free

The excerpt published by Dorset House quotes the Raspberry Jam Rule in a single sentence: the more you spread it, the thinner it gets. Weinberg then wrote several more "jam" laws, one of which says no one ever complains about grape jelly—meaning nobody cares about bland, unremarkable things.

Applied to FDE work, the arithmetic is simple. Split a 40-hour week across four clients and each gets 10 hours, and that's before subtracting the cost of context switching. At that point, you've become grape jelly: nobody complains, and nobody remembers what you did.

Markus Gärtner calls the book Jerry's classic, and says he often returns to one of its laws: keep at least a quarter of your time free to do nothing. On a 40-hour week, that means leaving 10 hours open.

That time is for thinking, taking clients' urgent calls, or discovering the real problem that the First Law asserts must exist.

Who Should Read It, and in What Order

Gärtner also emphasizes that this book is as much about receiving advice as giving it. The Personal MBA also notes it's equally helpful for people choosing consultants. For FDEs this is practical: to get your advice adopted, you first have to learn to listen to clients describing their own systems.

If you're a software engineer looking to transition into an FDE role, read the original first. The sequel, More Secrets of Consulting: The Consultant's Tool Kit, was published by Dorset House in 2002 and can wait. Diomidis Spinellis wrote in IEEE Software that the sequel isn't in the same league as the original, and turning every idea into a law becomes tiresome over time.

The First Two Laws Are Also Interview Answers

Return to that data pipeline that breaks every Monday morning, and walk through it in Weinberg's order. The client says everything is fine, just a few minor schema errors. The hidden problem is an export file nobody owns. And it's a problem between two teams, neither willing to take responsibility for their part.

When an interviewer asks you to "describe a time you worked with a difficult client," these are exactly the three steps they need: what the client said, what you discovered, and whose problem it ultimately was.

Your resume should follow the same logic. Instead of writing "integrated data pipelines for clients," write: you discovered an export file no team owned—which was why the pipeline broke every week—and helped two teams reach agreement on ownership.

If the Second Law holds, then it's no surprise that a book written before the cloud era still describes FDE work so aptly. Clients' tech stacks have been replaced many times over, but where the problem lies hasn't changed.
