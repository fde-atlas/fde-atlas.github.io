Your vendor’s forward-deployed engineer is moving in... Plan the move-out!

Procurement Tidbits · 2026-10-19 · 9 min read

Summary

The article examines the rise of forward-deployed engineers (FDEs) in procurement tech, with Coupa (Catalyst), Zip (AI Spend Automation) and Kinaxis launching FDE-based offerings in mid-2026. The author, a former implementation consultant, argues FDEs are an old role revived for a moment when both organizations and AI technology are immature. The model bridges two gaps: procurement teams unprepared for agentic AI, and fast-changing models and protocols. But it carries a lock-in risk worse than classic implementations, since process knowledge and business logic accumulate on the vendor's side. Gartner predicts 70% of enterprises will abandon vendor-built agentic AI by 2028. The author recommends planning exit from day one: build a center of excellence, move through shadow, co-build and own phases, and contractually secure IP, documentation and knowledge transfer.

Key points

  • Coupa, Zip and Kinaxis launched FDE offerings in 2026; the role originated at Palantir.
  • FDEs fill two gaps: immature procurement organizations and weekly-changing AI technology.
  • Gartner expects 70% of enterprises to abandon vendor-FDE-built agentic AI by 2028.
  • Key risk: process semantics and logic end up owned by the vendor, creating dependency.
  • Plan move-out from day one via a CoE and contractual knowledge-transfer and IP terms.

Procurement technology has a new job title. In May, Coupa launched Catalyst, an AI services offering built around forward-deployed engineers (FDEs). In June, Zip bundled its own FDEs into a new offering called AI Spend Automation. The same week, Kinaxis introduced forward-deployed engineering as a new engagement model. Further down the market, the job boards tell the same story.

If you have been around procurement technology long enough, this should sound familiar. Vendor engineers sitting at the customer, configuring, integrating and fixing things until the system works? We used to call them implementation consultants (and I have been one).

So is the FDE just a rebrand? Partly, and sometimes entirely. Analysts already warn about “FDE washing”: consulting services marketed as FDE, at premium fees, without the delivery depth to match. My view is that the FDE is an old role adapted to a very specific moment, one where both organizations and the technology are “immature.” It makes sense right now but it also comes with a risk that nobody selling it has much incentive to mention: the knowledge that makes your AI work ends up owned by someone else.

Where the title comes from

The term comes from Palantir. Its first customers were U.S. intelligence agencies with classified data, undocumented systems and requirements they could not openly share. Classic product discovery from headquarters was impossible, so Palantir put engineers inside the customer, close to the action (hence the military vocabulary). The company still describes the role as working on-site on customer problems, and by some accounts it employed more FDEs than regular software engineers until around 2016.

Three things set Palantir’s version apart from classic implementation:

  • FDEs write production code instead of configuring screens.
  • They answer for outcomes rather than a project plan.
  • What they learn in the field then flows back into the product.

Venture capital gave the model its economic justification. In Trading Margin for Moat, a16z argued in 2025 that AI startups optimizing for 80% gross margins were being shortsighted, and that hands-on deployment work is what makes software hard to displace. Then, in 2026, a Palantir specificity became a category in itself. OpenAI launched The Deployment Company, Anthropic set up an embedded services venture with Blackstone, Hellman & Friedman and Goldman Sachs, and AWS put $1 billion behind its own FDE organization.

An old job in a new context

If you take away the title and look at the responsibilities, it's clear that the job has a long history: field engineers at IBM, ERP implementers, systems integrators, the consultants who spent months at a customer’s site configuring a solution. Every generation of enterprise software has needed someone to close the gap between what was sold and what works in a specific company. What changed is the size of that gap and why it exists.

Why now: two immaturity gaps

The first gap is organizational. According to multiple studies from several research firms, a small majority of procurement executives are confident in their teams’ awareness of agentic AI and its applicability to their organization. Fewer than half of CPOs feel confident they can monitor and control agentic AI. Readiness is also uneven: procurement organizations score better on technology management than on strategy, governance or skills, and their most advanced practices are piloting and experimentation.

The numbers point to a more down-to-earth and pragmatic issue. An agent needs to know what “preferred supplier” or “critical category” means for a specific company. And the thing is that, in most organizations, that meaning lives in people’s heads, spreadsheets and habits. Clean data does not solve that. Someone has to go and find the meaning, and that is exactly what an embedded engineer does.

The second gap is technological. Models, protocols and agent frameworks change almost every week. For example, a footnote in Coupa’s announcement I mentioned earlier says the company expected to deliver 3rd-party integration and support for MCP (Model Context Protocol) and A2A (Agent2Agent) in September 2026, four months after launch.

Coupa’s chief product and technology officer, Salvatore Lombardo, put it this way at Inspire: “To win in the agentic era, your ability to absorb change must match the speed of the technology.” And he is right. The FDE is, therefore, the vendor’s way of lending customers that ability while they do not have it yet. It bridges a product that keeps moving and an organization that is not ready to absorb it.

Procurement vendors are already hiring

Coupa’s Catalyst combines FDEs with business solution architects and consultants who “work side-by-side with customers to bridge the gap between product capability and AI value delivery,” paired with outcome-based pricing.

Zip’s AI Spend Automation pairs platform access and AI consumption credits with FDEs “who build and deploy custom agents directly inside customer environments.” In the same announcement, Block’s CPO lists “forward-deployed engineers who specialize in procurement AI and build alongside us“ among what his team gets.

Kinaxis operates in an adjacent segment, supply chain planning, but its move matters for another reason: experience. Its chief of agentic solutions, Manik Sharma, previously led supply chain work at Palantir, told Steve Banker at Forbes that “putting 25-year-old engineers with the customer is a problem,” because they do not understand constraints or the bullwhip effect.

Startups and vendors haven’t issued press releases on the topic, but their hiring says enough. Some have even created a “Forward Deployed Procurement Engineer” role that asks for a background in procurement or strategic sourcing. An example is Zip’s own procurement FDE posting, which asks for enough domain knowledge to “earn credibility quickly with an AP Director or CPO.” An engineer who cannot explain a three-way match will build agents that automate the wrong thing very efficiently.

Is it a good model?

Opinions split sharply. Anaplan’s CEO, Charlie Gottdiener, told Forbes that FDE is a good selling model and a good initial deployment model, but “not a great model for running the software.” His concern is what comes after the proof of concept: “Now they’ve got forward-deployed engineers that they have to pay for and rely on in perpetuity.”

Peter Bendor-Samuel of Everest Group draws a line based on context. In his view, FDEs are essential in dynamic agentic environments and can be harmful in stable systems that change through configuration and controlled updates, where they bypass governance. He calls them “a signal, rather than a solution.“ In a more recent piece, he goes further and argues that some services will become inseparable from agentic software.

Gartner adds a number that undercuts the model's main argument. It expects less than 20% of FDE engagements through 2028 to turn recurring customer needs into core product capabilities. If field learning doesn't flow back into the product, companies are left with custom work they will pay to maintain.

I think they are all partly right because they describe different stages of maturity. Early on, with an immature organization and a fast-moving technology, the embedded engineer is the fastest and probably safest way to get something into production. Later, that same arrangement becomes a dependency. The trouble starts when the arrangement outlives the stage (and duration) it was designed for.

The risk: from bridge to prison

Look at what an FDE actually builds during an engagement: integrations, agent configurations, prompts and workflows. More importantly, they learn how a company’s procurement process really works: the exceptions, the unwritten approval rules, the categories that don’t behave like the policy says they should.

At the end of the engagement, that knowledge lives in the FDE’s head and in the vendor’s environment. Very little of it stays with the customer’s organization.

This is worse than the lock-in from classic implementations for two reasons. First, a classic implementation had an end, at least in theory: go-live, handover, hypercare, done. Agentic systems never stop changing, so there is no natural moment when the knowledge gets handed back. Second, the FDE captures the meaning layer of a company’s procurement operations (definitions, rules, exceptions), which is the most valuable asset in an AI deployment. Customers end up renting access to their own logic.

Gartner sees the same trap coming and predicts that by 2028, 70% of enterprises will abandon agentic AI built by vendor FDEs, because they end up “trapped by soaring costs and unable to evolve it on their own.”

Incentives do not help. The a16z argument I mentioned earlier is explicit: deployment work is what makes a vendor hard to displace. From the vendor’s point of view, that dependency is the moa and the cost has to land somewhere eventually. Looking at one marketing tech. vendor’s accounts, Martech Therapy found an implementation business running at a gross margin of 0.08%. Nobody runs a business like that forever, so expect vendors to price back today’s cheap services at renewal.

A few signs that you are becoming a prisoner:

  • Every change to an agent or workflow requires the vendor’s FDE.
  • Nobody on your team can explain how the agents make decisions.
  • Documentation exists only in the vendor’s tools, if it exists at all.
  • Renewal conversations feel one-sided.

There is also the “big jelly” problem (organizations absorb change pressure and return to their original shape once the pressure goes away). When the FDEs leave, the organization drifts back to its old way of working unless something internal holds the new shape. Some vendors promise to address this change resilience/inertia. For example, AWS says its FDE teams deliver “systems structured for self-sufficiency, not ongoing dependency.“ That is a good sentence, so procurement teams should ask their procurement technology vendor to put it in their contract.

Plan the move out from day one

The appropriate “landing zone” for this organizational knowledge is a center of excellence (CoE) that consolidates activities requiring critical and/or specialized skill sets, with a focus on developing them as core competencies. AI deployment knowledge should become one of those core competencies.

The CoE doesn't need to be big in size; it “only” needs people who combine process knowledge, data understanding and enough technical fluency to follow what the FDE builds. The transition can then follow the three classical phases:

  • Shadow. The FDE builds, and the CoE sits alongside, documents and asks questions.
  • Co-build. The CoE owns the semantic layer (definitions, business rules, exceptions) and the documentation, while the FDE handles the engineering.
  • Own. The CoE runs and extends the deployed agents, and calls in the vendor only for new capabilities.

None of this happens by goodwill, so it has to be in the contract:

  • Knowledge transfer as a named deliverable, with acceptance criteria
  • Ownership of code and IP rights to code, configurations, prompts and business logic built for you
  • Documentation standards and access outside the vendor’s environment
  • FDE hours that decline over the contract term
  • Exit support if you change direction

Often, several objections come up quickly, and they deserve an answer.

The first is talent. If FDE skills are that scarce, how would a procurement team hire them? The answer is that the CoE does not need to hire FDEs. It needs fewer, more domain-heavy profiles, grown internally while the FDEs are still there. That is the whole point of the shadow phase.

The second is the track record of CoEs. Many turned into bottlenecks or produced documents nobody read. The difference here has to be the mandate: tie the CoE to specific deployments it will take over, not to a governance charter.

The third is the moving target. If the technology changes every week, can the CoE ever take over? Not entirely, so a realistic goal is for the CoE to own the stable core (process, semantics, governance) and bring in outside help, from the vendor or elsewhere, for new capabilities. Even partial ownership puts you in a very different position from depending on the vendor for everything.

Questions to ask before signing

If a vendor offers FDEs as part of a next deal, then it is important to ask:

  • Who, specifically, will work with us, and have they deployed in procurement before?
  • What do they build that stays with us, and in what form?
  • How will knowledge transfer to our team, and how will we know it happened?
  • What will FDE support cost after the pilot and after year one?
  • What happens to our agents and workflows if the FDE leaves or we end the engagement?
  • What is your plan to help us stop needing you within two years?

The last question is the most revealing because a vendor with a clear answer understands that the FDE is a bridge for this period. A vendor without one is probably counting on you never crossing it.
