Embedded FDE Team vs Project Delivery Team: Agency Guide

FWD Deployment · 2026-10-23 · 8 min read

Summary

This guide compares an embedded forward deployed team, which works alongside agency staff on live data and adjusts scope as it learns, with a project delivery team that builds to a written spec and hands off. Neither label is a defined federal acquisition term, so the contract type carries the weight. Embedded work may justify time-and-materials under FAR 16.601 only when extent or duration cannot be estimated, with separate fixed hourly rates per labor category. Project delivery of commercial services generally requires firm-fixed-price. DoD requires approval for T&M or labor-hour work above $1 million and pushes to limit its value or duration. Air-gapped operations and clearances are not governed by FAR contract-type rules and must be spelled out in the SOW.

Key points

  • Embedded teams adapt scope on live data; project teams deliver to a fixed spec.
  • T&M is allowed only when extent or duration cannot be estimated, per FAR 16.601.
  • DoD requires approval above $1 million and limits on T&M value or duration.
  • Commercial project delivery generally defaults to firm-fixed-price contracts.
  • Air-gap and clearance requirements belong in the SOW, not FAR rules.

An embedded forward deployed team works on your live data next to your staff and adjusts its work as it learns. A project delivery team builds to a fixed specification and hands the result off. That operational difference tends to push the first toward time-and-materials or labor-hour pricing and the second toward firm-fixed-price, and the FAR sets clear limits on when each is allowed.

This is general information, not legal advice, so have your contracting officer and counsel read the rules against your specific acquisition.

What is the difference between an embedded forward deployed team and a project delivery team?

Neither term is a standardized federal acquisition term. This article uses them as working definitions, and your contract has to spell out what you mean.

  • Embedded forward deployed team: engineers who sit with your program staff, work against real agency data, and change what they build as they see how the data and users behave.
  • Project delivery team: a team that builds to a written requirement, delivers it, and hands it off.

The practical test is whether you can write the finished product down today. If you can, a project team fits. If the answer depends on what the data turns out to contain, you are closer to the embedded model. For the role itself, see our page on what a forward deployed engineer does and who hires them, and our comparison of the forward deployed engineer vs software engineer.

Which contract type fits each model?

The contract type is where the two models separate on paper. As of October 2026, these are the FAR and DFARS rules that matter most.

Contract type What the rule says Better fit for
Time-and-materials Pays fixed hourly rates per labor category plus actual material costs. Allowed only when the government cannot accurately estimate extent or duration. Embedded work with uncertain scope
Firm-fixed-price or fixed-price with economic price adjustment For commercial products and services, generally required unless an exception applies. Project delivery with a defined spec
Indefinite-delivery contract with mixed order types If awarded with T&M or labor-hour pricing, structure it to permit fixed-price orders to the maximum extent practicable. A program that starts embedded and later fixes scope

When can you use time-and-materials for an embedded team?

Only when the government cannot accurately estimate the extent or duration of the work, or anticipate costs with reasonable confidence, at the time of placing the contract. That rule is in FAR 16.601.

That is the same uncertainty that makes embedded work hard to scope. Write that reasoning into your file, because it is the justification for the contract type.

FAR 16.601 also sets the mechanics:

  • The contract pays for direct labor hours at specified fixed hourly rates.
  • Those rates include wages, overhead, general and administrative expenses, and profit.
  • Materials are paid at actual cost.
  • The contract must specify separate fixed hourly rates for each labor category.

In practice, that means your embedded team needs defined labor categories in the contract, each with its own rate. A vague line like "engineering support" does not meet the rule.

Is time-and-materials fixed-price?

No. FAR Subpart 16.2 states that time-and-materials and labor-hour contracts are not fixed-price contracts. Your agency carries more of the cost risk, because you pay for hours worked rather than a defined deliverable.

For commercial services, the clause at FAR 52.212-4 establishes a ceiling price that the contractor must use best efforts to stay within. Set the ceiling deliberately, and review it at each funding or option point.

When does firm-fixed-price make sense for project delivery?

For commercial products and commercial services, FAR Part 12 says agencies generally must use firm-fixed-price or fixed-price-with-economic-price-adjustment contracts unless an exception applies.

A project delivery team building to a clear spec fits that default well. You define the deliverable, the vendor prices it, and the price holds.

The DFARS applies the same logic to software. DFARS Subpart 227.72 says commercial computer software and documentation should, to the maximum extent practicable, be acquired using firm-fixed-price contracts or orders under available pricing schedules.

What extra hurdles apply at the Department of Defense?

DoD adds guardrails to time-and-materials work in DFARS Subpart 216.6. Plan for these before you pick the embedded model:

  • Approval threshold: approval is required for contracts or orders where the time-and-materials or labor-hour portion exceeds $1 million.
  • Determination: the determination must explain why cost-plus-fixed-fee or another contract type is not appropriate.
  • Minimize T&M: structure requirements to minimize time-and-materials and labor-hour work, including by limiting its value or duration and establishing fixed prices for portions of the requirement.
  • Indefinite-delivery contracts: contracts authorizing T&M orders should also authorize cost-reimbursement, incentive, or fixed-price orders to the maximum extent practicable.

The message is that T&M is acceptable for a bounded, justified slice of work, not as an open-ended default.

How do you price a hybrid: software plus embedded labor?

The sources above do not set a general price for a software subscription plus deployment, an hourly rate, or a cost comparison between the two team models. Any such figure would come from vendor quotes and your own market research.

What the rules do support is a split structure:

  • Fixed-price line for commercial software. This follows the DFARS 227.72 preference for firm-fixed-price or schedule orders.
  • Time-and-materials line for embedded labor, with named labor categories, a ceiling price and a limited period.
  • Fixed-price conversion points. As the work becomes estimable, move portions to fixed price, as DoD guidance directs for T&M work.

Should you use an OTA or a standard FAR-based contract?

The FAR excerpts do not establish that an other transaction agreement is categorically the best vehicle for forward deployed engineering. The same goes for a schedule, an IDIQ, a task order or any other vehicle.

The right choice depends on your agency's specific authority and acquisition strategy. Take these questions to your contracting officer:

  • Which acquisition authorities does our agency actually hold?
  • Can we describe the requirement well enough for a fixed price, or does uncertainty point to T&M?
  • Do we want an indefinite-delivery contract that lets orders change type later?

If you use a FAR-based contract, the contract-type rules above apply directly. If you consider another vehicle, ask counsel which of them carry over.

What about air-gapped environments and clearances?

The FAR contract-type rules do not set requirements for air-gapped deployment. They also do not set a general rule for clearances for forward deployed engineers. Clearance eligibility, sponsorship, facility access and need-to-know all depend on the agency, contract, position, information system and the classified or controlled information involved.

That means your SOW has to carry the detail. For an embedded team in a closed environment, decide and write down:

  • how software, patches and model updates get into the environment, and who approves them;
  • how data and media move in and out, including any removable media;
  • logging, remote administration and incident response responsibilities;
  • which positions require access to which systems, and who sponsors it.

Get these answers from your security office and authorizing official, not from the vendor's template. Then confirm that each one is priced, since labor categories and ceilings should reflect them.

How do you decide?

  • Write the requirement. If you can define the deliverable, lean toward firm-fixed-price.
  • If you cannot estimate extent or duration, document why and consider T&M under FAR 16.601.
  • Define labor categories and fixed hourly rates, and set a ceiling.
  • For DoD, check the $1 million approval line and plan fixed-price portions.
  • Write the security and air-gap terms into the SOW with your security office.

Common questions

How do security clearances work for embedded forward deployed engineers?

There is no general rule in the contract-type sources. Clearance eligibility, sponsorship, facility access and need-to-know depend on the agency, contract, position, information system, and the classified or controlled information involved. Ask your security office which positions need access before you write the SOW.

What contract vehicles are best for hiring forward deployed engineering firms?

No source establishes one best vehicle. An OTA, FAR contract, schedule, IDIQ or task order each depends on your agency's authority and acquisition strategy. If you use an indefinite-delivery contract with T&M pricing, the FAR says to structure it to permit fixed-price orders to the maximum extent practicable.

How does pricing differ between embedded FDEs and traditional project delivery teams?

Embedded work often fits time-and-materials, which pays fixed hourly rates per labor category plus actual materials. Project delivery for commercial services generally uses firm-fixed-price or fixed-price with economic price adjustment. No verified source gives a general dollar comparison.

Can forward deployed teams operate on-premises in air-gapped government environments?

The FAR contract-type rules do not address air-gapped deployment, so it depends on your agency's security requirements and authorization. Your SOW should state how software, patches, model updates and data enter and leave the environment.

What should be included in the Statement of Work for an embedded AI team?

At minimum, name each labor category with its fixed hourly rate, set a ceiling price, and limit the period of T&M work. Add security terms for your environment, and identify portions that can convert to fixed price. Have counsel review the final language.
