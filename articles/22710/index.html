What's the difference between FDE and SES? Distinguish them by contract structure, chain of command, and evaluation criteria.

Original title: FDEとSESの違いは？契約構造・指揮命令・評価軸で見分ける。

株式会社Respectify · 2026-08-04 · 30 min read

Summary

This article from Japan's Respectify addresses a common objection that FDE-style support services are just rebranded on-site SES (system engineering service) contracts. The author concedes both typically use quasi-delegation (jun'inin) contracts and work at client sites, but argues the real difference lies in operational design, not contract wording. Three axes distinguish them: whether the deliverable is billed hours or a rhythm of working software; how deeply the vendor participates in setting priorities; and whether success is measured by logged hours or weekly demos of running software. Citing IPA model contracts and Japan's Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare's Directive 37, the article notes disguised-dispatch risk depends on who actually gives daily work instructions. It also argues that "being on-site" matters less than involvement in prioritization, with weekly demos replacing the reassurance of physical presence. The conclusion: neither model is superior—choose SES for short-term staffing gaps, FDE-style support for continuous joint prioritization with weekly visible progress.

Key points

  • SES and FDE support often share the same quasi-delegation contract form.
  • Three distinguishing axes: deliverable, priority-setting involvement, evaluation standard.
  • SES is evaluated on logged hours; FDE support on weekly demos of working software.
  • Disguised-dispatch risk hinges on who actually issues daily instructions.
  • Physical presence matters less than depth of involvement in client priorities.

When explaining FDE-type support services like Respectify STUDIO, we often receive the question, "Isn't that ultimately the same as on-site SES (System Engineering Services)?" It is not uncommon for a business manager considering external support due to a shortage of engineer hiring to write "quasi-delegated contract, fixed monthly fee" in their approval documents, only to be told by the internal information systems department or corporate planning that "that's just SES in different words." Given that both use the same contract term of quasi-delegation, this reaction is actually quite natural.

The problem in such situations is that pushing back with abstract ideals rarely convinces those who must approve the budget. An explanation like "It's a new AI-native way of working" does not answer the concrete questions from legal affairs or corporate planning: "What kind of contract is it, ultimately, and what is guaranteed?" For business managers exploring new forms of support when neither additional hiring nor outsourcing is feasible, what is needed is not abstract DX theory, but implementation-level specificity: which clauses of the contract differ and how, and what to check to tell them apart.

It is true that they share certain features: working at the customer's site, and the fact that the contract is often quasi-delegated. In fact, most SES contracts are also quasi-delegated, and there is no need to exaggerate or deny this point. The purpose of this article is not to rank FDE versus SES, but to clarify where the two intersect and where they diverge, using three lenses: contract structure, chain of command, and evaluation criteria. Our goal is for those who must get approval to come away with enough specificity to answer internal questions in their own words. Why FDE Looks Like SES

Behind the tendency to confuse FDE with SES lie practical similarities. First, both FDE and most SES engineers work by entering the customer's work environment (an office or project room, or a team on online meetings). Second, there is the contract type. Unlike worker dispatch, SES is in most cases concluded as a quasi-delegated contract. A quasi-delegated contract is one in which payment is made not for the completion of deliverables themselves, but for performing the work as a professional. In this respect, FDE-type support also generally adopts a quasi-delegated contract, so if you compare only the structure of the contracts, SES and FDE fall into almost the same category.

SES has long functioned in Japan's IT industry as a mechanism for flexibly supplying engineers with specialized knowledge to sites short on manpower. The demand that SES has filled is real: development projects suddenly needing more staff, situations where no one in-house is familiar with a particular technology, or times when help is needed only during busy periods. Much of Japan's software development has arguably been built on this mechanism. For individual engineers as well, SES has certain value as a contract form that allows them to hone their skills while working across multiple sites. This raison d'être is not negated by the emergence of FDE as a new way of working; the two should not be discussed in terms of superiority or inferiority, but understood as different options to be chosen according to purpose.

Let us also touch on why FDE has been attracting attention in recent years. For the reasons why this role has rapidly come into the spotlight over the past few years, see our companion article "Why OpenAI and Anthropic Are Both Hiring FDEs Now".

The similar appearance of the paperwork also fuels the confusion. For SES and FDE-type support, both quasi-delegated contracts, the contract templates, the line items on monthly invoices, and the formats of utilization reports all tend to look much the same on the surface. It is only natural that legal and accounting staff would find it hard to distinguish the two by looking at contracts and invoices alone. The difference lies not in the format of the documents, but in the design of the operations behind them. That is precisely why it is necessary to go beyond judging from documents such as contracts and invoices, and to verify the operational reality of how the work actually proceeds and at what rhythm.

This is why the confusion can be called a natural reaction. When the two keywords "quasi-delegation" and "on-site" overlap, the more familiar someone is with contract practice, the more naturally they feel "isn't this the same mechanism?" However, having the same contract type and what actually happens on the ground are separate issues. From the next chapter onward, we will look concretely at the "differences within the same contract type."

The Difference in What Is Provided: Man-Hours or a Rhythm of Deliverables

The first difference is what is being provided as the object of the contract.

Most SES provides the engineer's working hours themselves. Even if the contract is formally quasi-delegated, what is practically required is often "to work for a set number of hours in a set location (or online environment)," and what gets built within those hours is frequently left to instructions from the site. For example, when a project suddenly runs short of hands, what is expected of an engineer joining via SES is, first, to secure the working hours and complete assigned tasks by their deadlines. This way of working is itself reasonable as project management, and there is nothing wrong with it.

By contrast, what FDE-type support provides is not working hours, but the continuous arrival of deliverables at a steady rhythm. The "Information System Model Transactions and Contracts (Second Edition)" published by Japan's Information-technology Promotion Agency (IPA) organizes the difference between fixed-price (contract for work) and quasi-delegated contracts along the axis of whether payment is for the completion of deliverables or for performing the work as a professional (see: IPA "Information System Model Transactions and Contracts (Second Edition)" (2020)). FDE-type support falls under this quasi-delegated category in terms of contract type, but as a matter of operational design, it places the rhythm of delivering working software weekly at the center of the support.

To make this concrete: under a contract that provides only working hours, the end-of-month invoice is determined as the sum of "how many hours were worked this month." Under a contract that provides a rhythm of deliverables, "what will be working this week" is decided first, and the accumulation of these becomes a monthly result visible to the customer. In other words, rather than "selling time by the slice," the commitment is to "value accumulating at a fixed cycle," meaning that even within the same quasi-delegated contract, the substance of what is provided differs.

If this distinction is confused, misunderstandings arise on both the client and vendor sides. If a contract is premised on the provision of working hours but the client asks "What moved forward this week?"—demanding a deliverable rhythm—that means the contract's structure and its actual operation are out of alignment. Conversely, if a contract is premised on a deliverable rhythm but the client only asks "How many hours did you work last month?"—inquiring solely about working hours—that does not measure the value the contract is originally committed to delivering. The key to avoiding later misunderstandings is for both parties to share the same understanding, at the contract stage, of whether what is being provided is labor hours or a deliverable rhythm.

It may be easier to understand by analogy with taxi fare structures. It is similar to the difference between metered pricing, where the meter rises according to time and distance traveled, and a flat fare fixed for the destination. The provision of working hours corresponds to the former, where the basis for billing is the "input quantity" of how much work was done during the contract period. The provision of a deliverable rhythm is closer to the latter, where compensation is paid for the accumulation of "endpoints"—what becomes functional each week—within a predetermined monthly fee. Both contract forms have their own rationale, but the nature of what must be explained in internal approvals differs clearly.

Differences in the Structure of Command and Control

The second difference is the structure of command and control—that is, who gives instructions on how the work should proceed.

In the "Q&A on the Criteria for Distinguishing Worker Dispatch Businesses from Businesses Conducted under Contracting (Notice No. 37)" published by the Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare, the position is stated that whether a case falls under worker dispatch or under contracting/business outsourcing is judged based on the actual circumstances, not on the name or form of the contract. In addition, under the criteria for distinguishing worker dispatch from contracting (Notice No. 37) and the Ministry's Q&A, it is regarded as an important requirement for this distinction that, in a contracting/business outsourcing arrangement, the client does not directly give workers instructions regarding how to perform the work, working hours, and so on (see: Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare, "Q&A on the Criteria for Distinguishing Worker Dispatch Businesses from Businesses Conducted under Contracting (Notice No. 37)" (as of August 2026)).

To break this idea down a bit further, what separates a worker dispatch contract from a contracting/business outsourcing contract is the actual situation of "who is giving daily work instructions on the ground." If a manager on the client side assigns the day's tasks at morning meetings and gives detailed instructions on how to proceed, there is a risk that, even if the contract says "quasi-mandate" (jun'inin), the actual situation will be judged as closer to worker dispatch. Conversely, if the vendor company exercises command and control over its own engineers, and the client merely communicates requests about "what they want to achieve," the actual situation of contracting/business outsourcing is maintained. This criterion is a fundamental principle of contract practice that applies equally to both SES and FDE-type support, and it is a premise that both client and vendor should be aware of regardless of which contract form is chosen.

Since both SES and FDE-type support are quasi-mandate contracts, structurally the vendor company is required to hold the right of command and control over the engineers, and the client must not be in a position to give direct instructions. Because the contract form is the same, there is no essential difference between the two on this point. In practice, this boundary tends to become blurred in cases where the client's staff goes so far as to give detailed daily work instructions. Rather than this happening out of malice, it can occur out of good intentions—wanting to ensure progress—leading someone to inadvertently give direct instructions like "Please do this tomorrow." Regardless of the contract form, keeping this line of command and control from becoming ambiguous is a matter of practical risk management for both the client and the vendor.

For example, there is a difference in meaning, as an actual exercise of command and control, between a business owner on the client side conveying a request at a weekly demo—"We'd like this feature prioritized next week"—and giving detailed instructions at daily stand-ups: "Please do this task today, following these steps." The former is the communication of a request about "what to achieve," while the latter approaches intervention in "how to carry out the work"—the method of execution. The reason FDE-type support inserts a backlog mechanism in between can be understood as a deliberate device for consciously separating the sharing of priorities from direct intervention in daily work methods.

However, in practice, the position of who controls "what gets built, according to whose priorities" differs. In SES settings, the allocation of daily tasks tends to be effectively left to the client's managers, and the vendor's engineers often remain in the role of working through the tasks they are given. This is a matter of practical-level daily prioritization, separate from where the contractual right of command and control resides. In FDE-type support, by contrast, the process of prioritizing the backlog (the list of tasks to be worked on) jointly with the client's business owner is built into the design of the support itself, not outside the contract. The accurate way to state this difference is: while the legal structure regarding where the right of command and control resides is the same, the depth of practical involvement differs—namely, how far the vendor side steps into the decision-making of "what to build first."

Differences in Evaluation Criteria: Working Hours or Weekly Demos

The third difference is the evaluation criterion—what is used to judge whether things are "going well."

The most common unit of evaluation in SES settings is working hours. Metrics such as how many hours were worked in a month and what the utilization rate is serve as the primary yardstick for measuring contract performance. Under this evaluation criterion, as long as the working hours are met, the contractual obligation is considered fulfilled, and what actually became functional during those hours tends to be treated as a separate management item. To give an extreme example, a situation can arise where the monthly working hours are met, but when asked to show something that actually runs, many tasks are still in progress and completed features cannot be shown right away. In environments evaluated by working hours, the monthly regular meeting tends to center on reports like "We worked X hours this month," and the business owner often has to ask the engineers separately, "So what actually changed?"

The evaluation criterion adopted by FDE-type support is different. The "Information System Model Transactions and Contracts (Agile Development Version)" published by IPA positions agile development as "a methodology capable of flexibly responding to feature additions and changes, changes in priorities, improvements to partially released components, and the like," and explains that it is a model contract premised on a quasi-mandate contract in which compensation is paid not for the completion of deliverables but for the vendor company performing the work itself as an expert (see: IPA, "Information System Model Transactions and Contracts (Agile Development Version)" (2020)).

What this breakdown shows is that even with the same quasi-delegation contract, the substance of the evaluation can change dramatically depending on how the engagement is designed. In FDE-style support, the "weekly demo"—actually showing working software each week—is placed at the center of evaluation. Rather than the amount of hours worked, the evaluation criterion is maintaining a state where the customer can see with their own eyes how much the working product has moved forward within the short one-week cycle. In an environment evaluated through weekly demos, it is a given that the team must present, every week, how far the tasks started that week have become a working form, so the structure makes it hard for a "started but never finished" state to persist. While working hours are a metric that measures "effort invested," the weekly demo is a mechanism for directly confirming "value produced." This difference in evaluation criteria is also covered, together with cost expectations and contract details, in the companion article "Cost Expectations and Contract Structures for FDE-Style Support", so please refer to it as well.

The tendency for interpersonal skills to be valued more highly than technical ability is also not unrelated to this evaluation criterion. LeadDev, a media outlet for engineering leaders, points out regarding the FDE role that the ability to engage with customers is the strongest differentiator, and that the highest demand is for engineers with deep business knowledge (see: LeadDev, "The rise of the forward-deployed engineer (FDE)" (2026)). The point being made is that what is being tested is not the ability to carry out tasks as instructed, but the ability to grasp priorities together with the customer and keep converting them into working products. Precisely because the weekly demo evaluation occurs every week, the importance of these interpersonal skills and business understanding becomes built into the design of the contract itself. In a weekly demo setting, without waiting for a month-end regular meeting, you can directly see "what worked this week" in a 30-minute to one-hour session and give feedback on the spot. For business owners, there is also the advantage that actually seeing the screen in action makes it easier to decide what to prioritize next, compared to the effort of deciphering work-hour reports.

The three differences we have examined—what is delivered, direction and control, and evaluation criteria—are each independent issues, yet in practice they appear as an integrated whole. If you place the rhythm of deliverables at the center of the engagement, you inevitably need to jointly hold the backlog priorities with the customer, and precisely because priorities are held jointly, an operation that confirms results through weekly demos becomes meaningful. Conversely, if working hours are placed at the center of the engagement, evaluating by working hours is the more consistent approach. These three differences are best understood not as scattered variations but as connected parts of a single operational design.

The Changing Meaning of Being On-Site

In light of the three differences discussed above, what the word "on-site" (resident staffing) actually refers to also needs to be reexamined.

Physically sitting in the client's office does not in itself create an essential difference. Even sitting in the same place, an engagement that merely works through assigned tasks one by one and an engagement that keeps building working products while making daily judgments within the customer's priorities differ completely in the sense of ownership over the results. Conversely, the latter style of engagement can be fully achieved even without physical on-site presence.

It is also true that a sensibility that might be called "on-site faith" remains deeply rooted in Japan's B2B world. From the client's perspective, having an engineer at a desk in front of them, reachable at any moment, provides a real sense of reassurance. The feeling that progress is visible and that you can consult immediately when something goes wrong is also the flip side of the anxiety of not knowing what is progressing in a distant location. It is likely that the demand for this reassurance is part of why SES has been widely accepted in the form of on-site placement at client offices. Considering that, not only in Japan's IT industry, the era in which outsourcing was inseparable from the anxiety of "ordering work from someone you never see" lasted a long time, making the other party's presence visible through on-site placement had a certain rationality.

What is important here is that the weekly demo mechanism serves as a substitute for this reassurance. If there is an operation of actually showing working products at a fixed time every week, the client can concretely confirm "how far things have progressed now" regardless of whether a desk exists. In fact, being shown an actually working screen every week makes it easier to feel confident about progress than on-site staffing where you are merely told verbally that "things are going well."

Now that remote work and hybrid work have become the norm, the reality that the "on-site" label refers to is also changing. In the past, "commuting to the client's office every day" was the definition of on-site staffing, but now that online meetings and chat tools have become established, what has substantive meaning is whether you are moving at the same rhythm as the customer's team, sharing the same priorities, and actually doing the work. That Respectify STUDIO does not assume physical on-site presence is also because it defines the substance of the "workplace" not by the presence of a desk but by the depth of involvement in priorities.

These changes are not limited to Japan's IT industry. Centered on overseas tech companies, a style of working is spreading in which people work deeply embedded in client companies' operations while their bases remain distributed. As the aforementioned LeadDev analysis shows, what is valued in the FDE role is interpersonal skills and business understanding, not where you physically sit. In Japanese B2B as well, the shift toward a perspective that looks not at "whether someone is on-site" but at "how deeply they are involved in the customer's priorities" is expected to advance further going forward.

These five classifications matter in practice because, in approval meetings and contract reviews, if you cannot answer the question "what type of contract is this?", the discussion cannot move forward. Whether a contract falls under worker dispatch is a weighty issue that also relates to the risk of disguised subcontracting, so this must first be distinguished on an external basis. After that, whether it is a contract for work (ukeoi) or a quasi-mandate (jun'inin) is determined by a single point: whether there is an obligation to complete a deliverable.

When faced with this table, the first thing many procurement managers wonder about is "which category does our contract fall into?" When reviewing existing outsourcing contracts as well, confirming where the contract sits among these five classifications is the starting point for determining what to negotiate next.

The questions a procurement manager should confirm before signing a contract can be organized into three main points. First, which side holds the authority to direct and command under the contract. Second, is there an obligation to complete a deliverable, or is the performance of the work itself the object of compensation? Third, in what frequency and format can the actual progress of the work be verified? For SES and quasi-mandate-type FDE support, the answers to the first and second questions are nearly identical, but the answers to the third differ significantly. In practice, the way to distinguish them is not just the wording of the contract, but concretely confirming this third, operational aspect.

Note that "outsourcing (quasi-mandate contract, general)" in the table refers to general outsourcing, such as contracting on a one-off basis with an individual who has a particular skill. In this case, it falls into the same category as FDE-type support in terms of where the authority to direct resides and responsibility for deliverables, but by the nature of the contract, it does not necessarily include the operational commitment of continuously delivering deliverables at a regular rhythm. The biggest point that distinguishes FDE-type support from other quasi-mandate contracts is whether "that rhythm is built into the operations."

What this table shows is the fact that, looking only at the legal axes of contract type and the location of the authority to direct, SES and FDE-type support are positioned in almost the same category. What separates the two is not the wording of the contract, but a difference in design: what kind of operations (the rhythm of deliverables, backlog management, and evaluation methods) are built into that quasi-mandate contract. What procurement managers should confirm before signing is not just the single word "quasi-mandate" written in the contract, but, as its substance, the operational arrangements for in what rhythm and in what form the deliverables can be verified.

The practical picture in Japanese B2B

So, how do these differences manifest in practice? Using Respectify STUDIO, offered by our company Respectify, as an example, we will introduce a concrete picture of the operations.

Respectify STUDIO takes the form of a quasi-mandate contract with a fixed monthly fee. Rather than billing by accumulating working hours, support is provided at a fixed monthly rate, making it easy to fit into approval processes and budget planning.

In the first month of support, we start by understanding the client's business and on-the-ground situation, identifying the items to tackle and creating the prototype of a backlog. Rather than going through months of requirements definition before starting work, we begin building something small and working from the first week, confirming the direction while having the client actually try it.

At the center of the operations are the weekly demo and the backlog. In the first half of each week, we select the tasks for that week from the backlog together with the client's business owner and confirm priorities. In the second half of the week, we set aside time on a fixed day for the weekly demo, showing on screen the implementations that became functional that week. What we bring is not slides reporting progress, but software in a state that can actually be touched and run. If feedback comes up at the demo, we add it on the spot as the next backlog item and reflect it in the following week's priorities. Rather than proceeding according to a fixed specification document prepared in advance, if market reactions or the situation within the team change, the ordering of the following week's backlog changes accordingly. This approach aligns with the philosophy underlying IPA's model contract for agile development: flexibly responding to the addition and modification of features and changes in priorities.

The contents of the backlog are not an abstract list of task names, but items made concrete down to working units, such as "this screen will allow this operation" or "this data will be automatically aggregated under these conditions." Priorities are organized based on the criterion of whether a single item can be brought to a working state in about a week, which makes it unlikely for a situation to arise where work has started but remains incomplete for weeks.

We do not have staff physically on-site; instead, we use AI-native development tools to carry out implementation remotely. This is a direct operational embodiment of the idea stated in the previous chapter: "the substance of being on-site lies not in a desk, but in involvement in prioritization." At one Series A B2B SaaS company, even while engineer hiring could not keep up, this setup has maintained a steady weekly delivery of working software without interruption. There are many clients who say that, even without in-house engineers, the weekly demo allows the business owner to see progress with their own eyes, so they can operate without anxiety about having outsourced the work. Both the backlog and the weekly demo are designed to be operable without introducing any special new management tools, with the aim of not placing any new operational burden on the client.

A question that often comes up in approval meetings is also "how easy is it to stop the contract midway?" The fixed monthly fee and quasi-mandate format has the characteristic that, compared to long-term lump-sum contracts, it is easier to make decisions about continuation or revision in line with the state of the business. Rather than solidifying requirements months ahead before signing, the ability to decide on continuation each time while watching the accumulation of the recent backlog and weekly demos is an easy-to-handle contract format for startups and SaaS companies whose business phase changes frequently.

Under these operations, the way the business owner engages also changes. Since they attend the weekly demo every week and take on the role of making prioritization decisions each time, they cannot simply hand everything over to an external party and wait for results. In exchange, they gain the advantage of being able to continuously judge what is most important in their own business at the resolution of actual implementation. The point this operational design aims for is being able to remain deeply involved in business decisions themselves without bearing the burden of hiring and managing engineers. A design that separates the burden of hiring and developing people from the momentum of advancing implementation, while leaving decision-making authority with the business side, becomes one realistic option for companies in a phase where both increasing headcount and placing a lump-sum outsourcing order are difficult.

SES or FDE-type support: which should you choose?

Based on the three axes we have organized so far (what is provided, practical involvement in direction and command, and evaluation criteria), which one to choose is determined not by the superiority of the contracts, but by what you need at the current stage.

In situations where you want to cover a short-term labor shortage, or where you want to secure personnel familiar with a specific technology on an hourly basis, choosing SES is a rational option. In development projects that require sudden additional staff, or in situations where you only need help during busy periods, the clarity of a contract based on working hours becomes a direct strength.

On the other hand, in situations where the business owner wants to be continuously involved in backlog prioritization and make decisions while checking working deliverables in weekly demos, FDE-style support fits better as an operational design. The deciding factor for this choice is a situation where, rather than waiting for reports of working hours, you want to see with your own eyes "what has become functional" each week and decide the next move.

Also, in situations where the specifications are already clearly fixed and you want to explicitly state responsibility for the completion of deliverables in the contract, commissioned development under a fixed-price contract becomes an option. The difference between commissioned development and FDE-style support ("building and delivering" versus "continuously operating on-site") is covered in detail in the companion article "What is the difference between FDE and SIers/commissioned development?".

To summarize, rather than judging by contract form alone, returning to the two questions examined in this article—"Are you seeking the rhythm of working hours or of deliverables?" and "To what extent does your company want to remain involved in prioritization decisions?"—is the most reliable starting point for deciding which to choose.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q. If both FDE and SES are quasi-mandate contracts, are they ultimately the same thing? The contractual structure (quasi-mandate contract, work at the client's site) falls into the same category, but the substance of the operation differs: what is provided (working hours or the rhythm of deliverables), the depth of practical involvement in holding prioritization authority, and the evaluation criteria (working hours or weekly demos). The three axes organized in this article are the concrete points that distinguish the two.

Q. Are you saying that the SES contract model itself is problematic? No. SES has functioned for many years as a mechanism for flexibly supplying specialized knowledge to sites short on manpower, and the business model itself is not inferior. Summary

FDE and SES often fall within the same framework of quasi-mandate contracts as contract forms, and in that sense it cannot be flatly stated that they are "completely different things." What distinguishes the two is not the structure of the contract document, but the substance of the operation: what is provided (man-hours or the rhythm of deliverables), the position that holds prioritization authority, and the axis of evaluation (working hours or weekly demos). The SES business model itself is not inferior; it is simply that the roles and evaluation design assumed by the contract are different from the start.

When asked internally, "Isn't that the same as SES?", what matters is not to argue based on intuition, but to be able to explain concretely what is the same and what is different along the three axes of contract structure, direction and command, and evaluation criteria. I hope the content organized in this article serves as material for that explanation.

To reorganize: SES and FDE-style support stand on the same foundation of quasi-mandate contracts, clearly distinct from worker dispatch, but their substance diverges greatly depending on what kind of operation is built on that foundation. Providing working hours or providing the rhythm of deliverables; with whom prioritization is jointly held; whether evaluation is based on working hours or on weekly demos. The answers to these three questions speak more eloquently than the wording of the contract about the difference between the two. In internal approvals and explanations, articulating the form of support your company seeks along these three questions may seem like a detour, but it is the most reliable shortcut.
