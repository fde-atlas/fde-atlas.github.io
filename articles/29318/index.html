Book Review of Software Engineering at Google: A Must-Read for FDEs

Original title: Software Engineering at Google 书评：FDE 必读

FDE Times · 2026-10-08 · 3 min read

Summary

This review of Software Engineering at Google, by Titus Winters, Tom Manshreck and Hyrum Wright (O'Reilly, 2020, free HTML version on abseil.io), argues the book is essential reading for forward deployed engineers. It is not about coding or software design but about Google's engineering practices, organized into culture, process, and tools. The reviewer highlights three lessons relevant to FDEs whose code outlives their engagement: sustainable code means retaining the ability to change it; Hyrum's Law says every observable behavior will eventually be depended on; and upgrading is a trade-off based on cost, value, and project lifespan rather than a principle.

Key points

  • The book covers Google's engineering practices, not programming or design, in three parts: culture, process, tools.
  • Software engineering is programming integrated over time; code must remain modifiable after you leave.
  • Hyrum's Law: with enough users, every observable behavior gets depended on.
  • Whether to upgrade depends on cost, value, and expected project lifespan.
  • Free HTML edition available at abseil.io/resources/swe-book.

Key Takeaways

  • Written by Winters, Manshreck, and Wright, published by O'Reilly in 2020, with a free HTML version available on abseil.io.
  • The focus is Google's engineering practices, not programming or software design, and the content is divided into three parts: culture, process, and tools.
  • Three points worth remembering for FDEs: code must stand the test of time; every observable behavior will be depended on by someone; upgrades are a trade-off.

For a book with Google in its title, the first chapter is not about algorithms or architecture, but a short definition: software engineering is programming integrated over time.

This sentence is the key to understanding *Software Engineering at Google*. The book was co-authored by Titus Winters, Tom Manshreck, and Hyrum Wright. According to the Google Research page, the book was published by O'Reilly in 2020; the authors' page on Abseil gives a release date of March 2020.

For anyone who wants to become a Forward Deployed Engineer (FDE), this book is more worth reading than most "clean code" books. Think about the job: if the code you write at a customer site is still running after you move on to other projects, then the book's questions about time are your questions.

Not a Book About Coding, Nor About Design

The Abseil page states it plainly: this book is not really about programming, but about Google's engineering practices. The preface goes further: this book does not cover software design. The content is organized into three groups: culture, process, and tools.

The authors also make a point of saying that the experiences described in the book are not meant to dictate how your organization should operate. This matters for readers outside Google. You won't have a Google-scale monorepo or dedicated tooling teams, so what you should learn from this book is the way it asks questions, not a checklist of its processes to copy.

There's also a practical benefit: the authors offer a free HTML version at abseil.io/resources/swe-book, while also encouraging readers who want to support the work to buy the print edition from O'Reilly.

Point One: Code Should Outlive the Project

The book identifies three core differences between programming and software engineering: time, scale, and trade-offs. A script that runs once is programming; a data pipeline that runs at a customer site for three years and passes through several generations of engineers is software engineering.

Building on this, the book defines a sustainable codebase as one where: anything that should be modifiable, you have the ability to modify. This definition has nothing to do with whether the code is elegant; it's about the ability to change: swapping out a library, patching a security vulnerability, migrating to a new runtime version—all without the system breaking.

Suppose you deployed an agent for a bank. Six months later, the foundation model releases a new version, and the SDK changes its interface. If the customer can't upgrade on their own after you leave, then no matter how impressive the demo was on delivery day, the deployment is not sustainable.

Point Two: Every Behavior Will Be Depended On by Someone

Chapter 1 introduces Hyrum's Law, named after co-author Hyrum Wright. Its essence: when a system has enough users, every observable behavior will be depended on by someone.

For FDEs, this law explains almost every "but I didn't change anything" incident. Imagine you build an API for a customer that returns results in an order the documentation never promised. The customer's reporting team quietly comes to depend on that order. You optimize the query, the order changes, and the numbers on the executive dashboard are suddenly wrong.

The instinct to develop here is to ask, before changing anything: who is reading this output? How do they read it? What might they be depending on that you never promised?

Point Three: Upgrades Are Trade-offs, Not Principles

This book does not advocate upgrading forever. Whether to upgrade depends on the cost of the upgrade, the value it brings, and the expected lifespan of the project. A prototype that lives for three weeks doesn't need to follow the same discipline as a core system that has run for ten years.

Interview tip: if asked about a difficult technical decision, frame your answer around these three variables. An answer that clearly articulates cost, value, and project lifespan is more persuasive than "I chose the newest technology."

Who Should Read It, and Where to Start?

If you have two to four years of experience and have only done feature development within a single product, start with the preface and Chapter 1. These two sections alone are enough to change how you view your work. Then continue through the three groups—culture, process, and tools—starting with the group most relevant to your current problems.

If you have five to eight years of experience and have kept systems alive through multiple upgrades, this book will give you a vocabulary to articulate what you've been through. That vocabulary is valuable in interviews, and useful when persuading customers not to upgrade just yet.

If you're looking for a book on system design, this isn't it—and the authors say so themselves.

The measure of a good FDE comes down to a question rarely asked during demos: a year after you leave, can the customer's system still be modified?
