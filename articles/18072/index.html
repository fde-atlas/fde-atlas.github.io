How to Fill Out Security Questionnaires as an FDE: SOC 2 and ISO 27001

Original title: FDE 安全问卷怎么填：SOC 2 与 ISO 27001

FDE Times · 2026-10-09 · 6 min read

Summary

This guide teaches forward deployed engineers how to handle customer security questionnaires, which often arrive before any code is written and can take longer than hiring itself. It distinguishes SOC 2 (a CPA-issued report on whether an organization does what it claims) from ISO 27001 (a certificate defining ISMS scope). Citing compliance consultancy Agency, it notes a valid SOC 2 Type II report can answer roughly 60-70% of the CAIQ's ~260 questions, leaving only 78-104 to write. The article provides an answer template covering evidence, scope, and owners, advises organizing answer libraries by topic rather than framework, and warns outdated libraries are riskier than none. Common mistakes include sending Type 1 reports and ignoring certificate scope.

Key points

  • SOC 2 is a CPA report; ISO 27001 is a certificate stating ISMS scope — they prove different things.
  • A valid SOC 2 Type II report answers about 60-70% of the CAIQ's 260 questions.
  • Each answer should cite evidence, deployment scope, and an owner; a scoped "partial" beats a vague "yes".
  • Organize answer libraries by topic (access management, encryption), not by framework, with owners and review dates.
  • Common errors: sending Type 1 when Type 2 is needed, ignoring certificate scope, answering "yes" without evidence.

In your first week on-site, before a single line of code has run, an email arrives: a spreadsheet listing hundreds of security questions, plus one sentence—fill it out before we grant you access. If the client is a financial institution, that spreadsheet may be SIG Core, a questionnaire with more than 800 questions.

Appsierra, a company that provides Forward Deployed Engineer (FDE) staffing for enterprises, has observed that getting access provisioned, passing security reviews, and waiting for client approval often takes longer than finding the engineers themselves. That said, this is the claim of a company selling that service.

The good news is that this is a learnable skill. The FDE's job is not to rewrite the company's security policies, but to put correctly scoped evidence in front of the client at the right time, giving their security team enough reason to say yes.

SOC 2 and ISO 27001 prove different things

To use evidence well, you first need to know what each kind of evidence proves. Audit firm Schellman says the output of SOC 2 is a report in which a Certified Public Accountant (CPA) gives an opinion on whether the organization actually does what it claims to do.

The American Institute of Certified Public Accountants (AICPA) says SOC reports provide users with information to help them assess and respond to the risks of outsourced services. Its SOC 2 materials are organized around five categories: security, availability, processing integrity, confidentiality, and privacy.

SOC 2 reports come in two types. According to Schellman, Type 1 assesses a single point in time, while Type 2 covers a period, usually 12 months. That makes Type 2 more persuasive: controls are tested over the entire period, not just on one day.

ISO 27001 is different. Schellman says its output is a certificate stating the scope of the information security management system (ISMS), including the departments and locations covered, with a three-year certification cycle and annual reviews.

Every client's requirements differ, so ask early which kind of evidence their security team needs.

Send the report first, then fill out the form

Don't open the file and start filling from line 1. Security compliance consulting firm Agency recommends sending the SOC 2 report first, because buyers sometimes accept it in place of the full questionnaire. This company has a commercial interest, so treat this as a possibility, not a guarantee.

The same source says the Cloud Security Alliance's CAIQ has about 260 questions across 17 domains. It also estimates that a valid SOC 2 Type II report can directly answer about 60-70% of CAIQ questions.

Do the math on that estimate: 60% of 260 is 156 questions, 70% is 182. That leaves roughly 78 to 104 questions to write yourself, not 260.

Your actual numbers may differ, but the logic holds: a long questionnaire is really "the part already covered by evidence" plus "the part still blank." The first step is separating the two.

A complete example

Suppose you are an FDE deploying an agent that reads credit files for a bank. The client sends over the CAIQ.

Before answering any questions, ask your own security team three things: Is the latest SOC 2 report Type 1 or Type 2? How is the ISMS scope written on the ISO 27001 certificate? Does the company already have a completed CAIQ?

The second question is the one an FDE should watch most closely. If your agent runs in the bank's cloud environment, or depends on departments or locations not listed on the certificate, then the certificate's scope does not cover the work you are about to do. Sending the certificate without an explanation could later be treated as a misrepresentation.

Next, tag each row in the spreadsheet with one of three labels: "covered by the report" (noting which section to cite), "already in the answer library," or "needs to be written new." Every answer in the last group follows a fixed template:

问题：[客户的原题，一字不改]
回答：是 / 否 / 部分
说明：[1-2 句，不写营销话术]
证据：[SOC 2 报告章节、策略名称、配置截图]
范围：[适用于哪个系统/环境]
负责人：[由谁确认这条答案]
最后更新：[日期]

The "scope" and "owner" columns are where the FDE adds value. You know the actual deployment architecture; the security team knows the policies. A "partial" answer with a clearly written scope is far more credible than a vague "yes."

Taking the bank project above as an example, suppose the credit files are stored on the bank's cloud. A completed answer might look like this:

问题：客户数据是否进行静态加密？
回答：部分
说明：公司环境中的数据已做静态加密。信贷档案存放在银行的云上，遵循银行的加密配置。
证据：最新 SOC 2 Type 2 报告的加密章节；项目存储配置截图
范围：公司环境；不含银行的云
负责人：安全负责人确认，项目 FDE 起草
最后更新：[最近一次复核日期]

The client's security team can see at a glance: which part is your company's responsibility, which part is theirs, and which page of the report to check for verification. They don't need to send another email to ask.

Building an answer library by topic, and the cost of an outdated one

After a few questionnaires, you'll notice the same point gets asked in different ways: one way in the CAIQ, another in SIG, and a third in the bank's own template.

So organize the answer library by topic—access management, encryption, logging, incident response—rather than by framework. That way, one answer on access management can be reused across all three questionnaires.

But answer libraries have pitfalls too. The same source warns that an outdated answer library is riskier than having none at all.

Imagine you paste in an answer written before the company changed infrastructure vendors, and the client signs a contract based on it. From that moment, that wrong answer becomes a commitment sitting in the client's files; when the truth comes out, your company may be seen as having broken a promise to the client.

So every entry in the answer library needs an owner and a review date.

Get ahead of the client before they ask

Drata notes that questionnaires are a burden for already understaffed security teams. So some companies proactively send completed standard questionnaires to key prospects, answering the common questions up front.

According to Drata, proactively sharing these documents saves time and builds trust by demonstrating transparency.

If the company has nothing yet, CSA STAR Level 1 is a low-cost starting point: it is a self-assessment based on CAIQ v4, free of charge, and published in the Cloud Security Alliance's registry.

Package the report, the certificate, the completed CAIQ, and the project's data flow diagram into one document, and send it out at the project kickoff meeting.

Mistakes that get sent back

First mistake: the client needs to see controls operating effectively over time, but you sent a Type 1. Second mistake: sending the ISO 27001 certificate without checking its scope against the actual systems. Third mistake: answering "yes" without attaching evidence, forcing the client to follow up and adding another round of email back-and-forth.

One more mistake to avoid: the FDE answering everything alone to save time. You know the system, but security commitments are commitments for the whole company. When you're unsure, write "partial" and note the scope, then have someone with the proper authority confirm it.

If you want to move into an FDE role, this skill is worth putting on your resume, because a security review can take longer than finding the right person. When reading job descriptions, watch for terms like "security review," "vendor assessment," or "SOC 2."

Your resume should show concrete results, such as how you triaged a questionnaire or how much you shortened the review cycle, rather than just saying "familiar with security."

Next time a long spreadsheet lands in your inbox, don't start from the first row. First figure out what evidence already exists and what the scope covers, then work through the remaining blanks.
