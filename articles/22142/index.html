What is an FDE? | Job Description of a Forward Deployed Engineer and Openings at 3 Companies in Japan

A Forward Deployed Engineer (FDE) is an engineer who embeds themselves in a customer's operations to solve problems and feeds that knowledge back into their own company's product. Drawing on job postings from LayerX, Loglass, and SoftBank, this article breaks down how FDEs differ from contracted development and how to design your hiring around the role.

In engineering recruitment, the job title "Forward Deployed Engineer," or FDE for short, is appearing more and more often.

FDEs are often introduced as "engineers who go out to customer sites," a role that combines consultant and engineer in a single person. They go into the customer's workplace, hear about management and operational challenges, and use their own company's product to solve them on the spot. The person who shapes the requirements and the person who writes the code are one and the same here.

What sets FDEs apart from other roles is what comes after solving the problem: "what value to give back, and to what." The solutions discovered in the field are built into the company's product itself and left there. In consulting or contracted development, the knowledge accumulates in the individual project or the individual person, but not with FDEs. Instead, each problem solved is stacked up as features of the product.

By accumulating in this way, the product becomes, for the customer in front of you, something like a co-developed product tailored to their own challenges, and for the next customer, functionality that is there from the start. This creates major business impact.

What exactly is a Forward Deployed Engineer?

An FDE is a software engineer who dives deep into a customer's operations to solve problems, then brings the knowledge gained there back into the development of their own company's product.

The company that first created this role was Palantir. According to the software industry newsletter The Pragmatic Engineer, Palantir created the FDE role in the early 2010s as an internal role called "Delta."

In its early days, Palantir focused on deployments for government agencies, and customers often found it difficult to articulate in words "what they wanted." So the company began sending engineers directly to customer sites to build while observing. That was the origin.

According to Palantir's own description, the FDE is a role based on the idea of "one customer, many capabilities." According to the newsletter, FDEs work embedded in customer teams while simultaneously being members of the core product development team. Until around 2016, Palantir had more FDEs than regular software engineers on staff.

In LayerX's official YouTube video "What Is the Essence of an FDE?", the FDE is described as "an engineer who connects the business side that faces the customer with the company's product side and delivers results." When asked "Do FDEs work on-site at the customer full-time?", the answer was that they rarely do 100% on-site work. They visit customers to test prototype usability and interview people in the field, but how they spend their time is judged by what it takes to deliver results.

The term has existed since the 2010s, but for a long time it remained confined to Palantir's orbit. It was after 2025 that FDE began spreading as a job title in AI-first companies' hiring.

Why are FDEs increasing now?

Because it has become clear that the reason AI fails to produce results inside companies lies not in model performance, but in implementation into actual operations.

In Deloitte's 2026 survey "State of AI in the Enterprise," only 25% of companies had moved more than 40% of their AI pilots (trial deployments) into production. The survey covered 3,235 business and IT leaders across 24 countries. Only 30% of companies had redesigned their core business processes around AI. At many companies, AI was tried, but the way work gets done hasn't changed.

Models keep getting smarter. What's missing are people who can apply them to a company's own operations and turn them into results. Companies that realized this began increasing the number of people who go into the field and handle implementation. That is the FDE.

Both model developers and the consulting industry are moving in the same direction. In May 2026, Anthropic and OpenAI each launched joint ventures for AI deployment that send engineers into customer companies, partnering with major financial institutions. Both have adopted a Palantir-style FDE model of sending people in, rather than selling licenses. Deloitte Tohmatsu also announced in April 2026 that it would fully deploy FDE talent, and in June announced the establishment of an FDE Management Office. The judgment is that selling finished software or handing over documents is no longer enough to keep up with AI adoption.

In Japan, this trend is especially impactful. Overseas, many companies have standardized their operations around standard tools. Japanese companies, on the other hand, have operations and data that vary from company to company. Simply installing standard AI tools doesn't work; you have to go into the field and observe the operations to determine how to apply them.

Which companies in Japan are recruiting under the exact job title "FDE"?

From among Japanese companies whose public job postings could be verified from primary sources as of May 2026, we take up three: LayerX, Loglass, and SoftBank. Each of them lists "Forward Deployed Engineer" or "FDE" as the job title itself.

When we re-checked the three companies' job postings on October 22, 2026, all three were still recruiting. LayerX's posting has moved to a new job page. Loglass's job title has changed to "【New Business】Forward Deployed Engineer (FDE)," but the assigned division remains the AI Solutions Division, and the job content is as shown in the table below.

Forward Deployed Engineer for LayerX Ai Workforce

LayerX is hiring FDEs for its Ai Workforce division, which provides the generative AI platform "Ai Workforce." In its engineering blog, LayerX describes the FDE role as one that "stands on the front line with customers, deeply understands their business, and delivers AI onboarding."

The work is divided into three phases: pre-deployment, implementation, and operation. In pre-deployment, the FDE understands the customer's business domain and identifies the problems that truly need to be solved. In implementation, the FDE translates customer materials into workflows on Ai Workforce. In operation, with the mindset that "the real work begins after deployment," the FDE commits to continuous improvement.

What stands out about this FDE role is that frontline work is directly connected to the development of the company's own product. The engineering blog highlights achievements such as developing in-house tools that streamline document comprehension, R&D toward automatic generation of business workflows, and automatic generation of high-quality prompts.

The challenges FDEs face in the field become development themes for Ai Workforce as they are.

Loglass's Forward Deployed Engineer

Loglass is the company behind the management control cloud "Loglass." It is hiring FDEs for its AI Solutions Division, which reports directly to the CEO.

FDEs work in mixed teams of four to five members consisting of consultants, engineers, PdMs (product managers), and designers. Interacting directly with customers' corporate planning and administrative departments, they handle everything from implementing Loglass's data platform and LLM platform, through PoCs (proofs of concept that test whether an idea can be realized), to operation.

What stands out in this job posting is that it opens by stating that FDEs are "not merely AI implementation supporters." The posting says that use cases from each customer will be standardized and fed back into the evolution of the Loglass LLM platform. Preserving what is learned at customer sites in the product is written into the very definition of the role.

SoftBank's (SB OAI Japan) Forward Deployed Engineer

SoftBank is hiring FDEs in its SB OAI Division, which oversees its corporate business. After being hired, employees are seconded to SB OAI Japan G.K., a joint venture established by SoftBank and OpenAI.

The work is serving as the technical lead for LLM and generative AI adoption for strategic customers. The FDE implements end to end, from identifying customer issues and designing the technical scope and architecture, to building prototypes and deploying to production. This includes RAG (a method that improves LLM answer accuracy by referencing internal data), agent design, and workflow automation.

How far do domestic FDE job postings design "feedback into the product"?

Comparing the three companies' postings, even though they all use the same label "FDE," each has a different design for where and how frontline learnings are fed back.

LayerX's FDE connects frontline challenges directly to Ai Workforce development themes. FDEs themselves produce results from in-house tools and R&D and build them into the product. LayerX's design keeps the distance between the person facing the customer and the person building the product the shortest.

Loglass's FDE also places returning frontline learnings to the product at the center of the role. The posting explicitly states that use cases from each customer will be standardized and fed back into the evolution of the LLM platform. With FDEs leading mixed teams reporting directly to the CEO, customer engagement and product improvement are made contiguous.

SoftBank's FDE focuses on end-to-end implementation for strategic customers. The role applies a finished product—OpenAI's models—to customers' business operations. As far as the posting indicates, the quality of implementation for each customer, rather than feedback into the company's own product, appears to be at the center of the role.

The term "FDE" itself has no fixed definition. Even among postings with the same title, who feeds frontline learnings back, and where, differs by company. If you hire FDEs without deciding this, you are left with only a vague image of "an engineer who can go to customer sites," and your hiring requirements become blurry.

How is an FDE different from contract development or SES?

FDEs go into customers' sites and develop there. Looking only at that aspect, they resemble contract development or SES (System Engineering Service, a staffing service that places engineers on-site at clients). What differs is what counts as the deliverable and where the learnings remain.

What is provided to the customerSolving customer problems and continuous improvementDeliverables defined by contractEngineers' working hours
How they engage with customersCo-developing on-site based on requestsThe vendor develops from requirements and deliversStationed at the client, working under instructions

Contract development means building and handing over the deliverables defined in the contract. SES provides the engineer's working time itself. In both cases, ultimately, compensation is received for hours worked or for deliverables. When a project ends, the expertise gained there either closes out with the project or remains with the individual who handled it, making it hard to carry over to the next one.

What makes FDEs different is that, while developing based on customer requests, they build the expertise gained in the process into their own product. In the same video, Mr. Ohji says that things built for customers are sometimes incorporated into the product in a generalized form, and that FDEs not only voice opinions but also write code themselves.

From the customer's perspective, working with an FDE is different from "buying a finished product." The FDE becomes a partner who builds things together on-site based on the customer's own requests. Because someone who understands the business develops on the spot, requests are reflected directly into the product. It does not end with delivery; improvement continues during operation. It is an engagement closer to co-development than to contract development.

From the vendor's perspective, building solutions created for each customer into the product means that the next customer starts from a state where it already works. Mr. Oharu reflects that during his roughly three years at Palantir, the product's maturity clearly improved: things that were built case by case in the early days could increasingly be handled with existing features later on. The more field solutions accumulate in the product, the faster and lighter the FDE's work becomes.

This approach works for AI implementation support because the "way of applying LLMs to business operations" has not yet been sufficiently productized. So first, people go into the field. Then, the insights those people gain are left behind in the product and internal systems. Whether this can be done determines whether AI implementation support ends as a one-off or accumulates as a company asset.

We do work that involves going deep into the field of engineer recruitment support, and we feel the same way. We build the insights gained in the field—such as scout design and candidate pool formation—into forms that AI can reproduce. When the people who go into the field and the people who build the systems are separate, insights stay trapped in individuals' heads and don't benefit the next project.

Among roles that stand between customers and technology, there is also the Solution Architect. This role presents implementation approaches in sales meetings and supports the technical side through the initial ramp-up after a contract is won. The FDE, by contrast, continues implementing at the customer's site even after deployment, and is responsible all the way through to leaving the results in the product. This is the difference from a Solution Architect.

Key points when designing an FDE-like position in your own company

Whether or not to use the title "FDE" as-is is not the essential issue. When hiring people who will go into customers' field operations, have you designed how their learning will be put to use? Here are four points to use as reference for making that judgment.

The destination for insights

Does your company have a product or team that can absorb the learning gained in the field?

If you create an FDE-like position without this, even if the person does good work, their working style will end up resembling being stationed at the customer. The learning stays confined to the individual. Before placing an FDE, we recommend first confirming that you have a destination for their insights.

The evaluation criteria

Are you evaluating the person based solely on customer satisfaction?

Customer satisfaction is of course important. However, the FDE's value also lies in how much of the knowledge gained there was left in the product or internal systems. If you include "what was contributed back to the product" in the evaluation criteria, not just "how many projects were completed," the meaning of the role becomes less likely to drift.

The organizational structure

Is the distance between the people who go to customers and the people who build the product close?

LayerX's FDEs can turn field problems directly into development themes because the distance between the two is close. When many layers are interposed, the texture of the field is diluted by the time it reaches development.

How to pair the people who draw up strategy with the people who implement is also an issue. At Palantir, there is a DS (Deployment Strategist) who oversees the direction of projects, positioned ahead of the FDE. Deloitte Tohmatsu has also publicly described a structure that pairs strategy-focused DSs with implementation-focused FDEs. Should one person be expected to do everything, or should roles be divided and paired? We recommend choosing a realistic form based on your company's size.

The hiring requirements

Are you measuring people based solely on the ability to write code?

What is required of an FDE is threefold: the ability to understand and articulate the customer's business operations, the ability to give that form through code, and the ability to think about what to leave in the product. Looking at the job postings of the three companies, alongside engineering experience, experience facing customers directly is also listed as a required skill. If you hire on technical skill alone, there will be situations where communication breaks down in the field and the person struggles.

Conclusion | An FDE is not "an engineer who visits customers"

From the moment Palantir created this role, the core of the FDE has been leaving the learning gained in the field in the company's own product. For the customer, it becomes co-development based on their requests; for the vendor, the product grows. The job postings of Japan's LayerX, Loglass, and SoftBank also differ from one another in where and how they channel field learning.

If you are considering an FDE-like position in your own company, there are things you should sort out before drafting the job posting. What service model will you use to face customers, and how will it connect to your organization, product, and business model? Consider these as one integrated whole, then translate them into the form of a job posting. Proceeding in this order makes it easier for the FDE role to function in your company.
