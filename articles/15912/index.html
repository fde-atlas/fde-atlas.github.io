"We want the office to be more automated" is what brands say most often, yet it's a spec that's almost impossible to start building from. The core value of an FDE (Forward Deployed Engineer) lies in requirement translation: breaking down pain points customers can't articulate clearly into specs that technical teams can take on. 91APP's CDMP and personalized marketing implementation work has long been doing exactly this.

A client says "we want Microsoft Office to be more automated." Is this statement wrong? No. But you can almost never start building from it.

Over the past year, the job title "Forward Deployed Engineer" (FDE) has gone from obscure term to hot commodity in the AI industry. According to job market data from the HR website Indeed provided to the media, activity on FDE-related job postings grew 729% year over year, measured from January 2025, far outpacing most tech roles. A survey by headhunting firm Christian & Timbers also showed that while only 5% to 10% of companies planned to hire FDEs in early 2026, by the end of Q2 that figure had surged to 70%. The survey's sample came from the headhunter's own client base, not a full market census, but the direction is worth noting.

These numbers are eye-catching, but easily misread. Most coverage focuses on "how scarce this role is and how high the pay is," without answering a more critical question: what do these people actually do all day that makes companies fight over them?

The answer isn't just coding ability. What's truly scarce is the ability to translate a customer's vague statements into specs a technical team can execute; the industry calls the first part of this work "Scoping." Full Scoping also includes scope boundaries, exclusions, resource constraints, and acceptance criteria. This article focuses on the most underrated and most error-prone part: translating a vague sentence clearly.

In 91APP's CDMP and personalized marketing implementation work, we've heard similar lines at many new project kickoff meetings: brands want "Office to be more automated," want "AI to help make decisions," want "more precise membership management." Not one of these statements is wrong, but not one can directly become a line of code or a backend configuration screen. We recently discussed this phenomenon with marketing executives at several brands and found they all share an unspoken unease: they know something is wrong with the status quo, but can't articulate which layer the problem is on. This is exactly what requirement translation solves, and it's the core work this article aims to break down.

This is the second article in the "FDE series." The series' theme article already covered why 91APP believes it has always been doing the retail version of FDE work. Later articles will dig deeper into "speed isn't the point," "the reskinned consultant controversy," "the last mile of technical implementation," and "how to tell real FDEs from fake ones." This article focuses on the first hurdle, and the most underrated one: requirement translation.

Breaking one sentence into three layers: what the customer says, what the customer is actually stuck on, and the spec the technical team can take on

The sentence "Office more automated" is hard to handle because it contains three different layers of information compressed into one statement. Let's break it apart:

  • Literal statement: "We want more automation." Everyone understands this layer, but no one can build from it.
  • Implicit pain point: some process requires daily manual labor and is error-prone. This layer only surfaces through follow-up questions.
  • Executable spec: which data to use, what conditions trigger it, which channels it outputs to, how often acceptance checks happen. Only at this layer can the technical team actually start working.

This three-layer breakdown is drawn here to illustrate the translation process; it's not some complex model. The point is simple: the customer is only responsible for stating the first layer, and often isn't even sure what the second layer is. If a consultant or engineer takes the first layer at face value and starts writing specs, the result will most likely be a system that "runs technically but solves no one's pain point."

The Harvard Business Review once traced a saying widely circulated in business circles but with no evidence Ford actually said it: if he asked customers what they wanted, they would have said a faster horse. Whether or not it's true, it precisely captures a real phenomenon: when customers describe needs, they use the most familiar vocabulary from their existing experience, not what the solution should look like. When a brand says "Office more automated," what they may be thinking is "no more people manually reconciling Excel at midnight," but the sentence itself says nothing about "which sheet to reconcile, which system to reconcile against, how often." Treating the first layer directly as the spec is like interpreting "a faster horse" as going to a stud farm to find a faster horse, instead of thinking about what the customer really wants: to arrive at the destination faster.

How Palantir turned "translation" into a business: the origin and working methods of the FDE

The FDE title isn't an invention of the last two years. According to BusinessNext, in 2011 Palantir merged its previously separate Solutions Engineer and Integration Engineer roles into a single function called the Forward Deployed Engineer, who sat directly in the customer's office, closely observing how on-site staff worked and where they got stuck. At the time, many people thought this model was too expensive to scale like pure software products—essentially a costly consulting service.

This "close observation, repeated questioning" approach is actually what mature requirements interviews do too. What truly sets FDEs apart from traditional consultants is that FDEs are also accountable for the subsequent build, integration, and deployment outcomes—they don't exit after handing over a spec document. Citing consultant Piotr Kraus, LeadDev notes that what FDEs really rely on is the courage to question the solutions clients propose and back alternatives with data and evidence; technical depth is secondary. An FDE's starting point is to first ask "what proportion of what the client says holds up under scrutiny," and only then start building. Taking everything at face value just throws the translation responsibility back onto the customer.

The logic of "staying with the customer until success" actually has a precedent in the semiconductor industry. MediaTek targeted the white-label phone market early on with turnkey integrated solutions, relying on a team of Field Application Engineers (FAEs) embedded at customers' production sites, accompanying them from component selection and design-in all the way to mass production ramp-up. The core logic of FAEs and FDEs is the same: it doesn't end with delivering a spec document; you stay on site and work with the customer to truly integrate the technology into daily workflows.

91APP's consultants, CS, and implementation teams have long been doing the same thing, just in a different industry. In membership management health-check meetings with brands, we usually don't start by asking "what features do you want," but rather "what are your current membership tier criteria," "is private-domain communication currently sent manually or with automation tools," "are big-campaign decisions based on experience or data." These questions sound trivial, but each one breaks down abstract statements like "we want precision marketing" into "where exactly are we stuck, how complete is the data, and what's the first concrete scenario to solve." This is the first step of requirement translation: understand the current state before you're qualified to discuss where to go.

Why "doing whatever the customer says" makes most AI projects a waste of money

Requirement translation sounds like a process detail, but what it actually determines is the survival rate of the entire AI implementation project.

The MIT NANDA research project's preliminary report, "The GenAI Divide: State of AI in Business 2025," published in July 2025, was based on 300 publicly disclosed AI adoption cases, structured interviews with 52 companies, and a survey of 153 business executives. It reached a striking conclusion: despite companies investing $30-40 billion in generative AI, 95% of organizations are seeing no tangible returns, and only 5% of integrated pilot programs are truly creating significant business value. The report specifically points out that the root of this gap is neither poor model quality nor regulatory constraints, but rather that most companies, when embedding AI tools into actual workflows, lack sufficient "learning" and translation processes, leaving general-purpose tools at the level of individual use and never truly embedding them into the organization's operational rhythm.

The same report contains another figure that is easy to overlook but highly valuable for brands choosing partners: AI projects procured from specialized vendors and built through partnerships have a deployment success rate of about 67%, while projects built entirely in-house have a success rate of about 33%—the former roughly twice the latter. The report's authors also honestly caution that this is self-reported data from surveyed companies, which does not necessarily prove that the partnership model itself caused the success; it may also reflect differences in companies' original risk appetites and technical capabilities. Even with that caveat, this direction still echoes the argument of this article: taking the time to sit in the customer's business environment and pressing on every vague term until it can be implemented is more likely to succeed than guessing what the customer wants based on one's own imagination.

This also echoes a judgment we have repeatedly made in our CDMP-related articles: when every brand has access to AI tools, the key differentiator ultimately comes down to who can define the problem, who makes the trade-offs, and who makes the judgments—model size or interface aesthetics are secondary. Requirements translation is the concrete working method for "defining the problem." Many brands have bought impressive AI agent demos, but the smooth operation shown in a vendor's demo environment is a completely different matter from the complex realities of fragmented data, outdated systems, and unclear cross-departmental responsibilities in a real business environment. The gap often lies in whether, before implementation, someone is willing to spend time translating phrases like "more automation" into concrete specifications: which process in which department, using which data, and what changes should be seen within what timeframe. It is no surprise that when we analyzed the implementation challenges of generative AI in e-commerce marketing in another article, we likewise observed that most brands get stuck at the step of data quality and process integration, not insufficient model capability.

How 91APP does requirements translation in the field during CDMP implementation

Putting this logic back into 91APP's daily work serving brands, requirements translation is a continuous activity that runs through the entire implementation process, not something that happens only in the kickoff interview of a project.

  • Take stock of the current state, not collect a wish list. We first confirm the brand's current member count, reach rates across channels, whether tiering is in place, and roughly where the first-purchase conversion rate of registered members falls. The purpose of these questions is not to grade, but to identify which segment of the existing workflow the brand's phrase "more automation" actually corresponds to.
  • Find "the person who runs this process every day," not just ask decision-makers. The pain points described by brand executives are often versions filtered through layers of reporting; the details that actually cause blockages are usually hidden in the complaints of frontline operators. This is why 91APP's CS and implementation consultants habitually sit down directly to watch how customers operate the backend and manually compile reports, rather than starting work based solely on the presentation content of a single meeting.
  • Replace every vague term with an acceptance-testable specification. Take a common scenario: a brand says "we want precision marketing," but when laid out, it actually means a frontline marketing specialist manually pulling birthday lists from the backend every month, then manually scheduling LINE messages and SMS discount coupons—time-consuming, with no way to track who actually came back to make a purchase because of the coupon. What requirements translation does is replace that sentence with a specification that can be directly implemented and acceptance-tested, such as "use CDMP to automatically filter members whose birthdays fall in the current month and who have not purchased within 30 days, automatically send a designated discount coupon via LINE, and track the conversion rate within 7 days." The minimal deliverable is usually a short specification clearly stating "current process, data used, trigger conditions, acceptance metrics, and owner," rather than a lengthy vision report.
  • Record the pitfalls discovered during implementation and feed them back. If the pitfalls a brand encountered in data integration or the edge cases discovered in script design remain only within that project without being consolidated, the next brand will step on the same pitfalls again. This is why we believe the role of customer success teams has long gone beyond answering questions—it is about continuously turning what is learned in the field into reusable analytical frameworks and judgment logic.

Requirements translation is not always worth the cost. If the data simply does not exist, the process is still changing frequently, the person who can actually make the final call is not present, or an existing standard configuration can already solve the problem, forcing a full round of translation is a waste of time. Judging whether it is worth doing is itself part of requirements translation.

What brands can prepare on their own before looking for an AI implementation partner

The responsibility for requirements translation does not fall solely on the consultant or the FDE. Before looking for a partner, brands can also do a few things to make the subsequent translation process more efficient:

  • First take stock of "how things are actually done now," and don't rush to describe "what you want": draw a simple process diagram marking which steps require manually downloading Excel files and which systems are currently not integrated—this is more valuable than listing a feature wish list.
  • Identify the person who actually executes this process every day and invite them to join the evaluation meetings: don't rely solely on the marketing manager or a director to relay information; descriptions that have been organized by supervisors often miss the most critical operational details and exception cases.
  • Press on every abstract term yourself first: when saying "more automation" or "precision marketing," try converting it into a concrete scenario, such as "push a specific new product on LINE to members who have not purchased within 30 days"—this can save consultants half the probing time.
  • Clarify whether the partner offers one-time delivery or is willing to accompany long-term iteration: requirements translation is not a one-time action before signing the contract; actual usage after implementation usually overturns some original assumptions, requiring someone willing to keep adjusting.
  • Confirm whether problems discovered during implementation will actually be recorded and fed back: a good partnership should turn every pitfall into a detour avoided later, rather than having every project start from scratch.

The truly underrated skill is not writing code—it is understanding what people mean

FDE job postings growing more than sevenfold in a year sounds like an arms race for technical talent, but coding ability is only the threshold. What determines whether a project can land is the ability to break a vague sentence into specifications that others can understand and execute, and then stay in the field and truly listen to newly discovered problems.

Brands do not need to first learn to articulate perfect requirements before they are qualified to talk about AI transformation. The real question to ask is whether the partner you find is willing to spend time breaking down a sentence like "make the office more automated" layer by layer until it can be implemented, and after finishing, continue to stay in the field and listen to newly discovered problems. 91APP's implementation consultant team has been doing this for many years—it just never had a trendy job title to describe it. Now it has a name, but the working method has not changed.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q1: What is "requirements translation" (Scoping)? How is it different from a typical requirements interview?

A1: Requirements translation breaks down the vague pain points a customer describes in everyday language into concrete specifications that a technical team can directly execute, including which data to use, trigger conditions, and acceptance criteria. The difference from a typical requirements interview is that translation emphasizes probing and challenging the customer's original statements, rather than simply recording everything as-is and calling it done.

Q2: Why are requirements stated by customers themselves often inaccurate?

A2: When customers describe requirements, they use the vocabulary most familiar from their own existing experience, not what the solution itself should look like. Moreover, the operational details that actually cause blockages are often in the hands of frontline executors, not in the words of decision-making supervisors. The gap between the two is exactly what requirements translation needs to bridge.

Q3: How do 91APP's consultants or CS team concretely do requirements translation?

A3: First, take stock of the brand's current data such as existing member counts, channel reach, and tiering criteria; then directly observe how frontline staff handle the processes; next, replace vague wording with concrete, verifiable specifications; finally, document issues discovered during implementation and feed them back as reusable decision logic.

Q4: Do small and medium-sized brands with limited budgets also need this level of detailed requirement translation?

A4: Yes, and they need it even more. Small and medium-sized brands have limited resources to draw on, so once budget is spent on incomplete specifications, the cost of trial and error is relatively higher. Brands can start by taking stock of existing processes and identifying one specific scenario; there is no need to cover all channels at once.

Q5: If requirement translation is done well, how long until results are visible?

A5: An initial version of the specifications can usually be completed within a few weeks of project kickoff, but that is only the starting point of requirement translation, not the end. Actual usage after implementation often overturns some of the original assumptions, and the accuracy of subsequent execution will continue to affect overall results; the actual timeline depends on the complexity of the first scenario the brand chooses.

Q6: How does requirement translation differ from hiring a general consultant for a diagnosis? What are common misconceptions?

A6: The key difference lies in continuous on-site immersion and feedback, rather than ending with the delivery of a diagnostic report. A common misconception is that requirement translation only happens before project kickoff; in fact, actual usage after implementation often overturns some of the original assumptions, so translation needs to run through the entire process.
