Security & Compliance Quick-Ref (FDE)

Vibe Engines · 2026-09-03 · 12 min read

Summary

This FDE-oriented quick reference argues that SOC 2, HIPAA, GDPR and similar frameworks are largely the same core controls in different vocabularies: know your data, encrypt it, restrict and log access, minimize retention, manage vendors, and plan for incidents. It maps which frameworks apply to which customers, covers data handling, secrets, AI/LLM-specific risks (prompt injection, PII redaction, zero data retention), and breach deadlines (GDPR 72 hours, HIPAA 60 days). A worked HIPAA example walks through an LLM pilot on clinic notes, and recipes include a one-page data-flow template, PII redaction code, and least-privilege access requests. The core advice: bring evidence—a data-flow diagram, access model, and pre-filled questionnaires—and involve security teams early.

Key points

  • Compliance frameworks share the same handful of controls; evidence beats argument in security reviews
  • SOC 2 is an attestation report, not a certification; Type II requires a 3–12 month observation period
  • HIPAA requires a BAA before any vendor, including LLM APIs, touches PHI
  • GDPR breach notification is due within 72 hours; erasure requests within one month
  • For LLMs: no-training terms, redact PII, permission-aware RAG, and list providers as subprocessors

Enough to earn a security team’s "yes": what SOC 2 / HIPAA / GDPR actually ask, how to handle data and secrets, and what reviews check.

Compliance frameworks are mostly the same handful of controls in different vocabularies: know what data you hold, encrypt it, restrict and log who can reach it, keep only what you need, manage the vendors who touch it, and have a plan for when something goes wrong. A security team’s job is to verify those controls exist, so the fastest route to their yes is arriving with the evidence: a data-flow diagram, the access model and completed questionnaires.

This is an engineer’s orientation, not legal advice; the customer’s security, privacy and legal teams have the final word.

The frameworks (what they care about)

  • SOC 2
  • Trust controls: access control, encryption, change mgmt, monitoring, vendor risk. Report, not a law.
  • SOC 2 Type I vs II
  • Type I: controls designed well at one point in time · Type II: shown working over 3–12 months
  • ISO 27001
  • certifiable international standard for an information security management system
  • HIPAA
  • US health data (PHI). Needs a BAA, encryption, access logs, minimum-necessary access.
  • BAA
  • Business Associate Agreement — required before any vendor, an LLM API included, touches PHI
  • GDPR
  • EU personal data. Lawful basis, data-subject rights (access/delete), data-residency, DPA.
  • CCPA / CPRA
  • California privacy law: rights to know, delete and opt out of the sale or sharing of data
  • PCI-DSS
  • Card data. Easiest win: never touch it — tokenize / use a processor.
  • FedRAMP
  • US federal authorization needed to sell cloud services to most agencies; slow and costly
  • EU AI Act
  • risk-tiered AI rules: some uses banned, duties for high-risk systems and general-purpose models

Data handling

  • Encrypt everywhere
  • TLS in transit; encryption at rest. Non-negotiable table stakes.
  • Data residency
  • Know which region/network data must stay in. Drives the deployment target.
  • Minimize + retain
  • Collect only what you need; delete on a schedule. Less data = less risk.
  • PII discipline
  • Redact/mask in logs and prompts. Never paste customer PII into a third-party API without approval.
  • Data classification
  • label data public, internal, confidential or regulated — every control keys off the label
  • Pseudonymization
  • swap identifiers for tokens; under GDPR it is still personal data while the mapping exists
  • Deletion that works
  • covers backups, logs, caches, search indexes and vector stores, not just the main table

Secrets & access

  • No secrets in code/repo
  • Use their vault/KMS + env injection. Rotate. Never log a token.
  • Least privilege
  • Scope keys to exactly what’s needed; short-lived over long-lived.
  • Audit trail
  • Log who did what, when. Immutable, exportable — reviewers will ask.
  • MFA + SSO
  • Integrate with their identity provider; don’t create a parallel login.
  • Service accounts
  • one per workload with a narrow role — never a person’s credentials in automation
  • Secret scanning
  • scan repos and CI logs for leaked keys, and rotate at once when one turns up
  • Break-glass access
  • emergency admin access that is logged, time-limited and reviewed afterwards

AI & LLM specifics

  • Provider data terms
  • confirm inputs are not used for training and how long they are kept — in the contract
  • Zero data retention
  • some providers offer ZDR for sensitive workloads; check which features it excludes
  • PII in prompts
  • redact or tokenize before sending; store prompt logs only in an approved, access-controlled place
  • Prompt injection
  • treat retrieved documents as untrusted input and limit which tools a model can call
  • Permission-aware RAG
  • filter retrieval by the user’s access rights at query time, not only at indexing
  • Residency for AI
  • regional endpoints or self-hosted models when data may not leave a jurisdiction

Logging & incidents

  • What to log
  • sign-ins, admin actions, data exports and permission changes: who, what, when
  • Never log
  • passwords, tokens, full card numbers, or prompts and payloads full of personal data
  • Log retention
  • keep audit logs as long as the framework or contract asks, protected from edits
  • Breach deadlines
  • GDPR: the regulator within 72 hours of awareness · HIPAA: individuals within 60 days
  • Incident runbook
  • who to call, how to contain, who decides on notification — written before you need it

Vendors & subprocessors

  • Subprocessor list
  • every third party that touches customer data; customers may object to changes
  • DPA
  • Data Processing Agreement: the contract GDPR requires between controller and processor
  • SCCs
  • Standard Contractual Clauses — the usual legal basis for moving EU data abroad
  • Vendor review
  • a security questionnaire, SOC 2 report and pen-test summary before onboarding

The security review

  • Bring the answers
  • Data flow diagram, where data lives, encryption, access model, subprocessors.
  • Smaller ask = faster yes
  • Request minimum scope + a bounded footprint; huge asks stall.
  • Involve them early
  • A veto at the end kills the deal. Loop security in during discovery.
  • Questionnaires
  • SIG, CAIQ or the customer’s own spreadsheet — keep a library of approved answers
  • Pen-test report
  • a recent third-party test summary answers many questions at once
  • Architecture diagram
  • data flows, trust boundaries and where encryption applies — reviewers start here
  • Exceptions in writing
  • when a control can’t be met, document the compensating control and get sign-off

Worked example: Scoping a HIPAA pilot: AI summaries of clinic visit notes

A clinic, a HIPAA covered entity, wants an LLM to draft summaries of visit notes. Walk the record through the stages before writing any code:

  • Classify. Visit notes tied to a patient are PHI. That makes your company a business associate, and every vendor that touches the notes (cloud host, model provider, error tracker) a subcontractor that needs a BAA. A provider that will not sign one is out, however good its model.
  • Minimize. Send the note, not the whole chart. If the pilot can run on de-identified data, the Safe Harbor method removes 18 listed identifiers, such as names, record numbers and every date element except the year; data de-identified that way is no longer PHI.
  • Store and use. Encrypt at rest, limit access to the pilot team through the clinic’s SSO, and log every view and export of a note. Keep prompts and outputs out of general application logs.
  • Retain and delete. Agree how long drafts live and delete them from backups and any vector index on the same schedule. HIPAA policies, procedures and risk analyses must themselves be kept for 6 years.
  • Plan the bad day. Affected individuals must be told without unreasonable delay and within 60 days of discovering a breach. A breach of 500 or more people also goes to HHS within those 60 days, and one affecting more than 500 residents of a state to prominent media there; smaller breaches are logged and reported to HHS within 60 days of the year’s end.

The deliverable for the clinic’s security review is a one-page data flow carrying each of these answers, the signed BAAs and the access list. That page usually moves a pilot faster than any demo.

Recipes

The one-page data-flow summary reviewers ask for

Data:           support tickets with names and emails (PII); no payment or health data
Flow:           customer helpdesk → our API (TLS 1.2+) → Postgres in eu-west-1 (encrypted at rest)
LLM use:        ticket text sent to the model provider under no-training, limited-retention terms
Access:         SSO + MFA; 4 engineers with production access; every query logged
Retention:      raw tickets 90 days, then deleted from the database, backups and the vector index
Subprocessors:  cloud host, model provider, error tracker (listed in the DPA)

Answering these six questions up front pre-empts most of a security questionnaire and shows the reviewer you already think the way they do.

Redact PII before it reaches a log or a prompt (Python)

import re

EMAIL = re.compile(r"[\w.+-]+@[\w-]+\.[\w.-]+")
CARD = re.compile(r"\b\d(?:[ -]?\d){12,15}\b")

def redact(text: str) -> str:
    text = EMAIL.sub("[email]", text)
    return CARD.sub("[card]", text)

Regexes catch only the obvious formats; for names, addresses and free text use a dedicated PII detector, and redact at the edge so raw data never lands in logs.

A least-privilege access request for an integration

Need:    read-only access to orders from the last 30 days
Grant:   a dedicated service account with role orders.read, scoped to one project
Avoid:   an admin's personal API key, wildcard scopes, keys that never expire
Rotate:  every 90 days, stored in the customer's vault, injected at runtime
Audit:   every call logged with the service account ID and reviewed monthly

A smaller ask gets approved faster and limits the damage if the credential leaks, which is exactly what the reviewer is weighing.

Handling a GDPR deletion request

1. Verify the requester's identity
2. Find every copy: database, replicas, backups, logs, caches, search and vector indexes, analytics
3. Delete or anonymize, and record what was removed and when
4. Tell your processors and subprocessors to do the same
5. Reply within one month (extendable when requests are complex)

The deadline runs from receipt, and the copies people forget, such as backups, exports and embeddings, are where audits find the gaps.

When to use it, and when not

Frameworks attach to the data and the customer, not to your tech stack. Work out which apply from the triggers:

If you… Expect
sell software to US enterprises a request for a SOC 2 Type II report and a security questionnaire
sell to buyers in Europe or global enterprises ISO 27001, often accepted beside or instead of SOC 2
offer services to people in the EU, or process data for EU customers GDPR, even with no EU office: a DPA, a lawful basis, data-subject rights
handle health data for a US covered entity HIPAA as a business associate, starting with a BAA
see card numbers PCI DSS; a hosted payment page or tokenizing processor shrinks your scope to the smallest self-assessment
sell cloud services to US federal agencies FedRAMP

HIPAA is narrower than people assume: a consumer health app not working for a covered entity is usually outside it, though state privacy laws and the FTC’s Health Breach Notification Rule may still apply. When it is unclear, the customer’s counsel decides, not the engineer.

Trade-offs and limits

Option Buys Costs
SOC 2 Type I first a report without waiting out an observation period many buyers still hold out for Type II
Self-hosted model data never leaves your environment; residency solved GPUs and operations, and often a smaller model than the best hosted ones
Zero data retention the provider stores no prompts features that need stored state may be unavailable
Pseudonymization less exposure if a dataset leaks; data stays useful still personal data under GDPR while the key exists
Anonymization outside GDPR entirely hard to do for real; rich records can often be re-identified
Long log retention forensics months later more personal data to protect, and to find on an erasure request

Every control also costs evidence. An auditor wants to see it operating, such as dated access reviews with sign-offs; a control nobody can show is, for the report, a control that does not exist.

Common mistakes

  • Calling SOC 2 a certification. It is an auditor’s attestation report: you receive a report, not a certification. ISO 27001 is the one that certifies, and reviewers notice the difference.
  • Hashing emails and calling them anonymous. A SHA-256 of an email is reversed by hashing a list of known addresses and comparing. It is pseudonymized personal data and keeps every GDPR obligation.
  • Production data in staging. A copy of the customer database for testing carries the same obligations as production, usually with weaker access control and no audit log. Use synthetic or masked data.
  • Adding a vendor without updating the subprocessor list. A new error tracker or LLM API that sees customer data is a contract change; many DPAs require advance notice so customers can object.
  • “Encrypted at rest” as the answer to access questions. Disk encryption stops a stolen drive, not a valid query. Reviewers want to know who can query the data and where that is logged.

Check yourself

Your team becomes aware of a personal-data breach on Monday at 09:00. Under GDPR, when must the supervisory authority hear about it?

Without undue delay and, where feasible, by Thursday 09:00, 72 hours after awareness, unless the breach is unlikely to put people at risk. A later notification must give the reasons for the delay.

A GDPR erasure request arrives on 3 March. By when must you respond?

Within one month, so by 3 April. It can be extended by two further months for complex or numerous requests, but the person must be told of the extension, and why, within the first month.

You use an LLM provider to summarize EU customers’ support tickets. Which documents must exist?

A DPA with the provider; the provider on your subprocessor list, with whatever notice your customer DPAs require; and, if data leaves the EEA, a transfer mechanism such as Standard Contractual Clauses or an adequacy decision like the EU-US Data Privacy Framework for certified US companies.

A customer needs a SOC 2 Type II report; you finished a Type I last month. What is missing?

Time. Type II covers an observation period, commonly 3 to 12 months, in which the auditor samples evidence that the controls actually operated. Type I only shows they were designed properly on one date.

Questions about Security & Compliance Quick-Ref (FDE)

What is the difference between SOC 2 Type I and Type II?

Both are attestation reports from an independent auditor against the AICPA Trust Services Criteria, most often the security criteria. A Type I report says the described controls were suitably designed at a single point in time. A Type II report says the controls also operated effectively over an observation period, usually three to twelve months, based on sampled evidence. Enterprise buyers generally expect Type II, because it shows the controls actually work day to day. Startups often get a Type I first and follow it with a Type II after the first observation window.

Can I send customer data to an LLM API?

Only with the customer’s approval and under the right terms. Check the provider’s data-use policy, which should state that API inputs are not used for training, confirm how long prompts and outputs are retained, and sign a data processing agreement, plus a Business Associate Agreement for health data under HIPAA. Residency requirements may call for a regional endpoint or a self-hosted model. Send the minimum the task needs, redacting personal data where possible, and list the provider as a subprocessor in the customer’s documentation.

What does a security review of a vendor usually check?

Reviewers want to know what data you will touch and how it is protected. Expect questions about the data flow and where data is stored, encryption in transit and at rest, authentication and single sign-on, who in your company can access customer data and how that access is logged, how you manage secrets and vulnerabilities, your incident response process, your subprocessors, and certifications such as SOC 2 or ISO 27001. Arriving with a data-flow diagram, a pre-filled questionnaire and a narrow access request is the fastest way through.

How quickly must a data breach be reported under GDPR?

A controller must notify the relevant supervisory authority without undue delay and, where feasible, within 72 hours of becoming aware of a personal data breach, unless the breach is unlikely to result in a risk to people’s rights and freedoms. When the risk to individuals is high, they must also be told without undue delay, and processors must inform the controller without undue delay. Contracts often impose shorter deadlines, so the incident runbook should name who decides, who notifies and which customers must hear first.
