FDE Needs Discovery: Design Thinking for Innovation Course

Original title: FDE 需求发现：Design Thinking for Innovation 课程

FDE Times · 2026-11-17 · 4 min read

Summary

This article reviews Design Thinking for Innovation, a roughly six-hour beginner Coursera course taught by Darden professor Jeanne Liedtka, and maps its four-question model (what is?, what if?, what wows?, what works?) to FDE discovery work. Using examples like an invoice-reading agent, it shows how to uncover the customer's real problem before coding, highlights four tools (visualization, storytelling, mind mapping, learning launch) usable in client meetings, and suggests turning a real discovery case into a resume line ahead of certificates.

Key points

  • Six-hour beginner course by Jeanne Liedtka, part of the Leading the Modern Day Business specialization.
  • The what is? question closely mirrors FDE needs discovery discipline.
  • Four tools taught: visualization, storytelling, mind mapping, and learning launch.
  • Before a pilot, write down which results would change your direction.
  • Best for engineers with two to four years' experience facing clients; veterans can skim.

Key Takeaways

  • A Beginner-level course, about six hours long, taught by Jeanne Liedtka of the Darden School of Business on Coursera
  • The four-question model, especially what is?, maps almost one-to-one onto an FDE's discovery work
  • Four tools (Visualization, Storytelling, Mind Mapping, and Learning Launch) can be used directly in customer meetings

The code can be perfectly fine and the project can still fail. All it takes is a team building what customers say they want instead of what they actually need. A roughly six-hour course on Coursera offers a set of questions to help you avoid this mistake from the very first meeting.

The course is called Design Thinking for Innovation, offered by the University of Virginia's Darden School of Business as part of the Leading the Modern Day Business specialization.

Judging by the specialization's name, it targets business professionals. But the course page states clearly: anyone, regardless of level or organization, can use design thinking to find new ways of working.

That's good news for Forward Deployed Engineers (FDEs). You already know how to build systems; what's often missing is the habit of first asking what should be built.

Who Teaches It, and Why Trust Them?

Jeanne M. Liedtka is a professor emerita at the Darden School of Business and former Chief Learning Officer at United Technologies Corporation. At Darden, she has worked with both MBA students and executives on topics including design thinking, innovation, and growth leadership.

She has also written books on the subject. The classic is Designing for Growth: A Design Thinking Tool Kit for Managers, co-authored with Tim Ogilvie and published by Columbia University Press in June 2011. Her most recent book is Experiencing Design: The Innovator's Journey, co-authored with Karen Hold and Jessica Eldridge.

The Four Questions Are the Skeleton

The entire course revolves around a four-question model: what is? (what is the current situation?), what if? (what would happen if...?), what wows? (what would delight people?), and what works? (what actually works?). Each module teaches one tool: Visualization, Storytelling, Mind Mapping, and Learning Launch.

Apply the model to a familiar scenario. Suppose a logistics company asks you to build an agent that reads supplier invoices. An engineer's first instinct is usually to immediately ask about PDF formats and which model to use.

Liedtka's model requires you to first stop at what is?: who currently reviews the invoices, how many hours per week it takes, where the errors occur, and who is responsible for rework.

You might discover that the bottleneck isn't reading invoices at all, but matching invoices to purchase orders. At that point, what if? opens up solutions quite different from the original request.

From an FDE's perspective, the other two questions act as filters. what wows? asks: which solution will end users actually want to use? what works? pulls the team back to a measurable small-scale pilot rather than a big project.

Three Things Worth Keeping After Six Hours

The first is the discipline of what is?. This question is nearly identical to customer discovery. If you take away only one thing, let it be this: at the start of every meeting, describe only the current state and don't mention solutions at all. Customers often feel genuinely heard, and you walk away with usable, real data.

The second is treating visualization and storytelling as engineering tools. FDEs are used to persuading with system architecture, but what moves customers is the story of a specific user on a specific day.

Sketching the current process on a whiteboard, paired with a story like "the accountant opens the 40th invoice of the day," usually clarifies scope faster than a lengthy requirements document.

The third is how an FDE should understand Learning Launch and what works?: the first experiment is for learning, not for proving yourself right. Practical advice: before the pilot starts, write down which results would make you change direction. When the data comes in, the conversation with the customer is about learnings, not failure.

Who Should Take It, and in What Order?

The course is rated Beginner, suited to engineers with two to four years of experience who are about to face customers directly for the first time, or software engineers without customer experience who are transitioning to FDE roles. If you've spent years in solutions engineering or consulting, most of the content will feel familiar; a quick skim to pick up the shared vocabulary is enough.

A sensible order is to take the course first, then read Designing for Growth if you want to go deeper. According to the publisher, the book teaches design thinking as a capability: turning abstract ideas into practical applications that drive business growth. Having the four-question framework in mind first makes it easier to absorb.

The final assignment asks you to pick one tool, write a reflection, and peer-review the work of three other learners; you must pass to complete the course. Don't treat it as a formality.

Suppose you're building an internal document retrieval assistant for a customer support team. After five interviews, instead of leaving with a page of messy notes, you use a mind map to group the complaints into categories: finding the wrong documents, outdated documents, and not knowing whom to ask.

Next, a short story, like "the night-shift support agent spends 15 minutes looking up the return policy," is enough for both sides to agree on which category to tackle first. That's exactly the kind of real experience to write in your reflection.

Apply the tools to a real customer or internal team, then distill the reflection into one line on your resume: which discovery meeting, which tool, and how the project scope changed as a result.

When applying for FDE roles, put this concrete example ahead of the certificate, because it shows the reader that you know to ask before you build.

Six hours won't turn you into a designer. But it's enough that the next time a customer says "we need an agent," your first question back is "who is doing this work today?"
