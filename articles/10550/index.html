A Panorama of FDE Teams Across Five Types of Chinese Companies: One Role, Five Businesses, Five Career Paths

Original title: 中国五类企业的 FDE 团队全景：同一个岗位，五种生意，五条职业路线

53AI · 2026-07-26 · 27 min read

Summary

This article argues that Chinese companies hiring "FDEs" are actually building five distinct roles. Using a four-part screening test and a six-dimension framework (business bottleneck, delivery target, team structure, production permissions, acceptance metrics, reusable assets), the author analyzes platform companies (01.AI, Alibaba Cloud), vertical SaaS firms (Beisen with 300+ FDE staff, Zhihuiya), IT services (iSoftStone), internal teams (COSCO Shipping), and physical AI (Dazhi Tech). The core claim: judge an FDE team by what business state it changes, who bears failure costs, and whether field experience becomes reusable assets—not by demo counts. The author predicts China will not converge on one standard FDE within three years, but grow five mature deployment models.

Key points

  • Chinese FDEs split into five types: platform, vertical SaaS, services, internal, physical AI.
  • Beisen reports 300+ FDE staff; Zhihuiya has also formed an FDE team.
  • Evaluate FDE teams with a six-dimension ledger, not demo counts.
  • Five types carry different failure costs, so they need different metrics.
  • Author predicts five mature deployment models, not one standard FDE, by 2028.

Full text

QUOTE

If you're doing FDE work, the real question to ask isn't "is this role hot," but: with the same acronym, what exactly does this company expect you to deliver.

Over the past few months, FDE signals from Chinese companies have clearly intensified.

01.AI has written "strategic consulting + FDE co-creation" into its enterprise AI deployment model; Beisen's annual report explicitly adopts FDE delivery, and the company's official website says it has built a team of over 300 people; iSoftStone put "AI Factory + FDE" into its 2025 annual report; COSCO Shipping Holdings proposed using FDE talent development to build an AI R&D team that integrates business and technology; Alibaba Cloud went as far as publishing FDE projects across solid waste, petrochemical, coal chemical, steel, and building materials production chains.

PatSnap has also established an FDE team. On the public side, you can see that official company news explicitly states it is using FDEs to push AI into organizational innovation workflows; public job postings describe the work very concretely: understanding customers' processes in intellectual property, R&D innovation, and strategic intelligence, configuring and deploying agents, Skills, reports, and automations, then translating customer-specific needs into standard product capabilities.

These companies all appear to be hiring FDEs, but what they actually need are five different kinds of people.

Some need to push general-purpose models into production, some need to teach AI the judgment in experts' heads, some need to turn consulting plans into working systems, some need to transform their own company's processes, and some need to keep robots performing professionally in the face of dust, lighting, and equipment tolerances.

The job title is only three letters, but the work content could fill an entire department store.

After more than a year as an FDE, I've become increasingly reluctant to judge a team by its job title alone. I usually ask four things first: whose business state does it serve, which production permissions can it touch, how does it prove results, and what does it leave behind after departure.

If these four questions have no answers, the FDE role often just concentrates work that no one in the organization fully owns and hands it to a few people who can shoulder the load.

This article is not a "comprehensive list of domestic FDE companies." Public job postings disappear, organization names change, and private teams are impossible to enumerate. What this article aims to answer is a more useful question for practitioners:

Why do five types of Chinese companies need FDEs, what services are they already delivering, and where will they take this role next?

DEFINITION

FDE is easily ruined by two ways of counting.

The first is to lump in solutions, pre-sales, implementation, on-site development, customer success, and AI training all together. Overnight, FDEs are everywhere—as if renaming yu-xiang shredded pork "Sichuan-style protein solution" would suddenly push it into the high-tech industry.

The second is to only recognize people whose job titles literally say Forward Deployed Engineer. This misses a large number of teams already doing isomorphic work.

I use four tests. A team should cover at least three of them to enter this article's core sample:

Going deep into external customer or internal business sites to understand real workflows;

Being able to directly build agents, write production code, connect data and systems, and push to launch;

Taking end-to-end responsibility for adoption, stability, effectiveness, or business outcomes;

Distilling field patterns into evals, components, methods, product capabilities, or business self-sustaining mechanisms.

Palantir makes a classic distinction between two types of engineers: a traditional software engineer is more like "one capability serving many customers," while an FDE is more like "one customer invoking many capabilities."

In China, I think half a sentence needs to be added: an FDE must not only deliver capabilities to the field, but also send the field back into the product.

Otherwise, after finishing each customer, all you gain is a set of code no one dares to upgrade, three permanent on-site group chats, and the phrase "you'll have to ask Lao Wang about that." The project may be delivered, but no mechanism has grown out of it.

Lao Wang is important, but Lao Wang is not a platform.

To keep the rest of this from becoming five sections of company introductions, I'll use the same six-dimension ledger to break down each type of FDE:

Business bottleneck: why the enterprise cannot rely solely on standard products, implementation, or consulting;

Delivery target: which business state the FDE ultimately changes, not just what features were built;

Organizational unit: in a small team that can run, which roles the FDE shares responsibility with;

Production permissions: what it can read, what it can write, and which steps must be confirmed by the business owner;

Acceptance metrics: how to distinguish a demo, limited production, and scaled adoption;

Reusable assets: after the project ends, what must leave personal computers and enter the product or platform.

This ledger is also the most central author's perspective of this article: to judge an FDE team, don't look at how many demos it has built—look at what state it has changed and what cost of failure it has borne.

PLATFORM

What services are they selling

These companies own models, compute, and development platforms, yet are farthest from customers' business outcomes. They establish FDE roles not to make customer service more enthusiastic, but to connect model capabilities, customer data, real processes, and production responsibility into one chain.

01.AI's public services include AI transformation consulting, business ontology construction, FDE on-site co-creation, and closed-loop operations; Alibaba Cloud's public samples cover industrial large models, agents, process optimization, and control platforms; roles at Volcano Ark, BytePlus, and others explicitly require building evals and observability, and bringing common problems back to the platform.

So what this type of FDE delivers is usually not a chat window, but:

Problem definition:

which business state is worth changing, and what counts as success;

Data and system integration:

which data is trustworthy, and which tools the agent can call;

Prototype to production:

evals, observability, permissions, cost, stability, and launch;

Product feedback:

which connectors, templates, and eval capabilities should enter the platform.

Six-dimension professional ledger: the platform FDE's task is not "shipping the model over"

Business bottleneck:

Models and cloud resources are general capabilities, but customers buy the results of specific processes. In between, problem definition, data integration, system integration, evals, and adoption responsibility are missing.

Delivery target:

Advancing from "model API is callable" to "a specific business task is reliably completed in production," and forming a closed loop among usage, adoption rate, and product feedback.

Organizational unit:

A more sensible setup is a strategic customer pod: the FDE owns problems and business architecture, the FDSWE or application engineer owns production implementation, with platform, security, and the customer's business owner jointly involved.

Production permissions:

The FDE can configure models, tools, and workflows, and integrate approved data; when it involves changing the customer's business state or industrial control, it should be risk-tiered, with the customer's owner retaining final authorization.

Acceptance metrics:

I would look at time to first production launch, target user adoption rate, task success rate and human coverage rate, latency and stability, cost per task, and how much field capability is reused by the platform.

Reusable assets:

Evals sets, observability, connectors, reference architectures, business object models, permission templates, and incident runbooks.

These metrics are my management suggestions, not a unified KPI publicly disclosed by the companies mentioned above.

Platform-type FDEs also have an easily overlooked two-way responsibility: forward, they must translate model capabilities into business actions; backward, they must judge whether a field problem should be productized, handed to partners, or explicitly stopped as customization.

If every requirement is accepted, the FDE becomes expensive human middleware; if every difference is pushed back to the customer, the FDE degenerates into a product manual that can write code.

Professionalism lies precisely in the third option: turn high-frequency differences into platform capabilities, keep low-frequency differences in the configuration layer, and keep requirements that generate no lasting value out of scope.

Deep dive scenario one: waste incinerator temperature fluctuation—not letting the large model "freestyle"

Alibaba Cloud has published a sufficiently concrete site: the solid waste incineration line at Green Power's Wuhan production base.

According to the company's disclosure, when the system detects furnace temperature fluctuation or insufficient steam stability, the agent correlates historical data with baseline values, analyzes deviations, and generates handling recommendations; after operations personnel confirm via natural language, the industrial control platform issues commands to adjust parameters such as feed rate and air volume.

This is a far more valuable small scenario than "AI helps enterprises cut costs and boost efficiency." It exposes the FDE's real work quite completely:

Furnace temperature, steam stability, and historical operating conditions must first form a usable data contract;

Model outputs must land on executable process variables;

Operations personnel must have confirmation authority, rather than being bypassed by a pretty explanation;

Commands must have scope limits, exception blocking, and recovery paths;

New strategies must go through shadow runs and comparative evals before permissions are gradually expanded.

Items 2 and 3 come from the control variables and human confirmation logic in the public case; items 1, 4, and 5 are acceptance requirements I propose from a production delivery perspective, not details publicly disclosed by Alibaba Cloud.

In industrial settings, "human confirmation" is a responsibility interface, not a sign that AI isn't advanced enough.

If an agent can directly change air volume yet no one can say under what conditions it must stop, that isn't a closed loop—it's pre-writing the incident report as an automation script.

My take: platform-type FDEs will become "small-team based," not infinitely on-site

Such teams will keep growing, but organizations will clearly stratify: FDEs handle problems and business architecture, FDSWEs or application engineers handle production implementation, and platform and security teams handle evals, observability, connectors, and the permission foundation.

Strategic customers will still get deep co-creation from small, strong Pods, while scale coverage goes to partners and standard capabilities. Because if every model call sold comes with three long-term on-site engineers, the revenue curve may look like cloud, but the cost structure will look increasingly like engineering contracting.

Therefore, leaders of such teams should not only count the number of POCs, but also track "account adoption" and "platform recapture." The former proves customers are actually using it; the latter proves the company isn't linearly buying growth with more engineers.

What this type of FDE should accumulate most is not another model API, but the ability to compile vague business problems into production systems.

VERTICAL SaaS

Vertical software companies usually already have products, customers, and industry data. What truly blocks AI is that the rules in each enterprise's head are different.

Beisen's public definition is very direct: the FDE must convert each customer's unique job models, staffing standards, evaluation logic, and organizational language into structured knowledge that AI can understand. Specific services include job model customization, localization of evaluation standards, and connecting agents such as the AI interviewer, AI talent officer, and scheduling into approval, decision, and exception handling workflows.

PatSnap puts this into R&D innovation and intellectual property workflows.

The company's publicly available Eureka service covers technical Q&A, technology pre-research, TRIZ solution exploration, feasibility analysis, invention disclosures, novelty searches, FTO, materials screening, biopharmaceutical intelligence, and strategic insights; the FDE's public responsibilities include configuring and deploying agent workflows, Skills, reports, and automation, supporting test iterations, and converting reusable requirements into standard capabilities.

In a public case, Yonyou Zhishikai's R&D Cloud and PLM Cloud have been integrated with nearly 10 of PatSnap Eureka's agents, covering technical insights, solution exploration, formula optimization, and patent portfolio planning. What this case shows is that products and workflows have made real connections; public materials do not say every integration was done by an FDE, so the two cannot be forcibly equated.

Beisen, PatSnap, FanRuan, and Bizhi Intelligence appear to belong to HR, R&D/IP, data analytics, and recruiting tech respectively, yet the underlying problem is the same:

The delivery object of vertical software AI is shifting from "feature configuration" to "professional judgment."

The six-dimensional professional ledger: industry knowledge is not a pile of documents, but a decision-making mechanism

The product already covers standard processes, but each customer's terminology, rules, exceptions, and expert judgments differ, and generic agents cannot automatically acquire organizational context.

It's not about "finishing the knowledge base," but getting a professional judgment into a real process, where it is accepted, corrected, traced, and continuously maintained by experts.

Industry experts own judgment boundaries, the FDE writes those boundaries into data, tools, and workflows, the product team recaptures commonalities, and the customer's business or professional Owner owns the final result.

HR data, unpublished inventions, R&D materials, and patent judgments are all sensitive. The FDE needs to design minimum visibility scopes, draft versus formal states, expert review, and audit trails.

I would choose metrics per scenario: expert review pass rate, critical omission rate, reasons for manual rejections, target process coverage, active adoption, time to first value, and cross-customer reuse rate of templates and evals.

Industry ontologies, terminology mappings, golden eval sets, rule and exception libraries, process templates, expert correction samples, evidence citation, and versioning mechanisms.

Here it's especially important to distinguish "same industry" from "same risk."

Beisen deals with job models, evaluation standards, and talent processes, with risks concentrated in bias, explanation, and organizational fairness; PatSnap enters R&D and IP processes, with risks more concentrated in unpublished technical information, evidence traceability, professional review, and the boundaries of formal legal actions. Both are vertical software FDE work, but they cannot be accepted with the same accuracy metrics and approval flows.

This is also where vertical FDEs are harder than ordinary implementation: implementation can ask "is the configuration complete," while the FDE must keep asking "whose judgment is this, who discovers it when it's wrong, and who maintains it when the rules change."

Deep dive scenario two: for an invention disclosure, the hard part isn't generating text

Shrink the scenario to an afternoon when an R&D engineer has just had a technical idea.

The engineer uploads technical descriptions, drawings, or experimental materials, hoping the system will produce an invention disclosure. Public product capabilities can help organize invention points, search related patents, and generate a first draft; but what enterprises actually want to deploy is not "a button that writes documents," but an R&D and IP collaboration process.

From the FDE perspective, I would keep asking:

Key points

Who can view invention content that has not yet been published?

Can the patents and literature cited by the AI be traced item by item?

Which fields must be confirmed by the R&D engineer, and which conclusions must be reviewed by the IP owner?

How does the first draft enter review, rejection, supplementation, and version tracking?

Can the system generate drafts, but never automatically proceed to formal applications without the business Owner?

These five questions determine whether the product is "helping write text" or truly entering the chain of responsibility for how enterprises form technical achievements.

The most professional moves here often look the least sci-fi: adding states to drafts, adding sources to evidence, adding confirmations to high-risk actions, and leaving rollback paths for errors. They rarely take center stage at launch events, yet they always get the microphone quickly when something goes wrong.

My take: vertical FDEs are the easiest to scale—and the easiest to become high-spec implementation

Beisen's official website says the team has grown beyond 300 people, and its annual report discloses rapid growth in AI Family contract value, customer count, and penetration. These numbers prove the company is scaling commercially; they do not prove the growth is entirely driven by FDEs, let alone serve as independently verified customer ROI.

The establishment of an FDE team at Zhihuiya (PatSnap) represents another clear trend: as industry software moves from "delivering tools" to "delivering outcomes," the FDE becomes the orchestration layer between data, agents, expert methods, and customer workflows.

The dividing line for such teams is very simple:

If customer knowledge is consolidated into industry ontologies, eval samples, process templates, and product capabilities, the FDE is a product flywheel;

If customer knowledge is consolidated into private prompts, special code, and rules only the original author understands, the FDE is just high-spec implementation.

From a business perspective, it also requires a "dual ledger": the customer ledger tracks adoption, quality, and outcomes, while the product ledger tracks which field knowledge has entered standard capabilities. If you only look at renewals and not product recovery, the team may accumulate non-upgradable forked versions amid growth; if you only look at product standardization and not customer outcomes, you end up with an architecture that is elegant internally but unused in the field.

More mature organizations in the future will separate the FDE from "customer model operations": the FDE is responsible for getting professional processes into production the first time, while subsequent rule updates, eval maintenance, and adoption operations are handled by long-term roles. Otherwise, every customer change will recall the scarcest engineers.

Assets compound, and so do exceptions. The former go into the product roadmap; the latter go into the 2 a.m. group chat.

04 SERVICES

These companies know the customer site best, and are also the most squeezed by the traditional man-day model.

Isoftstone's 2025 annual report states that the company has built an "AI Factory + FDE" model, using consulting to define high-value scenarios and FDEs for agile delivery and implementation, with a service chain covering business consulting, product implementation, technical foundations, and ongoing operations. The report also states that the integrated FDE consulting-implementation model has already landed in industries such as agriculture and animal husbandry.

Tianyun Rongchuang's public job postings describe the service as scenario surveying, process decomposition, agent delivery, context engineering, evals, and delivery assets; Feixiang Data's public postings lean more toward training, requirements decomposition, and lightweight POCs; Hejun Consulting has also begun recruiting FDEs.

These examples show that the IT services industry is repackaging work previously scattered across consultants, architects, developers, project managers, and operations into shorter outcome loops.

Repackaging is not a bad thing. Redesigning the responsibilities of six people into one Pod may improve efficiency; stuffing the work of six people unchanged onto one person will only increase the frequency of medical checkups.

The six-dimension professional ledger: the services FDE is first and foremost project economics

Customers want business outcomes, but traditional contracts are managed by man-days, features, and milestones, making it easy for consulting, development, launch, and operations to each complete their part while the whole fails.

Turning a strategic judgment into a runnable system, then using operational data to prove whether it has improved the agreed-upon state—rather than leaving after delivering a feature checklist.

Industry consultants define value and scope, the FDE connects requirements, engineering, and adoption, platform engineers provide the delivery foundation, and the customer's Owner, IT, and risk control jointly accept the work.

External team access should be temporary, tiered, and auditable; the customer retains production approval and high-risk actions, and accounts, keys, and write permissions are revoked after delivery ends.

Beyond time, budget, and features, I look at the value baseline, change request rate, production defect escapes, manual takeovers, customer self-sufficiency rate, maintenance costs, and delivery asset reuse rate.

Industry scenario packages, connectors, delivery harnesses, regression evals, SLA and change templates, runbooks, customer training, and exit checklists.

The most critical professional change here happens in the contract, not just in the code.

Outcome-based delivery must clearly define the business baseline, data definitions, controllable variables, external dependencies, and change mechanisms before work begins. Otherwise, "accountable for outcomes" easily becomes a slogan with unlimited boundaries and limited evidence: the customer believes you promised business improvement, the delivery team believes it only promised a system launch, and both sides only discover at the acceptance meeting that they were using different versions of the same language.

The services FDE can expand the loop of responsibility, but it cannot swallow the customer's business responsibility, nor absorb unlimited scope on behalf of the contract.

Scenario deep-dive three: port container truck hybrid dispatching, where what gets accepted is not a new dashboard

Isoftstone's annual report discloses that the company built an intelligent horizontal dispatching solution for ports with a large ICT enterprise, achieving a breakthrough in hybrid dispatching of port container trucks. Public materials do not state whether this project was delivered via the FDE model, so it cannot be written up as an "FDE success case"; but it is well suited to illustrate the real problems a services FDE faces.

If I were responsible for acceptance of this scenario, I would not first ask about the dashboard's colors, but would ask five more troublesome questions:

Which authoritative systems do shipping schedules, yards, vehicles, and task status each come from;

How do dispatch recommendations handle equipment failures, urgent insertions, and priority changes;

When can dispatchers override system recommendations, and is the reason for the override logged;

After a new strategy goes live, is it evaluated by waiting time, empty runs, throughput, or safety incidents;

When data is delayed or the model malfunctions, how does the system fall back to usable manual rules.

These are my delivery inspection framework, not the project configuration disclosed in the annual report.

The value of the services FDE is not delivering one more version of a solution for the customer, but compressing "solution—system—operations—retrospective" into a single execution chain.

This is also its biggest difference from traditional consulting. A PPT can declare a closed loop on page 38; a production system will usually require you to write out the closed loop's APIs, Owners, and on-call numbers.

My judgment: these teams will grow fastest, and dilute the FDE most easily

Agents will continue to compress solution first drafts, interface scaffolding, testing, documentation, and some operations labor, and service companies must shift from "selling inputs" to "selling outcomes." As a result, FDE Pods will grow rapidly.

Meanwhile, renaming will happen just as fast. Presales can be renamed FDE, on-site developers can be renamed FDE, and after an implementation team attends one agent training session, the entire staff may complete their species upgrade by Monday.

To judge real from fake, don't look at the English acronym—look at four powers: the right to production code, the right to product feedback, responsibility for evals and rollbacks, and clear exit conditions.

Unlike platform companies, service companies may not have an external software product to absorb field feedback. They should instead send experience back to their own "delivery factory": industry data models, eval baselines, connectors, deployment templates, contract boundaries, and operations mechanisms. As long as the next engagement still starts from a blank document and a newly created group chat, the AI Factory is just a meeting room name with an industrial vibe.

I expect such organizations to further split into two sets of responsibilities, "Build" and "Run": the former handled by FDE Pods for first production deployment, the latter maintained long-term by managed operations or customer teams. Mixing the two means key people are constantly recalled by alerts from old projects, while new projects forever lack the most experienced people.

Without these, the FDE may just be a consultant wearing an engineering vest; with them, one project can become a capability that the next project doesn't have to rebuild.

05 INTERNAL FDE

These teams don't sell services externally; the customer is in the same building.

Insta360's public job postings target internal scenarios such as marketing, sales, customer service, supply chain, and finance, advancing from Demo and POC all the way to production. Paifang Technology's postings require going deep into business departments to complete knowledge bases and RAG, internal API and database integration, workflow orchestration, production deployment, monitoring and alerting, and user training, while accumulating internal agent components and toolchains.

COSCO Shipping Holdings stated in its annual report that in 2026 it will use the cultivation of specialized FDE talent as a lever to build an AI R&D team with deep integration of business and technology. The word "will" must be preserved here: this is a public plan, not an already-validated organizational outcome.

Such public evidence mainly consists of job design and construction plans, not disclosed customer cases. Writing a recruitment JD up as a success story is like seeing someone's gym membership records and immediately announcing they already have an eight-pack—highly encouraging, insufficiently evidential.

The six-dimension professional ledger: the internal FDE is not a free external vendor

A central AI platform can provide models and tools, but lacks sufficient business context and departmental authority; business departments know the processes, but lack the ability to engineer those rules.

Change the cycle time, quality, or decision-making status of an internal process and have the business team take it over, rather than writing "how many agents were launched" into a quarterly poster.

A safer approach is Hub-and-Spoke: the central platform provides models, security, and engineering standards, FDEs embed into business squads, business product owners own the outcomes, and risk and data leads participate in granting authority.

Use enterprise identity, service accounts, least privilege, and change windows; an FDE can implement automation, but cannot grant itself business approval rights, payment rights, or the authority to make external commitments.

I would first establish a baseline of the old process, then look at cycle time, exception rate, manual takeovers, adoption by target users, whether the old steps have truly been retired, and whether the business team can independently handle common issues.

Internal business object models, data contracts, permission matrices, eval sets, shared tools, change logs, runbooks, and handover materials.

Internal teams most easily fall into the "requirements queue" trap. Once departments discover that the center has a group of people who both understand AI and are willing to communicate, requirements will converge as quickly as cars merging onto a highway entrance before a holiday.

Therefore, internal FDEs need a scenario admission mechanism. I would prioritize evaluating business value, frequency of occurrence, data readiness, process owner, reversibility of failures, and reuse potential; requirements that lack an owner, have no baseline, involve irreversible failures, yet are unwilling to set up manual confirmation should not start work just because an executive posted "ASAP" in a group chat.

The scarce resource for internal FDEs is not development time, but the organizational position of entering a process with authorization and exiting it on schedule.

My take: the core of the internal FDE is not technology, but authorization

Central AI teams usually understand models, platforms, and security, but may not know how sales qualifies opportunities, how supply chain handles exceptions, or at which step finance requires a manual signature. Business departments know the rules best, but lack the ability to turn rules into data contracts, tool permissions, and eval samples.

Internal FDEs stand exactly in this gap, but cannot monopolize business responsibility.

I favor "central platform + co-built business squad + phased rotation": the FDE completes problem definition, the first production version, evals, permissions, and handover; the business owner is accountable for outcomes; the platform team takes over common capabilities; and when the business can sustain itself, the FDE exits.

A good internal FDE measures success not by "the whole company will always need me," but by "this process finally no longer depends on me."

This gives rise to a career path different from the external FDE: the internal FDE is closer to a combination of business product owner, process architect, and AI governance engineer. Their value may not show up as direct revenue, but will be reflected in whether cross-departmental processes are truly rewritten, and whether the central platform can absorb commonalities from multiple departments.

Management must give this team "dual belonging": engineering standards belong to the central platform, while business priorities and outcomes belong to the scenario owner. With only a technical reporting line, it tends to become platform evangelism; with only a business reporting line, it tends to grow into departmental custom development.

Otherwise the company will get a new shadow IT department. Business says "ask the FDE about AI matters," technology says "ask the field about business rules," and the FDE ends up as the person in the company who understands the processes best, is busiest, and is most afraid to take a vacation.

06 PHYSICAL AI

Software FDEs face dirty data, legacy systems, and organizational politics. Physical world FDEs get all that, plus lighting, dust, network jitter, equipment tolerances, safety fences, and a robotic arm that insists on erroring only on Friday nights.

Dazhi Technology's official website has made clear that its platform capabilities are used for FDE process development and on-site system integration, with a public service chain covering task definition, simulation validation, workstation execution, PLC/IoT/MES integration, and sample feedback, spanning scenarios such as automotive and 3C flexible assembly, raw material handling and bag opening, recipe weighing, and material sorting. LimX Dynamics' public job postings involve customer technical liaison, data collection workflows, and embodied algorithm deployment.

The deliverables here are not just models and interfaces, but action primitives, process constraints, equipment compatibility, field data, quality assessment, and safety boundaries.

For such teams, I would ask about "rollback" in very specific terms: a software rollback might mean switching back to an old version, but a physical system rollback must first answer how the equipment stops moving and returns to a safe state.

A button turning gray does not mean the robotic arm has put down the wrench. The real world has always had limited respect for UIs.

The six-dimensional professional ledger: the physical world turns "rollback-able" into a safety issue

A lab can prove that a model can perform a task, but what the customer buys is the ability to continuously complete the task under specific equipment, process takt, material variability, and personnel collaboration conditions. Between the two lies field engineering, control systems, and safety responsibility.

It is not "the robot moves," but changing a measurable production state within the safety envelope, such as completing a grasp, assembly, weighing, or sorting operation, while stably meeting the agreed takt time, yield, and availability.

An FDE needs to deliver jointly with process or control engineers, robotics/applied algorithm engineers, field reliability staff, operators, and a safety owner. Missing any one of them, the system may look smart in demos and suddenly start relearning the world after a shift change.

Permissions must be distinguished into three levels: recommendation, supervised execution, and automatic control; the model must not bypass PLCs, safety interlocks, emergency stops, or process limits. Changes to versions, parameters, and action scopes require approval, change windows, and traceable records.

I would look at task success rate within safety conditions, cycle time or yield, manual intervention rate, false stop rate, fault recovery time, scenario drift, and data coverage. "No safety incidents occurred" alone is not a sufficient metric—it may just mean the equipment hasn't really done any work yet.

Action and process primitives, workstation templates, hardware compatibility matrices, failure taxonomies, simulation and offline replay samples, calibration specifications, remote diagnostics, runbooks, and safety recovery procedures.

These are likewise my launch and management framework, and do not imply that the companies mentioned have publicly adopted the same metrics.

Physical FDEs are most easily misled by the phrase "end-to-end." End-to-end is a system responsibility perspective, not a license for one model to hold all permissions from perception to execution. Safety PLCs, mechanical limit switches, emergency stops, and human takeover should form independent protection layers; the model is responsible for choosing actions within the allowed range, while the protection layers are responsible for still being able to say "no" when the model loses judgment.

I favor a five-level delegation path:

Simulation validation: first cover normal operating conditions, boundary conditions, and fault injection;

Offline replay: use real field data to reproduce model choices without affecting equipment;

Shadow operation: generate recommendations online without executing them, comparing against human decisions;

Supervised execution: execute after operator confirmation, recording takeovers and reasons for rejection;

Limited autonomy: execute automatically only within clearly defined operating conditions and action scopes, with immediate degradation upon anomalies.

What matters most about this path is not "eventually reaching level five," but that every level has entry conditions, exit conditions, and a recovery plan. For low-frequency, high-consequence tasks, staying long-term at supervised execution may actually indicate maturity, not lag.

The launch unit for a physical FDE is not a model version, but a combination of "model + equipment + process + safety state."

My take: physical FDEs will scale last, but have the highest professional barriers

Public team samples in this branch are still few, so it cannot be written up as an already-mature mainstream paradigm. But as industrial agents and embodied intelligence enter real workstations, it will gradually converge with applied algorithms, field reliability, data flywheels, and safety engineering.

Its regional delivery will be heavier and on-site presence deeper, with remote diagnostics, hardware compatibility matrices, sim-to-real evals, and safe shutdown becoming core assets. Its scaling speed may be slower than pure software FDEs, because what gets replicated is not a piece of code, but a combination of code with equipment, processes, and field responsibility.

But once action atoms, workstation templates, device compatibility, and failure data start to be reused, the moat becomes thicker than "building yet another agent workflow." More mature enterprises may form a three-layer structure of "central product and simulation platform + regional FDE network + on-site partners": the central team recaptures common capabilities, regional FDEs handle high-value differentiation, and partners take on standard installation and maintenance.

The operating metrics here cannot just look at project revenue; they must also look at "how much re-learning each new device or process eliminates from the next delivery." Otherwise, so-called platformization merely preserves each site's unique difficulties in a more digital form.

At the same time, we must guard against simply renaming traditional installation and commissioning as FDE. Field engineers are equally important, but if the role lacks software engineering, data evals, product feedback, and closed-loop outcomes, it is not the FDE discussed in this article.

No job is superior or inferior—just don't let an acronym add drama to a role.

07 FIVE MODELS

Putting the cases back into their business models makes the differences clearer.

Model, cloud, and general platform companies:

Buying a "value translator" that turns frontier capabilities into production adoption, then feeds field problems back to the platform.

Vertical SaaS and industry software:

Buying a "knowledge migrator" that turns expert judgment into industry ontologies, evals, and upgradable workflows.

IT services, consulting, and integrators:

Buying a "result delivery unit" that connects strategy, engineering, and operations into a closed loop.

In-house enterprise AI teams:

Buying an "organizational compiler" that turns departmental rules into data, permissions, processes, and accountability.

Embodied intelligence and industrial sites:

Buying a "reality adapter" that lets models work stably under device, personnel, and safety constraints.

"Behind the same FDE title lie five business problems, five sets of production responsibilities, and five career paths."

What truly differentiates careers is the cost of failure

The difference among these five types of companies lies not only in who their customers are, but in what constitutes the loss when the system fails:

Platform FDEs change the state of "model capability to production adoption." Failure typically manifests as stalled adoption, runaway costs, insufficient stability, or major customer customization swallowing the platform roadmap.

Vertical FDEs change the state of "expert knowledge to process judgment." Failure may be a missed professional judgment, sensitive data crossing boundaries, untraceable evidence, or rules changing with no one to maintain them.

Services FDEs change the state of "project milestones to continuous operations." Failure may be scope creep, project losses, systems with no one to take over, or features accepted while the business problem is still waiting for the elevator in place.

Internal FDEs change the state of "departmental needs to organizational processes." Failure may create shadow IT, accountability vacuums in approvals, old processes running in parallel with new agents, and all problems ultimately flowing into the same group chat.

Physical FDEs change the state of "software judgment to device action." The cost of failure falls directly on downtime, quality, equipment, and personal safety, so their delegation of authority and rollback must be the most conservative.

This also explains why the five types of teams cannot share the same performance scorecard. Platform teams counting POCs, vertical teams counting knowledge base entries, services teams counting person-days, internal teams counting agents, and physical teams counting lab accuracy can all produce weekly reports that look diligent yet prove no production value.

The more meaningful questions are: what state did this team change, who approved the change, who bears the failure, what assets were left on site, and can the next delivery be faster and safer.

An FDE's professionalism is ultimately determined by how important a state it can safely change, and whether it can replicate that capability.

If you are choosing a role, ask less about "how big is the company's model" and more the following five questions:

Which business state will I ultimately change, rather than which feature will I deliver?

Can I access production code, real data, and the business owner?

Who owns the eval sets, who decides on launch, and who can roll back?

How does field experience feed into the product roadmap or internal platform?

When does a project count as finished, and when should the FDE leave?

These five answers usually predict better than a job title or tech stack whether, a year from now, you will be accumulating compound interest or accumulating WeChat groups.

08 TRENDS

1. Job titles will keep inflating, while the jobs themselves begin to split

FDEs, FDSWEs, deployment leads, application architects, eval engineers, platform engineers, and industry experts will gradually separate. Early-stage teams can have one person wearing multiple hats, but at scale you can no longer expect the same person to pitch board-level strategy, write full-stack code, and carry production alerts.

If a JD simultaneously requires you to understand industry sales, Kubernetes, financial ROI, and executive communication, plus year-round travel, it may be describing not a job but an organizational wish list.

2. The biggest talent source is not fresh graduates, but the recombination of existing roles

Solutions Architects, product managers, delivery engineers, data engineers, industry experts, and enterprise digitalization teams will become the main supply for FDEs. Training programs in Shanghai and Shandong, along with Beijing's policies, have already signaled this.

But a career transition is not adding three letters after your old title. The real threshold is: moving from explaining features to shipping to production, and then from shipping to production to daring to sign off on permissions, evals, and rollbacks.

3. Project assets will become the team's true gross margin statement

Mature teams will mandatorily recapture business object models, data contracts, eval sets, permission templates, connectors, exception playbooks, and exit plans.

Whether the next delivery is faster is the most honest indicator of whether an FDE team has a product flywheel.

If more customers mean more exceptions and core members dare not take vacations, business growth is merely moving technical debt from a single room into an office tower.

4. External FDEs and internal FDEs will formally split

External FDEs are accountable for customer adoption, renewals, and product feedback; internal FDEs are accountable for process transformation, organizational adoption, and capability handover. Their skills are similar, but their power structures differ.

A customer changing from "another company" to "the department upstairs" does not automatically make requirements clearer—it only makes elevator encounters more frequent.

5. Governance will shift from a bonus item to a core capability

The implementation guidelines on agents issued by the Cyberspace Administration of China and other departments have explicitly stated requirements for decision authority, users' final decision rights, traceable behavior, and anomaly detection, intervention, blocking, and recovery.

This means FDEs can no longer just answer "can it run"; they must also answer: who authorizes, who maintains the evals, which actions require confirmation, how anomalies escalate, who can shut it down, and how data and state are recovered.

When an agent can send emails, change states, and issue commands, "it's pretty smart" is no longer a complete acceptance opinion. Parents praising their children can talk that way; production systems cannot.

09 DECISION

Not every company needs a team called FDE.

If the product is highly standardized, customers need almost no integration, and the distance between business outcomes and product usage is short, a mature product engineering, implementation, and customer success system may be more economical.

But if four or more of the following six questions are answered "yes," the enterprise should seriously consider FDEs:

Customers or business processes must connect to multiple systems, private data, and complex permissions;

The path from demo to stable production frequently gets stuck on organization, data, or processes rather than the model;

High-value projects require engineers and business owners to co-create continuously;

On-site differences cannot be resolved through configuration and documentation alone;

Project experience must continuously feed back into the product or internal platform;

After launch, long-term evals, observability, exception handling, and change governance are needed.

Before forming a team, I would also require management to write down five things clearly:

Who they report to:

Caught between sales and R&D with no clear owner, the team will live in a perpetual tug-of-war over priorities.

What counts as success:

Launch, adoption, stability, self-sufficiency, or improved business metrics — different answers lead to different behaviors.

What must be productized:

Without a mechanism for harvesting lessons, field experience will only sink into personal laptops and group chats.

Who is accountable for high-risk actions:

An FDE can design systems, but cannot serve as the ultimate stand-in for business accountability.

When must they leave:

"Forward deployment" without exit criteria usually ends up as "permanent deployment."

∞

THE END

The most notable change in China's FDE landscape is not a few more job postings, but that it has entered corporate strategy, listed-company annual reports, customer workflows, talent development, and industrial policy.

This means the focus of competition in enterprise AI is shifting.

In the past, people compared whose models were stronger, whose parameters were larger, whose demos were faster; going forward, more and more the comparison will be over who can enter real workflows, hold permission boundaries, continuously maintain evals, achieve organizational adoption, and turn one success into a foundation so the next one doesn't start from scratch.

The name FDE may keep trending, or it may be split into a dozen more precise roles. The name doesn't matter.

What truly matters is that enterprises are starting to build organizations for "the stretch between model and outcome that no one fully owns."

"My judgment is: over the next three years, China will not converge on a single standard FDE; instead, five more mature forward deployment mechanisms will emerge."

Good teams turn field work into products, projects into assets, and customers or business units into self-sustaining owners.

Bad teams dump all the ambiguous requirements, cross-department conflicts, and production incidents onto a few capable people, then give them an English acronym that sounds like a special forces unit.

Both kinds of teams can be very busy.

Only one will become increasingly valuable.
