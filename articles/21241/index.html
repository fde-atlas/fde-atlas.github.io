Cursor FDE Role Requirements and Interview Preparation

Original title: Cursor FDE 岗位要求与面试准备

Key Takeaways

  • Cursor's FDE EMEA role in London emphasizes ownership of production systems, not demos
  • What Cursor requires: end-to-end delivery, Python and TypeScript, requirements discovery alongside customer engineers, and ownership of evals, tracing, and cost
  • The best preparation is building a migration workflow with real data, then turning it into a runbook

Open the job posting for Forward Deployed Engineer, EMEA that Cursor (owned by Anysphere) published on June 4, 2026 for the London role, and one line is remarkably blunt: this is not a demo role—you are accountable for systems that must run in the real world.

Keep that line in mind as you read on. Many engineers treat job postings as keyword lists to stuff into their resumes. The right way to read this one is to treat every line as a task you would perform on-site at a customer in your first week, then ask yourself: have I done this? What evidence do I have?

The job posting actually describes a complete project

Read all the requirements together and you'll find that Cursor's posting describes a fairly complete work cycle. You will do requirements discovery alongside customer engineers, building Cursor workflows for refactoring, migrations, PR review loops, or incident-to-fix work.

After that, you will personally own the trade-offs between evals, tracing, and cost, and package what you build into reusable patterns.

On skills, Cursor requires experience building production-grade AI workflows, the ability to work end-to-end across frontend, backend, infrastructure, and prompt iteration, operational reliability awareness, and proficiency in Python and TypeScript/JavaScript.

Exponent's job summary page lists the experience requirement as five or more years, with at least two years working directly with customers.

How Cursor positions this role within the organization is also worth reading closely. The company describes the FDE as someone who sits with enterprise engineering organizations (possibly remotely), and Exponent's page also notes that Cursor is building a full FDE organization, not a support function.

This directly affects how you present your experience in interviews: you are the person who makes the product actually run, not the person who handles tickets.

How is Cursor's FDE team organized?

Cursor's Forward Deployed Strategist job posting, published on July 24, 2026, shows how the team is organized. Each pod consists of one Strategist and one or two FDEs; FDEs sit with customer teams to build deeper production systems. The posting also reiterates the company's mission: making programming automated.

At the management level, Cursor is hiring a Regional Director of Forward Deployed Engineering, posted on July 22, 2026, to lead and scale a team of 6–8 FDEs working on strategic projects.

This person needs eight or more years of experience leading engineering teams to deliver production systems, must be able to serve as a senior technical point of contact for customers, and must build reusable runbooks so the entire FDE organization keeps improving.

From this, one preparation tip follows: since the FDE organization's leadership is responsible for building shared runbooks, an FDE who can convert work done for one customer into outcomes applicable to the next will likely be highly valued.

Prepare evidence at both levels: one customer engagement that ran well, and one pattern others can reuse.

Example: a migration project with real data

Imagine a hypothetical customer that wants to migrate several hundred services from an old logging library to a new one. This is exactly the kind of work the job posting mentions. A good requirements discovery session with the customer's tech lead might look like this:

FDE: What conditions must a migration PR meet for your team to merge it directly without manual changes? Tech lead: CI passes, the log format read by the alerting system is unchanged, and each PR touches only one service. FDE: Who reviews, and how many hours a week can they dedicate to this? Tech lead: Two people, a few hours each.

The first three answers define your evals: CI passes, log format unchanged, one service per PR. The last answer defines the real constraint: human review capacity, not code generation speed.

Now consider a hypothetical calculation. The workflow generates 200 PRs, 150 pass CI, and reviewers merge 120. The merge rate is 60%, not the 75% the CI number suggests. If the total model cost for the whole batch is C, then the true cost per merged PR is C divided by 120, not C divided by 200.

This is where tracing proves its value. By saving a trace for each PR, you can see why the 30 PRs that passed CI were rejected: maybe the log format was changed, or two services were modified at once.

Each of these causes can become an automated check that runs before a PR reaches reviewers. That way, the two reviewers only read PRs that actually have a chance of being merged.

Turning the project into job-search material

The table below maps each requirement in the job posting to evidence you can prepare yourself through the project above.

Requirement in the postingEvidence to prepare
Requirements discovery with customer engineersA one-page record: what questions you asked and what merge criteria resulted
Building Cursor workflows (migrations, PR review)A repository containing a real series of PRs, each with a single scope
Evals, tracing, costA table: merge rate, rejection reasons, cost per merged PR
End-to-end, Python and TypeScriptA Python orchestration script and a small TypeScript dashboard
Reusable patternsA one-page runbook others can follow to reproduce the work

Let results speak in your resume: use one line to state the workflow, the merge rate, and which check you added after reading the traces. If you have less than two years of customer-facing experience, write about times you sat with other teams debugging production issues—that is the closest material to this job.

Common mistakes in preparation

The most common mistake is bringing a demo to an interview for a role that explicitly says it does not look at demos. A polished video of an agent fixing code cannot answer these questions: how many times did it run on a real repository? How many times did it fail? What did you do when it failed?

Another mistake is ignoring cost. The job posting is clear that the FDE owns cost trade-offs; if you cannot say how much a PR cost, you are leaving blank the part of the work directly assigned to you. Also, don't only talk about code: interviewers want to hear what you asked the customer before writing the first line of code.

The final mistake is stopping after one successful run. In an organization whose leadership builds runbooks, the next question is almost inevitable: can someone else repeat this without you?

This week, pick an open-source repository and use Cursor to migrate 20 files. Twenty PRs plus an honest data table are more persuasive than any demo.
