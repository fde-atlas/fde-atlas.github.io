Do FDEs travel a lot? How can Forward Deployed Engineers avoid burnout?

Original title: FDE 出差多吗？前线部署工程师如何避免倦怠

FDE Times · 2026-10-08 · 5 min read

Summary

This article examines travel intensity and burnout risk in FDE roles. OpenAI's job posting allows up to 50% travel, while Palantir prefers candidates able to travel 25-50%. The author argues travel itself is not the biggest danger; endless firefighting and customer integration work, combined with a fast-changing enterprise AI stack, can pull engineers away from core product work. The key test is whether on-site findings flow back into the product, and whether companies offer structured rotation and skill-building programs. Practical advice includes specific interview questions and resume framing to distinguish genuine FDE roles from consulting in disguise.

Key points

  • OpenAI's FDE postings specify up to 50% travel; Palantir expects 25-50%.
  • Burnout stems structurally from context switching and being the company's face to customers.
  • FDE roles without a feedback loop into the product are effectively rebranded consulting.
  • Rotation and capability-building should be institutional, not occasional rewards.
  • Ask interviewers for a customer discovery that actually reached the product roadmap.

Key Takeaways

  • OpenAI's job description states travel can be up to 50%, and Palantir prefers candidates who can travel 25-50%. Practitioners say outright that burnout arrives earlier than in other roles.
  • Travel isn't the biggest risk. What's more dangerous is endless firefighting and integration work, because the enterprise AI tech stack changes too fast.
  • The test: can work done at customer sites flow back into the product? Does the company have a clear rotation and skill-building plan?

According to LeadDev, OpenAI's Forward Deployed Engineer (FDE) job posting requires candidates to travel up to 50% of the time. Palantir's posting for a Forward Deployed Software Engineer is worded more gently: it prefers people who can travel 25-50%, noting that actual travel varies by team and location.

These numbers aren't just a scheduling issue. LeadDev notes that constant travel makes this role burn people out earlier than other positions. Unframe's global field CTO, who does this job himself, says bluntly that the model "doesn't look very sustainable."

Suppose you're a developer with two to eight years of experience considering an FDE role. The real question isn't whether you can live out of a suitcase year-round.

The question is: in three years, will you be a better engineer, or a disconnected, exhausted integrator? That depends more on how the company designs the role than on your stamina.

Half the Year on the Road

Start with simple math. A year has 52 weeks; 50% travel means roughly 26 weeks away from home. Even at the lower bound of Palantir's range, 25%, that's 13 weeks—nearly an entire quarter.

Time on the road is only the visible part. KDnuggets calls burnout a "structural risk" of this job and attributes it to two things: constant context switching, and the fact that the FDE is the company's face to the customer.

The consequences are easy to imagine. Sitting in a customer's conference room, it's hard to say "let me check with my team" and walk away. When the product breaks, it often lands on whoever is on site.

Palantir's own definition shows the pressure is written into the job description: the FDSE works directly with customers to rapidly understand their biggest problems. You're there precisely because the customer has a serious problem.

Imagine: two weeks stationed at a bank's headquarters, coming home to an overflowing inbox, with the next customer already waiting. Why learning time is the first thing sacrificed becomes obvious.

Endless Firefighting: What Happens to Your Skills?

Still, travel isn't the scariest risk. FDE Academy cites a warning from Underdog.io: if the role becomes an endless string of customer integrations and firefighting, you may end up further from core product engineering than you expected.

The same page emphasizes that the enterprise AI tech stack changes faster than almost any other field. Put these two together and you get a double trap: just when technology is changing fastest, you're drifting away from the engineering core.

Here's one quarter an FDE spent inside that trap.

In the first month, you write a connector for Customer A's ERP system. In the second month, you fix Customer B's broken data pipeline. In the third month, you patch a prompt bug in Customer C's agent. Everything is urgent and useful, but by the end of the quarter, you've learned nothing deeper and left behind nothing reusable.

The Line Between Growth and Burnout

KDnuggets offers a simple test: an FDE role without a feedback loop to the product is just consulting with a nicer title. This line determines whether the role drains you or helps you grow.

Back to Customer B's broken data pipeline. The first approach: you fix it so it runs, the customer is satisfied, and you move on to the next thing.

The second approach: you also fix it so it runs, but you document the root cause and bring it back to the product team. Three months later, it becomes a built-in schema validation feature that all customers can use.

Customer D will never hit this problem again.

Both approaches take roughly the same effort on site; the difference lies in everything that follows. The second approach makes you think like a product designer, write code that enters the core codebase, and prevent the next fire. Your skills accumulate instead of being drained.

Rotation Should Be Policy, Not a Reward

A feedback loop protects your skills, but it can't protect you from exhaustion. FDE Academy believes the common way to reduce this risk is planned rotation, combined with a skill-building program.

This is a practitioner's judgment, not a measured result. But it aligns with the logic above: if the pressure is structural, the solution must be structural too.

The table below lists several pressures mentioned by practitioners, what each one drains, and a question to help you spot it before signing.

None of these questions has a single "correct" answer. What matters is whether the company can give a concrete answer. If the recruiter can describe an example of an FDE pushing a customer-site finding onto the roadmap, the feedback loop is real.

How to Prepare

When reading a job description, find the travel line first and convert it into weeks. The phrase "varies by team and location" in Palantir's posting is a cue to ask more questions, not an insignificant detail.

If the role serves overseas customers, add flight time and jet lag to every trip—the number on paper may weigh heavier than you think.

Many Vietnamese developers who have done outsourcing projects are used to the rhythm of "build whatever the customer asks for." It's a good foundation for being an FDE, but it's also what most easily pulls you into the first approach from the pipeline example. Starting this week, record every firefight in two columns: what applies only to this customer, and what could become a general feature.

On your resume, don't just write "completed deployment for Customer X." Write what problem you found at the customer, how you brought it back into the product, and what later customers avoided as a result. That one line shows you understand the difference between an FDE and a consultant, and serious recruiters will notice immediately.

At your next FDE interview, ask the interviewer for one customer finding that actually made it onto the roadmap. If they can't name one, you're probably about to accept a consulting job with an FDE title.
