Today, many companies are accelerating AI adoption and DX initiatives.

However, in actual AI projects, we often hear comments like "We built a highly accurate AI model, but it can't be integrated into operations and goes unused." AI adoption and AI projects don't progress as expected. In many cases, the cause is not a lack of technical skill but "field implementation."

The one drawing attention today as the figure who bridges this gap and elevates AI from a mere experimental initiative to "a capability actually used in the field" is the FDE (Forward Deployed Engineer).

This article explains in detail the role of the FDE and why it is needed.

What is an FDE? The role that makes AI implementation successful

FDE stands for Forward Deployed Engineer, literally "an engineer deployed to the front line." Traditional software engineers and data scientists typically focused on algorithm development and system design at development sites or in lab environments. FDEs, on the other hand, go directly to the front line of operations—the customer's offices, factories, logistics sites, and stores.

Their purpose is not just system development; they

  • directly grasp what is truly causing trouble in the field,
  • identify where the bottlenecks are in business workflows,
  • determine which operations AI should be applied to,

and then embed AI solutions into operations by building and improving prototypes on the spot.

Why does AI adoption require an "embedded-in-the-field" approach?

AI differs fundamentally from traditional business systems. For conventional systems, "working according to spec" was the definition of success. AI, however, depends heavily on data quality and environmental conditions. For example:

  • The tacit knowledge of experienced field workers is not captured as data
  • Demand forecasting AI is affected by missing input data
  • Camera-based AI accuracy changes with lighting conditions

Such factors cannot be understood from a meeting room or development site. FDEs minimize the time lag and perception gap between development and the field. If something feels off in the field, they fix it immediately and deliver an improved version the next day. Repeating this rapid feedback loop is what evolves AI into a more practical tool.

How FDEs differ from traditional IT consultants and SEs

When designing an AI project team, FDEs are often confused with existing roles such as IT consultants and systems engineers (SEs), but their roles differ.

Differences from IT consultants

IT consultants organize business challenges, formulate DX strategies, and create roadmaps, playing an important role in setting the direction for AI utilization. However, where AI adoption projects actually stall is the "implementation stage."

  • The model is built but can't be integrated into operations
  • Data integration is more complex than expected
  • The field won't accept the new operations

FDEs understand the strategy and then become the executors who write the code themselves, build the infrastructure, adjust the UI (user interface), and implement it in the field, taking responsibility all the way to a working deliverable.

Differences from traditional systems engineers (SEs) and vendors

Traditional SEs and contracted development vendors proceed with development based on finalized specifications. In waterfall development, changes require additional work and contract amendments. In AI projects, however, the correct specification usually doesn't exist from the start (the spec evolves along the way).

FDEs iterate hypothesis testing in the field from the stage where specifications are still vague, searching for the optimal solution. The process of advancing implementation while running hypothesis testing in short cycles is the key to AI project success.

Why FDEs are needed now for AI adoption and AI implementation

With AI investment accelerating today, FDEs play a strategically important role in successfully leveraging AI to drive corporate growth.

1. Preventing "unused AI" and maximizing ROI

The biggest risk in AI adoption is that, despite investments on the scale of tens of millions of yen, the AI ends up unused in the field. FDEs immediately reflect the voices of field users, improving UI and UX (user experience) on the spot.

"Input is cumbersome," "notifications are slow," "the screen is hard to read"

These small complaints hinder AI adoption. FDEs resolve them quickly, evolving AI into something the field actually uses.

2. Solving data quality issues in the field

AI accuracy depends on data. In reality, however, core system data often does not accurately reflect actual field conditions. FDEs verify the data generation process in the field (the departments that actually use it) and, when necessary, redesign the collection methods and input flows themselves. This prevents the data pitfalls that cause AI projects to fail.

3. Shortening lead time from decision-making to implementation

In today's rapidly changing market environment, the speed of AI utilization directly translates into competitive advantage. Having an FDE shortens the cycle of

field requests → implementation → testing → improvement

from weeks to days, and sometimes to hours. This decision-making speed is what determines the success or failure of DX initiatives.

Three essential qualities for FDEs who make AI projects succeed

So what skill set does an FDE need to deliver results? Let's organize it into three points.

1. Technical problem-solving ability (hard skills)

FDEs need not only proficiency in specific languages and tools but also cross-disciplinary understanding spanning data science, cloud infrastructure, API integration, and front-end development. In AI projects, situations that don't go as planned—existing system constraints, inconsistent data structures, and the like—are the norm. Within that context, the implementation ability to connect and run things while leveraging the existing environment and deliver results is essential. Rather than merely talking about design and vision, the "implementation follow-through" to turn ideas into working systems and bring them to a state where the field can actually use them is the core value of an FDE.

② Understanding of business design: interest in domain knowledge (industry knowledge)

The success of AI adoption is directly tied to the depth of business understanding. For example, in manufacturing, you need to understand everything from production line processes and quality control standards to the judgment criteria that experienced operators value. These prerequisites and constraints differ greatly from industry to industry.

An FDE is not merely a technician, but someone who bridges technology and business. They must understand specialized terminology, engage in dialogue using the language of the front lines, and take an approach of structurally grasping business processes. Rather than imposing technology, an attitude of respecting the business is what earns the trust of the front lines and is the key to making AI stick.

③ Adaptability to situations (soft skills)

In AI implementation work, unexpected events such as requirement changes or data shortages occur daily. Projects that start with fully finalized specifications may actually be rare. An FDE must accept uncertain conditions as a given and find the essential issues within them. When facing chaotic situations, the flexibility and persistence to run hypothesis-testing cycles quickly and iterate on improvements are indispensable. A mindset that treats uncertainty not as a risk to avoid but as an opportunity to create value is what leads AI projects to success.

Summary: Making AI a real asset in the field

AI adoption is not a goal in itself. AI can only be called a "success" once it changes business operations, lowers costs, or increases sales. Will AI adoption end as an "experiment," or will it be transformed into a "competitive advantage"? The turning point is implementation capability that produces results in the field, along with continuous improvement.
