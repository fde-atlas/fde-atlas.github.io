FDE as a Service: How Forward Deployed Engineers Differ from Consulting

Original title: FDE 即服务：前线部署工程师与咨询有何区别

FDE Times · 2026-10-24 · 5 min read

Summary

Key Takeaways

  • A.Team rents out FDEs per engagement, while a16z and N47 warn that many FDE companies are quietly sliding toward becoming consultancies.
  • The difference between the two is not how many engineers are on-site, but whether each deployment makes the next one faster and cheaper.
  • For engineers, the value of this job lies in bringing things back to the platform, not just completing the last mile for a single customer.

Now, FDEs can be rented like cloud resources. A.Team provides senior, customer-facing engineers and places them at software vendors' customers, working under the vendor's name to handle the last mile.

The pitch is simple: sign the deal, send in a dedicated engineer, and pull them out once the work is done.

a16z, meanwhile, examines the FDE model from the risk side. The firm writes that the mistake is not doing services, but being a services company with an attached platform while presenting yourself as SaaS.

Put the two views together, and a question emerges that every FDE should think about early.

The same work—being on-site with customers, connecting data, iterating until the system runs—can produce an increasingly strong product, or leave behind a string of projects no one can maintain.

Which side you land on largely determines what this career ultimately leaves you with.

Why is "services" such a sensitive word?

First, gross margin. According to N47, traditional services companies have gross margins of 30-40%, while software companies can reach 70-80% or higher.

Palantir is often cited as proof that both can coexist. N47 points out that despite heavy on-site deployment, Palantir's gross margin remains above 80%, at software-company levels.

a16z's article cites an Everest report to explain why: unlike consultancies, at Palantir services are a means of getting customers onto the product, not the primary revenue source.

But this model is not cheap. Compound's business model database quotes a former Palantir CFO: seven-figure contracts can sustain this model, while eight- and nine-figure ones have plenty of room to spare.

So startups still signing small deals should think twice before copying it.

What do you get from copying only half?

The easiest part of Palantir to imitate is sending engineers on-site with customers; the hard part is the platform behind it. a16z warns that companies that only copy the on-site engineers will end up with thousands of custom deployments that cannot be maintained or upgraded.

N47 puts the same point differently: without a platform designed for composition and reuse, FDE deployments become expensive consulting projects. It also predicts that many companies trying the FDE model will slide into consultancies wearing software-company clothing.

Another slide mechanism hides in organizational structure. a16z argues that once FDEs are separated from product teams, the company loses its feedback loop and gradually becomes a pure services business. The engineers remain excellent and the customers remain satisfied, but what is learned on-site never makes it back into shared code.

The real test: is the next one cheaper?

Gross margin is the number CFOs care about. Engineers have a simpler test, which N47 states clearly: every deployment should make subsequent deployments faster and cheaper.

Suppose you are an FDE at an AI startup that sells an invoice-processing agent. The first customer uses a legacy ERP, and you spend weeks writing connectors, normalizing data fields, and building an eval set for their invoice templates. When a second customer in the same industry shows up, there are two possible scenarios.

The first: the connectors and eval set stay in customer A's codebase, with column names all hardcoded. Customer B has to start from scratch, and the company is essentially selling your hours.

The second: you have turned the connectors into configurable modules and migrated the eval set into the platform. Customer B only needs a new mapping file, and your time goes to what is genuinely unique about them.

Rocketlane offers a practical line you can use to classify everything you write: if a solution is only useful to one customer, it is custom. Custom work is not wrong in itself—every customer needs a little; the problem is when nearly everything you do falls into that category.

Three models, three fates for your work

The following three operating models differ greatly, even though hiring for all of them may use the same FDE title.

Main riskRequires contracts large enough to cover the costsKnowledge walks out the door with peopleThousands of deployments that cannot be upgraded

The middle column deserves the closest reading. A.Team's model solves a real problem: when the sales pipeline is unpredictable, vendors are reluctant to maintain a deployment team long-term.

But once the project ends and the engineer leaves, the question of who brings the experience back into the product falls entirely on the vendor. Hiring someone for the last mile is not the same as hiring a feedback loop.

Rocketlane also notes that there is no single right way to organize FDE work. If the vendor treats rented engineers as input to the roadmap, the rental model can work well; otherwise, it easily becomes a shortcut to the third column.

What does this mean for how engineers work?

If you are an FDE, treat the share of reusable code as your own metric.

At the end of every deployment, ask yourself: what should go back to the platform, what is reasonable customization, and what was hastily written for one customer that should have been built for reuse.

The third question is the hardest to answer, yet the one that helps you improve fastest.

If you are a rented FDE, or work at a small company whose platform is still immature, this discipline matters even more. You leave the project earlier than anyone else, so handover documentation, cleanly bounded modules, and notes written for the product team are your only evidence of leaving behind anything other than a working system.

In interviews, don't just ask how long you'll be on-site. Ask who FDEs report to, how on-site experience feeds into the roadmap, and whether any feature was born from a deployment.

Companies that answer these questions vaguely are likely sitting in the third column of the table above.

On your resume, show that your work compounds across customers, rather than just writing "completed a deployment for customer X."

You can write: you turned a connector developed for the first customer into a module that later deployments all reuse; or that a requirement you brought back became a product feature.

Descriptions like these are exactly the proof that you passed N47's "next one is cheaper" test.

When FDEs can be rented as a resource per engagement, being on-site with customers is no longer anyone's unique advantage. What truly sets you apart is evidence like this: every time you go, the company's next deployment gets a little easier.
