How FDEs Work

learn.netgramlabs.com · 2 min read

Summary

This beginner tutorial explains the typical FDE workflow as a repeating loop: discover, scope, build, demo and iterate, hand off or embed, then feed product gaps back internally. It lists six core responsibilities—technical discovery, prototyping, integration, enablement, internal advocacy, and risk management—noting duties shift with company size. A worked example shows an FDE summarizing support tickets for a logistics company, starting with 500 tickets from one region, fixing date-parsing issues after a demo, and reporting the noisy-date gap to the product team. Key advice: scope the first deliverable to days, not weeks.

Key points

  • FDE engagements follow a repeating loop: discover, scope, build, demo, hand off, feedback.
  • Six core duties: discovery, prototyping, integration, enablement, internal advocacy, risk management.
  • Responsibilities vary by company size—startups do all six, large firms specialize.
  • The logistics example scoped a first win of 500 tickets from one region.
  • Best practice: deliver something small and working within days to build trust.

Theory is useful, but FDE is a doing job. This tutorial walks through the typical workflow, the responsibilities you'll actually own, and a concrete real-world-style project so the abstract becomes concrete.

Most FDE engagements follow the same shape, repeated per customer:

  • Discover — Understand the customer's data, systems, and goal. What are they trying to do today, and why is it painful?
  • Scope — Agree on a concrete first win. Small, demonstrable, real.
  • Build — Write the integration, pipeline, or prototype in their environment.
  • Demo & iterate — Show it working, collect reactions, adjust.
  • Hand off or embed — Either transition to the customer's team / core product, or keep iterating.
  • Feedback — Send product gaps back internally so the next customer is easier.

The loop from discover to feedback is the heartbeat of the job. You rarely do it once; you do it continuously across accounts.

Day to day, FDEs own a predictable set of duties:

  • Technical discovery: Mapping the customer's stack and constraints before proposing anything.
  • Prototyping: Building fast, working proofs that the product fits their case.
  • Integration work: Connecting the product to the customer's databases, APIs, and tools.
  • Enablement: Teaching the customer's team to use and maintain what was built.
  • Internal advocacy: Representing the customer in product and engineering conversations.
  • Risk management: Flagging when a request is out of scope or a bad idea.

Note: Responsibilities shift with company size. At a startup you may do all six daily. At a large company, you may specialize in just integration or just discovery.

Situation: A logistics company wants to use an AI platform to summarize thousands of daily support tickets and route them. The product can summarize text, but the customer's tickets live in a legacy system with inconsistent formats.

What the FDE did:

  • Discover: Found the tickets were in a custom SQL database with free-text fields, often missing labels.
  • Scope: Agreed on a first win — summarize the top 500 tickets from one region.
  • Build: Wrote a Python script to pull, clean, and normalize the tickets, then fed them to the platform's API in batches.
  • Demo: Showed the customer auto-generated summaries; they noticed dates were being misread.
  • Iterate: Added a date-parsing fix and a confidence flag for low-quality tickets.
  • Feedback: Told the product team the platform needed better handling of noisy dates — a gap every customer would hit.

Why this works as an example: It shows the full loop — messy real data, a small scoped win, a live demo, a fix driven by customer reaction, and a product insight returned home. That is FDE work in miniature.

Best Practice: Always scope the first deliverable small enough to finish in days, not weeks. A fast, working "something" builds trust and teaches you more than a perfect plan.
