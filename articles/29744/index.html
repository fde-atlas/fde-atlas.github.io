Chapter 0: One-Page Conclusions

0.1 Answers to Five Questions

Question | One-Sentence Conclusion

What is an FDE, and why did they appear en masse in 2026?

It is not a new job title but a "delivery model" — compressing pre-sales, implementation, product, and customer success into a small frontline team that is accountable for business outcomes. The reason for the concentrated emergence is that model capability now outstrips organizational absorption capacity: tools have become cheap, while turning tools into outcomes has become more expensive.

How large is the real gap in the Chinese market?

On the demand side, a three-track estimate — "400,000 large-scale manufacturers + 1% of 52 million SMEs + OPC city service providers" — totals over 1 million people; the supply side is under 5,000, a gap ratio of roughly 200:1. ⚠ This is an estimation model, not a census (see §3.1 and Appendix C).

Why is manufacturing the main battlefield?

Five constraints stack up (extremely low fault tolerance, scarce data, physics that cannot be ignored, complex on-site conditions, and clear ROI requirements), causing "remote delivery" to fail here first; meanwhile, manufacturing offers the strongest verifiability — yield, energy consumption, downtime, and turnover are all ready-made metrics.

What are the obstacles, and which cannot be solved short-term?

Of the six major obstacles, delivery standardization and data/knowledge sovereignty are structural: the former requires an "asset accumulation" mechanism, the latter requires contractual and governance mechanisms — neither can be solved by buying a set of tools.

What is most likely to happen in three years?

Three certainties: ① FDE goes from "an individual company's playbook" to "the default move for enterprise AI sales"; ② platform vendors productize and externalize their FDE capabilities, while third-party service providers retreat into vertical industries; ③ delivery pricing migrates from "person-months" to "outcome/incremental revenue sharing."

0.2 Five Judgments for Readers (Ready to Use As-Is)

  • To judge whether an AI project will stall at the pilot stage: check whether it has the four essentials — "acceptance action + Owner + fallback plan + people using it daily." Missing two or more means it will most likely stall.
  • To judge whether a service provider is a true FDE: look at what the client has in hand after the project ends. Only a system = outsourcing; connectors/eval sets/templates + people who can take over = a delivery model.
  • To judge whether a scenario is worth doing first: measurable, bounded, with permissions, high-frequency, and acceptance-testable. If any one of the five is zero, fix the conditions first.
  • To judge whether a knowledge base is usable: ask a question only the new version can answer, ask a question involving cross-document conflicts, and ask a question with no answer. If any of the three responses is fabricated, it cannot go to production.
  • To judge whether a pricing structure is healthy: if revenue comes entirely from first-delivery engagements and delivery depends on a single person, the business will collapse on the second delivery.

# Chapter 1: Origins and Definition

## 1.1 Origins: A Model Grown from the Intelligence Front Line

Palantir was founded in 2003, and its first customers were the CIA, the FBI, and military intelligence agencies. The needs of such customers are written in classified documents and their processes are hidden behind security clearances — software could not possibly be delivered via standard requirements documents plus remote meetings. Palantir's solution was to send engineers directly to the customer site for long-term embedding, working side by side with analysts: analyzing workflows on site, identifying bottlenecks, developing in place, and iterating quickly [A25, same source, see Scene Society 2026 p10].

2004–2008 was the prototyping period for this model. The line later quoted again and again was: the biggest problems cannot be understood from the comfortable environment of headquarters.

## 1.2 Platform Evolution: Why On-Site Embedding Didn't Become "Warm-Body Outsourcing"

When many people first hear about FDE, they worry about the same thing: isn't this just stuffing consultants into the customer's site, getting heavier and heavier, and eventually becoming labor outsourcing?

Palantir spent twenty years answering this challenge — not by making people cheaper, but by making the platform one layer thicker with every delivery:

Stage | Year | Key Move | Impact on Delivery Cost

Gotham | 2004–2008 | Data integration and ontology modeling for intelligence and defense | Data structures reused from the second delivery to similar customers

Foundry | 2016 | Productized the ontology layer, extended to commercial enterprises | Delivery shifted from "writing code" to "configuring the ontology"

Apollo | 2020 | Continuous delivery and automated deployment across environments | On-site embedding no longer equals warm-body ops

AIP | 2023 | Added an AI capability layer on top of the platform | Prototyping cycles compressed from months to weeks or even days

This path shows: the FDE model can only work if "the delivery process itself produces assets." Without that layer, FDE degenerates into expensive on-site staffing.

### 1.2.1 Ontology: The Technical Foundation of FDE, and Its Reconstruction in China

"Ontology" is the layer most easily skipped in this model, yet the one that most determines long-term cost. Its definition is simple: modeling business objects, the relationships between objects, and the rules acting on objects into a computable structure.

Take the power grid as an example: transformers, cables, circuits, and towers are "objects"; supply relationships and upstream/downstream links are "relationships"; maintenance procedures and outage rules are "rules." Once assembled, the model doesn't need to re-understand the site each time — it answers questions on the same structure [A15].

The impact of this step on delivery cost is compounding:

With or Without Ontology Layer | 1st Delivery | 2nd Delivery | 5th Delivery

Without | Full business understanding | Re-understanding (starting over with each new customer) | Still starting over

With | 20–30% extra time on modeling | 50%+ reused; only differences handled

Three differences in the Chinese-style reconstruction (this report's judgment, for discussion):

  • Objects are more fragmented and less standardized: domestic small and mid-sized manufacturers' records often live in Excel and WeChat, so "finding the objects" must come before modeling;
  • Rules live in veteran workers' heads: procedure documents don't match actual practice, so the ontology must be corrected through interviews;
  • Private deployment first: data never leaving the factory is a hard constraint, so the ontology layer must support local deployment.

Conclusion: whether or not you build the ontology layer determines whether you are "an outsourcer starting over every time" or "a service provider that gets lighter with each engagement." This is the cost this report considers most worth investing in — and the one most easily omitted.

2.2 What the failure rate really means (and how to cite it correctly)

The most widely circulated figure is "95%." Its original source is MIT NANDA's "The GenAI Divide" (August 2025): roughly 95% of enterprise generative AI pilots produced no measurable P&L impact; another paraphrasing states "only 5% of 300 public cases successfully scaled" 〔B6〕.

There are three rules for citing this figure:

  • It describes "pilots," not "all AI projects";
  • Different phrasings from the same source (95% vs 5%) are two sides of the same study and cannot be added together into "100% fail";
  • The study's sample and evaluation methodology are disputed in the industry (a16z and others have publicly questioned it), so it should only be used as a trend reference.

More decision-relevant than "95%" is another set of data: Gartner warns that about 40% of agentic AI projects may be canceled by the end of 2027, due to escalating costs, unclear business value, and inadequate risk controls 〔B7〕.

The value of this warning is that it names three reasons for cancellation, and those three reasons are exactly the problems the FDE model is meant to solve: cost (runaway usage-based spending), value (can't articulate it), and risk (afraid to go to production).

6.3 Why "One-Shot Deals" Are Bound to Fail

The typical death of industrial AI is: project acceptance, system go-live, team withdrawal, and six months later an open rate below 10%.

The chain goes like this: acceptance by functionality → no one is accountable for usage → frontline staff keep using the old methods → data doesn't flow back → the model stops improving → complete abandonment.

The breakpoint isn't technical—it's the acceptance method. Once the acceptance criterion is "feature launched," the vendor's incentive is to launch as quickly as possible; but if the acceptance criterion is defined as "someone uses it in a real workflow for N consecutive weeks, with metrics met," the vendor must stay alongside the client until it is actually "in use."

This is also the easiest and yet hardest part of the FDE model to learn: changing the acceptance criterion is just one sentence; being willing to take responsibility for "getting it into use" is the real threshold.

6.5 Structural vs. Cyclical Barriers: Don't Wait at the Wrong Time

Of the six barriers, only two are structural—they will not ease as models become cheaper:

Type | Barrier | Why it won't ease on its own | What it depends on

Structural | Delivery standardization | Every client's workflows, data, and permissions are different; "replicability" must be carried by assets, and assets can only be accumulated by people | Mechanism: mandatorily deposit at least one asset per engagement

Structural | Data and knowledge sovereignty | Clients will increasingly care about "who owns what is learned on-site"; this is a negotiation issue, not a technical one | Contract: the SOW clearly defines ownership and reuse boundaries

Cyclical | Talent supply | Training and industry-education supply are increasing; clear improvement within 2–3 years | Time + project density

Cyclical | Client awareness | As peer case studies emerge, awareness will naturally rise | Time + reference clients

Cyclical | Ability to pay | Strongly correlated with macroeconomic conditions, and policy subsidies are expanding coverage [A24] | Policy + installment structures

Semi-structural | Organizational inertia | Redistributing "who is responsible" always faces resistance, but it can be bypassed by choosing the right first department | Site-selection strategy

The practical use of this distinction: if a service provider treats "can't hire people" as its main bottleneck, it will most likely discover two years later that the bottleneck has become "no assets"—because cyclical barriers ease on their own, while structural ones do not.

Chapter 7 Three-Year Judgment (2026–2029)

This chapter presents judgments, not predictions. Each one specifies "what signal you would see first if the judgment is wrong."

7.1 Three Certain Trends

Trend 1: FDE goes from "an individual company's playbook" to "the default move in enterprise-grade AI sales"

Evidence chain: Big 4 consulting firms, cloud providers, and traditional enterprise software companies have successively built FDE teams; OpenAI, Anthropic, and Microsoft are investing at the billion-dollar scale to build deployment organizations [A6] [A7] [A8].

Contrarian signal: If in 2027 major players lay off deployment teams or fold FDE into ordinary pre-sales, this trend should be downgraded.

Trend 2: Platform vendors productize FDE capabilities for external output; third parties retreat to vertical industries

Guosen Securities' judgment is that "SaaS shifts from selling dashboards to selling outcomes" [A13]; Xiaguang Think Tank judges that China will follow a "internal validation → external output" path, and after 2027 Volcano Engine, Alibaba Cloud, and others will productize FDE capabilities [Xiaguang 2026 p14].

The implication for third-party service providers is direct: generic scenarios will be eaten by platforms; what remains is industry know-how and on-site relationships.

Contrarian signal: If platforms turn industry delivery into standardized products as well (e.g., complete solutions for specific processes), third parties' room will be squeezed further.

Trend 3: Pricing shifts from "person-months" to "outcomes/incremental revenue sharing"

Domestic practice already exists [A19]. The driver is client-side ROI pressure (only 25% of projects achieve expected ROI [B8]).

Contrarian signal: If contract disputes over incremental revenue sharing increase (unclear baseline determination), the industry will retreat to an intermediate state of "project-based + strict acceptance."

7.4 The Fusion of FDE and OPC (One-Person Company)

Background from Alibaba Cloud's "OPC Development Practice Report in the AI Era": nationwide, there are over 16 million business entities registered as single-shareholder limited liability companies

households, with 2.86 million newly registered in the first half of 2026 (up 47% year over year); 23 provinces (municipalities/regions) have already issued OPC cultivation measures (source: Alibaba Cloud - AI Era OPC Development Practice Report 2026.pdf, p2).

This means FDE capability now has an individualized outlet: a one-person company + AI tools + a small number of external experts can take on lightweight delivery that previously only a team could handle.

The boundaries must be made clear:

What can be taken on: single-scenario work with clear boundaries that does not require cross-system integration;

What cannot: work requiring multi-department coordination, long-term on-call coverage, or accountability for production incidents.

What this means for individuals: FDE does not necessarily mean joining a company; it can also be an individual delivery capability—but your capacity to bear delivery responsibility determines how large a contract you can take on.

  • Betting on "asset-based delivery": every delivery must leave behind connectors/templates/eval sets; otherwise the second delivery starts from scratch again (the "2 months → 1 day" in §5.3 is the only verifiable metric);
  • Not betting on "general-purpose tools": general capabilities will be swallowed by platforms—this is not a matter of skill, but of structure.

Chapter 8: Advice for Five Types of Roles

Role | Main recommendation | First thing to do | What not to do

Business executives | Start with one measurable small scenario; acceptance based on business change | Have finance provide the baseline numbers for this process | Do not start by launching an "AI factory"-scale project

Internal implementation leads | Build evidence along L1→L4; address blockers first | Fill in the four evidence cards, honestly writing "to be verified" where needed | Do not substitute "completed four courses" for "delivered one scenario"

Individual career changers | Use existing engineering skills to enter one business scenario; prove L2 first | Run one real process end to end and leave behind a failure sample set | Do not start with "learn first, do later"—do it while filling gaps

Service providers/partners | Deliver and accumulate: leave behind connectors/eval sets/templates every time | List the assets from your last delivery and see how much can be reused | Do not sell work that requires accumulation on a per-person-day basis

Parks/associations | Combine industry and education + a city service provider network | Use the "full diagnostic coverage" policy to build the first batch of enterprise lists | Do not run only training without issuing projects

Below, each role is expanded into "situation → three actions → judgment criteria → common mistakes."

8.1 Business executives (owners / VPs in charge)

Situation: surrounded by peer case studies and vendor proposals, with limited budget, yet afraid of falling behind by missing out. The real difficulty is not "whether to do it" but "which one to do first and how to judge it was done right."

Three actions

  • Calculate the baseline before discussing solutions: have finance provide the real current numbers for this process (hours/month, error rate, rework count). A project without a baseline cannot prove its value even after completion.
  • Appoint one person, not form a committee: a committee means no one is responsible. The appointed person must have the authority to access data and change processes.
  • Write acceptance into the contract: "someone has used it in the real process for N consecutive weeks and the metrics are met," not "system launched."

Judgment criteria: three months later, you can answer "how many hours per month does this process now save." If you cannot answer, it was not done successfully.

Common mistakes: launching three scenarios at once (and abandoning all three halfway); using "employee satisfaction" instead of business metrics; putting the entire budget into platform procurement with nothing left for accompaniment and adaptation.

8.2 Internal implementation leads (may be IT managers, business backbone staff, or newly appointed "AI leads")

Situation: given the mandate to "drive AI," but with no team, no budget authority, and no clear acceptance criteria. The biggest risk is being busy for six months and being unable to articulate what you did.

Three actions

  • Recognize only one scenario: screen with the five criteria, pick the one that is "within your authority + verifiable," and put the other candidates on a "not doing" list.

  • Work by the four evidence cards: business consulting, architecture orchestration, AI debugging and evals, delivery operations—each must have supporting material; where there is none, write "to be verified."
  • Build the failure sample set first: 20 real questions covering normal/boundary/conflict/no-answer cases—this is the only thing that proves "I know when it will fail."

Judgment criteria: launchable by day 30, knowing where it fails by day 60, and handover-capable by day 90. Achieve the three in order; do not skip.

Common mistakes: treating "launch" as the result; accepting every requirement (and closing the loop on none); writing code but no documentation (once you leave, no one can take over).

8.3 Individual career changers (developers / product managers / industry professionals)

Situation: you have technical skills but have never done work "accountable for business outcomes." On your resume you cannot state "what business results I delivered"—you can only list your tech stack.

Three actions

  • Find a real process in an industry you know well (even a friend's company), run it end to end once, using anonymized data.
  • Leave behind three things: a runnable repository, a failure sample set, and an operations manual—these three are your portfolio, more powerful than certificates.
  • Self-assess along L1→L4 and fix blockers first: inability to protect data, inability to explain key code, no one accountable for release rollback—these three issues take priority over any skill learning.

Judgment criteria: can another person, looking only at your documentation, run it end to end once? Every place they get stuck is a gap in your work.

Common mistakes: treating "learn first, do later" as the path (you will never finish learning); using a demo as delivery (a demo proves "it's understandable," not "someone wants it"); writing code without documenting boundaries (if you cannot state what it does not do, no one will dare use it).

8.4 Service providers / partners

Situation: you can win contracts, but the work gets more exhausting: every client feels like the first, revenue grows linearly with headcount, and the project is at risk the moment a core engineer leaves.

Three actions

  • Force every delivery to leave behind one asset: a connector / template / eval set—choose one of the three, put it in the delivery checklist, and acceptance is not complete without it.
  • Put "capability transfer" into your pricing: a client buying "can take over" and buying "can run" are two different prices; the difference is the value of assets and methodology.
  • Establish delivery SOPs and gates: you can reference the industry's existing "iron triangle + G1–G9 quality gates" (diagnosis review → project kickoff with four-party sign-off → solution review → data readiness → launch approval → trial-run acceptance → value confirmation → capability transfer → retrospective archiving) [A26].

Judgment criteria: whether the hours for the second delivery of the same type drop significantly. If not, the first delivery left nothing behind.

Common mistakes: selling work that requires accumulation on a per-person-day basis (the more you sell, the more you lose); promising results to win contracts; keeping assets on personal computers instead of in the team repository.

Situation: you hold enterprise lists and policy mandates, but lack "people who can implement." Organizing training is easy; organizing delivery is hard.
