Outcome-Based AI Agents: How FDEs Define "Resolved"

Original title: 按结果计费的 AI Agent：FDE 如何定义“已解决”

FDE Times · 2026-10-09 · 6 min read

Summary

This article examines outcome-based pricing for AI agents and the dispute over what counts as "resolved." Sierra charges only when an agent completes a task, while Intercom's Fin charges $0.99 per outcome and counts customer silence as resolution, which drew a complaint on its community forum. The author argues that defining, measuring, and improving outcomes is the FDE's job: each point of resolution rate directly becomes revenue, and inflated definitions erode trust. It compares per-seat versus outcome pricing, cites Sequoia's three conditions for outcome pricing, and offers job-hunting advice for aspiring FDEs.

Key points

  • Sierra charges only when agents complete tasks; Fin costs $0.99 per outcome.
  • Intercom counts silence as resolution, prompting customer complaints.
  • Fin reportedly hit 56% average resolution in its first 30 days per an AWS case study.
  • Sequoia's three conditions for outcome pricing all fall to FDEs.
  • Candidates should explain how they defined, measured, and corrected outcome metrics.

Key takeaways

  • Sierra only charges when its agent completes a task. Intercom's Fin charges $0.99 per outcome.
  • The definition of "resolved" is genuinely contested: one Intercom customer objected to Fin getting credit when a human agent gave the final reply.
  • Sequoia lays out three conditions: define success for each customer, measure outcomes reliably, and deliver outcomes consistently. All three fall to the FDE.

Sierra, founded by Bret Taylor, has an explicit rule: the company only gets paid when its agent completes a task for the customer. Intercom prices Fin at $0.99 per outcome, not per seat and not per token.

Yet on Intercom's own community forum, a customer wrote that as long as a human agent replies to the customer after Fin, the company should stop defaulting to Fin having resolved the issue.

These three pieces of information point to the same thing. Money only comes in when a problem is solved, so what counts as "resolved" determines revenue. The people who answer that question, measure it, and push the number up are the engineers working alongside the customer.

If you want to move into a Forward Deployed Engineer (FDE) role, this is worth truly understanding. When customers pay per seat, an engineer's work can end on delivery day. When customers pay per outcome, how the engineer deploys and runs the agent directly affects how much money the vendor can collect.

"Resolved" is a contract term, not a feeling

Sierra defines outcomes as business events: a resolved support conversation, a saved cancellation, an upsell, a cross-sell. Unresolved conversations mostly go unbilled. That sounds fair, until someone has to write code to count it.

Intercom's definition shows where the difficulty lies. An outcome can be counted when a customer completes a certain workflow, or when a customer asks no further questions after Fin's answer. In other words, silence counts as resolution. That's an assumption, and every assumption eventually meets a customer who objects.

The complaint on Intercom's forum is a textbook example. Imagine a concrete scenario: a customer asks about a refund, Fin gives an answer, and then an employee steps in because Fin's answer wasn't enough. The system logs a Fin resolution, while the customer's support team sees it as paying for work their own staff completed.

Manny Medina pointed out this ambiguity in a Sequoia analysis. Is the outcome handle time? A follow-up purchase after the customer closes the ticket? CSAT? Or NPS? Each answer implies a different measurement pipeline, and the choice must be made at the deployment stage, not left on a sales slide.

Every percentage point of resolution rate is revenue

An AWS case study on Intercom and Anthropic notes that Fin achieved an average resolution rate of 56% in the first 30 days after deployment. For a vendor billing by outcome, that number is no longer just a quality metric—it's a revenue lever.

Do a back-of-envelope calculation. A customer with 10,000 conversations per month at 56% means 5,600 billable outcomes.

If an engineer identifies three categories of questions the agent frequently gets wrong, fixes the knowledge base and tool-calling flows, and raises the resolution rate to 60%, the vendor gains 400 extra outcomes per month without signing a single new contract.

The math works in reverse too. If the outcome definition is too loose—for example, counting every silent customer as resolved—the dashboard looks great, but customer trust erodes, as the forum complaint illustrates.

So a good FDE doesn't push the number up at all costs. First make the number honest, then improve it.

Sierra also makes clear that the work doesn't end at launch. The company writes that after go-live, it continues targeted, directed optimization so the agent's performance improves over time. In an outcome-based billing model, it's the revenue from those outcomes that pays for this work.

All three of Sequoia's conditions are FDE work

Sequoia analyzed how pricing models for agentic AI companies are maturing and listed the conditions that must be met before charging by outcome. One is clearly defining what success means for each customer; the other two are measuring outcomes reliably and delivering outcomes consistently.

Note the words "each customer." A telecom company may view retaining users about to cancel as the most important outcome, while an e-commerce platform cares about how many returns can be processed without human involvement. No single definition fits everyone, so someone has to sit down with each customer and agree on their own definition.

Reliable measurement is also real engineering work. You need to determine which event in the customer's system marks a case as complete, handle cases where employees step in, and decide how long silence must last to count. These decisions should go to people who can both read agent logs and understand the customer's operations.

Putting the old and new pricing models side by side shows how FDE work changes. The table below is derived from the facts above and does not describe any specific company.

Why Sierra calls these people agent engineers

Sierra's Natalie Meurer believes the best way to define the FDE is by their accountability to the customer, not by role shape or type of work.

Even so, Sierra deliberately named the role "agent engineer." The company explains that the title should reflect the technical nature of the work, not just dedication to the customer.

Both ideas align with the pricing model. According to ZenML's summary of a Sierra talk, these engineers' responsibilities are tied to concrete outcomes, such as a completed sale or a resolved customer request. And those are exactly the things that appear on the invoice.

Bret Taylor's advice to vendors captures the required mindset: face the customer as a partner and help them solve pressing business problems. In an outcome model, that's not just a slogan. If the customer's problem isn't solved, the vendor has no revenue.

If you want this job, what to practice

The scarcest skill here isn't writing prompts—it's turning vague business goals into measurable definitions, then defending that definition in front of both the customer's operations team and your own sales team. Developers who have worked on support systems, CRM, or analytics pipelines already have half the foundation.

When reading job descriptions, watch for phrases like "resolution rate," "outcome," "success metrics," and "agent engineer." When you see them, ask the employer directly: after launch, is the engineer accountable for the numbers, or just doing integrations?

In interviews, you can also ask: how does the company determine that a case is resolved? And who has the authority to change that definition?

On a resume, a line like "built a customer service chatbot" says almost nothing. You should spell out: how you defined the metrics, which events you used to measure them, how you handled exceptions, and how much the numbers changed.

A candidate who can explain how they discovered the old counting method was inflating results and how they fixed it is far more memorable than one who only shows a high resolution rate.

Outcome-based billing forces vendors to share risk with customers. An engineer who can agree with the customer on what "done" means and keep the counting honest after launch will be the hardest person for a company to replace.
