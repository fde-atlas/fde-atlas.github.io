What does a day in the life of an FDE look like? A close-up look at how they really work in the field

Original title: FDEの1日はどんな流れ？現場のリアルな働き方に密着

FDE総研 · 2026-07-23 · 6 min read

Summary

Based on Palantir's public descriptions of FDE work, this article reconstructs a typical day for a forward deployed engineer: mornings aligning with customers on the real problem, afternoons designing, implementing and testing workflows (including configuring platforms like Gotham to unlock features), and evenings validating with end users and gathering feedback. The author stresses that FDEs own the full cycle from problem definition to field validation, feeding same-day feedback back into design. Job postings from Anthropic (25–50% customer-site visits) and OpenAI's Tokyo role (three office days a week plus domestic travel) show that field presence is inherent to the role, and fully remote FDEs are rare.

Key points

  • An FDE's day cycles between problem alignment, implementation, and on-site validation.
  • Morning dialogue digs past stated requests to the underlying real problem, reducing rework.
  • Palantir counts platform configuration (e.g., Gotham) as part of FDE implementation work.
  • Anthropic postings cite 25–50% customer-site visits; OpenAI Tokyo requires three office days weekly.
  • Fully remote FDE work is uncommon; hybrid arrangements are the norm.

What does a day in the life of an FDE look like?

The FDE way of working is a back-and-forth within a single day between dialogue with customers, design, implementation, and on-site verification. Unlike typical development where you stay at your desk building continuously, an FDE (Forward Deployed Engineer) moves between "building" and "delivering" in short cycles. Rather than quietly completing a single task, the basic approach is to feed insights from the field back into the design the same day.

The following is a representative day reconstructed based on the actual FDE duties published by Palantir (the actual balance varies by project).

MorningAlign on the problem with the customerDefine the true problem
AfternoonWorkflow design, implementation, testingTurn the problem into something that works
EveningVerify and improve with end usersToward a state where it is actually used

What does "cycling between building and delivering" mean?

A defining feature of the FDE is that their work doesn't end with implementation; they consistently own everything from problem definition to on-site verification. The problem defined in the morning is turned into something that works in the afternoon, and delivered to end users in the evening to check their reactions. Because this whole loop closes within a single day, gaps that tend to arise between "builders" and "users" can be fixed in small increments the same day, not the following week. The FDE's position is to take on not just building, but everything up to actual use, as part of their own job.

How does the daily balance change by project?

The weighting of morning, afternoon, and evening is not fixed; it flexes depending on the project phase. In the early stages when the problem is still vague, morning dialogue gets longer; during implementation-heavy periods, afternoon design and testing take up more time. The schedule shown in the table is merely a representative example, a rough guide reconstructed from Palantir's published information. The most accurate understanding is that an actual day takes shape depending on which phase of the customer's project the FDE is involved in.

What happens in the morning? (Aligning on the problem)

The morning is the time to talk with people on the ground and determine what the real problem actually is. An FDE's work doesn't start with building to a given spec; it starts with defining the problem itself. Whether or not ambiguity can be untangled here greatly shapes the day's results.

Why start from the problem rather than the spec?

Because an FDE is not in the position of receiving a spec; they get involved from the point of sketching the outline of the problem together with the customer. What the field says they "want" is, in many cases, a manifestation of the deeper real difficulty behind it. Implementing the surface request as-is can produce something that works but never gets used. It is precisely because the morning dialogue digs down to that underlying problem that the afternoon design stays on target. Not leaving problem definition to others is the core of an FDE's morning.

What changes when ambiguity is untangled?

The more the ambiguity of the problem can be reduced in the morning, the smaller the rework later in the day. If implementation begins without a clear definition of what to build, the evening verification may overturn the whole direction. Conversely, if the language of the problem is aligned with the people on the ground, design, implementation, and verification all move toward the same goal. The morning dialogue is not just a meeting; it is best understood as an investment that determines the precision of the entire day.

What happens in the afternoon? (Design and implementation)

The afternoon is the time to turn the problem defined in the morning into something that actually works. Palantir's published information describes the FDE's daily work as "designing, implementing, and testing workflows" and "unlocking new capabilities through platform configuration (e.g., Gotham)" (Palantir official blog).

What implementations does Palantir cite?

Palantir cites workflow design, implementation, and testing, as well as unlocking new capabilities through platform configuration such as Gotham, as hands-on FDE work. What's important here is that they don't necessarily write everything from scratch. Configuring existing platforms to extract value is also positioned as part of an FDE's implementation. The word "build" includes both writing code and combining infrastructure into usable functionality.

Why does the morning's problem definition pay off?

The afternoon's design and implementation don't go off track because the problem was defined in the morning. The clearer what needs to be solved, the less hesitation in design choices, and the clearer the points to verify through testing. The morning dialogue and afternoon implementation are not separate processes but continuous work advancing the same problem from different angles. That is why FDEs don't lean exclusively toward dialogue or implementation; they cycle through both as part of their own responsibilities.

What happens in the evening? (On-site verification)

The evening is the time to try what was built together with end users, gather feedback, and improve. Palantir expresses the depth of this involvement with the phrase "we actually implement the solution in collaboration with end-users" (same blog).

What does collaboration with end users mean?

Collaboration with end users means not just handing over a solution and calling it done, but building it side by side with the people who will actually use it. As Palantir's wording shows, the FDE stands in a position of "implementing collaboratively." To reach a state where something is genuinely used in the field, it must fit not only the developer's assumptions but also the users' hand movements and business workflows. The evening verification is the final stage for confirming whether what was built fits the field.

How is feedback turned into improvement?

Feedback gathered in the evening is carried back into the next day's morning problem definition and afternoon design. Shortcomings and misalignments revealed by testing in the field become new problems to solve in the next cycle. This is why an FDE's day is not simply a timetable, but a back-and-forth cycle. Improvements that bring things closer to a usable state generate the next round of dialogue and implementation, and daily work continues to be updated in line with the field.

How is it different from a typical development role?

The biggest difference is the distance to the customer's site. FDEs don't just work at their own desk; they embed themselves in the field, so business travel carries more weight. Anthropic's FDE job posting mentions 25-50% customer site visits (Anthropic official), and OpenAI's Tokyo posting assumes three days a week in the office plus domestic travel (OpenAI official).

How much travel is involved?

FDEs tend to visit customer sites frequently depending on the project, spending more time in the field than typical development roles. Anthropic's FDE job posting indicates customer site visits of around 25-50%, and OpenAI's Tokyo posting assumes three days a week in the office plus domestic travel. The numbers vary by posting, but what's common is that "entering the customer's field" is a given. The job listings also make clear that it's not purely desk work.

Can you work fully remotely?

FDEs who work entirely remotely are a minority. Given the nature of embedding into customer sites, a hybrid approach combining in-person and remote work is the norm. Implementation can proceed remotely, but the morning problem alignment and evening field verification benefit greatly from being in person.
