FDE deployment scenario interview: Distyl round

FDE Times · 2026-11-09 · 6 min read

Summary

This guide examines the customer scenario round in Distyl AI's FDE interviews, which candidates tend to underrate but which Distyl weights most heavily. The scenario: the interviewer, playing the client's head of operations, says compliance has blocked sending data to the model one week before go-live. The article argues the winning response takes ownership of the process—drafting documents, scheduling the compliance review—without jumping straight to architecture or blaming the client's systems. It provides a line-by-line practice script showing the right question order (people, then data, then deadlines), and lists common mistakes: equating ownership with having an immediate answer, overcommitting, and asking unordered questions.

Key points

  • Distyl weights the customer scenario round more than any other.
  • Ownership means leading the process, not offering architecture first.
  • Question order matters: who decides, what data, what deadline.
  • Never blame the client's systems, even when they are at fault.
  • Raise risks, trade-offs and a fallback before being asked.

When the interviewer plays a client whose compliance team has blocked go-live, take ownership of the problem, but ask questions before you discuss architecture.

In brief

  • Candidates tend to underrate the customer scenario round, yet Distyl AI weights it more heavily than any other.
  • A strong answer takes on the job of setting up the compliance review process instead of handing the work back to the client, and never blames the client's systems.
  • Taking ownership does not mean producing an answer at once: scope the problem with questions first, then make a proposal.

The onsite is under way when the interviewer’s tone shifts. Now they are the client’s head of operations: “Our compliance team has just blocked sending data to the model. Go-live is next week. What do you propose?”

Many candidates fall back on one of two reflexes. The first pushes the work back to the client: “You’ll need to get that approved on your side, and once it’s cleared we’ll deploy straight away.” The second dives into architecture: mask the data, run the model in-house, add a filtering layer. Either can cost you the offer, each for its own reason.

This guide walks through a third answer: take ownership, but don’t rush to a solution. It is a skill you will need in every client meeting once you are hired, and passing the interview is only the first use for it.

The most neglected round carries the most weight

An analysis of Distyl AI’s FDE hiring process on techinterview.org describes the customer scenario round as the one candidates tend to underrate, and the one Distyl weights most heavily.

The same piece makes a point that should shape how you prepare: the interviewer will not volunteer details, and your score comes from the questions you ask and the order in which you ask them.

FDE Academy’s interview guide sums up what assessors want to learn in simulated client sessions in a single question: do you take ownership, or do you deflect? The Distyl analysis adds that ownership is tested in every round, including following a problem all the way to production rather than stopping at “it works on my machine”.

So don’t prepare for this round as a system design exercise with some role-play attached. It tests whether, when a project gets stuck, you step up to unblock it.

Ownership does not mean having the answer

Two pieces of advice seem to pull in opposite directions. FDE Academy writes that in a compliance-conflict question, the strong answer is to take on setting up the compliance review process rather than pushing responsibility back. The same source warns that jumping straight to a solution will sink you, every time.

In fact they fit together, because what you take on is the process, not yet the solution. You commit to leading the search for an approved path, without claiming to know what that path is before you know who blocked it, which data they blocked and why.

The Distyl analysis makes the same point: strong candidates define the problem clearly before they discuss solutions.

There is one more line not to cross. FDE Academy advises never blaming the client’s systems, even when that is where the problem lies. “Your process is far too slow” may be true, but it will alienate the person across the table and quite possibly cost you marks with the assessor.

One script, line by line

Back to the opening scenario. What follows is a hypothetical practice script, not a transcript of a real interview. Watch the order of the questions: people first, then data, then deadlines.

Candidate: “Thanks for flagging this early. Just so I understand: who on the compliance side made this call, and did they give a clear reason?”

Client: “Lan, in legal. She’s worried about customer data leaving our systems.”

Candidate: “All of the data, or only certain fields? Phone numbers, account numbers, for example?”

Client: “Mainly identifying information.”

Candidate: “And the go-live date next week: who is that commitment to? Could we launch part of it first, or does it have to be everything?”

Only now does the candidate restate the problem and take on the work: “So as I understand it, the sticking point is the identifying fields, not the whole project.

Here’s what I suggest: today I’ll write up a data-flow description showing exactly which field goes where, then ask Lan for 30 minutes so we can agree the list of approval conditions together.

I’ll handle the document and the meeting.”

The closing lines are where the marks are won: “There are two risks I want to raise up front. If we mask the identifying data, quality on some tasks may drop, so we’ll need to measure it again. If Lan needs more time, I’d suggest going live first with the non-sensitive data, so that something is running for real next week.”

Set against the answer that pushes work back, the difference shows up in every sentence, not just in attitude.

Pushing the work back to the client

  • Let me know once you have approval
  • Your process is far too slow
  • Proposes architecture in the first sentence
  • Does not say who owns the next step

Taking ownership

  • Asks who decides, which data is blocked, and who the deadline was promised to
  • Restates the problem for the client to confirm
  • Takes on drafting the document and scheduling the review meeting
  • Raises risks, trade-offs and a fallback up front

Practice the five beats

The first beat is asking questions to scope the problem. Because the interviewer playing the client won’t volunteer information, prepare three sets of questions in advance: who decides, what exactly is in scope, and what the time constraints are. Aced’s guide to the ElevenLabs case study round likewise says assessors want to see you start with customer discovery before you propose anything.

The second beat is restating the problem in your own words, so the client can correct you if you have misunderstood. The third is taking on a specific piece of work, with an owner and a time, instead of a vague promise along the lines of “I’ll help out”.

The fourth beat is naming failure modes and trade-offs before you are asked. According to Aced, in Anthropic’s solution design round candidates must scope the problem themselves, raise failure modes themselves and spell out trade-offs; the “problem ownership” criterion scores precisely whether you do this unprompted.

The final beat is agreeing the next step towards production, because that is where ownership is proven.

Through all five beats, keep your voice steady. Aced describes Cognition’s simulated client call as scoring separately whether you answer clearly and calmly on the call.

The mistakes that sink candidates

The most common mistake is treating “taking ownership” as “having the answer”. Keen to look proactive, candidates propose an architecture in their first sentence and fall straight into the trap FDE Academy warns about. The right order is questions first, then the process, then the solution.

The second mistake is overcommitting: promising to win over legal, or pledging to go live on schedule at any cost. Take on what you control, such as the document, the meeting and the fallback plan, not decisions that belong to someone else.

The third mistake is asking plenty of questions in no particular order. Ten disconnected questions still don’t show that you are scoping the problem, and the Distyl analysis is explicit that question order is scored too.

The skill should show up on your CV as well. When a job description mentions “ownership” or “customer-facing”, look through your experience for a time a project was blocked and you were the one who set up the process to unblock it. Write that line in terms of what you took on, what obstacle you removed and how the system reached production.

In this round, the candidate who gets the offer often isn’t the one with the best answer. It is the one the client, after fifteen minutes, trusts to see things through to the end.
