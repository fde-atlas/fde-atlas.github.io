How to Advance In-House DX Development | 5-Stage Exit Criteria, Team Structure, and How to Prevent Failure

Original title: DX内製化の進め方｜5段階の出口条件と体制・失敗の防ぎ方

FDE総研 · 2026-09-09 · 16 min read

Summary

This FDE Soken article argues that DX insourcing means bringing decision-making authority back in-house, not eliminating outsourcing. It proposes a five-stage process—issue inventory, first production deployment, template standardization, operations handover, and self-running—each judged by exit conditions rather than calendar time. It gives criteria for choosing what to insource versus outsource, compares FDE-style engagement with SIer, consulting, and SES models, and identifies common failure patterns, with understaffed part-time commitment cited as the most frequent cause of failure.

Key points

  • Insourcing is about relocating decisions, not zero outsourcing.
  • Five stages with exit conditions replace time-based milestones.
  • Insourcing vs. outsourcing decided by change frequency and domain knowledge density.
  • Most common failure: members lack at least 50% dedicated capacity.
  • FDE-style support fits stages 2–4 and requires a business-side product owner.

What is DX in-house development, and why is the approach being questioned now?

According to the definition by the FDE Research Institute, DX in-house development is organizational design that brings decision-making from requirements definition through operations back in-house; it does not mean zero outsourcing. Behind this lies the "2025 Cliff" raised by METI's "DX Report" (2018).

In-house development means shifting where decisions reside, not "zero outsourcing"

The essence of in-house development is creating a state where you can make decisions and fix things yourself without waiting for external quotes every time a change occurs.

If in-house development is defined as "having development resources internally," it tends to be reduced to a matter of hiring and headcount. The practical dividing line is not the number of people, but whether you can decide who decides the specifications and who fixes them. Even if code is written externally, if your company holds the business hypotheses and priorities and can judge whether modifications are warranted, the substance of in-house development is achieved. Conversely, even with a dozen or more engineers in-house, if the setup is merely that business departments finalize requirements and hand them over, decision-making remains the same as in the outsourcing era.

Since METI's "DX Report" (2018) raised the "2025 Cliff," discussions on legacy system renewal and talent allocation have continued. The IPA's "DX White Paper" also repeatedly points out that in Japan, IT talent is skewed toward external vendors rather than user companies. The current challenge is that this imbalance persists as external dependence in decision-making.

Why is the "approach" being questioned as of 2026?

There are two reasons: the change in development speed brought by generative AI, and the reality that an outsourcing-first approach cannot keep up with business changes.

Generative AI has increased the speed of code generation, but what increased is only the "speed of building." The speed of deciding what to build and the speed of integrating what is built into business operations depend on how much hands-on work people with business knowledge can do. The lower implementation costs become, the more the bottleneck shifts to business understanding and decision-making. This is why the approach to in-house development is being questioned anew.

What tends to appear first in organizations that adopt generative AI is a state of "able to build but unable to decide." Prototypes come out within the week, yet the decision to put them into production stalls for months. Resolving this asymmetry is becoming the practical purpose of in-house development.

What makes the FDE model different as an option?

The FDE model refers to a way of working where engineers enter the customer's business front lines and take on everything from requirements definition to implementation and operations in the first person.

FDE (Forward Deployed Engineer) is a job concept popularized by Palantir Technologies, characterized by engineers being deployed forward to the business side and handling business understanding and implementation without separating them (for details, see What is an FDE?). While traditional in-house development aimed to "improve the client's requirements definition capability," the FDE model can be summarized as an approach that eliminates the boundary between requirements definition and implementation itself. In the context of DX in-house development, eliminating this boundary directly translates into decision-making speed.

What is the order and sequence of steps for DX in-house development?

The DX in-house development approach recommended by the FDE Research Institute consists of five stages: "inventory → first one → standardization → operations handover → self-sufficiency," with exit criteria set for each stage (for the company-side implementation procedure, also see How to Introduce FDEs).

Overview of the 5 stages and exit criteria

In practice, the 5 stages should not be judged by calendar duration, but by whether the exit criteria for moving to the next stage have been met.

Stages 1–2: Inventory and "the first one"

The purpose of the first two stages is not to produce results, but to leave in-house members with the sense that "we fixed it ourselves."

In stage 1, rank processes by "change frequency" and "density of business knowledge." Trying to map out the entire company's processes here will burn months, so it is realistic to limit the scope to 3–5 processes. For the subject of stage 2, choose one that meets the following three conditions.

  • Business staff can participate: Someone you can secure in regular meetings, even just one hour per week
  • Success or failure is visible in numbers: Existing metrics such as processing time, rework count, or inquiry volume are available
  • Business doesn't die even if it stops: Since the first attempt always brings surprises, choose an area where fallback options remain

The third condition is especially effective. If you choose the heart of your core systems as the first project, fear of failure pushes decision-making back to waiting for external reviews, and no in-house learning occurs.

Stages 3–5: Standardization, operational handover, and self-sufficiency

The latter three stages are the process of turning one success into a repeatable pattern and shifting responsibility for operations and maintenance to your own company.

In stage 3, set a goal of launching the second project at less than half the cost of the first. Template the non-functional parts such as authentication, data connections, deployment, and monitoring, creating a state where things work by writing only the business logic. In stage 4, transfer the operations rotation and incident response procedures in-house. Skipping this cements a half-hearted structure of "in-house builds, outsourced fixes." The criterion for stage 5 can be summed up in a single point: are improvements happening without request tickets?

A checklist to verify which stage your company is at

Determine your company's current position by answering the exit criteria of each stage with "yes/no" and judging at the first stage where the answer is "no."

  • Three to five target business processes have been ranked by impact and frequency of change (Stage 1)
  • One business process is running in production, and an in-house member has completed a modification on their own once (Stage 2)
  • Authentication, data connections, deployment, and monitoring are in a form reusable for the second project (Stage 3)
  • Operations procedures, monitoring, and rotations are in-house, with no cases waiting for external responses during incidents (Stage 4)
  • Improvements originating from business departments are happening monthly without request tickets (Stage 5)

How should in-house development and outsourcing be used appropriately?

FDE Soken's decision criteria are two: "frequency of change" and "density of business knowledge." The principle is to keep in-house only the areas requiring judgment.

The decision criteria are frequency of change and density of business knowledge

In areas with high change frequency and high business knowledge density, outsourcing tends to have back-and-forth confirmation costs that eat away at results.

Outsourcing becomes inefficient not because rates are high, but because back-and-forth confirmations occur. In areas where specifications aren't settled, a single change involves four rounds of estimation, approval, implementation, and acceptance. Continuing this for business processes that change many times a month makes coordination effort larger than implementation effort. As a metric to measure this structure, recording "decision-waiting time per change" makes it easier to judge how to allocate work.

Areas where external procurement is more rational

Areas with stable specifications, such as authentication platforms, monitoring, and generic SaaS integrations, tend to be more rational to procure externally.

Nature of the areaRecommendationReason
Business logic, data definitions, prioritizationIn-houseHigh change frequency, and tacit knowledge directly affects specifications

Common platforms such as authentication, monitoring, CI/CDExternal procurement (SaaS preferred)Specifications are stable, with little room for differentiation through in-house development
Large-scale data migration, temporary loadOutsourcingTime-limited, no need to permanently retain the skills
Specialized areas (regulatory compliance, advanced security)Outsourcing + internal point of contactThe cost of permanently retaining specialized personnel isn't justified

How to design contracts and knowledge transfer

Even when using outsourcing, include documentation and review participation in the deliverables, and make contracts that leave knowledge in-house.

Specifically, write the following three points into the contract.

  • Include documents recording the reasons for design decisions in the deliverables
  • Have in-house members participate in code reviews
  • Make handover meetings for operations procedures an acceptance condition

If only "something that works" is the deliverable, the rationale for decisions remains outside the company at contract end, and in-housing moves back one stage.

How does the approach to in-housing change depending on company size and current structure?

FDE Soken's five stages are common regardless of company size, but where to start depends on whether in-house engineers exist and the degree of outsourcing dependence.

When there are no in-house engineers or outsourcing dependence is strong

For organizations with zero in-house engineers, it's realistic to handle stage 1 alone and partner with external FDE-type talent in stage 2.

The inventory assessment can only be done by in-house members who know the business, so don't hand it to outsiders. In stage 2, pair external FDE-type engineers with in-house business owners, with the exit condition being that the in-house side "completes one modification on their own." The contract must always include the three knowledge-transfer points from the previous chapter. With this approach, even small companies can start without waiting for hiring.

When in-house engineers exist but in-housing isn't progressing

Even with engineers, if the structure merely receives requirements, restart from stage 1 and first place a business-side product owner.

In organizations in this state, the bottleneck is often "who decides" rather than technical skill. Appoint a product owner and send some existing engineers into business operations as FDE types, and you can advance to stage 2 without increasing headcount. If multiple systems are already being built in-house, starting with stage 3's commonalization tends to be more effective.

When the organization is large with many departments

In large companies with many departments, don't roll out company-wide all at once; run the five stages through one department, then horizontally deploy that pattern to other departments.

Assessing all company business simultaneously easily spends several months on stage 1 alone. Advance one department to stage 3 to create a common template, and start the second and subsequent departments from that template. This lets the whole company benefit from the stage 3 goal of "launching the second project at less than half the cost."

How does FDE-type accompaniment differ from SIers, IT consulting, and SES?

FDE Soken's comparison axis is "who makes business decisions and who retains the knowledge"; FDE types differ in that the same person handles both implementation and knowledge transfer.

Organizations poorly suited to FDE-style accompaniment

FDE-style accompaniment is not suited to organizations that cannot place a business-side decision-maker or cannot allocate 50% of capacity to in-house development.

Because the FDE model decides specifications together on-site, if there is no one to make decisions, external talent becomes merely someone who listens to requests. Also, if you want to build a system with stable specifications once and be done with it, contracting or external procurement offers better value for the cost. The model suits organizations that have frequently changing business processes, can appoint a product owner, and can secure internal members' capacity.

How should you design the organization and talent that support in-house development?

The key to the organizational design recommended by the FDE Research Institute is to separate the role of FDE-style engineers who work within the business from the role of maintaining the common platform, eliminating overlapping responsibilities.

The three required roles

An in-house team needs three types of roles: FDE-style engineers embedded in the business, a responsible person who makes decisions, and a person in charge of maintaining the platform.

  • FDE-style engineer: Works permanently within the business site, handling everything from requirements gathering to implementation and modification. The speed of in-housing is largely determined by the headcount and utilization rate of this role
  • Product owner (business side): Decides priorities and what "not to do." If this seat remains vacant, the list of requests grows endlessly
  • Platform manager: Maintains authentication, environments, monitoring, and the data platform. Supports reproducibility from stage 3 onward

When headcount is insufficient in the early stages, combining the FDE-style and platform roles can work temporarily, but combining the product owner role should be avoided. When the decision-maker starts doing hands-on work, decisions get postponed.

Two paths: hiring and development

Rather than relying solely on external hiring, simultaneously open a development path that adds engineering skills to internal staff who know the business.

What FDE-style engineers need is three things: business understanding, implementation skills, and communication skills. External hiring is a path that adds business understanding to people with implementation skills, while internal development is a path that adds implementation skills to people with business understanding. In many cases, the latter gets up to speed faster. Knowledge of the business's key points and internal relationships are assets that are hard to acquire from outside in a short period.

For development, pair work tends to be more effective than taking courses. Using actual business issues as material, a format where two people—an experienced person and a learner—carry one improvement through to completion is the fastest way to make the first project a teaching tool. The procedures for hiring and development are covered in How to conduct FDE hiring and How to conduct FDE development.

How to position evaluation and career paths

Evaluation should be designed to measure not delivery volume, but the magnitude of improvement in business metrics and the volume of assets reused by other departments.

FDE-style engineers tend to produce results that "contributed to the business but are technically unglamorous," and are often undervalued by standard engineer evaluation criteria. Conversely, anxiety arises that the deeper they go into the business, the further they drift from a technical career. Explicitly including "improvement of business metrics" and "reused shared assets" in the evaluation criteria, and placing a promotion track for specialists on par with technical staff, is a practical measure to prevent turnover.

Why does DX in-housing fail, and how can it be prevented?

According to the FDE Research Institute's analysis, DX in-housing failures tend to arise not from lack of technical skill, but from organizational issues such as ambiguous objectives and insufficient staffing (for the implementer's perspective, see Failure patterns in FDE implementation).

Five typical failure patterns

Typical failures can be classified into five categories: unclear objectives, excessive role combination, over-dependence on individuals, stalling at PoC, and neglected operations, each with warning signs that appear early.

Failure patternStage where it occursWarning signs on the groundCountermeasures
Ambiguous objectivesStage 1"DX promotion" remains the goal, with no articulable success metricsSelect one existing metric for the target business and define pass/fail by the magnitude of improvement
Excessive role combinationStage 2In-house members' actual working time stays at 1-2 days per weekSecure at least 50% of capacity. If that is not possible, postpone the start
Stalling at PoCStage 2Verification succeeds but never reaches productionAt the start, finalize the approver and conditions for production migration
Over-dependence on individualsStage 3Systems that only one person can handle increaseMake reviews mandatory, and set another person performing one modification as an exit condition
Neglected operationsStages 4-5Post-launch improvements stop, and operations revert to ExcelIncorporate an operations rotation and a monthly improvement slot into the business side's workload

The most common of these is excessive role combination. Even though budget and approvals are in place, the setup becomes one where the person in charge handles existing duties and works only half a day per week, so learning never accumulates. Rather than technical difficulty, whether capacity can be secured determines success or failure.

The failure where things stop working after external talent leaves has the same root. If the contract ends without confirming the exit conditions of stage 4 (zero external dependencies), knowledge of incident handling remains outside the company. Align the contract end date not with the calendar, but with the point when the stage 4 exit conditions are met.

Detecting problems early with leading indicators

Failures often show up in leading indicators—such as review backlogs or a decrease in questions from business departments—before results emerge.

The four leading indicators to monitor are as follows.

  • In-house members' actual working hours
  • Number of questions and requests from business departments
  • Average number of days reviews are stalled
  • Number of items awaiting external confirmation

Particular caution is needed when indicator 2 starts to decline. A lack of requests is not proof of satisfaction; it often signals that the business side has given up hope.

Decide the criteria for continuing or winding down in advance

It is safest to decide the criteria for continuing or winding down before starting, and to have all stakeholders agree in advance on the downsizing procedure if results do not materialize.

At the end of stage 2, judge three points: ① whether it went into production, ② whether an internal member could make one modification independently, and ③ whether business metrics moved. If all three are unmet, there is a problem either with how the target business was chosen or with securing capacity. Deciding withdrawal criteria in advance avoids the twisted logic of "continuing because we invested." In-housing is not something to complete in one attempt; designing it so you can switch targets and try again raises the probability of long-term success.
