Few companies actually deserve an FDE.

Original title: 没有几家公司，能配得上 FDE

53AI · 2026-08-05 · 2 min read

Summary

The article argues that FDE is not a universal role but an outgrowth of a specific business model: high contract values, heavy integration, and few large customers with long, non-standard delivery cycles. At companies like Palantir and OpenAI, FDEs exist because contracts run for years and are worth millions of dollars. The author warns that many firms are simply renaming on-site teams as FDEs, echoing how operations teams rebranded as SRE without changing their work. This serves managers reporting upward and misleads job seekers into unscalable grunt work. Four tests are proposed: match the role to the business model, avoid copying peers, recognize that renaming is not organizational upgrade, and verify contract size, customer count, and delivery timelines before accepting an FDE title. Full text

Over the past year, the FDE (Forward Deployed Engineer) has completely blown up.

Several leading Silicon Valley AI companies keep talking about FDEs in their hiring, interviews, and podcasts—so much that bosses are getting anxious: should we build an FDE team too?

The FDE wasn't a role designed out of thin air; it was forced into existence by a very particular kind of business.

Whether at Palantir or OpenAI, an FDE usually sits behind a contract worth millions or even tens of millions of dollars (note: in USD) spanning several years. The customer's problems are highly non-standard, and the product requires deep integration; without engineers embedded closely with the customer, it might take half a year just to produce the first genuinely useful result.

In this kind of business, FDEs are a hard requirement—a complement to the business model, not a decoration for organizational upgrading.

But once a role gets popularized by top companies, we already know what happens next.

Back when Google made SRE famous, overnight countless companies renamed their ops departments "SRE departments." The name changed, the prestige went up, but the work stayed the same, the people stayed the same, and salaries didn't rise much either.

Now it's FDE's turn.

The on-site engineers are still on-site, still doing the heaviest, dirtiest, least replicable work—just wearing a trendier vest.

This vest usually serves two purposes.

Internally, managers use it to report to the boss: look, we've completed our organizational upgrade.

Externally, it becomes a beautiful pit dug for job seekers. Lured in by the shiny new title, they eventually discover they've been handed the company's hardest-to-scale grunt work; and after a few years, when they want to job-hop, they don't even know where to go next.

So when it comes to FDEs, there are four things you must think through clearly.

First, the FDE is not a capability upgrade—it's a complement to the business model.

It fits high deal values, heavy integration, few-but-large customers, and long, complex, non-standard delivery. If your business doesn't look like this, there's no foundation for the FDE role to exist.

Second, doing it just because you see peers doing it may look the most diligent, but it's actually the laziest approach.

Because you've quietly outsourced your hardest business judgment to the company next door. Yet what they figured out is their game, not yours.

Third, changing a name is not the same as upgrading the organization.

Ops becomes SRE, on-site becomes FDE—but as long as the underlying business model, delivery approach, and organizational capabilities haven't changed, the so-called upgrade exists only in PowerPoint.

Fourth, if you're a job seeker and you see "FDE," don't be swayed by the title first.

Ask first: How big are this company's contracts? How many customers do they have? How long do projects take to deliver? How standardized is the product, really? Does the company genuinely need engineers embedded long-term with customers solving problems?

A hot role can be a rocket—or a fire pit.

Not every company needs FDEs.

In fact, few companies actually deserve them.
