How to Develop an AI Chatbot | Steps and Key Considerations for Internal Deployment

Original title: AIチャットボット開発の進め方｜社内に入れるまでの手順と注意点

FDE Consulting · 2026-10-29 · 8 min read

Summary

This Japanese-language guide walks companies through launching an internal AI chatbot, distinguishing employee-facing bots (answering from internal policies) from customer-facing ones, where wrong answers carry legal weight. The author cites Air Canada's 2024 tribunal loss over a chatbot's incorrect refund guidance, and argues projects stall when three decisions are skipped: audience, answer scope, and escalation when the bot cannot answer. It outlines four build approaches (from off-the-shelf to custom development), a six-step process from collecting real questions to production operations, and risks including prompt injection, personal data rules from Japan's PPC, and EU AI Act disclosure duties. Cost-wise, document cleanup and ongoing operations typically exceed API fees, illustrated with Claude Sonnet 5.5 pricing.

Key points

  • Three pre-decisions matter most: audience, answer scope, and fallback to humans.
  • Air Canada's chatbot misinformation made the airline legally liable in 2024.
  • Four build types; start simple and move down only when limits appear.
  • Six steps include building an eval question list and a small pilot.
  • Document organization and ongoing operations cost more than model API usage.

Requests to introduce an AI chatbot into a company generally fall into two broad categories: one that answers employee questions based on internal regulations and procedure manuals, and one that answers customer inquiries. Both look the same on the surface, but they differ in the severity of mistakes and in what must be decided before building them.

This article summarizes the steps and precautions for bringing AI chatbot development into a company, covering three things to decide before building, patterns for how to build, six steps, precautions, and how to think about implementation costs. How to build a system that searches internal documents to answer questions is covered in detail in How to Build RAG, and design approaches to reduce incorrect answers in Hallucination Countermeasures.

Three Things to Decide Before Building

In my view, the reason AI chatbot development stalls is often that these three things have not been decided before the technology.

#What to decideWhat happens if you don't
1Who it is for (employees or customers)The severity of wrong answers is unclear, so you can't decide how much verification effort to apply
2What it answers (scope of answers and source documents)It gets asked anything, and answer quality can't be measured
3What to do when it can't answerIt answers even questions it doesn't know convincingly, and wrong answers reach users as-is

The first is especially significant. In Canada in 2024, a tribunal for dispute resolution held an airline liable in a case where Air Canada's chatbot gave guidance about fare refunds that differed from its policy. The company's argument that the chatbot was a separate entity was rejected, and the tribunal stated that Air Canada is responsible for all information on its website. A chatbot's answer to a customer can be treated as the company's answer.

The third is about having a path to pass out-of-scope or low-confidence questions to the inquiry desk or the person in charge. In my view, whether this is included in the initial design determines whether the pilot run earns trust.

Four Patterns for Building

Ways to build an AI chatbot are easier to choose from when divided into four patterns by effort and flexibility. The table is my own organization of them.

PatternContentSuitable situations
Use an off-the-shelf service as-isContract a chatbot product for inquiry handling, etc., and register documentsNarrow answer scope, little need to connect with internal systems
Build with enterprise generative AI plan featuresRegister documents and instructions in the generative AI service employees already use and share itFor employees, and you want to start small
Assemble from cloud componentsBuild by combining a cloud provider's search and model servicesNeed to connect with internal systems and data, and fine-grained access control
Develop in-houseUse model APIs and build search, UI, and logging yourselfWant deep integration into business workflows, need custom control

The lower patterns offer more flexibility but require people to build and operate. In my view, rather than developing in-house from the start, it's clearer what you're paying for if you solidify the answer scope and evaluation methods with the upper patterns and move to lower patterns only for the parts that fall short.

Six Steps to Introduce It In-House

Once you've chosen a pattern, proceed through the following six steps.

1. Collect questions

Collect the questions that actually come in from the target department. Inquiry records and frequently asked questions in internal chat are useful materials. Keep the collected questions as-is, since you'll use them for evaluation later.

2. Organize the source documents for answers

If old versions of regulations or procedure manuals that differ by department are mixed in, the chatbot will answer with the outdated one. Decide which documents are authoritative and who updates them. How to organize documents for easier search is summarized in How to Build RAG.

3. Design access permissions

Even for employees, not everyone can read every document. OWASP's list of LLM application risks (2025 edition), under LLM08 "Vector and Embedding Weaknesses," cites the risk that insufficient access control lets unauthorized people access embeddings containing sensitive information. Reflect per-document viewing permissions at the search stage so the chatbot doesn't reveal content of documents the user shouldn't be able to read.

4. Build a prototype and evaluate it

Turn the questions collected in step 1 into a list of evaluation questions along with correct answers. Run the prototype chatbot through that list and count whether it answered correctly, cited the correct source documents, and declined questions it couldn't answer. Measurement methods are described in How to Evaluate LLMs.

5. Run a small pilot

Limit the release to one department or a subset of customers. Let users give feedback on answer quality, and record questions it couldn't answer. Add the questions gathered during the pilot to the evaluation list.

6. Go to production and set up operations

Before going to production, decide who will handle document updates, review of the evaluation list, and the point of contact for reporting wrong answers, and how often. In my view, the effort of AI chatbot development lasts longer after release than during building.

Precautions: Wrong answers, attacks, personal information, and disclosure

Wrong answers

Generative AI can produce plausible errors. Attach source documents to answers so users can verify them, and decline or hand off questions outside the answer scope. Detailed design thinking is summarized in Hallucination Countermeasures.

Attacks that rewrite instructions

There are attacks that make the chatbot behave in ways the developer didn't intend, through user input or text hidden in ingested documents. These are called prompt injection. If the chatbot performs operations like sending emails or updating data, restrict operation permissions and require human approval for significant operations. Mechanisms and countermeasures are described in What Is Prompt Injection.

Personal information

In its June 2, 2023 "Cautionary Notice on the Use of Generative AI Services," the Personal Information Protection Commission required businesses handling personal information to sufficiently confirm that inputting prompts containing personal information is within the scope necessary to achieve the purpose of use. It states that inputting prompts containing personal data without prior consent of the individual, where that data is handled for purposes other than output in responses, may violate the Act on the Protection of Personal Information, and advises sufficiently confirming that the provider does not use personal data for machine learning. A chatbot handling customer inquiries requires this verification. How to check settings is summarized in Settings to Prevent ChatGPT Training on Your Data.

Disclosing that it is AI

When offering it to users in the EU, Article 50 of the EU AI Act requires AI systems that interact directly with people to inform them that they are talking to an AI. In my view, even for a chatbot aimed only at Japanese users, it is better to display on the screen that answers are generated by AI and to show a way to contact a human. I wrote about the EU regulations as a whole in EU AI Act and Japanese Companies.

How to think about the cost of introducing an AI chatbot

The cost of introducing an AI chatbot can be estimated in the following three categories. The table is my own summary.

BreakdownContentsItems easily missed in estimates
Initial development costCollecting questions, organizing documents, designing permissions, prototyping and evaluationOrganizing documents and designing permissions
Usage fees based on volumeModel API fees, search service fees, product feesThe amount of documents read per question
Ongoing operation costUpdating documents, revising evaluations, handling incorrect answersAlmost all of it

Usage fees can be estimated from a price list. According to Anthropic's pricing table, Claude Sonnet 5.5 costs 2 dollars per million tokens for input and 10 dollars for output. For example, assuming a single question uses 5,000 input tokens including retrieved documents and 500 output tokens, it comes to 0.015 dollars per question. At 10,000 answers per month under this assumption, that is 150 dollars. These figures are a calculation example; actual volume varies greatly depending on document length and the number of questions.

In my view, what tends to become larger than usage fees is the document organization within the initial development cost and the ongoing operation cost. When comparing estimates, it is a good idea to first check whether these two are included. I wrote in detail about how to read the breakdown of development costs in Breakdown of AI Development Costs.

What to check when outsourcing

In my view, who the chatbot is for, what it answers, and what to do when it cannot answer should be decided in-house. Outsourcing is most valuable for the parts that involve connecting with internal systems and data, designing permissions, and building an evaluation framework.

When choosing a vendor, it is a good idea to check the following four points.

  • Whether they will create a list of evaluation questions together with you and show quality in numbers
  • Whether they can explain how per-document viewing permissions will be reflected
  • Whether the design includes a flow for handing questions it cannot answer to a human
  • Whether they will hand over document updates and evaluation revisions after launch in a form your team can run in-house

Beyond simply building and delivering a chatbot, the role of working on-site, integrating it into business workflows, and staying involved until it is actually used overlaps with the job type known as FDE (Forward Deployed Engineer). I summarized the FDE job type in What is an FDE? The Meaning and Role of a Forward Deployed Engineer, and how to choose a vendor in How to Choose an FDE Support Company.
