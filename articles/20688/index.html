How FDE Offer Equity Is Calculated: Fully Diluted Shares and Liquidation Preferences

Original title: FDE offer 期权怎么算：完全稀释与清算优先权

FDE Times · 2026-10-18 · 6 min read

Summary

This article explains how FDEs should evaluate startup equity offers. Share counts alone are meaningless without the fully diluted denominator: 20,000 options could be 0.2% or 0.05%. It covers liquidation preference stacks, strike prices tied to 409A valuations, four-year vesting with one-year cliffs, 90-day post-termination exercise windows, and non-automatic acceleration on acquisition. Citing a 2026 negotiation guide, it notes equity now makes up 55–70% of FDE compensation at top employers, and provides a checklist of questions to send recruiters, arguing that asking these questions is itself the opening move in negotiation.

Key points

  • Always ask for your percentage of fully diluted shares and the as-of date.
  • Liquidation preferences determine what common shareholders actually receive in an exit.
  • One-year cliffs, 90-day exercise windows, and no acceleration can zero out equity.
  • Same-level grants can vary by $50,000–200,000, so asking details starts negotiation.
  • Clarifying a vague offer mirrors how good FDEs clarify vague client requirements.

Key Takeaways

  • Share count alone tells you almost nothing. Ask for the percentage, and specifically the percentage as of a specific date, calculated on a fully diluted basis.
  • Total liquidation preference, a one-year cliff, a 90-day exercise window, and no acceleration—any one of these can reduce your equity's value to zero.
  • Grants vary widely even at the same level, so asking for details is itself the first step of negotiation.

At top employers, equity may now account for 55-70% of a Forward Deployed Engineer's (FDE) total compensation, up from 35-45% in 2024. This figure comes from a 2026 FDE salary negotiation guide published on a vendor's blog and should be treated as a reference only.

It's the largest component of an offer, and the hardest to understand. Base salary is a number with a currency attached; equity is usually stated only as "X shares" or "X options," with no denominator and no indication of who gets paid before you.

If you're considering or targeting an FDE role at a startup, missing one question could cost you more than all the haggling over base salary could ever gain. The good news: there aren't many questions to ask, and the way to ask them is exactly the same as how a strong FDE conducts customer discovery.

Share count alone tells you nothing

The Startup Law Blog suggests the first question: what percentage does this grant represent, based on what share structure, as of what date. The logic is simple division. The share count is the numerator; what it's worth depends entirely on the denominator.

Suppose the company grants you 20,000 options. If the company's fully diluted share count is 10,000,000 shares, you hold 0.2%; if it's 40,000,000 shares, you hold only 0.05%, a quarter of the former. The same line in an offer letter can represent vastly different value.

So if a company won't tell you its fully diluted share count, treat the offer as incomplete until it does. This is a hard rule from Underdog.io. There's no need to be aggressive; simply state that without the denominator, you can't compare offers.

"As of what date" matters just as much. Underdog.io points out that for venture-backed companies that keep raising, dilution isn't a theoretical risk—it's the default outcome.

Companies don't need employee consent to dilute employee equity. Today's 0.2% will shrink with every funding round, so factor this in from the start.

Who gets paid before you?

Knowing the percentage isn't enough, because different shares don't get paid out in the same order. Ask for the total liquidation preference (the preference stack), i.e., the sum of the liquidation preferences held by preferred stock investors (Underdog.io). When the company is sold, this gets paid first; the common stock held by employees only gets what's left.

Imagine a startup that has raised a cumulative 100 units, corresponding to an equal amount of liquidation preferences, and is then acquired for 120 units. Investors take the first 100.

All common stock—including your 0.2%—splits the remaining 20 units. If the sale price is only 90, you get zero, even if the headlines still call it an "exit."

This is why the FDE negotiation guide mentioned above recommends valuing the grant on a fully diluted basis, and checking it against both the 409A valuation and the preferred stock valuation. In other words, don't just multiply the share count by one price. Calculate both prices, then look at the gap between them.

Options or RSUs, and what does it cost to buy in?

Options and restricted stock units (RSUs) are two different instruments. Stock options give you the right to buy shares at a preset price (the strike price), while RSUs are shares granted outright (Levels.fyi). For options, your value is the difference between the share price and the strike price, not the full share price.

The strike price is what you pay when you exercise—that is, buy the shares. In the US, the strike price is anchored to the 409A valuation, so Ledgy recommends asking for both the strike price and the date of the most recent 409A valuation. An outdated 409A valuation may no longer reflect the company's current state.

Setting the strike price based on a 409A valuation is a US mechanism. If your employment contract is in another country, ask the company directly: how is the strike price determined where you work, and when do taxes come due.

The vesting clock and the 90-day window

According to Ledgy, the most common vesting schedule is four years with a one-year cliff. Leave before one year, and you get nothing. If you're unsure whether you'll stay in the new role through the first year, include a scenario in your offer comparison: this equity is worth zero.

The second risk appears when you leave. For incentive stock options (ISOs), the post-departure exercise period is typically 90 days, but depending on the company's equity policy, it can be as long as 10 years (Qapita). Loom is one example: it offers employees with two or more years of service a 10-year exercise window.

Do the math. You have 5,000 vested options with a strike price of 2 currency units per share. To keep them, you must come up with 10,000 units (pre-tax) within 90 days to buy stock in a private company.

If you can't, the options are forfeited. A 10-year window turns a rushed decision into a choice you can weigh calmly.

When the company is acquired, and the 30-day deadline

Don't assume an exit means you get everything. Unless the grant documents explicitly say so, an acquisition does not automatically accelerate the vesting of unvested equity (The Startup Law Blog). Ask about acceleration clauses and read the actual language in the grant documents.

Early exercise has its own traps. If you exercise before vesting, the statutory deadline for the 83(b) election is 30 days from the actual transfer of the shares, and it is generally not extended. This deadline should go on your calendar the day you sign, not left for next week.

Ask like you'd ask a customer

Asking these questions is itself the opening move of negotiation. The FDE negotiation guide mentioned above states that at the same level, RSU grants over a four-year vesting period can differ by $50,000-$200,000, depending on the compensation committee's decision.

That gap is your negotiating room. But you can only use it after converting the grant into a percentage and figuring out who gets paid before you.

The same guide suggests discussing equity first, before anything else. That makes sense when equity is the largest part of the compensation package. Armed with your fully diluted percentage and the total liquidation preference, you're negotiating with data, not gut feelings.

The skills used here are exactly the skills the job requires. When an FDE gets a request to "integrate the systems," they don't just take it at face value without asking about scope, which data to use, and who signs off on acceptance. You can make this point in the interview: the way you clarify a vague offer is the way you clarify vague client requirements.

Read the offer letter the way you'd read a client requirements spec: wherever a number is missing, ask about it before signing.
