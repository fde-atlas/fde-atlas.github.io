Your AI Product Needs Evals: The Foundational Article on the Three-Layer Testing System

Original title: 你的 AI 产品需要评测：三层测试体系的开山之文

FDE中国 · 2024-03-29 · 15 min read

Summary

Using Rechat's real-estate AI assistant Lucy as a case study, the article argues that AI products stall after demos because they lack a proper eval system, then lays out a three-tier approach: cheap unit tests (assertions) run on every code change; model- and human-based evals run on a cadence, but only after trace logging and friction-free data inspection are in place; and expensive A/B testing reserved for mature products. The author warns against skipping unit tests and against relying on generic eval frameworks, and shows how eval infrastructure doubles as a debugging and fine-tuning data curation engine. Rechat runs hundreds of assertions through CI with Metabase dashboards.

Key points

  • Stalled AI products usually lack a robust eval system.
  • Three tiers by cost: unit tests, model/human evals, A/B tests.
  • Master unit tests before moving to LLM-based judging.
  • Generic eval frameworks don't work; write domain-specific assertions.
  • Eval infrastructure also powers debugging and fine-tuning data curation.

This piece answers a recurring question: why do AI products stall after the demo? The answer is the lack of an evals system, and one that can be built following a methodology. The most valuable takeaway is the ordering logic of the three eval layers: unit tests are cheap and run on every code change; model and human evals are mid-cost, run on a cadence, with the prerequisite of first reducing the friction of trace logging and "looking at data" to zero; A/B testing is the most expensive and reserved for mature products. The two layers most prone to misuse: first, skipping unit tests and jumping straight to LLM-as-judge; second, treating a generic evals framework as the answer—the author explicitly says generic frameworks don't solve the problem. Chinese readers should read this because symptoms like "whack-a-mole" and "vibe check" are just as common in domestic teams, and Rechat's approach of using hundreds of assertions plus a CI dashboard for evals can be replicated without buying any new tools. One addition for the Chinese context: when generating synthetic test data, use authentic Chinese expressions rather than copying English templates. One action after reading: pick one feature of your product, write three scenarios and three assertions, and wire them into CI to run. — FDEChina Editorial Team · Sharp Review

This article explains how to build a domain-specific LLM evaluation system.

Why I Wrote This

I started working with language models five years ago, when the team I led created CodeSearchNet—the precursor to GitHub Copilot. Since then, I've seen many paths to success and failure in building LLM products. I've found that failed products almost all share the same root cause: failing to establish a robust evals system.

This article was originally written while I was helping companies build domain-specific AI products. I hope that companies that read it carefully can save thousands of dollars in consulting fees.

This article outlines my thinking on "building an evals system for LLM-powered AI products."

Fast Iteration == Success

As with software engineering, the success of an AI project depends on your iteration speed. You must have processes and tools that cover the following three things:

  • Assessing quality (e.g., tests);
  • Debugging problems (e.g., logs and data inspection);
  • Changing system behavior (prompt engineering, fine-tuning, writing code).

Many people focus only on the third item, which leaves them unable to keep improving their LLM product after the demo.¹ Doing all three well creates a virtuous cycle that separates excellent AI products from mediocre ones (visualized in the figure below).

If you get the evals process right, everything else becomes easier. This is much like testing in software engineering: significant upfront investment, huge long-term returns.

To make this article concrete, I'll walk through a full case study—how we built a system that could improve quickly. I'll focus on evals, since it's the most critical component.

Case Study: Lucy, a Real Estate AI Assistant

Rechat is a SaaS application that lets real estate professionals accomplish a variety of tasks: managing contracts, searching listings, creating marketing materials, managing appointments, and more. Rechat's core philosophy is that everything can be done in one place, without constantly switching context among many tools.

Rechat's AI assistant Lucy is a typical AI product: a conversational interface that frees you from clicking, typing, and hunting for features across the software. In Lucy's early days, prompt engineering brought rapid progress. But as Lucy's feature surface grew, the AI's performance plateaued. Symptoms included:

  • Fixing one failure mode only for other failure modes to pop up, like playing whack-a-mole;
  • Beyond the "vibe check," a lack of visibility into the AI system's effectiveness across tasks;
  • Prompts bloating into long, hard-to-maintain monsters trying to cover a large number of edge cases and examples.

The Problem: How to Improve AI Systematically?

To break through the plateau, we built a systematic, evals-centered improvement methodology for Lucy. The figure below shows our approach.

This diagram is my best attempt at presenting a mental model for improving AI systems. The actual process is nonlinear and takes many shapes; it won't necessarily look exactly like this diagram.

Below, I discuss each component of this system in the context of evals.

The Three Layers of Evals

Rigorous, systematic evals are the most important part of the whole system. That's why "Eval and Curation" is highlighted in yellow at the center of the diagram. You should spend most of your time making evals more robust and smoother.

There are three layers of evals to consider:

  • Layer 1: Unit Tests;
  • Layer 2: Model & Human Evals (including debugging);
  • Layer 3: A/B Testing.

In terms of cost, Layer 3 > Layer 2 > Layer 1, which determines the cadence and manner in which you run them. For example, I typically run Layer 1 evals on every code change, run Layer 2 on a fixed cadence, and only run Layer 3 after major product changes. Also, before moving to model-based testing, it helps to nail down most of the Layer 1 tests first, since the latter require more work and time to execute.

There's no strict formula for when to introduce which layer of testing. You need to balance quickly obtaining user feedback, managing user perception, and your AI product goals—much the same balancing act as with product development in general.

Layer 1: Unit Tests

Unit tests for LLMs are assertions, just like the ones you write in pytest. Unlike ordinary unit tests, you should organize these assertions into forms usable outside unit tests, such as data cleaning and automatic retries during model inference (using assertion errors to correct course). The key is that these assertions should run fast and cheap while developing the application, so you can execute them on every code change. If you can't think of assertions, go carefully examine your traces and failure modes. And don't be shy about asking an LLM to brainstorm assertions with you!

Step 1: Write Scoped Tests

The most effective way to think about unit tests is to break down your LLM's scope into features and scenarios. For example, one of Lucy's features is finding listings, and we can break out scenarios like this:

Feature: Listing Finder

The feature under test is a function call that responds to a user's request to find listings, such as "Please find me listings in San Jose, California with 3+ bedrooms under $2 million."

The LLM converts it into a query executed against the CRM, and assertions verify that the number of returned results matches expectations. In our test suite, each scenario has three user inputs to trigger it, followed by the corresponding assertions (this is an oversimplified example for illustration):

ScenarioAssertion
Exactly one listing matches the user querylen(listing_array) == 1
Multiple listings match the user querylen(listing_array) > 1
No listings match the user querylen(listing_array) == 0

There are also generic tests not tied to specific features. For example, the code for the following generic test ensures no UUIDs appear in the output:

const noExposedUUID = message => {
  // 去除双花括号内的所有文本
  const sanitizedComment = message.comment.replace(/{{.*?}}/g, '')

  // 搜索暴露的 UUID
  const regexp = /[0-9a-f]{8}-[0-9a-f]{4}-[0-9a-f]{4}-[0-9a-f]{4}-[0-9a-f]{12}/ig
  const matches = Array.from(sanitizedComment.matchAll(regexp))
  expect(matches.length, 'Exposed UUIDs').to.equal(0, 'Exposed UUIDs found')
}

The CRM results returned to the LLM contain fields that shouldn't be shown to users, such as the UUID associated with each record. Our prompt tells the LLM not to include UUIDs, and a simple regex assertion verifies that the LLM's response indeed contains no UUIDs.

Rechat has hundreds of unit tests like this, continuously updated based on new failures observed in the data when users "stress-test" the AI or as the product evolves. These unit tests are key to getting fast feedback while iterating on AI systems (prompt engineering, improving RAG, etc.). Many people eventually "outgrow" unit tests and move on to other levels of evals—but this step must never be skipped!

Step 2: Create test cases

To run these assertions, you must generate test cases or inputs that trigger all the scenarios to be tested. I often use an LLM to synthesize these inputs; for example, here is the prompt Rechat uses to generate synthetic inputs for the "create and query contacts" feature:

写出 50 条不同的指令，内容是一位房地产经纪人可以让助手在 CRM 中创建联系人的话。
联系人细节可以包含姓名、电话、邮箱、伴侣姓名、生日、标签、公司、地址与职位。

每条指令都要配第二条指令，用于查找刚创建的联系人。

结果应为一个 JSON 代码块，每项只含一条字符串指令，例如：

[
  ["Create a contact for John ([email protected])",
  "What's the email address of John Smith?"]
]

Using the prompt above, we generate test cases like this:

[
    [
        'Create a contact for John Smith ([email protected]) with phone number 123-456-7890 and address 123 Apple St.',
        "What's the email address of John Smith?"
    ],
    [
        'Add Emily Johnson with phone 987-654-3210, email [email protected], and company ABC Inc.',
        "What's the phone number for Emily Johnson?"
    ],
    [
        'Create a contact for Tom Williams with birthday 10/20/1985, company XYZ Ltd, and job title Manager.',
        "What's Tom Williams' job title?"
    ],
    [
        'Add a contact for Susan Brown with partner name James Brown, and email [email protected].',
        "What's the partner name of Susan Brown?"
    ],
...
]

For each test case, we first execute the first user input to create the contact, then execute the second query to retrieve it. If the CRM doesn't return exactly 1 result, something went wrong in the creation or query step. We can also run generic assertions, such as verifying that the response contains no UUIDs. You must keep updating these tests based on data observed through human evals and debugging. The key is to make these tests as challenging as possible while genuinely representing how users interact with the system.

You don't need to wait for production data to test your system. You can make informed guesses about how users will use the product and generate synthetic data; you can also have a small group of users use the product first and use their real usage to improve your synthetic data generation strategy. One signal that the tests and assertions you've written are good: the model starts to struggle to pass them—these failure modes become problems you can later solve with techniques like fine-tuning.

By the way: unlike traditional unit tests, you don't necessarily need a 100% pass rate. The pass rate is a product decision, depending on which failures you're willing to tolerate.

Step 3: Run and track tests regularly

There are many ways to orchestrate Level 1 tests. Rechat has always relied on CI infrastructure (such as GitHub Actions, GitLab Pipelines, etc.) to run these tests. However, tooling in this area is still primitive and evolving rapidly.

My advice is: choose the orchestration approach with the least friction in your tech stack. Besides tracking the tests themselves, you also need to track how test results change over time, so you can see whether you're improving. If you use CI, you should collect metrics along with test/prompt versions outside the CI system, for easier analysis and tracking.

I recommend starting simple and using your existing analytics systems to visualize test results. For example, Rechat uses Metabase to track how LLM test results change over time. On their Metabase dashboard, you can see how often a particular error (in yellow) appeared before versus after it was fixed in Lucy.

Level 2: Human evals and model-based evals

Once Level 1 tests have laid the foundation, you can move on to forms of evals that can't be verified by assertions alone. Doing human evals and model-based evals requires first logging your traces.

Logging traces

Traces are a long-standing concept in software engineering, referring to a log of a sequence of events, such as a user session, or the flow of a request through a distributed system. In other words, tracing is the logical grouping of logs. In the LLM context, a trace usually refers to a single conversation with the LLM. For example: a user message, an AI reply, then another user message—that's a trace.

There are more and more solutions for logging LLM traces.² Rechat uses LangSmith: it logs traces and displays them in a human-readable way, with an interactive playground for iterating on prompts. Sometimes logging traces requires instrumenting your code. Rechat was using LangChain at the time, which automatically logs trace events to LangSmith.

I really like LangSmith—it doesn't require you to use LangChain, and it's intuitive and easy to use. Searching, filtering, and reading traces are basic capabilities any solution must have. I've found that some tools don't even get these basics right!

Looking at your traces

You must eliminate all friction in "looking at the data." This means rendering traces in a domain-specific way. I often find it better to build my own data viewing and annotation tools, bringing all the information I need into a single screen. Take Lucy as an example: we needed to look at multiple information sources (trace logs, the CRM, etc.) to understand what the AI did—this is exactly the friction that needs to be eliminated. In Rechat's case, this meant adding this information:

  • Which tool (feature) and which scenario was being evaluated at the time;
  • Whether the trace came from synthetic input or real user input;
  • Filters for switching between different "tool × scenario" combinations;
  • Links to the current record in the CRM and the trace logging system.

I've built different variants of this kind of tool for every problem I've worked on. Sometimes I even need to embed another application to see what the user interaction looks like. Another design choice for Lucy: we noticed that many failures involved small errors in the LLM's final output (formatting, content, etc.), so we decided to make the final output human-editable, so it could be curated and fixed as data for fine-tuning.

This kind of tool can be built in a day with lightweight frontend frameworks like Gradio, Streamlit, Panel, or Shiny. There are also tools like Lilac that use AI for semantic search and filtering of data, which are extremely useful for finding a group of similar data points when debugging.

I usually start by labeling samples as "good/bad." I've found that scores or finer-grained ratings are harder to manage than binary labels. There are advanced techniques to make human evals more efficient and accurate (such as active learning, consensus voting, etc.), but I recommend starting with something simple. Finally, as with unit tests, you should organize and analyze the results of human evals to assess whether you're improving over time.

As described later, these labeled samples can measure system quality, validate automated evals, and curate high-quality synthetic data for fine-tuning.

How much data should you look at?

I'm often asked how much data one should look at. In the early stages, you should look at as much data as possible. I usually read through at least all traces generated from test cases and all traces produced by users. Looking at data should never stop—there's no free lunch. But over time, you can sample more to lighten the load.³

Automated evals with LLMs

Many vendors want to sell you tools that promise "you'll never need humans to look at data again." Having humans regularly evaluate the traces of at least a sample is a good idea. I often find that "correctness" is somewhat subjective, and you must align the model with humans.

You should track the correlation between model-based evals and human evals to decide how much you can rely on automated evals. Additionally, by collecting critiques where annotators explain the reasoning behind their judgments, you can iterate on the eval model, aligning it with humans through prompt engineering or fine-tuning. However, I prefer using prompt engineering for eval model alignment.

I like to use low-tech solutions like Excel to iterate on "aligning model-based evals with humans." For example, for another use case—a natural language query generator—I would send my colleague Phillip a spreadsheet every few days containing the following information for him to score:

  • Model response: the prediction made by the LLM;
  • Model critique: a critique of your original LLM prediction, written by another (usually stronger) LLM;
  • Model outcome: the "good/bad" binary label the critique model assigns to the model response.

Phillip then fills in his own version of the same information—his comments, conclusions, and expected responses, 25-50 examples at a time. This lets me iterate on the comment model's prompt so that it stays well aligned with Phillip over time. This is also well suited to being tracked continuously in a low-tech way, such as a spreadsheet.

A few general tips for model-based evals:

  • Use the strongest model you can afford. Critiquing something well often requires advanced reasoning ability. Compared to the model used in production, you can often tolerate a slower but stronger model for generating critiques;
  • Model-based evals are a meta-problem within your larger problem. You must maintain a small eval system to track its quality. At this stage I sometimes fine-tune a model (though I try to avoid it);
  • After aligning your model-based eval with humans, you must also continue doing regular alignment exercises to monitor how well the model aligns with humans.

Important note: be careful when using "agreement rate" as a metric. In this example, we use the agreement rate between the model and human raters because our dataset is roughly balanced (about 50% of samples are failures). But when classes are imbalanced, using agreement rate directly is generally not recommended and can be misleading. A better approach is to measure precision and recall separately, to more accurately characterize how well your reviewer aligns with humans.

My favorite byproduct of building a good eval model: its critiques can be used to curate high-quality synthetic data, as mentioned later.

Layer 3: A/B Testing.

Finally, A/B testing is always beneficial—it ensures your AI product is driving the user behaviors or outcomes you expect. A/B testing for LLM products isn't much different from other types of products. To learn more about A/B testing, I recommend reading the Eppo blog (created by former colleagues of mine who are top experts in the field).

It's perfectly fine to defer this level until you're confident enough that your AI product is ready to show to real users. Evals at this level are usually only appropriate for more mature products.

Evaluating RAG

Beyond evaluating the system as a whole, you can also evaluate subcomponents of the AI, such as RAG. Evaluating RAG is beyond the scope of this article; you can dive deeper into this topic in Jason Liu's articles.

Superpowers unlocked for free by an eval system

Beyond letting you iterate quickly, an eval system also unlocks fine-tuning and debugging capabilities that can take your AI product to the next level.

Fine-tuning

Rechat solved many failure modes that prompt engineering alone couldn't fix through fine-tuning. Fine-tuning is best suited for learning syntax, style, and rules, while techniques like RAG provide the model with context or up-to-date facts.

99% of the work in fine-tuning lies in assembling high-quality data that covers the feature surface of your AI product. But if you have a solid eval system like Rechat's, you already have a robust data generation and curation engine! I'll expand on the fine-tuning process in a future article.⁴

Data synthesis and curation

To illustrate why data curation and synthesis are almost free once you have an eval system, imagine you want to create more fine-tuning data for the property finder mentioned earlier. First, you can use an LLM with a prompt like this to generate synthetic data:

想象 Zillow 能解析自然语言。想出 50 种用户在上面搜索房源的方式。
使用真实的城市与社区名称。

你可以使用以下参数：

<出于保密省略>

输出应为 JSON 代码块数组。例如：

[
"Homes under $500k in New York"
]

This is almost identical to the exercise of generating test cases! Next, you can use the Level 1 and Level 2 tests to filter out bad data that fails assertions or is judged incorrect by the critique model. You can also use off-the-shelf human eval tools to review trajectories and curate trajectories for your fine-tuning dataset.

Debugging

When you receive complaints related to your AI product or see errors, you should be able to debug quickly. If you have a robust eval system, you already have:

  • A searchable, filterable database of trajectories;
  • A mechanism to help you flag errors and bad behavior (assertions, tests, etc.);
  • Log search and navigation tools to help you find the root cause of errors—the root cause could be RAG, a code bug, or poor model performance;
  • The ability to make changes in response to errors and quickly verify their effect.

In short, there is a huge overlap between the infrastructure needed for evals and the infrastructure needed for debugging.

Conclusion

An eval system creates a flywheel that lets you iterate quickly. It's almost always where people get stuck when building AI products. I hope this article has given you an intuition for how to build your own eval system. Keep the following points in mind:

  • Eliminate all friction from the "look at the data" process;
  • Keep it simple. Don't buy fancy LLM tools—make good use of what you have first;
  • If you're not looking at lots of data, you're doing it wrong;
  • Don't rely on generic eval frameworks to measure your AI quality; instead, create an eval system tailored to your problem;
  • Write lots of tests and update them frequently;
  • LLMs can be used to streamline the creation of your eval system, e.g., generating test cases, writing assertions, generating synthetic data, critiquing and annotating data, etc.;
  • Reuse your eval infrastructure for debugging and fine-tuning.
