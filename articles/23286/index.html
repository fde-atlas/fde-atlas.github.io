Summary

This Japanese-language article by Shohei Tokumitsu, CEO of DeFactory, examines the growing market for side-job and concurrent product manager (PdM) work. It argues demand surged between 2024 and 2026 because startups cannot hire full-time PdMs, lack budget, and founders often lack product experience. Side PdM roles fall into three types: advisory (5,000–15,000 yen/hour), upstream design and research (4,000–8,000 yen/hour), and delivery management (4,000–8,000 yen/hour), with one day per week often yielding 100,000–300,000 yen monthly. The article covers the main obstacles—limited overlapping hours and weak authority—and proposes asynchronous communication, explicit contract scoping, and early trust-building. It also details using generative AI like ChatGPT and Claude to halve PRD writing time, and outlines a phased path from side work to full-time freelance external CPO roles paying 2–3 million yen monthly.

Key points

  • Side-job PdM demand grew due to startup hiring constraints.
  • Advisory roles pay the highest rates at 5,000–15,000 yen/hour.
  • One day weekly can yield 100,000–300,000 yen monthly.
  • Success requires async communication and explicit scope contracts.
  • A phased path leads to external CPO roles at 2–3 million yen/month.

Since side-gig PdM involves many situations where you must act on your own judgment, it is a demanding working style for junior PdMs. The profile most likely to deliver value in side-gig projects is a senior PdM with at least 3-5 years of PdM experience with their own company's product and experience handling multiple products.

Conclusion: Side-gig PdM projects fall broadly into three types depending on the working style: "advisory type (hourly rate of 5,000-15,000 yen)", "upstream design/research-focused type (hourly rate of 4,000-8,000 yen)", and "delivery management type (hourly rate of 4,000-8,000 yen)". The advisory type has the highest hourly rate and offers the best income efficiency per hour worked.

Conclusion: The advisory-type side-gig PdM role functions as a "sparring partner" for executives, current PdMs, and junior PdMs, with an extremely light workload of a few hours a month to about 5 hours a week. The primary deliverable is not hands-on work but "judgment and advice", with rates ranging from 5,000-15,000 yen per hour, exceeding 20,000 yen for high-class positions.

Here is a summary of the typical work involved in advisory-type projects.

  • Participation in strategy meetings: Advising in monthly executive or product strategy meetings (1-2 times per month)
  • PdM mentoring: One-on-one mentoring for junior PdMs and career consultations
  • Decision-making sparring: One-on-one discussions during difficult decision points
  • Document review: Expert review of PRDs, roadmaps, and proposal documents
  • Hiring support: Sitting in on interviews with PdM candidates and evaluating them
  • Industry insights: Sharing the latest trends in similar products and the industry
  • Pinpoint problem solving: Focused advice on specific product issues

The greatest appeal of the advisory type is its "high hourly rate" and "low time commitment". It is not uncommon for senior PdMs to earn 100,000-300,000 yen per month in side income by working about 10-20 hours per month alongside a full-time job. On the other hand, it requires "accurate judgment in a short amount of time" to deliver value, making it a difficult area for less experienced PdMs to take on.

Conclusion: The upstream design/research-focused type consists of projects carved out as side work involving "hands-on upstream tasks" such as writing PRDs (Product Requirements Documents), designing user research, and competitive analysis. The standard range is 4,000-8,000 yen per hour, reaching over 10,000 yen if you have a track record in a specific area.

Conclusion: The delivery management type supports "ongoing product operations" such as acting as a scrum master for agile development, backlog management, QA, and release management. Conclusion: The two major hurdles facing side-gig PdMs are "1) the time barrier: working mainly at night and on days off, making it hard to communicate synchronously" and "2) the authority barrier: earning trust from the internal team as an external part-timer". Both can be overcome through contract design, initial behavior, and the use of AI, but there is a risk of the project falling apart if you dive in without knowing about them.

Conclusion: The biggest struggle for side-gig PdMs is the "time mismatch": since they mainly work during off-hours (nights and days off), their working hours rarely overlap with the client's business hours. This barrier is overcome through two approaches: "contract design premised on asynchronous communication" and "strategically securing synchronous touchpoints".

Here is a summary of practical techniques for making asynchronous PdM work.

  • Fixed weekly synchronous meeting: A focused 90-120 minute discussion using paid leave or a day off from your main job
  • Asynchronous updates via Slack or chat tools: Daily catch-up and comment replies
  • Leveraging shared documents: Build up decisions asynchronously on Notion and Confluence
  • Thorough meeting minutes: Catch up on minutes from missed meetings without delay
  • Using video messages: Convey your intent asynchronously via video with tools like Loom
  • Managing decision logs: Keep Slack discussions as decision logs
  • Pre-organizing questions: For synchronous meetings, list in advance "what you need to decide"

Designing these mechanisms at the start of the contract greatly influences the success rate of a side-gig PdM. Work must be designed on the assumption of lag, such as "I sent a message at night, but the client replies the next morning."

Conclusion: Since a side-gig PdM's working hours are limited, leaving scope creep unaddressed leads immediately to overtime. It is essential to put in writing "what to do" and "what not to do" at the contract stage and to establish a mechanism for regularly reconfirming scope.

Particularly important are "putting the scope of work in writing" and "setting a cap on working hours." When these are vague in a contract, the client tends to inflate expectations, thinking "they'll do everything even as a side gig," and as a result, the side-gig PdM frequently ends up exhausted from overwork.

Conclusion: The biggest key to a side-gig PdM earning the trust of the internal team is their behavior during the first 2 to 4 weeks after the contract starts. It is important to concentrate on actions early on that dispel the impression of "an external part-timer who will only be minimally involved" and establish a reputation as "someone who brings valuable decisions even in a short time."

Here is a summary of specific actions to focus on during the first 2 to 4 weeks.

  • Thoroughly reading existing materials: Read through product-related documents before starting work
  • 1-on-1s with key people: Hold individual meetings with the CEO, CTO, PdM, and engineering leads in the first week
  • Immediately grasping metrics: First check product KPIs, user counts, churn rate, etc.
  • Asking thorough questions: Don't hesitate to ask about anything unclear; absorb context as fast as possible
  • Presenting small wins early: In weeks 1-2, make one concrete proposal of the form "this can be improved"
  • Praising existing members' contributions: Don't push your own opinions; show respect for existing decisions
  • Actively using synchronous touchpoints: Make your presence felt in limited synchronous meetings, without taking decisions away with you

In particular, "presenting small wins early" is decisively important for a side-gig PdM to earn trust. By dispelling on-the-ground anxiety—"what will change with this outsider coming in?"—through concrete improvement proposals, long-term relationship building becomes possible afterward.

Conclusion: PRD creation is one of a side-gig PdM's main tasks, but by using ChatGPT/Claude, you can complete a first draft in less than half the traditional time. The current standard is becoming a hybrid approach in which the human PdM improves the AI-generated first draft by taking into account "business context," "the user's perspective," and "implementation constraints."

Here is a summary of specific steps for using AI in PRD creation.

  • Textualize the overview: Input the product overview, target audience, and problem hypotheses as text
  • Structured proposal: Ask the AI to "propose a PRD outline"
  • Generate first drafts by section: Generate "functional requirements," "non-functional requirements," and "user stories" in sequence
  • Identify edge cases: Ask "What edge cases should be considered for this use case?"
  • Incorporating competitive examples: Ask "Show me 5 implementation examples from similar products"
  • Expanding user stories: Have the AI generate derived cases from a single story
  • Quality check: Ask "Please point out 5 perspectives missing from this PRD"

By leveraging these techniques, productivity improves to the level where PRD creation that used to take 5-10 hours can be completed in 2-4 hours. For more on streamlining upstream processes with generative AI, also see How Freelancers Can Win High Rates in Upstream Processes.

A: Non-compete obligations and NDA conflicts with your main employer are the most important legal risks to watch in side-gig PdM work. First, you must carefully read your employer's work rules and employment contract to clearly understand the scope in which side work is permitted. Second, avoid supporting obvious direct competitors (same customer base, same product category) and choose areas that do not overlap in terms of industry or product. Third, if your employer's prior approval is required, obtain written approval before signing the side-work contract. Fourth, include clauses in the side-work contract such as "not bringing in the main employer's confidential information" and "not diverting knowledge gained from the side work to the main job." If you are unsure how to proceed, we strongly recommend consulting a lawyer in advance.
