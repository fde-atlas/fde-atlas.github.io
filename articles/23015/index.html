The FDE Project Delivery Process: A Full-Chain Analysis from Requirements Diagnosis to Knowledge Transfer

Original title: FDE项目交付流程：从需求诊断到知识转移的全链路解析

HyDe - 企业软件顾问与全栈开发者 · 2026-09-25 · 17 min read

Summary

This article breaks down the standard delivery process for FDE projects and contrasts it with traditional outsourcing. The author divides delivery into five phases—diagnosis and planning, design and prototyping, iterative development, launch and validation, and knowledge transfer—using a typical 6-week project as the reference timeline, with tasks, deliverables, and decision points for each phase. The core argument is that FDE work uses agile iteration instead of waterfall, is validated against quantified business outcomes rather than feature checklists, and must end with the client team able to maintain and evolve the system independently. The piece also covers staffing and timelines by project size, client-side time commitments, key success factors, and common pitfalls like poor data quality and scope creep.

Key points

  • Five-phase delivery: diagnosis, prototype, iterative development, launch, knowledge transfer.
  • Six weeks is the standard cycle for a single-scenario AI project, with two-week iterations.
  • Acceptance is based on quantified business metrics, not feature lists.
  • Knowledge transfer has three levels—artifacts, methods, and decision-making mindset.
  • Client side invests roughly half a person's time; sponsor support is the top success factor.

Many companies are interested in FDEs (Forward Deployed Engineers), but they have a question in mind: How is an FDE project delivered? How is it different from traditional outsourcing?

The delivery process of traditional outsourcing is familiar to everyone: requirements research → solution design → development → testing → launch → acceptance. But the FDE model's process is completely different—it emphasizes embedding, iteration, and results orientation rather than linear, stage-by-stage progression.

This article breaks down the standard delivery process of an FDE project, from initial diagnosis to knowledge transfer—what each stage does, what it delivers, and how to measure results—helping you build a complete understanding.

Core Characteristics of the FDE Delivery Process

Before diving into the specific stages, first understand the fundamental differences between FDE delivery and traditional delivery:

CharacteristicTraditional WaterfallFDE Model
ApproachLinear progression; the next stage starts only after the previous one endsIterative progression; each iteration is a complete closed loop
Requirements managementFixed upfront; changes go through formal processesContinuously evolving; adjustable every two weeks
Delivery frequencyOne-time delivery at the end of the projectDemonstrable results every week
Acceptance methodFinal acceptance against a requirements checklistContinuous acceptance based on business outcomes
Customer involvementResearch upfront + acceptance at the end, with little involvement in betweenDeep involvement throughout, with daily communication
Risk controlScope fixed upfront; risks borne laterSmall, fast steps; identify and adjust issues promptly

In one sentence: Traditional outsourcing is "delivering features per the contract," while FDE is "achieving results together with the customer."

The Five Stages of the Standard Delivery Process

Stage 1: Diagnosis and Planning (Week 1)

This is the starting point of the project and also the most critical stage. Many projects fail because the diagnosis wasn't done thoroughly.

Core Tasks

TaskDescriptionRoles Involved
Business status diagnosisGain a deep understanding of current business processes, pain points, and goalsFDE + business owner
System status assessmentExisting system architecture, data situation, tech stackFDE + technical lead
Data quality assessmentData completeness, accuracy, and availabilityFDE + data/business team
Success criteria alignmentDefine the metrics for project successFDE + project sponsor
PrioritizationDetermine what to do first and what to do laterFDE + business + technical teams
Risk identificationIdentify potential risks and obstacles in advanceFDE + customer team

Deliverables

  • Status diagnosis report: current business processes, system status, data quality assessment
  • Project proposal: goals, scope, technical solution, implementation plan
  • Success metrics definition: quantifiable success criteria (e.g., "increase lead conversion rate by 15%")
  • Risk list and mitigation plan: identified risks and corresponding countermeasures

Key Points

  • Always meet the decision-makers: without their involvement, driving the project forward later will be difficult
  • Success criteria must be quantified: vague statements like "improve efficiency" won't do—there must be numbers
  • Data is a core prerequisite: for AI projects especially, data quality must be assessed first—if the data is too poor, even the best FDE can't deliver results
  • Don't promise what can't be done: a good FDE will tell you at this stage "what can be done and what can't," rather than agreeing to everything
This stage is often called "Discovery Week"—use one week to thoroughly understand every aspect of the project, then decide whether to continue and how to proceed.

Stage 2: Design and Prototyping (Week 2)

Once the diagnosis is clear, move into the design and prototyping stage. The goal of this stage is to quickly build a demonstrable prototype to validate the feasibility of the solution.

Core Tasks

TaskDescriptionRoles Involved
Detailed solution designArchitecture design, data flow design, interface designFDE + technical team
Prototype developmentBuild a runnable prototype of the core featuresFDE (customer technical team may participate)
Data integration validationVerify whether key data can be obtained and whether quality is sufficientFDE + data team
Prototype demo and feedbackDemo to the business team and collect feedbackFDE + business team
Solution adjustment and optimizationAdjust the solution based on feedbackFDE

Deliverables

  • Technical solution document: architecture diagrams, data flow diagrams, interface definitions
  • Runnable prototype: a demo version of the core features
  • Data integration plan: data sources, integration methods, cleaning rules
  • Iteration plan: the iteration schedule for subsequent development

Key Points

  • The prototype must actually run: not a PPT mockup, but a runnable prototype you can click through and see data in
  • Use real data: use the customer's real data for the prototype whenever possible, not fake data—fake data looks good but masks problems
  • Iterate quickly: the prototype isn't finalized in one version; it may take 2-3 revisions
  • Don't chase perfection: the purpose of the prototype is to validate the direction, not to build the final product—as long as it works, details can be refined later
At the end of this stage, the customer can clearly see "what the final result will roughly look like" and decide whether to keep investing. This is an important decision point—if the prototype doesn't validate well, you can cut losses early, and the loss is small.

Stage 3: Development and Iteration (Weeks 3-5)

This is the main body of the project and also the stage where the FDE model differs most from traditional outsourcing.

Traditional outsourcing is "develop everything, then test together," while FDE is "one iteration per week, with each iteration having a complete develop-test-demo-feedback loop."

Iteration Rhythm (a two-week cycle)

1 2 3Monday: iteration planning meeting → determine what this iteration will do; Tuesday-Thursday: development + continuous testing; Friday: demo + retrospective + priority adjustment

Core Activities in Each Iteration

ActivityFrequencyRoles InvolvedPurpose
Daily standup15 minutes dailyFDE + point of contactSync progress, surface blockers
Iteration planningEvery two weeksFDE + business + technical teamsSet the iteration's goals and tasks
Iteration demoEvery two weeksFDE + business team + sponsorShowcase results, collect feedback
Iteration retrospectiveEvery two weeksFDE + core teamSummarize lessons learned, improve the process
Code reviewOngoingFDE + customer technical teamEnsure code quality, enable knowledge transfer

Deliverables

Each iteration delivers:

  • Runnable incremental features: new features completed in this iteration
  • Iteration demo record: demo content, feedback, action items
  • Updated documentation: technical docs, user manuals, etc.

At the end of the entire stage:

  • A complete functional system: all planned features fully developed
  • Test report: functional, performance, and security test results
  • User operation manual: a usage guide for business users
  • Operations Manual: An Operations Guide for Technical Teams

Key Points

  • Small steps, fast pace, timely feedback: Don't wait until it's "done" to review—review results every week
  • Priorities can be adjusted: If the business side discovers new, more important requirements, the priorities of the next iteration can be adjusted
  • The client team must participate in development: It's not the FDE coding alone in isolation—the client's technical team should do code reviews and testing together
  • Quality cannot be compromised: Fast iteration doesn't mean poor quality—every iteration must include complete testing
The standard development cycle for an FDE organization is 6 weeks to deliver an end-to-end AI workflow in production. The first 2 weeks are diagnosis + prototyping, and the last 4 weeks are development + iteration. This rhythm has been validated across many projects—it ensures both speed and quality.

Phase 4: Launch and Validation Period (Week 6)

After development is complete, the project enters the launch and effectiveness validation phase.

In traditional outsourcing, launch means "deploy it and you're done." For an FDE, launch means "not only getting it up, but also getting it running and delivering results."

Core Tasks

TaskDescriptionRoles Involved
Production environment deploymentDeploy the system to the production environmentFDE + operations team
Data cutover/integrationConnect real data and verify data accuracyFDE + data team
User trainingTrain business users on how to use the systemFDE + business owner
Trial run observationObserve the system after launch and address issues promptlyFDE + everyone
Effectiveness validationVerify actual results against success metricsFDE + sponsor
Issue fixes and optimizationFix issues based on the trial runFDE

Deliverables

  • Production-ready system: Officially launched and running
  • Training materials: User manuals, video tutorials, FAQs
  • Effectiveness validation report: Comparative analysis of actual results vs. expected goals
  • Launch issue list and fix log: Issues discovered during launch and how they were handled

Key Points

  • Launch is not the finish line—it's the starting point: The first week after launch is the most critical; monitor operations closely
  • Have a rollback plan: If something goes wrong, you should be able to quickly revert to the old system
  • User training must be thorough: No matter how good the system is, it's useless if users don't know how to use it
  • Effectiveness validation must be objective: Let the data speak, not "it feels pretty good"—check against the success metrics defined at the start of the project, one by one
The core goal of this phase is to "ensure the system actually works, is actually being used, and actually delivers results." Many projects die from "launched but unused"—this is exactly where the FDE model shines: launch isn't the end; meeting the effectiveness targets is.

Phase 5: Knowledge Transfer and Handover Period (Weeks 6-8)

This is one of the biggest differences between the FDE model and traditional outsourcing.

In traditional outsourcing, handover means "hand over the code + hand over the docs," and then goodbye. FDE knowledge transfer means "teaching hands-on, ensuring the client team can independently maintain and iterate."

Core Tasks

TaskDescriptionRoles Involved
Code walkthroughExplain the code architecture and implementation logic module by moduleFDE + client technical team
Operations trainingDeployment, monitoring, alerting, troubleshootingFDE + operations team
Iteration methodology trainingHow to continue iterating features using agile methodsFDE + product/technical team
Documentation completionComplete all technical and operations documentationFDE (with client team participation)
Handover acceptanceConfirm that all knowledge and documentation have been deliveredFDE + project sponsor
Ongoing support agreementDetermine the support arrangements after the project endsFDE + client

Deliverables

  • Complete codebase: Source code with standardized comments
  • Comprehensive technical documentation: Architecture docs, API docs, database docs, operations manual
  • Knowledge transfer confirmation checklist: Item-by-item confirmation of knowledge transfer completion
  • Ongoing support plan: If needed, agree on post-project support arrangements and costs

Three Levels of Knowledge Transfer

LevelContentImportance
Level 1: ArtifactsCode, documentation, toolsThe foundation—essential
Level 2: MethodsHow to do things, why they're done this way, how to troubleshoot problemsCore—determines whether the team can maintain the system independently
Level 3: MindsetHow decisions are made, the logic behind trade-offs, future evolution directionThe highest level—determines whether the team can continue iterating

Many outsourcing projects only reach Level 1; FDE projects must reach Level 2, and excellent FDEs can reach Level 3.

Key Points

  • Knowledge transfer starts on day one: It's not something done at the end of the project—it happens in parallel throughout the process
  • Replace "telling" with "doing": Don't just explain—have the client team do it hands-on while the FDE guides them
  • Have a handover checklist: Check items one by one to make sure nothing is missed
  • Leave a safety net: Agree on post-project support arrangements, such as 1 month of free support or on-call support billed hourly

Efficiency Advantages of Iterative Development

Why do FDEs use agile iteration instead of waterfall? Because the iterative model is more efficient overall:

MetricWaterfall DevelopmentFDE Agile IterationImprovement
Time to first usable feature deliveryEnd of project (after 3-6 months)Week 2-380-90% earlier
Response time to requirement changes2-4 weeks (change process)1-3 days (handled in the next iteration)80-90% shorter
Time to discover a wrong directionMid-to-late project (large losses)Checked every two weeks (small losses)5-10x faster course correction
Customer engagementLow (only at the beginning and end)High (weekly communication)Significantly improved
Final product fit60-70% (requirements changed but weren't tracked)85-95% (continuously adjusted throughout)20-30% improvement
Rework rate20-30% (problems discovered all at once at the end)5-10% (corrected promptly during the process)60-70% reduction
Team moraleLow (no visible results for a long time)High (sense of accomplishment every week)Significantly improved
Data sources: Aggregated agile development industry research (Standish Group Chaos Report, Forrester Agile Benchmark) and FDE project practice data. For AI projects, where requirements are highly uncertain, the advantages of the iterative model are even more pronounced—the failure rate of waterfall AI projects exceeds 70%, while the agile iteration model can achieve a success rate above 80%.

Key Success Factors for FDE Projects

Based on lessons learned from many projects, several key factors drive FDE project success:

1. A clear Sponsor with decision-making authority on the customer side

This is the most important factor. Without support from a decision-maker, even the most capable FDE can't move things forward. The Sponsor doesn't need to be involved every day, but must be able to make the call and coordinate resources at critical moments.

2. Data and system access secured in advance

Especially for AI projects, data is the foundation. Before the FDE arrives, data permissions, system access, and API interfaces should be coordinated in advance. Otherwise, the first week is spent entirely on approval processes with nothing getting done.

3. Reliable liaisons with time to invest

FDEs don't come to "do it for you"—they come to "do it with you." If the customer-side liaison is busy with other things every day and can't be reached, the project will definitely be slow.

4. Clear success criteria, aligned by both parties

Define "what counts as success" before the project starts. The more specific, the better—for example, "after the lead scoring system launches, sales follow-up efficiency improves by 20%," rather than "improve sales efficiency."

5. An atmosphere of trust and transparent communication

The FDE model is built on trust. Customers must trust the FDE's professional capabilities, and FDEs must be transparent with customers—raise difficulties promptly instead of hiding them until the end.

FDE agile delivery vs. traditional waterfall delivery: a comprehensive comparison

Many people ask: how is the FDE process different from traditional project management? One table makes it clear:

The essence of the core difference: waterfall assumes "requirements are certain, and success comes from executing the plan"; FDE agile assumes "requirements are uncertain, and require continuous exploration and adjustment along the way."

For AI projects, the latter is clearly closer to reality.

Symptom: as work progresses, requirements keep growing, the project keeps expanding, and timelines keep stretching.

How to avoid it:

  • Set clear priorities for each iteration and only work on the highest-priority items
  • Put new requirements in the backlog and evaluate them in the next iteration
  • Review scope every two weeks to ensure alignment with the goal
  • Remember: doing fewer, better features is more valuable than doing many, rough ones

Pitfall 2: Data quality is too poor

Symptom: the model performs poorly, and after much investigation you find the data is terrible.

How to avoid it:

  • Run a data quality assessment in the first week; don't wait until the development phase to find out
  • If data quality is poor, do data remediation first before talking about AI
  • Manage expectations — garbage in, garbage out; that's a law, not something an FDE can fix

Pitfall 3: Users don't accept or use the system

Symptom: the system is built, but the business team doesn't use it, making it a waste of effort.

How to avoid it:

  • Involve user representatives from day one, rather than pushing the finished product on them
  • Provide thorough training and patiently answer questions
  • Find "seed users" — get a small group using it first to create a demonstration effect
  • Show results with data — when users see it genuinely saves them time and effort, they'll naturally accept it

Pitfall 4: Inadequate knowledge transfer

Symptom: after the FDE leaves, no one can maintain the system, and it gradually falls into disuse.

How to avoid it:

  • Start knowledge transfer from day one, not at the very end
  • Have the client's technical team participate in development throughout, not just look at the code at handover
  • Maintain a detailed handover checklist and confirm each item
  • Leave a 2-4 week "warranty period" with remote FDE support to ensure a smooth transition

Closing thoughts

The FDE delivery process is complex if you want it to be, and simple if you want it to be.

What's complex: it requires FDEs to have a comprehensive mix of skills — technical, business, communication, and project management — to collaborate deeply with the client team, and to keep iterating amid uncertainty.

What's simple: the core logic is just one thing — stand in the client's shoes, take responsibility for the outcome, and get it done.

Traditional outsourcing operates on the logic of "doing what the contract says" — I do what's written in the contract and ignore what isn't. FDE operates on the logic of "delivering results" — do whatever it takes to achieve the goal.

These are two completely different mindsets, and that's the fundamental reason the FDE model is becoming increasingly popular.

❓ FAQ

Q1: Why is the FDE project 6 weeks? Can it be faster? 6 weeks is the standard cycle that FDE agencies have summarized for "single-scenario AI implementation" — 1 week of diagnosis + 1 week of prototyping + 3 weeks of development + 1 week of launch validation. Can it be faster? It depends on project complexity. Simple scenarios (e.g., integrating an AI API) can be done in 2-3 weeks; complex scenarios (e.g., a full CRM AI upgrade) may take 2-3 months. The key is not speed, but that each stage has clear deliverables and decision points, allowing the client to choose to continue, adjust, or stop at any time.

Q2: What if we discover halfway through that the direction is wrong? This is exactly the advantage of the FDE model — cutting losses in time. With a biweekly iteration review, you can assess whether the direction is correct. If the direction turns out to be wrong, you can adjust immediately, or even terminate the project. What you lose is only 2-4 weeks of time and fees, not the huge loss of traditional outsourcing where "after six months of work, everything turns out to be wrong." A good FDE will proactively tell you "this direction may be wrong, should we try a different approach," rather than stubbornly pushing through to the end.

Q3: How much client staffing does an FDE project require? It depends on the project size and stage. Taking a standard 6-week project as an example: the business owner spends about 5-8 hours per week, the technical liaison about 5-10 hours per week, and the sponsor about 1-2 hours per week. In total, that's roughly "0.5 of a person" of workload. This investment is worthwhile — the more the client invests, the better the project results and the more thorough the knowledge transfer. If the client is completely hands-off, what the FDE builds may well not match actual needs.

Q4: How is an FDE project accepted? By what criteria? Unlike traditional outsourcing, which is accepted against a "feature checklist," FDE projects are accepted based on "business results." Quantified success metrics are defined at the start of the project, such as "increase lead conversion rate by 15%," "reduce customer service response time by 50%," or "cut contract review time from 2 days to 2 hours." After launch, the system runs for 2-4 weeks, and the metrics are checked against real data — meeting them means passing. Of course, feature completeness is also part of acceptance, but the core is results, not a feature checklist.

Q5: What if there are new requirements after the project ends? Several options: first, continue on a "per person-day/hour" basis, engaging the FDE for a few days whenever needed; second, a "subscription model," with a fixed number of FDE days per month for continuous iteration; third, a "new project model," where new requirements are handled as a separate FDE project. Many companies choose the subscription model — it's like having a senior expert on call, used when needed and not wasted when not.
