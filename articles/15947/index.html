AI Makes Distance Unaffordable

Michael Couch | couch.cx · 2026-11-09 · 4 min read

Summary

Michael Couch argues that AI has collapsed the cost of building software, workflows, and campaigns, so the traditional distance between agencies and clients—once a tolerable tax—is now a fatal bottleneck. When execution is cheap, the main risk becomes building the wrong thing at speed. He points to OpenAI and Palantir's forward-deployed engineering model as proof that partners must work beside operators, in production, rather than behind briefs and handoffs. The scarce assets are sustained context, authority to execute, critical judgment about when not to deploy AI agents, and shared accountability. Continuous co-creation replaces the agency's traditional 'reveal', and agencies that keep their distance will be automated into irrelevance.

Key points

  • Cheap building makes misunderstanding the primary business risk.
  • OpenAI and Palantir's FDE model shows proximity beats remote delivery.
  • Four scarce assets: context, execution authority, judgment, shared accountability.
  • Continuous co-creation loops replace the traditional agency reveal.
  • AI eliminates agency-client distance, not the agency itself.

When building software, workflows, and campaigns was slow and expensive, distance between an agency and its client was an acceptable tax. With AI-native build loops and forward-deployed engineers, execution capacity is abundant. The scarce constraint is now context, judgment, and trust. As the cost of building falls, the cost of misunderstanding rises.

For decades, the physical and organisational separation between clients and their agency partners was justified by the sheer friction of production.

Building a custom marketing interface, standing up an attribution pipeline, or launching an omnichannel campaign took quarters of engineering, design, and media planning. In that world, an agency sitting three miles or three time zones away—working behind a wall of briefs, Jira tickets, and milestone reviews—was tolerable. The latency of communication was dwarfed by the latency of implementation.

That foundation has evaporated.

Today, an AI-native practitioner can research, architect, prototype, and deploy a working system in days. When implementation speed collapses to the speed of thought, distance stops being an operational buffer and becomes a catastrophic bottleneck.

SCQA (at a glance)

Situation: Foundation models, agentic runtimes, and vibe coding have driven the marginal cost of software, copy, and workflow assembly toward zero. Complication: Traditional agency models still rely on serial handoffs and distant communication—introducing latency and context decay that destroy value. Question: Where does competitive advantage live when execution capacity is commoditized? Answer: In operational proximity. The bottleneck is no longer how fast an agency can build; it is how intimately the partner understands the client's real constraints, data realities, and human outcomes. As building gets cheaper, misunderstanding becomes fatal.

As the cost of building falls, the cost of misunderstanding rises

When building was expensive, the primary business risk was building too slowly. You invested capital cautiously, ran elaborate feasibility studies, and held endless discovery workshops to ensure that expensive engineering capacity was not wasted.

When building is cheap, the primary business risk inverts: it is building the wrong thing at hyperspeed.

A team isolated from the real work can now generate fifty plausible marketing agents, twenty web apps, and a hundred creative variants by lunchtime. But if they do not understand the regulatory nuances of the client's customer data, or the reality of the operator handling exceptions on a Tuesday, they have simply industrialised error.

As I argued in The End of Software Built by Strangers, the proxy tax—the cost of translating intent through layers of intermediaries who do not understand the work—is the single greatest drag on enterprise performance. In an AI-native world, that tax is unaffordable.

The Forward-Deployed lesson: OpenAI, Palantir, and the agency

The technology industry has already learned this lesson under a different name: Forward Deployed Engineering (FDE).

Consider how OpenAI deploys its frontier teams into enterprise clients today. They do not ship a software SDK and send an invoice. They dispatch forward-deployed engineers who sit shoulder-to-shoulder with the client’s underwriters, claims adjusters, or supply-chain directors.

These engineers do not ask for a sanitized requirement document. They observe the actual screens, the broken integrations, the spreadsheets hidden beneath the official CRM, and the edge cases that never appear in compliance manuals. They build the harness right on the line, test it against live production inputs, reach verifiable utility, and transfer operational ownership back to the client team.

Agencies should not mechanically copy a software company's job descriptions. But they must internalize the underlying philosophy:

The most valuable partner is not the one who retreats to build in secret, but the one who stands beside the operator at the moment the work occurs.

I detailed this architecture in The FDE Frontier. It applies just as powerfully to creative direction, performance marketing, and CRM as it does to deep learning.

The four scarce assets in an AI-native partnership

If raw execution capacity is no longer scarce, what is?

  • 1. Sustained context: The deep, unwritten knowledge of how the client’s organization actually functions: which metrics are political, which systems are fragile, and how real customers react under stress. You cannot acquire this context through an RFP. You acquire it through tenure and presence.
  • 2. Authority to execute: Having the trust and security clearance to touch production environments, query the actual data warehouse, and deploy experimental workflows without six weeks of committee approvals.
  • 3. Critical judgment: Knowing when not to deploy an AI agent. Understanding where probabilistic outputs create unacceptable risk, and recognizing that an elegant, deterministic script or an honest human conversation is often superior to a complex autonomous pipeline.
  • 4. Shared accountability: Willingness to be judged on whether the system actually solved the customer's problem, rather than whether the agency delivered the agreed number of assets on time.

Continuous co-creation replaces the grand reveal

The traditional agency rhythm culminated in "the reveal": curtains pulled back, dramatic keynote slides, and an emotional pitch designed to persuade an executive committee.

In an AI-native partnership, the reveal is dead.

Instead, work happens in continuous co-creation loops. The strategist, the builder, and the client operator sit in the same harness. They run an autoresearch loop overnight. They inspect the synthetic results over morning coffee. They adjust prompt constraints and data feeds by noon. They launch to a live customer cohort by mid-afternoon.

There is no need for a presentation to explain what was built, because the client was in the room while it was being shaped.

AI does not eliminate the agency. It eliminates the distance between the agency and the client. The partners who insist on maintaining that distance will find themselves automated out of relevance. The partners who step into the trench, embrace forward-deployed intimacy, and own the loop will become the most valuable allies an enterprise can have.
