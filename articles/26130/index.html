How to Conduct an AI Data Readiness Assessment: A Practical Guide for FDEs

Original title: AI 数据就绪度评估怎么做：FDE 实操指南

FDE Times · 2026-10-26 · 6 min read

Summary

This guide argues that forward deployed engineers should assess a client's real data for AI readiness before committing to a launch date, not mid-project. Dashboards running fine does not mean data is AI-ready, and readiness always depends on the specific use case. The author proposes scoring five attributes—accessibility, consistency, completeness, accuracy, timeliness—on a 2/1/0 scale per checklist item, with any standard below 70% treated as a blocking gap. A single failing standard should halt development regardless of other scores. Using a parts distributor example (only 124 of 200 orders had ship dates), the article walks through the full process and stresses asking data governance questions—ownership, approval, sensitive fields—in the first week.

Key points

  • Score data readiness per use case, not in general.
  • Five attributes scored 2/1/0; below 70% is a gap.
  • One failing standard should stop development.
  • Ask data governance questions in week one.
  • Don't wait for perfect data; fix the weakest standard first.

Before committing to a launch date, score the client's real data against a specific use case—don't wait until development is halfway done to discover the gaps.

Key Takeaways

  • A dashboard running smoothly does not prove the data is AI-ready.
  • Readiness depends on the use case: the same data may be sufficient for one question and insufficient for another.
  • Score each item: failing even one core criterion is enough to halt development before it starts.

In your second week on-site, after a smooth demo, the head of operations asks: "So can we go to production within three months?" The agent in the demo answered almost every question correctly. But it was running on a CSV file that an analyst cleaned for you last Friday.

Whether you answer "yes" or "not yet" at that moment will determine the entire project's trajectory going forward. An article on the Varsity blog argues that the work of a Forward Deployed Engineer (FDE) starts with real production data, along with security reviews and access controls. And production data is often messy, locked behind permissions, and held by people who have no particular reason to help you.

If you discover this halfway through development, every promise you made before becomes a liability. So the FDE's job is to answer the data question before signing the commitment. This can be broken down into clear, ordered steps.

Why do client dashboards mislead you?

The first obstacle is the client's own confidence. Easy.bi observed that most mid-sized companies believe their data quality is good simply because their dashboards are still running, and points out that this does not mean the data is AI-ready. Veeam distinguishes between the two concepts: usage describes activity, while readiness describes capability.

The reason is easy to understand. Dashboards aggregate thousands of rows into a single number, and hundreds of rows of missing values disappear from view. An agent that needs to answer specific questions one by one will stumble precisely on those rows.

Dashboards also mask another thing: readiness is always tied to a specific use case. The same data may be sufficient for one question and insufficient for another. So don't score "the client's data" in general—score "the client's data for task X."

Phos AI Labs lists five attributes of AI-ready data: accessible, consistent, complete, accurate, and timely. Easy.bi uses a different set of 4 criteria, each scored on a 1–5 maturity scale, with an assessment cycle of 5 days.

Phos's five attributes are enough to check the data itself, but they can't tell you whether you're allowed to use it. That question gets its own section, after the example below.

A Complete Example

Imagine a parts distributor that wants an agent to answer two questions from dealers: "Is this part in stock?" and "When will it ship?" Its inventory dashboard has run well for years. You write the use case in one sentence: at the moment a dealer asks, the agent must give the correct inventory level and estimated ship date for each order.

Next, you request direct read access to the order system rather than exported files, then randomly sample 200 orders. Suppose the results are: only 124 orders have an estimated ship date. Two warehouses use two different product coding schemes, and the data is manually exported by one person every night.

Phos scores each checklist item as "yes / partial / no" (2 / 1 / 0 points), aggregates scores by criterion, and treats any criterion below 70% as a gap that must be closed before development. Another vendor, RaftLabs, also uses 70 as the threshold, but out of a total of 100. So this number should be treated as vendor experience, not something mandated by any industry standard.

Take the "complete" criterion as an example, assuming its checklist has three items. Every order has a part code: yes, 2 points. Inventory is recorded by warehouse: only one warehouse is fully recorded, so partial, 1 point. Orders have estimated ship dates: 124 out of 200, i.e. 62%, so partial, 1 point.

That's 4 out of 6 points, about 67%, below the 70% threshold. Note that 62% is not the score; it's only the evidence that makes you choose "partial" over "yes." The two are different measures, and conflating them is the fastest way for a scorecard to lose credibility in front of the client.

A sample scorecard for the five attributes is as follows:

The "timely" row shows why scoring must follow the use case. If the client wants monthly demand forecasts, a one-night lag doesn't matter. But for an agent answering in real time, that lag is enough to make it report wrong inventory.

Should one failing criterion halt the project?

Yes, and that's exactly the point. Phos applies the threshold to each criterion separately, so four good rows can't make up for one bad row. Veeam puts the same idea differently: the ceiling of AI readiness is always determined by the floor of data readiness.

The Revenue Experts newsletter applies this logic to budget approval: every AI project must pass four core data checks, and failing any one of them—no matter how high the scores elsewhere—marks the project as not ready. One of the four checks is: data quality must have been tested within the past 90 days.

For this parts distributor, the simplest question you can ask is: when was the last time anyone checked the ship date field? If no one can answer, the nightly manual export and the 62% figure have already answered for them.

Ask about data governance in week one

The Varsity blog article points out that production data sits behind permissions and has owners. In the example above, ask: can each dealer's contract prices appear in the agent's answers? Who signs off on approval?

If the answer is the legal department, and legal has never heard of this project, you've found your biggest risk. The same article stresses that requirements gathering should be done with the people who actually run operations, so don't just ask the person who signed the contract.

Add three questions to your first requirements session: who owns the data, who approves the model reading the data, and which fields are personal or sensitive data. Ask in week one and it takes one email; discover it in week ten and it may cost you the whole project.

Doing it yourself, and common mistakes

The whole process can be completed within a week. Write the use case in one sentence, request read access to production rather than exported files, sample the data and score the five attributes, then raise the data governance questions. Finally, write a one-page go/no-go memo listing every gap, what it takes to close it, and who is responsible for the fix.

The most common mistake is trusting the client's dashboard. RaftLabs estimates that most organizations rate their own data maturity 2-3 levels higher than reality, and recommends fixing the weakest criterion first. The second mistake is scoring data in general, detached from the use case.

The opposite mistake is also real: waiting for perfect data. Easy.bi found that most mid-sized companies are only at level 2-3, and warns that those who wait for level 5 to start will never deliver.

The worst mistake is doing the assessment after the commitment is signed. By then, every gap you find sounds like an excuse rather than a valuable finding.

This week's exercise

Take a dataset you already have access to and imagine two AI use cases with different tempos: one that needs instant answers, and one that only needs a monthly report. For each use case, score the five attributes on a 2/1/0 scale, then circle the rows where the conclusion changes.

Write the result as a single sentence with numbers, such as "Evaluated data for an agent use case and found only 62% of orders have a shipping date field; recommend closing the gap before development." This sentence is worth putting on your resume; when an FDE job description mentions requirements definition, requirements research, or data assessment, this is the story you should tell.

The next time someone asks "Can we go to production within three months?", you will first ask "For what purpose?" before answering.
