When Not to Hire Forward Deployed Engineers: Five Cases

Falkster.AI · 2026-10-24 · 12 min read

Summary

Part 11 of The FDE Transition argues companies should not hire forward deployed engineers unless a feedback loop exists: someone in product must take what the FDE learns from one customer and ship it into the product. Without that loop, the hire is a premium-priced custom developer. The author lists five cases where the req should not be posted, citing Gartner figures of roughly 2,000 active FDEs with demand around four times that, FDE fees possibly exceeding $200,000 per quarter per use case, and Milos Mandic's estimate that about three in four self-described FDE teams are actually development shops. Each case has a cheaper alternative hire.

Key points

  • The core test: a loop must return field learnings into the product, or you are hiring custom developers at a premium.
  • Five no-hire cases: opinionated product, deals too small, no product owner for handback, buyer scope already clear, and title-following.
  • Gartner counts about 2,000 active FDEs with demand around four times that; DXC had 86 trained against tens of thousands promised.
  • Gartner estimates FDE consulting fees could top $200,000 quarterly for one use case; Looker needed roughly $25,000 ACV to justify the model.
  • Cheaper alternatives exist for each case: implementation engineers, sales engineers, partners, or an internal AI engineer.

Accenture is sending about a thousand forward deployed engineers into its Google business, and this week the first cohort got pointed at the federal government.

Larry Dignan reported it at Constellation Research on October 19. Accenture Federal Services and Google Public Sector are rolling out Gemini Launchpads, and the engineers come with it. Accenture Federal is training its first cohort of Google Cloud-skilled FDEs, drawn from the roughly 1,000 in Accenture's Gemini Enterprise Business Group. Ron Ash, who runs Accenture Federal Services, says they'll help "scale value" across government operations. Thomas Kurian's line is that Accenture engineers "work directly inside customer environments."

The next day Milos Mandic published a number at FDE Hub that he says he can't verify, and I believe anyway: around three in four teams calling themselves FDE teams are development shops. He got it from the teams he's talked to and worked alongside. Then he put it next to Gartner's forecast that fewer than one in five FDE engagements will turn customer needs into product, which implies a share a little above 80 percent, and said he wouldn't rely on either as a measurement. An analyst firm and a practitioner, a few points apart.

So the seat is spreading a thousand at a time, and most of the seats being created aren't the seat. That's the week for the part of this series I've been putting off.

This is part 11 of The FDE Transition. Part 10 was the buyer's contract. This one is for the person deciding whether to post the req at all, on either side of the table.

The short version

  • Don't hire forward deployed engineers unless there's a loop for the work to come back through, and you're willing to pay for it.
  • The seat has one job: absorb what one customer needs, then make the product absorb it, so the next customer needs less of an engineer.
  • If nothing in your company can take the work back, you're hiring custom developers at a premium. Five honest cases where you shouldn't post the req. Your product is opinionated and the work is implementation. Your deals can't carry the seat. Nobody in product will read what comes back. You're the buyer and the scope is already clear.
  • And you're hiring the title because everyone else is, in a market with about 2,000 real ones and demand around four times that.
  • In every case there's a cheaper hire that does the actual job. Name that job and hire for it.

The test comes first

Every part of this series rests on the same mechanism. The loop chapter describes it, part 3 wrote down the packet an FDE owes product, and part 7 put generalization rate on the scorecard. An FDE overfits on purpose for one customer, then someone with authority over the roadmap takes the reusable part and ships it to everyone. Hours per deployment fall. That's the whole business case. Without the second half, you have an engineer building custom software in someone else's building, which is a fine trade, and an old one, with a name and a price already.

Jesse Zhang at Decagon put the harsh version to Tom Dotan at Newcomer in August: embedding software engineers long term just means your software doesn't work. I don't fully agree, because the seat exists to find out what the software needs to do before it can do it. But he's right about the long term. Mandic says it from inside the seat: an FDE who can't leave isn't doing discovery, and a team billing month after month against the same account is a services firm.

So one question before the five cases. Who takes the work back, and what can they take it into? If you can't name the person and the surface, don't post the req.

Case one: the product is opinionated and the work is implementation

First Round Review ran a panel in February on exactly this. Shilpa Balaji ran FDE recruiting at Palantir, and her first question for a founder is "How opinionated are you about what your product should be in the future?" FDEs pay off when the product's shape is still open to discovery. "FDEs are for organic product growth." A SaaS product with a fixed vision and a tight customer profile gets less from the seat, because there's less for the field to find.

Tiffany Siu, First Round's head of talent and a former Palantir recruiter, had the sharper read on the founders who come asking. Most want a software engineer or a post-sales person. If success is measured in deals closed and demos run, "they probably want someone closer to sales, not an FDE."

I'd go further. If the work is onboarding, configuration, and integration against a product that already does what it does, you want implementation engineers, and you should be glad. They're cheaper and there are more of them. The FDE versus sales engineer chapter has the one-question test: does the job start when the contract is signed, or end there? Add a second: when the job's done, did the product change? If no, by design, the seat you're staffing isn't this one.

Case two: the deal can't carry the seat

James Honsa built legal engineering at Ironclad and co-founded Genera. His line on the same panel: "Forward deployed engineering is definitionally an upmarket motion." And the corollary: "You should not be doing this if you believe the end shape of your product is some sort of product-led growth freemium fit."

Frank Bien gave the arithmetic. At Looker, "We needed to be in the neighborhood of $25,000 a year per customer," and "by the time we had 2,000 customers, we could be doing $100 million dollars in ARR." A business shaped like that can't put an engineer in a customer's office for a quarter. So Looker didn't. It ran free trials with pre-sales implementation on the prospect's real data, the cheap cousin of the seat.

About 2,000

Gartner's number is the one to hold next to your contract value. Matt Ashare's report at Channel Dive has the firm estimating that "the bill for FDE consulting fees alone could top $200,000 quarterly" for one use case. The economics chapter ran the margin math. If the deployment can't produce expansion or a reusable product, the engineer is a cost of sale that never comes down, and that's a consultancy's P&L.

Case three: nobody in product will read what comes back

The quiet one, and I think the most common.

The company posts for FDEs. The engineers are good. They land deployments. And every packet they send back goes into a folder nobody owns, because product was never told it had a new input. The product changes chapter lists what has to exist before the first FDE lands: a configuration boundary, a sandbox, an eval harness the customer's cases can join, an observation record, and a feedback path with an owner. Most companies hiring the title this year have none of the five. They have a roadmap and a release train, and the engineer's deployment is a ticket in it.

Honsa saw it from the inside. He described work at Ironclad where "there was no clear path toward a software upside," and the panel's answer was managers who handle the scope creep. I'd put it earlier than the manager. If no PM is named as the reader of the handback, and no quarter has shipped a product change that started in a customer's building, the loop isn't slow. It doesn't exist. Hiring more engineers into the front of a loop with no back is how you get Mandic's three in four.

Case four: you're the buyer and the scope is already clear

Part 10 argued the buyer should contract for the loop, not just the exit. This is the step before: whether to buy the seat at all.

Gartner's advice to buyers, in the same Channel Dive piece, is more conservative than vendors would like. Customers who don't build their own engineering skills may pay "premium rates for work that, once the scope is clear," a traditional services or partner model "could potentially deliver more cost-effectively." Read the condition. Once the scope is clear. An FDE earns the premium on the problem you can't yet write down. After that, you're paying discovery prices for delivery.

So buy the seat for the workflow nobody can specify, with the month-two condition attached: a named person on your side who can change the customer layer when the engineer leaves. For the workflow you can specify, hire a partner or your own engineer and skip the premium. Praveen Jonnala's "backward-deployed domain experts" from part 10 is this case from the CIO's chair.

Case five: you're hiring the title because everyone else is

Here's the arithmetic the hiring plans leave out. Mukul Saha at Gartner counts about 2,000 active FDEs. "Demand is probably quadruple that, so there aren't enough engineering resources available." Against that: Microsoft's 6,000 engineers headed into the field, AWS's $1 billion unit, Accenture's thousand, and this week's federal cohort. DXC committed to training tens of thousands of Claude-certified FDEs and, per Raul Fernandez on the July earnings call, "had 86 on the books and was operating in 57 customer environments."

Nothing wrong with 86. It's an honest number. But if you're hiring 500 into the title this year, the question is who the other 414 are, and in most plans the answer is consultants with a new line on their profile. Saha's warning to product vendors is the one I'd pin above the headcount request: don't build "a large team of consultants sitting on the bench bleeding money." His advice is to train partners instead. Mine is narrower. Size the FDE team to the loop that can absorb its output. The org design chapter said plan to shrink. A team you can't shrink was never an FDE team.

The Accenture federal cohort may be fine, by the way. Dignan's report describes outcome-based agents built on Gemini and integrated into agency systems. That's deployment work, and deployment work is real. I'd only ask that it's priced and measured as deployment. The premium is for the loop.

What this means in the seat, and at the top

If you're the FDE: before you take the seat, ask who reads the handback and what shipped last quarter because of the field. A blank answer is a consultant's job at a consultant's retention.

If you run product: the FDE req on your desk is a claim that your product can absorb what the field finds. If the five changes don't exist, say so before the hire, not after the first packet lands.

If you run the company: the title is cheap and the seat is not. Name the job you actually have open. Implementation, sales engineering, your own AI engineer, or the loop. Then hire for that one.

One thing to try this week: before posting the FDE req, write three lines next to it. The PM who reads the handback. The last product change that started in a customer's building, with a date. The deal size the seat has to pay back. Any blank, post the other req. You'll save the premium, and you'll have told yourself which business you're in.

Frequently asked

When should a company not hire forward deployed engineers?+

Five cases. The product is opinionated and the real work is implementation, so an implementation engineer does the job at the right price. The deals are too small to carry the seat, since forward deployed engineering is an upmarket motion by definition and Gartner estimates FDE fees above $200,000 a quarter for a single use case. Nobody in product owns the handback and nothing in the product can take the pattern back. The buyer's scope is already clear, where ordinary services or a partner deliver more cheaply. And the company is hiring the title because everyone else is, in a market with about 2,000 active FDEs and demand around four times that.

What is the test for whether a company needs FDEs at all?+

Whether there is a loop for the work to come back through. The FDE absorbs what one customer needs and the product absorbs it next, so the following customer needs less of an engineer. That requires a product manager who reads the handback, a configuration boundary the pattern can land in, and deals large enough to pay for the engineer's time. If none of those exist, the company is hiring custom developers at a premium. Milos Mandic's estimate is that around three in four self-described FDE teams are development shops, and Gartner's forecast implies a share a little above 80 percent.

What should a company hire instead of a forward deployed engineer?+

The job that is actually open. If the product is standardized and the work is onboarding, configuration, and integration, hire implementation engineers. If success is measured in deals closed and demos run, hire sales engineers. If the buyer's scope is clear, use ordinary services or a partner. If the company wants its own people to own the trade-offs, hire an internal AI engineer and have the vendor's engineer train that person. Each of these is cheaper than an FDE and is the job most FDE requisitions describe.

Why is forward deployed engineering an upmarket motion?+

Because an engineer embedded with one customer costs more than a small contract returns. James Honsa, formerly head of legal engineering at Ironclad, told First Round Review that forward deployed engineering is definitionally an upmarket motion and that companies expecting a product-led or freemium end state should not do it. Frank Bien built Looker around roughly $25,000 a year per customer and 2,000 customers, a model with no room for a visiting engineer. Gartner's estimate is that FDE consulting fees alone could top $200,000 a quarter per use case.

What did Accenture and Google announce for federal agencies?+

On October 19, 2026, Larry Dignan reported at Constellation Research that Accenture Federal Services and Google Public Sector are rolling out Gemini Launchpads, with embedded forward deployed engineers building outcome-based agents on Gemini for agencies. Accenture Federal Services is training its first cohort of Google Cloud-skilled FDEs, drawing on about 1,000 FDEs in Accenture's Gemini Enterprise Business Group. Ron Ash, CEO of Accenture Federal Services, said the engineers will help scale value across government operations, and Thomas Kurian said Accenture engineers work directly inside customer environments.

How many real forward deployed engineers are there?+

Gartner's Mukul Saha, as reported by Matt Ashare at Channel Dive on September 30, 2026, counts about 2,000 active FDEs and says demand is probably quadruple that. DXC committed to training tens of thousands of Claude-certified FDEs and, per CEO Raul Fernandez, had 86 on the books as of July 31, working in 57 customer environments. Anyone hiring the title at scale is mostly hiring people who are not yet FDEs.

What is The FDE Transition series?+

A twice-weekly series on falkster.com for forward deployed engineers and the leaders building around them. Part 1 is the thesis that the FDE is the Product Builder arriving from services. Parts 3 through 6 cover the handback to product, the codebase contract with engineering, the promise with sales, and month two with customer success. Part 7 is the scorecard, part 8 the career out of the seat, part 9 comp and equity, and part 10 the buyer's contract. Part 11 is when not to hire FDEs. The parts that follow cover the FDE as the author of the eval and what the tooling changed.
