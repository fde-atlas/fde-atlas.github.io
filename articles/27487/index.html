Today's Judgment

What's most notable today isn't that AI agents gained yet another feature, but that China has started building out the transaction infrastructure for "once agents can act, who authorizes, who pays, who bears liability, and how to trace accountability": Ant Group launched APASS for agent commercial activities, linking agent identity, authorization, risk control, and transaction records into a single chain.

On the same day, two signals about "people" echoed each other: Beijing Shijingshan's "No.1 Galaxy" OPC community showcased an organizational sample of a vertical ecosystem; meanwhile, Palantir's former FDE lead published a piece cutting to the industry's essence—if an FDE only solves the last mile for individual clients without writing insights back into the product, it's still just a consulting firm under a different name.

Key Signals

Ant Group launches APASS: agent identity, authorization, risk control, and transaction records linked into a chain of accountability

  • Sources: Alipay APASS official product page; China Securities Journal
  • At the Bund Summit, Ant Group launched APASS (trusted agent identity infrastructure) for agent commercial activities: it establishes a three-layer model of static identity, runtime identity, and accountable-party identity, continuously verifying environments, instances, code components, commercial authorization, and behavioral permissions, and chaining together pre-transaction authorization, in-transaction risk control, and post-transaction record-keeping. On-site disclosures stated that over 1 million agents are already connected, covering 442 service providers.
  • Why it matters: When agents can invoke services and make payments, the delivery target upgrades from "an application that answers questions" to "a business participant with identity, permissions, budget, and proof of fulfillment". The FDE task list must expand accordingly: principal binding, authorization scope, quotas, exception escalation, logging, acceptance, and dispute handling. A complete agent transaction accountability chain has nine segments: principal → agent identity → accountable party → authorization and quotas → service confirmation → payment → fulfillment → business acceptance and credential return → dispute/liability/rollback—if any segment has only technical invocation without a principal, evidence, and an accountable person, it doesn't count as a production-ready commercial loop. Note: connection and activation figures are cumulative conference metrics, not equivalent to production activity or successful transactions.

Beijing Shijingshan's "No.1 Galaxy" OPC community: an organizational sample of a vertical ecosystem

  • Source: People's Daily Online Beijing Channel | Original link
  • Shijingshan's "No.1 Galaxy" OPC (one-person company) community organized its CIFTIS showcase around "tool enablement—asset operations—frontier achievements—immersive experiences—individual creation," with nearly 30 companies presenting over 60 exhibits; the community says it has nearly 40 companies onboarded, focused on the vertical direction of "AI + sci-fi," forming a closed loop of IP sources, AI production tools, and creator bases.
  • Why it matters: This is an observable sample of China's OPC (super-individual/one-person company) ecosystem—its lesson lies not in scale but in vertical focus: rather than generically listing all AI resources, it concentrates on one direction to build a deep closed loop. Evaluate such communities with a seven-stage funnel: onboarding → activity → real demand → deals closed → delivery → acceptance and settlement → repeat purchases. Onboarded company and exhibit counts prove supply aggregation; only the last four stages prove a commercial closed loop. Note: active companies, orders, and revenue were not disclosed.

Palantir's former FDE lead: if you only solve the last mile, you're still a consulting firm

  • Source: Latent Space | Original link
  • Vinoo Ganesh, former FDE at Palantir and former head of Project Frontline, wrote about FDE best practices (the rotation program had about 250 participants). His core claim: FDEs should act as an extension of the product team, going on-site to collect the "nouns and verbs" of real business (business objects and action semantics), and writing last-mile insights back into general capabilities that all subsequent clients can access.
  • Why it matters: This statement hits the industry's soft spot—the dividing line between FDE and consulting isn't being on-site, it's writing back. The measure of replicability is the second client's delivery cycle, reuse ratio, and quality changes, not project count. The on-site insight write-back flywheel: observe real users → collect business nouns/verbs → align data semantics and accountability → complete a minimal delivery → client validation → write back into general products/templates → reuse with the next client and measure cycle time. On-site delivery captures high-value context; only through write-back and reuse can project revenue accumulate into product assets.

Implications for FDE Practitioners

  • Build an "accountability object table" for transactional agent projects: requester, provider, agent, accountable party, authorization scope, budget quota, fulfillment evidence, acceptor, dispute handling, refund and rollback—assign each of the ten items to a specific person.
  • Stop using person-months as your core product language: keep labor costs in internal pricing worksheets, but restructure client proposals as "paid diagnostic package → single-scenario delivery package → operations support package → reuse/revenue-sharing mechanism," each with clearly defined deliverables and acceptance criteria—scaling by person-days will be locked down by headcount and acceptance disputes.
  • Collect "nouns—verbs—accountability" in every project: nouns correspond to business objects and data semantics, verbs to actions, permissions, and state changes, accountability to confirmation, exceptions, and sign-off. Every project must write back into general assets—this is the only path from consulting to a product company.
