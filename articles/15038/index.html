A problem others gave up on · FDE behavioral question

FDE Interview Prep · 2026 · 2 min read

Summary

This is a practice guide for an FDE behavioral interview question about solving a problem others gave up on. Citing First Round Review's February 2026 report on former Palantir recruiting lead Shilpa Balaji, it notes grit is a hallmark of strong FDEs, but persistence alone is insufficient. The article outlines an answer structure: name prior attempts and their cost, identify the shared wrong assumption, present a new hypothesis with a cheap test and explicit time budget, prove the fix with measurements, and leave behind a monitor or runbook. It warns against hero stories with no method and lists likely follow-up questions.

Key points

  • Grit matters, but answers must explain what you did differently.
  • Structure: prior attempts, shared assumption, new hypothesis, cheap test.
  • State a time budget and a stopping point agreed with your lead.
  • Prove the fix against the symptom and leave a monitor or runbook.
  • Avoid unmanaged risk and disparaging colleagues who gave up.

How to answer

First Round Review reported, in February 2026, that Shilpa Balaji, a former Palantir recruiting lead, called grit probably the hallmark quality of the best FDEs she worked with at Palantir. Source 1So You Want to Hire a Forward Deployed EngineerPublisherFirst Round ReviewSource typenews report The question tests grit, but a strong answer also explains why you got further than those before you. “I kept at it longer” means you had more hours, not a better idea.

  • Who gave up, what they tried, and what it cost. Name the attempts: “two engineers had each spent a sprint tuning the retry logic”, and what it cost the customer while it sat.
  • The assumption every attempt shared. Maybe the frame: everyone treated it as a performance problem when it was a data problem. Or ownership: it sat between two teams, so it was nobody’s sprint.
  • Your new hypothesis, the cheap test, and the budget. What was new: a packet capture, a query nobody had run, an hour with the night-shift operator. Say the time you asked for and your stopping point: “I asked my lead for three days; if the capture showed nothing, I’d hand it back with what I’d ruled out.”
  • The fix and the proof. What changed, measured against the symptom people had stopped chasing.
  • What you left behind. The monitor, test or runbook that stops it coming back.

Say the pivot as one sentence: “Everyone had asked why the job was slow; I asked why it was only slow on Mondays.” The rest supports it.

Keep it fair: credit what their dead ends ruled out, and if the problem was parked for a good reason, say what changed to make it worth your time. The trap is the hero story with no method, late nights and a lucky break. If you can’t say what you did differently, pick another story.

Follow-ups

What the interviewer may ask next, once your first answer is on the table.

  • What had the people before you ruled out, and how did that narrow your search?
  • How long were you prepared to give your new hypothesis before you would have stopped too?
  • Why was it still unsolved? Was it hard, or was it nobody’s job?
  • What did you stop doing to make time for it, and who agreed to that?

Where answers go wrong

  • Tells a story of late nights and stubbornness with no new hypothesis, tool or measurement, so nothing explains why they got further than the people before them.
  • Makes the colleagues who gave up sound careless, or calls a problem abandoned when it had been parked for a good reason.
  • Spends weeks on it without telling their lead or the customer what was set aside, so even the win reads as unmanaged risk.

Answer this in two minutes

Write the answer you would say out loud. The clock starts with your first word.

Illustrative answer about a fictional project

I was an engineer at a company that runs payroll processing for mid-sized employers. Every night our system put each client’s payment file in an SFTP folder, and each client’s bank collected it from there.
