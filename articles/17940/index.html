Designing Business Applications on Your Own Cloud | Permissions, Data Location, and Personal Information

Original title: 業務アプリを自社クラウドに置く設計｜権限・データの所在・個人情報

TIMEWELL · 2026-09-29 · 25 min read

Summary

TIMEWELL's Hamamoto Ryuta argues that once a company decides to rebuild a business app in-house, hosting it in its own cloud tenant (AWS, Azure, GCP) is usually easier to operate than SaaS: unified identity and access management, direct data connections, self-held audit logs, and control over change timing. The trade-offs are legal and operational: whether handling personal data counts as entrustment depends on who can access it, not where it is stored; choosing a Japan region does not remove the duty to understand the cloud vendor's home-country regulations; and patching, monitoring, backup, and deletion become the company's responsibility. The article cites NIST SP 800-145, AWS's SaaS Lens and shared responsibility model, Japan's PPC guidelines, and the 2026 APPI amendment, and describes how the company's FDEs deliver such migrations in 45–90 days, ending when the client team can run operations independently.

Key points

  • Own-tenant hosting offers unified permissions, data access, and audit logs.
  • Entrustment status hinges on who can access personal data, not location.
  • A domestic region does not eliminate foreign-regulation obligations.
  • Patching, monitoring, backup, and deletion shift fully to the company.
  • The firm's FDE engagements run 45–90 days, ending at client self-sufficiency.

Hello, I am Ryuta Hamamoto of TIMEWELL Corporation.

The choice of canceling expensive SaaS and owning your own business applications. In this series, I have covered the decision criteria, how to identify which business processes are worth building, a five-year total cost estimate, and the steps for cancellation and migration. In this fifth installment, I will address the final remaining question: where to host the rebuilt application.

I will state my conclusion first. In my view, once you have decided to own the application yourself, hosting it in your company's contracted cloud environment—what this article calls a "self-managed tenant"—is often the easier option to operate. This is because you can align it with your internal identity platform and permissions, connect it more easily with existing data, and keep audit logs in-house. However, changing the hosting location also moves the responsibility for handling personal information and operations along with it. It is not simply a matter of choosing a domestic region. For those in information systems departments, security and personal information roles, and ISMS offices, I will cover both the benefits and the burdens based on primary sources. If you want to start from the question of whether you should cancel at all, please begin with the decision criteria article in this series.

How does multi-tenant SaaS differ from a self-managed tenant?

Let me start by clarifying the terminology. NIST SP 800-145, the definition of cloud computing widely cited by the US National Institute of Standards and Technology, lists "resource pooling" as one of the characteristics of cloud computing, stating that the provider's computing resources are provided to multiple consumers in a "multi-tenant model" 1. The same document defines SaaS as a model in which the consumer does not manage or control the underlying network, servers, operating systems, storage, or even individual application capabilities (with the possible exception of limited user-specific configuration settings). In other words, what SaaS users can touch is essentially just the settings screen.

The "self-managed tenant" referred to in this article means placing your business applications and data as your company's assets within an account on a cloud platform your company contracts (AWS, Microsoft Azure, Google Cloud, etc.). While the cloud platform itself is the cloud provider's shared infrastructure, the application administrator, the data owner, and the person granting permissions are all your own company. This also differs from "single-tenant SaaS," where the vendor prepares a dedicated environment for each customer. Even with a dedicated environment, if the vendor is the one operating it, the keys to the application and data remain in the vendor's hands.

AWS's Well-Architected "SaaS Lens" provides design guidance for SaaS providers. It calls the model where all tenants share infrastructure "pool" and the model where each tenant is separated "silo," and lays out the advantages and disadvantages of each 23. The disadvantages listed for the pool model include the "noisy neighbor" problem where other tenants' load affects yours, the difficulty of tracking costs per tenant, the broad blast radius of failures (the original text states that a pool model failure "will likely impact all tenants in the system"), and friction in scenarios where compliance or regulatory requirements demand strict resource isolation. The silo model is the mirror image: it makes it easier to meet isolation requirements and narrows the impact of failures, but costs increase and simultaneous updates across all tenants become harder. Although this document is written for companies building SaaS, reading it from the user's side reveals what trade-offs the service you are using has made.

What becomes easier to operate when hosted in a self-managed tenant?

When I say "easier to operate," there are four things I mean.

The first is being able to align the identity platform and permissions. If you use ten SaaS products, you have ten management screens and ten permission designs. Every time someone joins, transfers, or leaves, you have to fix accounts on each screen, and somewhere an account gets forgotten and left active. With a self-managed tenant, you can set permissions using the same groups as your internal identity platform. For example, AWS IAM Identity Center is described as being able to connect to an existing identity provider and synchronize directory users and groups 4. Once a transfer is entered into HR data, the permissions for business applications change through the same flow. This consolidation also pays off when explaining access rights reviews during ISMS internal audits.

The second is being able to connect easily with existing data. Quote ledgers, internal FAQs, application records. If such data is in the same tenant, you can connect without going through external APIs. When connecting SaaS products to each other, the fields and call volumes available for integration tend to be limited by the vendor's API and plans, and some data cannot be extracted without a higher-tier plan. I wrote about how to identify which business processes to bring in-house in the second article of the series, but I feel that the processes that make good candidates are often precisely those whose value emerges only when connected with other data.

The third is being able to keep audit logs in-house. Who did what, when, and to which data. With SaaS, the vendor decides how much of this record can be exported and how long it is retained. With a self-managed tenant, your company decides the storage location and period. AWS CloudTrail is a service that records actions taken by users, roles, and AWS services as events, and the event history viewable without any setup covers the past 90 days of management events 5. If you want to retain them longer, you configure the storage destination yourself. Put the other way, if you do nothing, they become invisible after 90 days—this is one of the items that should be decided at the design stage.

The fourth benefit is that you become less at the mercy of vendors' specification changes. With SaaS, screen changes, feature deprecations, plan restructuring, and terms-of-service revisions arrive at times convenient to the vendor. Japan's Civil Code provides that standard form contracts can be modified without individual consent when the changes conform to the counterparty's general interest or are reasonable in light of the necessity and appropriateness of the change (Article 548-4) 6. Whether a B2B SaaS's terms qualify as a standard form contract is judged case by case, but at minimum you need to read the contract on the premise that "the vendor can change the terms." With your own tenant's application, your company decides when changes happen. That said, changes such as runtime end-of-life also come from the cloud platform side. The number of parties that can disrupt you decreases, but it does not go to zero.

All four issues share the same root: the application and data live in the same place as your company's other systems. This is why I believe that if you are going to drop SaaS and rebuild, designing the system including where things live makes the rebuild far more worthwhile.

Does personal data handling count as outsourcing? The deciding factor is "who can touch it"

Next, let's sort out personal information. I think this is the part most easily misunderstood.

The Personal Information Protection Commission's "Guidelines Q&A" Q7-53 states that whether using a cloud service constitutes third-party provision requiring the data subject's consent, or outsourcing, is judged not by "whether the stored electronic data contains personal data" but by "whether the business operator providing the cloud service comes to handle the personal data" 7. As examples of cases where the operator is deemed not to handle it, it cites situations where the contract stipulates that the operator will not handle personal data stored on servers and appropriate access controls are in place. In such cases, no provision occurs, so neither the data subject's consent nor supervision of the outsourcing party under Article 25 is required. This is the framework sometimes called the "cloud exception" in practice.

However, the following Q7-54 draws a line: even when no provision occurs, the business operator using the service "must take appropriate safety management measures as part of the safety management measures it should implement itself" 8. In other words, while the duty to supervise an outsourcing party disappears, what your own company must do remains unchanged.

So what about SaaS? On March 25, 2024, the Personal Information Protection Commission issued a warning in response to a case where a cloud service provider was judged to be a personal information handling business operator 9. In that case, a business system suffered unauthorized access, and the personal data of employees of customers of many client companies was encrypted. The Commission considered three factors: the terms of service stated that the operator could monitor, analyze, and investigate data when it judged necessary for maintenance or operations; the operator held maintenance IDs and could access users' personal data without technical access controls; and after exchanging a confirmation document, the operator actually handled personal data. Based on this, the Commission called for "clarifying as objectively as possible" the agreed safety management measures in the terms and contracts, and verifying them through periodic reporting. With SaaS, as long as the vendor operates the application, the structure tends to be one where the vendor can touch users' data through maintenance and support. I read this warning as the administration's way of signaling exactly that.

Here is the point you cannot miss when considering your own tenant. The Q7-53 criterion was not "where the data lives" but "who handles the personal data." If so, even with your own tenant, if an external contractor responsible for development or maintenance can touch production personal data, the relationship with that contractor should properly be treated as outsourcing. The Q&A does not directly address this scenario; this is my interpretation applying the Q7-53 criteria and the warning's considerations. But an external contractor who can view production data with a maintenance ID stands in a position very similar to the case in the warning. This applies without exception even when FDEs (Forward Deployed Engineers—engineers who develop on-site at the customer) like those at our company handle maintenance.

The 2026 legal amendment also comes into play in this classification. The amendment to the Personal Information Protection Act, passed on July 10, 2026 and promulgated on July 17, explicitly imposes on business operators entrusted with the handling of personal information the duty not to handle it beyond the scope necessary to perform the entrusted work 1011. It also introduces a mechanism that, in principle, exempts the entrusted party from its duties when it does not decide the handling method itself (e.g., cases where it merely mechanically processes data per the entruster's instructions), provided the outsourcing contract agrees on the full details of the handling method and on measures for the entruster to grasp the situation (such as prompt reporting upon learning of a leak). However, the duty not to handle data beyond the necessary scope and the duty of safety management remain. Enforcement is within two years of promulgation, and details are still being finalized through cabinet orders and commission rules. Whether work involving the contractor's own judgment, such as maintenance, falls under this exemption will need to be confirmed once the rules are in place.

Even so, the direction is clear: the weight given to writing "what is handled and by what method" and "how the situation is monitored" into outsourcing contracts will increase. If you bring an external maintenance contractor into your own tenant, putting this language into the contract now should spare you a scramble when the law takes effect.

The above is written as a general discussion. Please confirm with your legal department or specialists whether a specific configuration constitutes outsourcing.

Even choosing a domestic region, there is still something to verify

You often hear the phrase "it's safe if it's in a domestic region." I think it is half right and half misleading.

I will write the correct half first. In the cloud, you can choose where to place your data at the country or region level. NIST's definition also states that while users normally do not know the exact location of resources, they may be able to specify higher-level units such as country, state, or data center 1. AWS has regions in Japan: Tokyo (four Availability Zones) and Osaka (three) 12.

The half that causes misunderstanding is in the Personal Information Protection Commission's Q10-25 13. When using the cloud of a business operator located abroad, even if the operator does not handle personal data, the company using it "must understand the personal information protection system of that foreign country and take necessary safety management measures, because personal data will be handled in a foreign country." The answer continues: "This applies even when personal data is stored on servers located within Japan." Furthermore, as measures taken for safety management, it says you must disclose the name of the foreign country where the cloud operator is located and the name of the foreign country where the servers are located, and keep the content of the measures taken in a state where the data subject can know them. Storing data on foreign servers itself does not constitute provision to a third party abroad (Article 28 of the Act) if the arrangement is that the operator does not handle the data, but the need to understand the foreign country's system still remains (Q12-3) 14.

Even if you choose the Tokyo region, if the cloud operator is a foreign company, the work of understanding that country's system does not disappear. What changes with region selection is mainly the "server location country" field among the published items. Placing data domestically does not mean no explanation is needed.

When incorporating generative AI, there is one more layer. The location where data is stored and the location where AI processes inference are separate. For example, AWS Bedrock has a feature that distributes inference requests across multiple regions, and you can choose between profiles limited to specific geographic areas such as the US or EU, and a global profile that distributes across commercial regions worldwide 15. According to AWS's explanation, if you have requirements about data location, choose a geographic scope; global has no geographic constraints but is roughly 10% cheaper. Even if storage is in Tokyo, where inference is processed depends on your configuration.

So how should you design it? I recommend not fixing the region to a single choice from the start, but designing so it can be selected according to requirements. For data whose domestic processing is required by contracts or industry regulations, use settings that process within the domestic scope. For other data, choose the model and processing location with the best balance of accuracy and cost. Record which one you chose, and keep it consistent with the published items to data subjects and your internal ledger. Our FDEs also do not uniformly restrict processing regions, and build with this approach.

What you should verify before regions is whether the data you input is used for model training. At our company, we only select services and models where it can be confirmed through terms of service, contracts, or settings that customer data is not used for training, and we record the basis for that. What you verify is the cloud operator's or model provider's own statements. For example, AWS states in the Bedrock FAQ that neither AWS nor external model providers use Bedrock inputs and outputs for model training 16. Keeping such statements in your ledger as the basis for selection shortens the explanations needed during audits.

Operational responsibility falls on you. Decide this in design first

I have written mostly about the advantages so far, so from here I will talk about what you pay.

AWS's "Shared Responsibility Model" is a clear way to understand cloud responsibility division 17. AWS takes responsibility for "security of the cloud"—protecting hardware, software, networks, and facilities—while users hold "security in the cloud." The scope of user responsibility varies depending on the service you choose. If you choose IaaS such as virtual servers (Amazon EC2), guest OS updates and security patches, applications on top, and firewall configuration are all the user's responsibility. It is safe to assume that work a vendor handled under SaaS becomes your company's work the moment you move it to your own tenant.

For ISMS administrative departments, you should also look at control responses. The 11 new controls added in JIS Q 27001:2023 (ISO/IEC 27001:2022) include "5.23 Information security for use of cloud services," "8.10 Information deletion," "8.16 Monitoring activities," and "8.28 Secure coding" 18. When using SaaS, the focus of 5.23 was "how to choose and use SaaS." When you move to your own tenant, the scope of 5.23 changes to use of the cloud infrastructure, and 8.16 monitoring and 8.28 coding come on top of it as your company's work. You may also need to review your Statement of Applicability. I have summarized the overall picture of ISMS in an introductory article on ISMS.

8.28 carries more weight now that people write code with generative AI. A user study presented by Stanford University researchers at ACM CCS 2023 reported that participants who had access to an AI assistant wrote less secure code than those who did not, and were also more likely to believe their code was secure 19. Since this was a result with models of that time, it does not necessarily apply directly to today's tools. Even so, I believe the procedure of having humans review AI-written code should not be skipped for the sake of speed.

The deletion of 8.10 also comes up during migration. Verifying that data left behind in the terminated SaaS has actually been deleted, and building a mechanism in the new application to delete data past its retention period, are two separate tasks. The former procedure is covered in the article on termination and migration practices, and a cost comparison including operational labor costs is in the article on 5-year total cost estimates. The failure to avoid most of all is deciding "it's cheaper than SaaS" without estimating the operating costs of your own tenant.

What Our FDE Can Do

Here is how our FDE takes on what has been discussed so far. An FDE is an engineer who works on-site with customers, observing their operations while building things that work. We believe that SaaS termination and in-housing is work an FDE can put into practice. An overview of the service is summarized on the FDE service page.

The first step is an inventory before deciding where things will live. Which SaaS will be terminated and brought in-house? Where does that business's data come from and where does it go? Where is personal data included? We write all this out before moving into design.

Next is the design of your own tenant. We decide how accounts are separated, integration with your internal identity platform, permission models, where audit logs are stored and for how long, and backup and recovery testing. Regions can be chosen according to requirements, and we record separately which data must be processed domestically and which need not. When incorporating generative AI, as noted earlier, we select only services and models that can be verified not to use data for training, and we keep the evidence for that.

Development proceeds using data with personal information masked. Features that write to production business systems are not shipped until four conditions are met: permissions, pre-execution simulation, human confirmation for critical decisions, and audit records. If our company must take on maintenance that touches production personal data, on the premise that this constitutes outsourcing, we write into the contract the scope of handling, access methods and logging, and breach reporting. We are certified under ISO/IEC 27001 (registration scope: planning, development, and provision of SaaS products leveraging AI technology).

The engagement runs for 45 to 90 days. The completion condition is that your team can operate it on its own. The period includes getting your staff to the point where they can run patch procedures, permission inventories, and recovery testing themselves. How to divide what is done in-house versus outsourced is also covered in the article on boundaries of AI in-housing.

Differences from Contract Development

Contract development can also build applications in your own tenant. The differences lie in how effort is treated, where the learning goes, and how things end.

In contract development, effort is revenue. The longer maintenance continues, the more revenue is booked, so a design that leaves operations with the vendor is a natural choice for the vendor. As a result, the application that was supposed to live in your own tenant ends up in a state where only an external maintenance contractor can touch it, and supervision of the contractor goes on endlessly. That merely swaps dependence on SaaS for dependence on another party.

Our FDE treats effort as an investment. We aim to reduce on-site effort for each customer over time, and we end the contract once your team can run on its own. Common mechanisms usable by other customers are returned to our products and used from day one with the next customer, while configuration and business data unique to you remain yours. Having the location be your own tenant pairs well with this way of ending. Since both the application and the data are in your environment from the start, there is nothing for us to take away when we leave. The differences between an FDE and other forms of support are covered in detail in the comparison article on FDE and AI accompaniment support.

Our Practice and Its Limits

To be honest: your own tenant is not a location we recommend for every company.

It is hard to recommend for companies with no one to handle operations. The tasks listed in the table above recur every month after the build. If there is no one in-house who can run operations, or if you cannot decide who to entrust operations to, continuing with SaaS or a dedicated environment operated by a vendor may be a better fit. We will state this judgment frankly in the initial consultation.

If you choose a configuration where we continue maintenance, the outsourcing relationship also continues. The burden of supervision remains with you. We set self-sufficiency as the completion condition, but there may remain work that is hard to run internally alone, such as nighttime incident response. In that case, please consult us about maintenance with a limited scope under a separate contract. Even in that contract, we make the scope of handling explicit.

We do not uniformly restrict processing regions. For projects where domestic processing is mandatory, we arrange it in an individual contract and reflect it in costs. To avoid the impression that "using us automatically means domestic processing," we confirm this upfront.

We are not a law firm. The final judgment on whether something constitutes outsourcing, and how far foreign regulations must be understood, is left to your legal team or specialists. What we can provide are the architecture diagrams, access paths, and records needed for that judgment. The ISO/IEC 27001 registration scope is as stated above, and we do not make claims suggesting the certification extends to individual work performed in your tenant.

Finally, capacity. We are a small company, so the number of engagements we can take on simultaneously is limited.

Summary

My assessment is that business applications you decide to own yourself are often easier to operate when placed in your own tenant. That is because you can align with your internal identity platform and permissions, connect to existing data, hold audit logs yourself, and decide the timing of specification changes yourself.

In exchange, you take on two things. One is organizing personal information. Whether something constitutes outsourcing is determined not by the location but by "who handles the personal data." If external contractors for development or maintenance can touch production data, treat it as outsourcing; if you design a configuration where they cannot, back it up with permission design and audit trails. Even if you choose a domestic region, with a foreign provider's cloud, understanding foreign regulations remains. The other is operational responsibility: patching, monitoring, backups, and deletion become your company's work directly.

If you do just one thing on Monday, for the SaaS you are considering terminating, check the contract and terms of service for "who on the vendor's side, with which ID, could touch your data." If the answer is unclear, that itself is one reason to consider your own tenant.
