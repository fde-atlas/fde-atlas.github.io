Summary

This long Japanese guide by Shohei Tokumitsu, CEO of DeFactory, explains what a DX consultant is: a professional who transforms business models with digital technology, beyond mere IT implementation. It contrasts DX consultants with IT and strategy consultants, details three core skills (BPR, technology selection, stakeholder management), and covers common failure traps such as PoC stagnation and black-boxed legacy systems. It argues generative AI is shifting DX from efficiency gains to new business creation, and outlines a freelance path: acting as a neutral external DX advisor with direct contracts, earning roughly ¥800k–1.5M monthly, exceeding ¥2M for specialized work. FDEs are framed as implementation partners who turn strategy into working AI solutions.

Key points

  • DX consultants transform business models, not just install systems.
  • Core skills: BPR, technology selection, and stakeholder management.
  • Common traps: PoC stagnation, black-boxed legacy systems, aimless tool adoption.
  • Generative AI shifts DX focus from efficiency to new business creation.
  • Freelancers earn ~¥800k–1.5M monthly; neutral direct contracts can exceed ¥2M.

Many people probably have vague images of the "DX consultant" role, such as "someone who helps introduce IT" or "someone who makes money off buzzwords." However, its essence lies not in IT adoption but in driving "transformation of the business model itself." Now that we have crossed the "2025 Cliff," the market value of DX consultants who can speak to both management and technology is soaring.

In this article, from the professional perspective of someone who has supported DX promotion and legacy system modernization in the field, we thoroughly and systematically explain the definition, job responsibilities, and essential skills of DX consultants. Furthermore, we dig deep into topics that typical explanations don't cover - "pitfalls of DX failure," "strategic shifts in the generative AI era," and "the path to going independent as a freelancer and securing high-rate projects exceeding 1.2 to 2 million yen per month" - incorporating first-hand information and real-world insights. Please use this as a practical guide for career planning that AI summaries can never provide.

A DX consultant is a specialist who supports the transformation of a company's business model and organization itself through digital technology, creating competitive advantage. What sets them apart is that their role extends beyond mere IT adoption or systemization to include creating new businesses, fundamentally redesigning business processes, and renewing corporate culture. They understand both business strategy and technology, and function as "transformation companions" who lead change.

With DX promotion now a top management priority and many companies facing legacy system modernization and new value creation, demand for DX consultants has never been higher.

In conclusion, a DX consultant is a specialist whose mission is to "rebuild a company's competitiveness itself using digital as the means," and their role goes far beyond IT adoption.

The main projects DX consultants work on are as follows.

  • Creating new businesses and services: Launching new revenue streams leveraging digital
  • Business process transformation (BPR): Fundamentally redesigning operations with digital as the premise
  • Developing digital talent: Building the people and organizations that embed DX within the company
  • Renewing corporate culture and organizational structures: Shifting to a culture that embraces change
  • Building data and technology foundations: Optimizing the IT infrastructure that supports transformation

As a professional opinion, the essence of a DX consultant is not "installing tools" but "changing what a company is." A common misconception is that "DX = system implementation," but systems are merely a means. The decisive difference between a true DX consultant and everyone else is whether they can answer management-level questions such as "What do we achieve with digital, and how do we win the competition?"

Additionally, METI's Digital Skills Standard defines the type of talent that drives "what to achieve with digital and how to win the competition" while engaging stakeholders as a "Business Architect." For details, see What is a Business Architect? Roles, Skills, and Criteria for Development vs. External Hiring.

In conclusion, DX consultants are a role responsible for "business transformation through digital," and their scope and approach clearly differ from IT consultants, who handle IT optimization of existing businesses, and strategy consultants, who handle business strategy.

Here is a summary of the differences between the three roles.

RoleMain MissionApproach FocusScope
DX ConsultantBusiness and organizational transformation through digitalStrategy × Technology × TransformationFrom new business creation to organizational renewal
IT ConsultantIT optimization and operational efficiency of existing businessesTechnology × Process ImprovementMainly system selection and process improvement
Strategy ConsultantFormulating business strategyManagement and businessCompany-wide strategy, M&A, etc. (shallow on technology)

As a real-world insight, while IT consultants think about "how to make existing operations more efficient," DX consultants think about "how should this business itself be transformed by digital in the first place?" The unique strength of DX consultants is their ability to translate the visions drawn by strategy consultants into forms that can be implemented with digital technology. Their rarity in being able to speak to both management and IT underpins their market value. For IT consultant careers in general, also see IT Consultant Salaries and Career Paths.

In conclusion, while DX consultants play the role of "designing transformation strategy and the overall picture," FDEs (Forward Deployed Engineers) play the role of "embedding themselves in the client's field, implementing AI solutions, and delivering results," and the collaboration between the two is key to ensuring DX doesn't end as pie in the sky.

Here is a summary of the differences and collaboration between the two.

PerspectiveDX ConsultantFDE (Forward Deployed Engineer)
Main RoleDesigning transformation strategy and overall visionOn-site AI implementation, operation, and adoption
PerspectiveManagement, business, organizationField, technology, implementation
DeliverablesDX strategy and roadmapAI solutions that work in production
Distance from ClientDialogue with managementDeep immersion in the field

From a professional perspective, a typical example of DX failure is "crafting an impressive strategy that ends up never being implemented in the field." The FDE turns the transformation vision drawn up by consultants into systems that actually work on the ground. Only when these two wheels—strategy and implementation—mesh together does DX produce results. A framework where DX consultants and FDEs collaborate is the ideal model for driving transformation going forward. For details on the FDE role, see What Is an FDE (Forward Deployed Engineer)? The work of a DX consultant rests on three core skills: "business requirements definition and BPR," "technology selection and architecture optimization," and "stakeholder management." Understanding business challenges, deep technical knowledge, and the negotiation skills to move people—the multiplication of these determines the success or failure of transformation projects. This section explains each skill in concrete terms.

In conclusion, the first core skill is the ability to visualize existing business processes and fundamentally redesign operations with digital in mind (BPR: Business Process Reengineering). At the same time, change management to handle the organizational disruption that accompanies transformation is also required.

The main tasks involved in this skill are as follows.

  • Visualizing current business processes (As-Is) and identifying issues
  • Designing ideal processes (To-Be) based on digital assumptions
  • Aligning the ideal state of operations and systems
  • Change management to handle confusion and resistance in the field during transformation
  • Developing migration plans and supporting adoption in the field

A common failure in the field is systematizing existing business processes as-is, resulting merely in inefficient operations being reproduced digitally. An excellent DX consultant questions whether the process is needed in the first place and redesigns operations from zero base with digital in mind. Whether one can perform this "subtraction of operations" determines the success of BPR.

In conclusion, the second core skill is technology expertise: understanding the overall landscape of technologies such as cloud, SaaS, and data platforms, and designing the optimal architecture to support transformation. This presupposes understanding both the language of management and the language of technology.

The main considerations in technology selection are as follows.

ConsiderationExample of what to examine
Cloud utilizationWhich of AWS/Azure/GCP to use and how to combine them
SaaS selectionIn-house development vs. leveraging existing SaaS
Data platformHow to integrate, store, and utilize data
Legacy integrationHow to connect to and migrate from existing systems
Scalability and future-proofingWhether the configuration is flexible enough to withstand change

Real-world criteria for technology selection: excellent DX consultants do not jump on the "latest technology" bandwagon. What matters is the perspective of "can it be operated continuously with the company's own resources?" and "is it truly necessary for the transformation's purpose?" Rather than insisting on in-house development and stalling, the ability to make decisions that combine appropriate SaaS solutions to deliver value quickly is what makes a consultant valued in the field. For overall cloud architecture design, What Is a Solution Architect? Roles, Essential Skills, Salaries, and the Path to High-Rate Independence is also a helpful reference.

In conclusion, the third core skill is advanced negotiation ability: bridging the gap between the transformation vision set by management and the resistance of field staff who dislike change (friction), and moving the entire organization toward transformation. Even with the right technology and strategy, DX will not progress if you cannot move people.

The required approaches in stakeholder management are as follows.

  • Proposals to management: Explain the significance of transformation and ROI in business language
  • Consideration for the field: Empathize with anxieties and resistance to change and win understanding
  • Interdepartmental coordination: Find common ground between departments with conflicting interests
  • Staging small wins: Show results early to increase supporters of transformation

From a professional perspective, the biggest cause of DX failure is not technology but "resistance from people and organizations." The structure of "management wants to do it, but the field doesn't want to change" exists in every company. An excellent DX consultant understands the feelings of field staff and turns resistance into support by letting them experience the "benefits of changing" firsthand. This gritty, human-centered coordination ability is the decisive factor in making transformation succeed.

In conclusion, DX projects contain typical failure traps: "PoC death," "legacy system black-boxing," and "purposeless tool adoption." Excellent DX consultants know these traps well and have strategies to avoid them. Furthermore, with the rise of generative AI, the main battleground of DX is shifting from "operational efficiency" to "new business creation." This section explains real-world failures and the latest strategies.

In conclusion, the most common DX failure is getting stuck at the PoC (proof of concept) stage—so-called "PoC death"—and to prevent it, it is essential to "clearly define success and withdrawal criteria (KPIs) in advance."

Specific measures to prevent "PoC death" are as follows.

MeasureDescription
Clarify purposeDefine "what the PoC is for" based on business goals
Set KPIs in advanceDefine numerically "what must be achieved to go to production"
Set withdrawal criteriaAlso decide in advance "when to stop"
Path to productionDraw a roadmap for production deployment from the PoC stage onward

A common failure: companies repeatedly run vague PoCs with the attitude of "let's just try AI for now," wasting budget and time with none reaching production. An excellent DX consultant agrees with management on "what counts as success" and "when to withdraw if it fails" before starting a PoC. This discipline is the key to preventing the endless swamp of PoCs and converting investment into results.

In conclusion, for the "black-boxing of legacy systems (a state where no one grasps the whole picture)" faced by many Japanese companies, the realistic solution is not a complete overhaul but a "phased migration strategy."

The main approaches to phased migration are as follows.

  • Assess the current state: Visualize the specifications and dependencies of black-boxed systems
  • Prioritize: Migrate step by step, starting with areas of high business impact
  • Distribute risk: Avoid a complete overhaul (big bang migration) and proceed in small increments
  • Parallel operation: Run the old and new systems in parallel for a certain period to reduce risk

As a professional view, as the "2025 Cliff" warned, legacy systems have become a shackle for many companies. However, rushing into a full-scale overhaul increases the risk that a massive investment will end in failure. An excellent DX consultant has the courage to "not change everything at once" and draws up a realistic strategy that advances migration steadily while keeping risk under control.

In conclusion, with the rise of generative AI (LLMs), the main battleground of DX is shifting from "improving the efficiency of existing operations" to "creating new businesses premised on AI." The value of DX consultants who can capture this change is rising rapidly.

Let us organize how value in DX is changing in the era of generative AI.

Traditional DXDX in the generative AI era
Improving efficiency and automating existing operationsCreating new businesses and services centered on AI
Cost reduction as the main goalCreating new revenue and competitive advantage
Partial tool adoptionRedesigning operations and organizations premised on AI
Replacing human workValue creation through human-AI collaboration

As a professional view, generative AI is not merely an efficiency tool; it has the impact to "change the very way business is done." For example, when AI takes over specialized work previously done by humans, new service models emerge. The DX consultants most in demand in the market today are those who can envision not just "what to make more efficient with AI" but "what to newly create with AI." The ability to convert generative AI into business value is the defining trait of the next generation of DX consultants.

In conclusion, DX consultants are an extremely high-market-value profession: as freelancers, they can aim for high-rate projects exceeding 1.2 to 2 million yen per month. The rarity of being able to speak to both management and technology, combined with the surge in DX demand, supports these high rates. Here we explain the real state of market value and a concrete roadmap to going independent.

In conclusion, the monthly rate for freelance DX consultants is generally centered around 800,000 to 1.5 million yen, with rates exceeding 2 million yen within reach for highly specialized projects. Demand far exceeds supply, and a seller's market continues.

What is noteworthy is that the number of projects in the PM/PMO and IT consulting areas has grown significantly year-over-year. As companies are pressed to modernize legacy systems and drive DX in response to the "2025 Cliff," demand for DX consultants with both technical and management capabilities is exploding. This structural talent shortage is the basis supporting high rates.

For the background of the DX talent shortage and solutions companies can adopt, see Causes and Solutions of the DX Talent Shortage: "How to Acquire DX Talent" for a detailed explanation.

In conclusion, the shortest route to becoming a DX consultant is to combine the technical skills built as an SE or senior IT engineer with a "business and management perspective." Technical people who learn management tend to succeed more easily than management consultants who learn technology.

An "inventory of experience" for stepping up is as follows.

  • Confirm your technical foundation: Take stock of your development, infrastructure, and design experience to date
  • Gain upstream experience: Get involved in upstream processes such as requirements definition, technology selection, and proposals
  • Learn a business perspective: Deepen your understanding of ROI, domain knowledge, and management challenges
  • Experience transformation projects: Participate in business reform or DX promotion projects
  • Go independent: Once you have accumulated a track record and network and can foresee a steady supply of projects, become a freelancer

As a professional view, what makes the biggest difference is "combining technology and business." There are many people with only technical or only management skills, but those who can speak to both at a high level are rare. "I understand systems, and I can also explain how they benefit the business"—this combination is what determines your market value as a DX consultant. The roadmap for What Is a Technical Architect? Role, Essential Skills, and High-Rate Independence Techniques is also a useful reference for moving into upstream processes.

In conclusion, the royal road to a high-rate independent career as a DX consultant is to contract directly with companies as a "neutral external DX advisor" or "CDO (Chief Digital Officer) assistant," free from ties to specific vendors or SIers. Neutrality is the greatest value of an external DX consultant.

The reasons demand for neutral external advisors is high are as follows.

  • Vendor neutrality: Objective technical judgment free from bias toward specific products is required
  • Difficulty of hiring: Talent capable of driving DX is scarce, and hiring them as full-time employees is extremely difficult
  • Absence of a CDO: Companies that cannot appoint a digital officer seek that role externally
  • Accompanying management: Not one-off proposals, but a presence that accompanies the transformation to the end

As a professional view, when companies consult vendors or SIers, a bias toward "wanting to sell their own products" inevitably comes into play. In contrast, a neutral external DX advisor with no conflicts of interest can propose purely "what is best for that company." Leveraging this trust, the strategy that balances rate and stability is to start through agencies, build a track record, and gradually move to direct long-term contracts.

In conclusion, to maximize income as a freelance DX consultant, two defensive techniques are essential: "contract design that prevents unpaid work outside the scope" and "rate negotiation based on ROI (return on investment)."

Concrete measures to prevent scope creep and win fair rates are as follows.

Defensive techniqueSpecific content
Document the scope of workClearly state "support scope, deliverables, and working days" in the contract and SOW
Set a cap on working daysDefine a limit such as "up to X days per month," with excess billed as additional compensation
Visualize resultsShow how much cost reduction or revenue creation your involvement has achieved
Negotiating with ROIMake your case with cost-effectiveness: "An investment of X yen per unit delivers X yen in impact"

A common failure is when you keep accepting "while you're at it, also do this" requests, and your scope of work gradually expands until your hourly rate no longer makes sense. Professionals don't "refuse"; instead, they constructively draw the line by saying, "That's outside the scope, so I'll take it on as additional work." And in rate negotiations, you justify a high rate by quantitatively demonstrating the business value created by DX (cost reductions, revenue from new businesses, etc.). If you consider incorporating, you can further optimize your take-home pay through tax savings. In this article, we comprehensively covered the role, work content, and essential skills of DX consultants, common pitfalls, strategies for the generative AI era, and going independent as a freelancer. Let's review the key points.

  • What is a DX consultant: A specialist who transforms business models and organizations themselves through digital technology
  • Differences from other roles: Goes beyond IT consulting (efficiency of existing businesses) to cover new business creation and organizational transformation
  • Three core skills: Business requirements definition and BPR / technology selection and architecture / stakeholder management
  • Pitfalls of failure: Discipline to avoid PoC death, legacy black-boxing, and purposeless tool adoption
  • Market value: Over 800,000 to 1.5 million yen per month as a freelancer, and over 2 million yen depending on specialization
  • Path to independence: Combining technology and business, and direct contracts as a neutral external DX advisor

DX consultants are a high-market-value profession whose demand never dries up as long as companies need transformation. In particular, the spread of generative AI continues to increase the scarcity of "people who can create new value through digital technology," and that ability is maximized when you go independent as a freelancer. Combine technical skill with a management perspective and transformation leadership to become one of a kind—beyond that lies great potential in both career and income.
