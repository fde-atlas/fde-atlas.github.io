Coinbase Finance & Compliance FDE Role: Internal Forward Deployed Engineer

Original title: Coinbase 财务与合规 FDE 岗位：内部前线部署工程师

Key Takeaways

  • Coinbase is hiring two FDEs in the Office of the CTO, one for finance (posted October 13, 2026) and one for compliance (posted October 22, 2026). The customers for both roles are internal colleagues.
  • The success criteria in the two job descriptions are nearly identical: every automated output must be traceable, reviewable, and able to withstand scrutiny—before auditors for the former, before regulators for the latter.
  • The finance role also owns the automation roadmap and build-vs-buy decisions—it is a product role, not just writing code.

On October 13, 2026, Coinbase posted a job: Forward Deployed Engineer (FDE), Finance, in the Office of the CTO. Nine days later, a second posting appeared, this time an FDE for compliance. Neither role's customers have ever signed a contract with Coinbase. They are colleagues at the same company.

If you are an FDE, or want to become one, this detail is worth a close look. The common image of an FDE is an engineer dispatched to work on-site at external customers.

Coinbase's two postings follow that same playbook, only pointed inward, at two departments. In these two departments, every automated result must stand up to examination by auditors or regulators.

The key lies in the success criteria the two job descriptions set. It is not enough for the agent to run; every result it produces must be explainable to the person with the authority to veto it. In these roles, what the FDE actually delivers to the business is a chain of evidence; automation is merely the means of producing it.

Why does Coinbase need this role now?

In May 2026, Coinbase laid off roughly 14% of its staff. CEO Brian Armstrong gave two reasons: AI, and a downturn in the crypto market that forced the company to adjust its cost structure immediately. In the same memo, he wrote that non-technical teams are now shipping code to production themselves.

Armstrong also described the operating model he wants: AI at the center, humans on the periphery doing calibration. Work is being reorganized into small AI-native teams (pods), some with only one person.

Viewed in that context, the logic of these two postings is clear. If, as Armstrong says, non-technical teams can already ship code to production, what is likely missing is not people who write code, but people who know how to make automation hold up when the auditors arrive.

"Humans on the periphery" sounds simple, but making it real within a system requires someone to design it. The two job descriptions hand that job to the FDE.

Two postings, one standard

Read separately, the two job descriptions look like two very different jobs. Read together, they share the same structure—only the examiners differ.

The last row matters most. The two job descriptions use nearly the same sentence: every automated output must be traceable, reviewable, and able to withstand scrutiny. The sentence stays the same; only "auditors" is swapped for "regulators." Coinbase treats it as a single standard, applied to two departments.

Where must a number be traceable to?

Consider a concrete scenario. The FP&A and treasury teams cover more than 60 subsidiaries. Suppose you build an agent that writes a monthly variance analysis between actuals and budget for each subsidiary. Over a year, that is at least 720 write-ups, and an auditor could pick any one of them to challenge.

The three requirements in the job description apply to every one of them. Traceable means every number can be traced back to its source data row, plus the prompt and model version that generated it.

Reviewable means someone can see the agent's draft, the human edits made to it, and the name of the person who signed off. Able to withstand scrutiny means that when someone asks "why," the answer is already in the system, not in someone's memory.

Segregation of duties makes the problem harder. The agent that drafts journal entries should not also be the one that posts them. The engineer who builds the data pipeline should not be the person who approves its output. These boundaries must be written into the permission design from the start; retrofitting them after a demo runs is extremely difficult.

So in your first week working with this kind of customer, don't rush to ask which process is most time-consuming. Ask the controller what the auditors asked about last year. That answer is the true requirements spec for the system you are going to build.

Compliance: when the examiner is a regulator

In the compliance department, the approach is the same, but the stakes are higher. Imagine a sanctions screening alert where the agent recommends closing it as a false positive. If a regulator asks about it later, the system must be able to show what data and rules the agent relied on, and who pressed the close button.

The compliance job description also contains a very pragmatic requirement: build by default on Coinbase's existing AI platform. For a founding engineer, this is a discipline reminder. Your job is to put AI in the right places within transaction monitoring and audit trails, not to spin up a new tech stack just because it is more interesting.

"Founding engineer" also signals how broad the scope is. As the first engineer in a business unit, you do the requirements research yourself, decide what to automate first, and win the trust of people long accustomed to manual work. There is no account manager between you and your users.

Build or buy—who decides?

The finance job description requires the FDE to own the build-vs-buy strategy for finance tools and the automation roadmap. This is a product role. An internal FDE must know when to buy off-the-shelf software instead of writing it, and be accountable for that choice.

The qualifications also reveal what Coinbase values. The job description asks for 5+ years of experience shipping systems to production, but does not require a CPA. Finance knowledge can be learned on the job. The judgment of someone who has run real systems cannot be learned that fast.

Compared with FDEs serving external customers, internal roles have their own difficulty: there is no contract to lean on. If the automation goes wrong, the colleagues on the same floor are the ones who will face the auditors—and they will remember it.

Which skills are in demand?

If you want to target this kind of role—whether at Coinbase or at any company where finance is tightly constrained—watch for three keywords when reading job descriptions.

"Build-vs-buy" means you will make product decisions. "Human-in-the-loop" means you must design intervention points for people. "Traceable" means your deliverables include audit trails.

In a portfolio, one agent with complete audit logs is more persuasive than ten polished demos. Every output should record the input, version, reviewer, and approval time. When writing your resume, describe your results in a way an auditor can understand: which process you automated, at which step human control was retained, and who signed off on it.

In interviews, be ready to explain separation of duties using examples from your own systems. The interviewer likely doesn't care which model you used. They will ask: if this output is wrong, who discovers it, and how?

When even non-technical teams can ship code to production, the scarce skill becomes: making automation withstand the sharpest scrutiny from auditors and regulators. At least at Coinbase, this skill has already turned into two dedicated FDE roles, based right in the Office of the CTO.
