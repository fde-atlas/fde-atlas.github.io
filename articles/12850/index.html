Without accumulation, you're not an FDE

Original title: 没有沉淀，就不是 FDE

53AI · 2026-07-12 · 10 min read

Summary

This article argues that what separates an FDE from outsourcing or consulting is not technical skill or customer proximity, but the delivery mechanism: FDE work runs two loops simultaneously. The inner loop solves the customer's immediate problem; the outer loop captures reusable patterns and feeds them back into platform capabilities, templates, and tools. Without the outer loop, FDEs degrade into high-end on-site developers. The author cites OpenAI FDEs whose real-time eval framework, built for a call center voice project, later shaped the Realtime API and Agent SDK. A 70% asset-reuse rate is framed as an organizational health signal, not an individual KPI. The piece also offers a three-tier framework—generic patterns, industry templates, pure customization—and argues AI projects' high uncertainty makes this dual-loop accumulation essential.

Key points

  • FDE differs from outsourcing by leaving reusable assets after delivery.
  • Inner loop solves current problems; outer loop deposits solutions into the platform.
  • OpenAI's call-center eval framework influenced Realtime API and Agent SDK.
  • 70% reuse rate signals organizational health, not an individual KPI.
  • Deposition criterion: will it make the next project faster?

Full text

Conclusion first: The biggest difference between FDEs and outsourcing is not that the former understands technology better, nor that they are closer to the customer, but whether reusable assets are left behind after delivery. FDE delivery has two tracks: one track solves the problem at hand, and the other distills the experience of solving it. Without the second track, an FDE quickly degenerates into a senior on-site developer.

The previous article made a judgment: AI FDEs and traditional consultants are two completely different professions. If we break this down further, the core difference is actually not "who writes better code" or "who understands the customer better." These matter, but they are not the essence.

The real difference lies in the delivery mechanism. Traditional outsourcing or consulting operates on the core logic of delivering the current project. The customer has a problem, the team comes in, sorts out requirements, develops features, launches and gets acceptance, and the project ends. As long as everything within the scope of the contract is done, delivery is considered complete.

But the problem is, a completed project does not mean the organization has become smarter.

Were the patterns discovered in this project reused in the next one? Did the scripts written on the fly on-site become platform capabilities? Were the processes that repeatedly appear in a certain industry distilled into templates? If these things are not caught, a project is just a project.

Finish one, end one. When the next customer arrives, start all over again.

This is the biggest problem with traditional delivery: it can complete projects, but it struggles to accumulate capability. This is exactly the problem FDEs are meant to solve.

▲ The two tracks of the FDE: delivery solves immediate problems, while distillation creates reusable assets

FDE delivery is not a single track. It has at least two.

The first track is the inner loop. That is, solving the problems within the current customer, current scenario, and current process. Does this thing actually run? Are business users willing to use it? Does it produce real value? These are the questions the inner loop must answer.

The second track is the outer loop. That is, distilling the reusable patterns discovered while solving problems back into the platform, product, templates, tools, and methods. Can the next project go faster? Can similar problems avoid starting from scratch? These are the questions the outer loop must answer.

These two tracks must run simultaneously. Running only the first track is project-based outsourcing. Build whatever the customer asks for, deliver it, and the project ends. Even with strong engineers, it is essentially one-off development.

Running only the second track, without looking at real frontline problems, is product planning done behind closed doors. No matter how polished the platform is, if the frontline doesn't use it and the business doesn't buy in, it is meaningless.

The key to the FDE role is solving specific problems on the frontline while extracting the commonalities behind them. This sounds simple, but it defines the dividing line between FDEs and traditional delivery.

When an FDE arrives at the frontline, the first thing to do is solve specific problems. Not writing a complete requirements document first, nor making a polished solution presentation first, but entering the real process and seeing how the business actually operates.

In a process: who fills out the forms, who approves them, where the data comes from, why the same information has to be entered three times across three systems, which node looks like approval but is actually just forwarding, and which step looks slow but whose real problem is inaccurate upstream data.

These things are not in the product manual, and they are hard to uncover through meetings alone. They must be seen on the frontline.

After seeing them, what the FDE should do is not package the problem into a big project, but first make a working improvement. It is fine if it is rough; what matters is that it enters the real process. Because only when it runs can you know whether it truly solves the problem.

This is exactly where many enterprise software projects go wrong: everything looks right at the solution stage, but after launch nobody uses it. Why? Because the solution addressed the problems in the documents, not the problems on the ground.

The inner loop of the FDE exists precisely to avoid this. It usually goes through several steps: discover the real problem, walk through the business process, build a working prototype, validate it in the real environment, see whether users actually use it, and keep iterating based on feedback.

Only after completing this cycle is it a valid delivery. Note that the focus here is not the "deliverable," but the business outcome.

A system going live does not mean success. People logging in does not mean success either. Real success is when target users genuinely start relying on it in their daily work, the original inefficient process gets replaced, and the time, error rate, or waiting cost of a certain step actually goes down.

That is the first track: solving the problem at hand. But if an FDE only gets this far, it is not enough. Because this only means one project succeeded; the organization has not become smarter.

The second track is what truly distinguishes FDEs from outsourcing.

While solving a specific problem, an FDE cannot only focus on the current customer. They must constantly judge: Is this problem unique to this company? Would other enterprises have this same process? Can this temporary solution be extracted into a component? Can this configuration become a template? Can this eval method become a standard tool?

These judgments make up the outer loop.

The outer loop is not a retrospective meeting after the project ends. It should happen during the project. When an FDE sees something reusable on the frontline, they should capture it on the spot, not wait three months to write a summary.

Because truly valuable discoveries are often small. Not grand strategic judgments, but perhaps a data field mapping approach, a common node in an approval process, a validation script written on the fly, a recurring permission model, or an eval method forced out by the customer's on-site conditions.

If these things are not recorded in time, they easily get dismissed as "minor project details." But if they are captured and picked up by the product and platform teams, they can become foundational capabilities for future projects.

The outer loop roughly works like this: the frontline discovers a reusable pattern and records in a few sentences where it appeared, what problem it solves, how it is currently done, and why it might be reusable; then quickly discusses with the product or platform team whether it is worth standardizing. If it is, the platform team refactors it into a general capability. When the next project encounters a similar problem, it can be reused directly.

There is a key point here: the frontline is not necessarily responsible for standardizing it. What FDEs build on-site is often "rough but effective." That is normal. The frontline pursues speed—getting the business running first.

But platform capabilities cannot stay rough forever, so a more sensible division of labor is: the frontline is responsible for discovery and validation, while the platform team is responsible for refactoring and productization. The frontline provides real material to the platform, and the platform turns that material into reusable assets.

This is the relationship between the inner loop and the outer loop. The inner loop gets the current project running; the outer loop ensures the next project does not start from scratch.

▲ What is truly worth distilling is often an unremarkable little discovery made on-site

When many people hear "distillation," they think of very formal things: methodologies, product modules, industry solutions, knowledge base documents. These are certainly important. But in FDE work, distillation usually does not start at that scale.

It is more like a series of small flashes of insight.

For example, two customers both need to sync customer data from legacy systems into new processes, with only different field names. Three projects all show similar approval chains, with only different approvers and node counts. Different customers all need to judge whether a piece of AI output can proceed to the next step, with only slightly different eval criteria. Multiple scenarios all need to convert human operation logs into structured records readable by systems.

None of these things is big on its own. But if they appear repeatedly, it means there is a commonality behind them.

Once a commonality is extracted, it can become a platform capability. The next project no longer needs to ask "how do we do this," but can directly call the existing capability, tweak a bit of configuration, and keep moving forward.

This is the FDE flywheel. The more projects there are, the more patterns the frontline sees. The more patterns there are, the thicker the platform's accumulated assets. The thicker the platform, the faster the next project is delivered. The faster the delivery, the more energy FDEs can devote to genuinely new problems.

OpenAI's FDE once worked on AI voice assistant integration for a call center. The problems encountered on site were very specific: voice latency and throughput fluctuations were hard to evaluate in real time. To get this customer's system running, the forward deployed team built a real-time evals framework.

Under traditional delivery logic, the story would end here. The customer's problem was solved, the project went live, and the evals framework stayed in that customer's environment as a one-off deliverable.

But the FDE's judgment was different. It kept asking: are voice latency and throughput fluctuations just this one call center's problem? Obviously not. Anyone doing real-time voice interaction will run into similar issues.

So the evals framework forced out by this field engagement was escalated, abstracted, and refactored, ultimately influencing the underlying mechanisms of OpenAI's official Realtime API and shaping the final form of the Agent SDK.

A temporary field tool ended up becoming a platform capability.

This example matters not because OpenAI is so impressive, but because it illustrates the essence of the FDE: the front line is not the end of the product; the front line is the entry point of product evolution.

In the traditional model, the product is planned first, then implemented on the front line. In the FDE model, the front line continuously discovers real problems, the product continuously absorbs real problems, and the platform keeps getting stronger. That's the difference.

▲ 70% is not an individual KPI, but a health signal for the organization

FDE discussions often mention a number: 70%. It means that when the mechanism is working well, roughly 70% of front-line solutions can be refactored into platform capabilities, standard components, industry templates, or methodological assets.

This number is easily misunderstood. It is not a KPI imposed on individual FDEs. It doesn't mean every FDE must productize 70% of their code after finishing a project. If you evaluate people that way, you're heading in the wrong direction.

The FDE's first job on the front line is to solve business problems, not to force out assets just to hit an accumulation metric. 70% is more like an organizational health signal.

If over the long run only 10% or 20% of work can be accumulated, it means the front line is most likely doing one-off customizations. Every project starts from scratch, every customer gets bespoke development—the more you do, the heavier the burden. That's the consulting trap.

But demanding 100% accumulation is unrealistic too. The front line will inevitably encounter purely custom requirements. Every enterprise has its own legacy baggage, organizational habits, approval preferences, and system constraints. These things shouldn't all be crammed into the platform.

If a platform absorbs too much special-case logic, it will eventually become a giant monster nobody dares to touch.

So the meaning of 70% is this: it acknowledges that the front line has special cases, but it also requires the organization to continuously extract commonality from them.

Teams just starting out with FDE may have an accumulation rate of only 20% to 30%. That's normal. The platform is still thin, experience is still limited, and the front line is still feeling its way. But as long as the outer loop exists, this number will slowly rise.

What's truly dangerous is not a low accumulation rate, but nobody caring about it. Projects end when they're done, code stays with the customer, experience stays in individual heads, and the organization never gets smarter.

This is the state FDE should avoid most.

▲ Distinguish clearly between general patterns, industry templates, and pure customization

Not every front-line discovery is worth accumulating. You need a simple judgment framework here.

The first category: general patterns. Things like data integration approaches, authentication flows, permission models, log auditing, and evals frameworks. These appear across customers and across projects. Once accumulated, subsequent projects benefit directly—highest priority.

The second category: industry templates. Things like typical approval workflows, compliance requirements, data structures, and business actions in a particular industry. Not every enterprise needs these, but they recur within the same industry. They're worth accumulating, but you can't just copy one customer's approach—you need to abstract out the industry's commonalities.

The third category: purely custom requirements. Things like a particular customer's unique internal approval habits, special fields left over from a legacy system, or a report format one executive personally prefers. These can be done, but they shouldn't go into the platform—leaving them in the customization layer is enough.

There's really only one criterion: will this make the next project faster? If yes, it's worth accumulating. If not, don't force it.

This criterion is more effective than a complex review system. Because FDE accumulation isn't about making documentation look good—it's about lowering the cost of the next delivery.

▲ Project silos just repeat the grunt work; platform assets make the organization smarter

Now we can return to the opening question. What exactly separates FDE from outsourcing? Both go on site, both solve customer problems, both may involve writing code, tuning systems, and doing integrations—the difference lies in the second loop.

If the outer loop exists, every front-line delivery feeds back into the platform. When a project ends, the organization gains a bit more capability. When the next project starts, it doesn't begin from zero—it moves forward carrying the components, templates, experience, and judgment methods accumulated from the previous project.

Projects get lighter. The platform gets thicker. The team gets smarter.

If the outer loop doesn't exist, it's the exact opposite. Every project is an island, every delivery relies on individual experience, every customer requires re-understanding, re-development, and re-launch—the more you do, the heavier the maintenance burden.

In that case, even if everyone on the team is strong, you're just a stronger outsourcing team.

The key to FDE is not "strong individuals." Strong individuals certainly matter, but relying on them alone makes the organization increasingly dependent on hero engineers. Once that person leaves, the experience leaves with them.

The real FDE model turns what individuals see on the front line into things the organization can reuse. That's the true meaning of accumulation.

▲ The uncertainty of AI projects needs to be converted into reusable assets

Why does FDE become even more important in the AI era? Because AI projects carry higher uncertainty.

In traditional software delivery, many requirements are relatively certain. How to configure workflows, how to fill in fields, how to set permissions—tedious, perhaps, but broadly predictable.

AI projects are different. They often have to be figured out through trial in real business settings. Which steps are suitable for automation, which judgments must stay with humans, how to evaluate AI outputs, when users will trust it, when guardrails must be added—these questions are hard to fully resolve in a meeting room.

You have to get to the front line, try fast, and iterate fast.

And precisely because uncertainty is higher, accumulation matters more. Otherwise every AI project becomes a fresh exploration. Every time you redo prompts, workflows, evals, permissions, and exception handling from scratch, the cost is enormous.

The FDE's double loop is, in essence, a way of turning this uncertainty into accumulable assets.

The first time you encounter something, it's a problem. The second time, it's a pattern. The third time, it should become a platform capability.

So FDE is not "sending a brilliant engineer to the field." That statement is only half right.

The real FDE is a delivery mechanism that lets front-line experience continuously flow back. The inner loop is responsible for solving the problem at hand; the outer loop is responsible for turning solutions into reusable assets for next time. The inner loop ensures the value is real; the outer loop ensures capability accumulates.

Without the inner loop, FDE becomes armchair theorizing. Without the outer loop, FDE becomes high-end outsourcing.

Only when both loops run simultaneously does FDE truly exist.
