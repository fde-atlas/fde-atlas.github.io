FDE Delivery Lifecycle: Discovery to Handover

FDE Council · 2026-10-25 · 5 min read

Summary

The FDE Council outlines a seven-stage delivery lifecycle for forward deployed engineers: discovery, design, build and evaluation, deployment, adoption, handover, and feeding field learning back into product and practice. It stresses the guide is iterative rather than a rigid waterfall, and that an FDE's job goes beyond prototyping to running and transferring a working solution. It maps to the Council's public draft competency framework, which is not yet formally validated.

Key points

  • Seven stages run from discovery through handover and learning feedback.
  • Deployments may return to earlier stages when evidence changes.
  • Handover is complete only when customer owners can operate independently.
  • Not every problem requires AI or new software.
  • The guide rests on an unvalidated draft framework (v0.1).

In brief: an FDE does more than build a prototype. The work is to identify the right problem, fit a solution to the customer’s technical and operating environment, validate it, put it into production, support adoption, transfer ownership and feed useful learning back into the product and team.

The sequence below is a practical guide, not a rigid waterfall process. Discovery and implementation may overlap, and a deployment may need to return to earlier decisions when tests, constraints or user feedback change the picture. It aligns with the domains in the Council’s open FDE Council Professional Capability Framework.

1. Discover the problem and define success

Start with the work people are trying to do, not only the first solution they request. Talk with users, operators, technical owners and decision-makers. Map the current workflow, the systems involved, the constraints and the cost of the problem.

Turn what you learn into a problem statement and a testable outcome. Record a baseline where possible, agree how success will be measured, and identify assumptions that need checking. If access, data, ownership or feasibility is missing, recommend a smaller experiment, a different scope or a pause before committing to a build.

Useful checkpoint: the people responsible for the result agree on the problem, the desired outcome, the constraints and the next decision.

2. Design for the real environment

Choose an approach that fits the customer’s actual systems, identity model, network, data, security requirements and operating capacity. Confirm integration boundaries and ownership early. Prefer the simplest design that can meet the agreed outcome reliably; not every problem needs an AI model or a new service.

Make trade-offs visible. Identify risks, dependencies, ongoing costs, failure modes and what the customer will need to run the system. Agree on a minimum useful production scope rather than allowing a demo to become a substitute for a deployment plan.

Useful checkpoint: the architecture, integrations, risks, success measures and operational responsibilities have named owners and a path to test.

3. Build and evaluate against real cases

Implement the smallest slice that can test the most important assumptions. Use representative cases, agreed acceptance criteria and repeatable checks. For AI or LLM components, evaluate quality and failure cases alongside latency, cost, data handling and the level of human review required.

Show progress to the people who will use and operate the result. Their feedback can reveal missing requirements, misunderstood workflows or edge cases before they become expensive production problems. Keep a record of known limitations; do not describe a successful demo as evidence of production readiness.

Useful checkpoint: the solution passes agreed functional and quality checks for relevant cases, and remaining gaps are documented with owners and decisions.

4. Deploy and operate it safely

Prepare the production environment with the customer’s technical owners. Verify access controls, secrets handling, data flows, logging, monitoring, rollback, incident ownership and support expectations. Test the deployment path and agree how changes will be reviewed after launch.

Production readiness depends on the risk and context of the system. The right evidence is not a generic checklist alone; it is proof that the service can be operated, observed and recovered in the environment where it will be used.

Useful checkpoint: the responsible operators can identify service health, respond to likely failures and restore or disable the solution when needed.

5. Support adoption and verify outcomes

A working system does not automatically change a workflow. Explain what changed, provide practical guidance, and listen for friction from users and operators. Compare observed outcomes with the baseline and success measures agreed during discovery. If the expected value is not appearing, investigate whether the issue is the solution, the workflow, the assumptions or the measure itself.

Useful checkpoint: intended users can perform the changed workflow, and the team can report what the evidence says about the target outcome.

For how to judge that evidence, read how to measure whether an FDE deployment succeeded.

6. Hand over ownership

Plan the handover from the start. Provide the documentation, runbooks, architecture decisions, evaluation assets, known limitations, access details and escalation paths the customer needs. Walk through normal operation and likely failures, then verify the customer can complete those tasks without relying on the FDE being present.

A handover is not complete because a document was delivered. It is complete when the named owners can operate and maintain the solution, understand its boundaries and know how to get help when something changes.

Useful checkpoint: an identified customer owner demonstrates the agreed operational tasks and accepts responsibility for the ongoing system.

7. Feed field learning back

Capture patterns that can help other deployments: repeated integration friction, missing product capabilities, successful evaluation methods, adoption barriers or unclear role boundaries. Share useful lessons without disclosing customer-confidential information. Turn repeated work into better playbooks or product feedback rather than treating every deployment as isolated.

Useful checkpoint: the learning has an owner and reaches the product, engineering or practice team that can act on it.

Why the stages are iterative

Real deployments contain uncertainty. A data assessment may change the architecture; an evaluation may expose an unmet requirement; user feedback may challenge the original problem statement. Returning to discovery or design is responsible delivery when new evidence changes the decision. The important discipline is to make the change, its rationale and its effect on the outcome visible to stakeholders.

How the lifecycle relates to the FDE role

The role is accountable for connecting customer context with engineering delivery, not simply completing a list of tasks. The Council’s Forward Deployed Engineer role overview describes the role and neighboring jobs. The draft competency framework organizes skills across discovery, architecture, AI application engineering, data, deployment and operations, communication and adoption, and delivery leadership.

The framework is a public draft, open for comment, and has not yet been validated through formal job task analysis. This lifecycle is a practical interpretation of that draft and should be adapted to the risk, scope and operating environment of each deployment.

Frequently asked questions

What is the first step in an FDE deployment?

Clarify the problem and the desired outcome with the people affected by the work. Confirm the baseline, constraints and decision-makers before committing to a solution.

When is an FDE project ready for handover?

When named customer owners can operate and support the solution, understand its limitations, and complete the agreed routine and recovery tasks without depending on the FDE.

Does every FDE deployment need AI?

No. Choose the simplest approach that meets the need and fits the customer’s environment. That may be deterministic software, an existing capability, an AI component, a process change or no new software.

Basis: FDE Council’s public draft competency framework v0.1 and role definition, accessed October 25, 2026. This practical guide is not a validated standard or a substitute for context-specific engineering and operational review.
