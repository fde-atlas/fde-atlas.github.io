This article comes from the WeChat public account: Silicon Valley 101. Interview/text and images: Yiwen. Cover image: AI-generated

The hottest job in Silicon Valley right now is none other than the FDE. FDE stands for "Forward Deployment Engineer," which can be directly translated as "Forward Deployed Engineer." They need to understand both models and technology, as well as customers' data, processes, and business pain points. Their core task is to turn AI from a demo into AI-native workflows for each profession. A 2026 survey of 1,500 FDEs by Perspective AI showed that the median total annual compensation for senior FDEs at frontier labs reached $485,000, while at the senior staff level it was as high as $725,000, with total compensation ranges at top labs falling between $350,000 and $550,000.

Behind this boom is a major strategic shift by top AI companies. In May of this year, OpenAI partnered with 19 PE firms to invest over $4 billion in total to establish a Deployment Company, and acquired an AI implementation services company, Tomoro, taking 150 FDEs along with it. Anthropic also announced a partnership with several financial institutions, including Blackstone, to form a joint venture worth $1.5 billion. These top model companies have all begun betting on the same kind of service: not just providing AI tools, but going deep inside enterprises to truly deploy AI capabilities in specific business scenarios.

In this episode, we invited two frontline practitioners to discuss FDEs and deployment companies. We talked about the specific nature of the FDE job, its origins in Palantir's early military deployment model, and how private equity firms and the consulting industry are changing amid this wave of AI implementation.

Jove, head of the FDE team at Cresta, which provides AI agents for enterprise call centers—Jove began expanding the FDE team at the start of last year; Oliver, former McKinsey consultant and currently VP of enterprise business at Invisible Technologies.

Below are selected highlights from this conversation:

I. Why are model companies getting into deployment?

Yiwen: Jove, what do you think about the recent moves by OpenAI, Anthropic, and other model companies to start doing AI deployment?

Jove: I think this is part of why FDEs suddenly got everyone so excited. As a job type, FDEs have existed for over a decade, starting from Palantir. Our company started this kind of FDE deployment in January of last year. At the time, there wasn't a clear mandate to hire FDEs, but there was a vague sense that we needed to pull some engineers closer to customers, and through this very close service, understand what they wanted and put our product to good use.

But over the past month or two, especially in the last week or two, there has been a huge amount of discussion about FDEs. I knew this moment would come, but I didn't expect it to come so fast and so fiercely. But I think this confirms a few things: first, model companies will realize that the model itself is not a product—getting a product to land requires a lot of work, which traditional model companies tend to overlook or even look down on. But without this kind of very close collaboration, even people with money who want to buy the model feel that after buying it, they don't know how to use it. We found that the FDE is a very good format for this.

Also, the boundary between model companies and application companies has become very blurry. OpenAI's and Anthropic's approach is more like: I still hire the best people to train my models—including Anthropic, as people may know, whose models are particularly good but also particularly expensive, can be sold at a high price, and in terms of efficiency per person, may be very high. But as for landing in each of the 360 trades, that requires a lot of people, and that's not necessarily something I want to do myself—which is why some of this is done through acquisitions, and some through capital, letting other companies do the FDE work. So the friend-yet-foe relationship between model companies and applications has become even more entangled.

II. The specific work of an FDE

Yiwen: If you had to define an FDE in one sentence, what would you say?

Jove: An FDE is an engineer who works closely with customers to make AI applications actually run, and who also takes on the responsibility of making the product better. So an FDE must, first, land the AI, and second, take these lessons learned and use them in a direct way to change the product and make it better. It's almost like being a "Forward Deployed CTO"—you are a very well-rounded person: you need to close the deal, land the AI application, and lock in the customer; but as a CTO, you're not just thinking all day about how to make the product better—you even have to disrupt yourself. So that's what an FDE does: land the AI and use these lessons learned to make the product stronger and stronger.

Yiwen: Among the customers you've worked with, how do FDEs change their existing workflows? For example, the Fortune 100 companies you serve are in many different fields, with different needs for AI-powered call centers (Cresta's core business), and the work surely involves a lot of terminology, knowledge, and even data from their professional domains. How do these customers communicate their needs to you? And how do you understand those needs and carry out the deployment?

Jove: In the customer experience field, we have relatively deep accumulated experience, having started back in 2017. So before this wave of AI, a large number of person-to-person conversations were already in the Cresta system—of course, we put a lot of effort into compliance and such. So, for example, big companies like Marriott had human agents before the agent era, so there's a large amount of text and voice corpus stored at Cresta in a compliant way.

So you can imagine that once we decide to pick a few AI agent use cases to work on, we will definitely analyze which use cases have high volume but aren't too complex to implement. That is, cases that don't require much human judgment—essentially where the SOP is fairly clear and the volume is large. Perhaps 80% of the business volume comes from 20% of the use cases. With some rough judgments in place, we can draw on past history to abstract out what typical questions customers are asking, and as a customer service agent—whether human or AI—how you should effectively resolve their issues. This avoids a lot of guesswork, because I believe AI and data need to be well combined.

When we take on a relatively large deal, the customer has often already been using other Cresta products for a long time. We can extract patterns from their human conversation data, and we can even use their data to train a small model, and use that model to run more simulations. So the FDE has to act as a relatively experienced AI implementation officer, judging which use cases we can start with and whether the resources corresponding to those use cases are in place; if not, we don't tend to just fix a batch for them—instead, we co-create with the customer, including later producing a version of the agent, which also requires extensive testing and optimization, and takes a lot of effort.

Yiwen: Do you need to go on-site to see how customers do this work?

Jove: The term "forward deployed" is quite eye-catching. Including myself and my colleagues, we've never spent more than a week at a single customer. Because we FDEs don't need to do initial outreach or pre-sales-type things—more often, the customer already has strong intent, and they bring us in as experts to figure out the best way to land it.

So we might have a kickoff meeting where everyone flies to their office, and we might hold a closed-door meeting for two or three days to set high-level goals, set KPIs, validate the corresponding APIs, and if things go well, we can even do a small PoC (proof of concept) on-site to get them interested. But after that, we each go back home. We have meetings weekly or even daily, and development happens either in the office or at home.

We might get together again during what's called UAT (User Acceptance Testing) on a project, or when we're discussing how to approach the next wave of use cases. And being face to face, people have eye contact and can even build some personal rapport. Building that rapport and trust early on is very useful for later work. A lot of things people don't feel comfortable putting in writing can be conveyed through casual chats and conversations, which builds more tacit understanding and reveals more background—things that are hard to achieve unless you're on site.

But all of these purposes together are about making AI implementation more solid—not forcing customers to learn, but rather understanding what you need and building it for you. After it's done, you can maintain it yourself if you want, but AI implementation is inherently difficult and time-consuming, and the FDE model makes it a bit easier.

Yiwen: The FDE concept was originally invented and popularized by Palantir. In the earliest days, Palantir actually had two teams, one called Echo and one called Delta, and together these two became the FDE. But the Deltas were probably closer to what we now understand as the FDE—the engineer role—while the Echo folks were more familiar with the professional domain. Could you tell us what you think the core problem Palantir was solving at the time?

Jove: Right, as the original creator of this model, Palantir definitely deserves respect. Of course, its business is quite unusual, because not every vendor can do military work. When it started 10 or 15 years ago, I think it was because many clients were unwilling to spell out exactly what they wanted—you had to be face to face with them, go into the same military tent, see the data before they were willing to get into details, and in between it might involve things like data modeling or building APIs on the fly. So they hired these two teams: one was somewhat like on-site forward deployed software engineers, and the other was more business-owner oriented—people who were familiar with combat operations or rescue missions, for example. One was technical, one non-technical.

The FDE, in the usual sense, is still a very technical role. For Cresta, we also felt this approach was a good fit. The ideal scenario, of course, is one person who can do everything—like the so-called one-person company, being both CEO and CTO—but such people are hard to hire, and everyone's energy is limited.

So we've made some attempts. For example, early on we had so-called "conversation designers," who were more familiar with the details of human interaction and empathy and didn't need technical skills. Over the past year or so, our approach has been to have FDEs work together with FDPMs (Forward Deployed Product Managers), where the FDPM doesn't need to be as technical.

The FDE is like a Forward Deployed CTO, and you can imagine the FDPM as the Forward Deployed CEO. The FDPM uses their interpersonal, communication, and negotiation skills to work closely with the customer, spending a lot of time building trust and figuring out what they really want. This includes things like building an agent—what it should say and what it shouldn't, how to create your test sets—many things not directly related to coding. They don't need to know how security should be done or how the network is configured, but this is itself a substantial chunk of work, and the FDPM can specifically take ownership of it.

Just as a CEO is responsible for the whole company, the FDPM is responsible for the overall behavior and capability level of the AI agent; the FDE ensures from a technical standpoint that the implementation is sound and that our corresponding tests are robust. The FDE also carries the responsibility of bringing these lessons learned back to the company to make the product better.

With an FDPM and FDE combined like this—since we easily have many meetings with two or three customers in a day—not every meeting needs the FDE's attendance, because they might still be discussing what to say first and what to say later. Once there's a conclusion, implementation usually isn't that hard, so the division of labor is different.

The FDE can focus more on industry best practices for AI, turning frequently repeated development work into SDKs, toolkits, and CLIs, contributing more from a technical angle; while the FDPM can grasp specific requirements, including handling risks and escalating them when needed, and even upselling—if you've already done three use cases, can you turn it into six? There's a lot of this. Just think of it as the difference between a CEO and a CTO—I think that's a valid framing. It avoids setting the bar too high for any single hire, and avoids having one person spend huge amounts of time every day on completely different things. Splitting it into two roles works better.

Yiwen: So to summarize: the FDE is itself a fairly technical role; the FDPM is more about industry knowledge—I'd guess many of these people come from consulting backgrounds, or more from business operations backgrounds. I'd like to take this opportunity to ask: what kind of person is best suited to be an FDE? What does an excellent FDE look like?

Jove: I think I'll push myself to say this: I want to build the world's best FDE team. Yes, that's the goal. Although we've achieved some results, I honestly think it's hard to say what proportion of Palantir's enormous success, or its rising stock price, is attributable to the FDE model, because so many factors are involved—but it did pioneer this model. The FDE has now become hot because AI implementation involves so many difficulties, and you don't want to throw that complexity directly at the customer. The FDE can absorb those layers of complexity and deliver a great solution to the customer.

Moreover, for many product companies, or SaaS and platform companies, the FDE takes lessons learned on the front lines and directly uses them to change the product. Think about it: 10 years ago, what could a Palantir FDE do even if they knew the product had many flaws? They could probably only go back and write a letter or file a ticket begging someone to fix it, and maybe it would get fixed six months later.

But because AI coding is so powerful now, our FDEs—though of course we set a high bar when hiring, looking for strong engineers—on the other hand, with these AI coding agents, Claude Code, or these models, if you know where something is wrong, where there's room for improvement, where things could be done differently—even if you have 5 or 10 different repos, different languages, different stacks—you can easily have the AI build it that way for you, then find the right person to review it. So iteration is very fast.

Beyond coding, skills themselves are now a great model—a way to distill things. Previously everything stayed in people's heads, and a lot of time had to be spent on knowledge transfer. Now you can write it as a skill—a long markdown file, some scripts, some reference docs. After doing two or three similar things, you can turn it into a great skill, and that capability can be quickly reused in the next project. Or, say we now have 30 FDEs and 20 more FDEs join—those 20 just need to install the skill and access it; they don't even have to learn it. So it easily becomes a snowball effect. The FDE is definitely in a good state right now, and hiring more FDEs enables more implementations.

Including my trip to New York TechWeek this time—I'm hoping to run some events and hire the right people. First, they need to be a qualified engineer, because this role is essentially like a CTO—you certainly wouldn't want someone who can't write code to be your CTO, so they must be technically strong. Since the team I'm hiring for is limited to AI agent FDEs, I don't have particularly strong requirements around data engineering or information security, but you absolutely must know AI agents.

A lot of times when I see a resume that says "I'm an AI engineer," I think—that goes without saying. Nowadays, if you're a software engineer and you're not an AI engineer, you're already out of the game. Nobody expects you to write every line of code yourself anymore. You have to know how to use these harness frameworks, you have to use Cursor, use Claude Code.

But not many people know how to develop and test AI agents, and that's a skill we really care about. We don't want to spend two or three months training someone—they should be able to join a project within two to three weeks. So you need to be a good developer, and you need to have developed and tested AI agents.

The other thing is that they need solid, convincing experience working directly with clients. After all, we're doing forward deployed engineering—even if in the past you only had online meetings, or occasionally went on-site for a few days, you still had to communicate with the client's CTO, their IT director, their senior people; sometimes you also talk to non-technical people.

You should know how to simplify a complex problem, or be able to pick up specific points from what they're saying and verify them; sometimes you have to say no. This isn't just about communication in English—it's about being seasoned as a person, essentially like a CTO—a CTO can't be someone who only writes code either.

So for these skills, I generally expect them to have either done consulting, or been a founding engineer themselves, or done a lot of freelance work—I think all of those are great. Beyond coding and beyond client-facing work, the rest is about being reliable and resilient, because being an FDE is genuinely busy—you're multitasking on many things at once, under a lot of pressure, facing a very imperfect world—the APIs are paper-thin, the SOPs are basically nonexistent, and the documentation is all over the place.

The pressure is intense, and people may have unrealistic expectations, so you need a strong ability to face enormous complexity and enormous uncertainty. You need independent judgment—to know how to push things forward, and how to step outside the box to get things done.

That's why I love hiring founders, co-founding engineers, or people who have weathered many storms—people who understand that nothing is guaranteed to succeed, and that you simply have to work extremely hard to get to a slightly better state.

So this kind of reliability and resilience is something we hope to see demonstrated through these experiences. I don't hire any junior FDEs, because as I said, a project might only have one or two people, co-creating with the client's CTO. If you're a very junior person, it's hard to build that kind of trust, and you often won't have a clue what to do—you can't just open AI and let it tell you what to do; if you lack your own judgment, that's a serious weakness. So your technical skills definitely need to be solid, your client-facing skills need to be there, and you need to be able to juggle many things, end to end.

Yiwen: You just said that FDEs face an imperfect world, and are working with agent tools that are still fairly new. So will this role be a long-term position, or is it a transitional one? In the AI era, as tools keep iterating and maturing on their own, do you think this role will change? Or will it disappear?

Jove: The only constant is change, but compared to many other in-house engineering jobs, I think there's still a long way to go before FDE work can be replaced by AI. In the short term, say the next one to two years, there will definitely be more and more tools that make FDE work more efficient. For example, a lot of our calls and communications are now recorded by tools like Gong, which can transcribe them, and you can even ask questions against the recording; yes, and we have tools like Glean that can search the latest chat records and even look at code.

These tools make those of us who juggle multiple things at once more efficient. Sometimes I might have two or three meetings booked in the same time slot on my calendar—you're bound to miss some of them—but with these tools you can be present in multiple places at once, or at least avoid missing many specific points.

These tools allow an FDE—for example, suppose we currently average two to three projects per person—to eventually handle five or six, so improvements in tools will make us more efficient, or able to do more. But further out, things will diverge: there will be a demand for many highly senior FDEs to do the really hard things. You can use all kinds of tools, but the tools themselves can't solve those problems—you still need very experienced people to do them.

On one hand, there will be a large number of companies that previously didn't need an FDE thinking, "Can I hire a cheap FDE?" Just like what's happening with software engineers—hiring demand is actually rising, because companies that previously couldn't afford to hire software engineers—small clinics, small sole proprietors—now think they too can hire a software engineer to productize one of their workflows.

So correspondingly, there will emerge a batch of FDEs targeting small and medium-sized businesses, targeting this long tail, even remote ones—for example, in Vietnam or other less developed places—there may be many FDEs who can never go on-site, but who combine the client's needs with their AI skills to build what the client needs. If they can build a product of their own along the way, that's another model.

So as long as this complexity on the client side still exists, there will always be a gap in what AI can fully automate, and that gap needs to be filled by FDEs. Only when one day there's a fully AI FDE—even today, SDRs (Sales Development Representatives) haven't been well automated by AI—suppose one day 99% of FDE work can be done by AI, whether it's understanding clients, writing prompts, running evals, communicating with clients, or even the AI doing agent-to-agent communication and alignment with another client's AI—at that point, what we'd worry about isn't the FDE itself; the entire industry and world would already be different, and humans would be involved very little. But I think there's still a long way to go before that.

I'm still very confident about the FDE role—this profession will become richer and richer, more and more people will join it, and people will increasingly recognize its importance.

III. Private Equity and a Key Entry Point for AI Deployment

In our interview with Jove, two layers of questions came up for us: First, what deployment companies do—including going deep into enterprises, transforming processes, and helping them use AI well—is actually quite similar to the logic of traditional consulting. Will the consulting industry be replaced by this wave? Second, as Jove said, why would model companies seek partnerships with PE firms? We know that institutions like Blackstone hold large portfolios of portfolio companies, many of them traditional businesses that have been operating for decades. For PE, what exactly is the appeal of this partnership?

Jove talked about part of this from his perspective; afterward, I also spoke with Oliver, who has a consulting background and long experience serving PE clients, about these two questions from the angle of industry change.

Yiwen: Hello Oliver, please briefly introduce yourself and the company you currently work at for our listeners.

Oliver: Thank you, Yiwen. I'm Oliver, VP of the enterprise business at Invisible Technologies. My job is to help enterprise clients deploy AI using our solutions. Before that, I did private equity consulting at McKinsey, on a team called Rewired, which specialized in helping companies rethink their business models and become more technology-driven and AI-driven.

Yiwen: You said you help companies deploy solutions—what exactly do those solutions look like?

Yiwen: It sounds like what you're doing is actually quite similar to the "Deployment Company" that OpenAI announced a while back—they're also helping enterprises deploy AI. What do you make of that move? Why do you think they're doing it?

Oliver: I think they're doing exactly the right thing. Over the past six months, you can clearly feel that CFOs and corporate executives are talking more and more about cutting costs. Meanwhile, research reports from MIT and Stanford also show that very few companies have actually gotten AI working and scaled it up. This gap is unsustainable—it can't go on like this. So large model vendors must drive real enterprise adoption and prove ROI; just selling a chatbot won't accomplish that. If they want to open this door, they have to take the same path we did.

So I think this move is well-timed. Of course, they have massive capital and strong technical capabilities, so they'll surely pull it off. It's just that their original playbook was very horizontal—although they had some vertical applications, at their core they were building general-purpose large models, and now they're suddenly pivoting to building customized workflows for enterprises. That's a completely different market move, a completely different sales approach, very different from what they were used to. I believe they can figure it out, but it will take some time.

Yiwen: I'd like to start this question from the private equity side. Since you said you serve a lot of financial institution clients, I feel there are two threads here: one is these institutions using AI internally, and the other is the companies they've invested in—portfolio companies, many of which are traditional SaaS companies that PE firms invested in and may also need to transform. What do you think they need most right now? What are they afraid of? And is that why they're going to partner and invest with OpenAI and Anthropic?

Oliver: I think PE and private capital firms have three core needs.

The first is signaling value. I've been dealing with PE firms for a while now: three years ago, people were still asking "can you come explain how AI works"; two years ago it became "can you help me think about how to roll out AI across the entire portfolio"; this year it has completely changed—they come to me saying "I need to fundraise from LPs, from pension funds, from my capital providers, and I have to prove I'm at the forefront of AI. I need case studies showing I've created value through AI, otherwise LPs won't give me money." It's a completely different logic. Now, for GPs, being able to demonstrate AI adoption is a matter of fundraising survival. Partnering with the loudest names in the industry is a great form of endorsement, so the signaling value is very high.

The second is portfolio value creation. This is also a very real need. When AI is used right, it really can create significant value. The details are complicated, but this part is genuinely real.

The third is the investment return itself. The structure of these partnerships is quite attractive—essentially, it lets GPs enter a high-return track and gain exposure to high-growth assets. From that angle, the logic makes complete sense too.

Yiwen: I find your first point really interesting. What do you think is driving LPs to push AI?

Oliver: The same reason most companies are pushing AI. From an ordinary consumer's perspective, the more related content you read, the more you can feel what AI can do. The change is frighteningly fast—everything seems to be moving at a crazy pace. So everyone realizes AI can do so much, and if you're not seriously working on AI, you're falling behind.

From the LP's perspective, if I give my money to a GP, of course I want to make sure they're also using AI to transform their portfolio companies—that's a very real need. One more point, since you mentioned SaaS: over the past five to ten years, PE's two biggest asset classes were healthcare and software, and almost every PE firm has exposure to software companies. This year, the "SaaS is dead" narrative has been making a lot of noise, and both LPs and GPs are highly anxious. GPs are all working hard to prove "we're fine," so the signaling value is further amplified.

But then again, if you look at those Anthropic and Coatue partnerships, the counterparties aren't pure software investors—because you're quite right, the places where AI creates the most value are often not software companies.

Yiwen: Right, it's those traditional businesses—industrials, manufacturing, and the like.

Oliver: Business services, industrials, healthcare—especially healthcare, which is huge. Basically every industry where software couldn't previously help much can now do really interesting things with software. Another great example is GPs themselves. What do PE firms do? Source deals, value them, invest, and manage assets. This is very labor-intensive work that requires very expensive people—whether internal teams or external advisors. This workflow is exactly the kind that AI is best suited to transform. I have a major client, a very large asset management firm, and we've been able to help them transform these workflows with astonishing results.

Yiwen: Could you give us a few concrete examples? I feel that when I talk to many finance practitioners these days, they're still using AI for research and summarization—still the same use cases related to large language models. I'm quite curious how you achieve truly automated workflows.

Oliver: Sure, there's a lot to talk about. If you break down an investment fund's business, there are roughly a few modules: fundraising, investment management, compliance and finance, and fund operations. Let me pick a few workflows as examples.

First, fundraising. I have a major client, a very large asset management firm, that wanted to partner with a smaller asset manager—the partner would include their products in its own product line and take a commission. Sounds good. But the partner said every client meeting would need one of their sales managers present—of course the large asset manager wouldn't accept that, because the profit would be gone; it's simply not feasible. So they came to us asking if we could build an AI sales assistant to participate in those conversations.

The workflow goes like this: first, they have roughly a thousand products, so you need to build the data infrastructure to integrate those thousand products; then build an input layer so the other party can enter client data, with permissions properly isolated; then a computation module that calculates the optimal product mix for that client—this part is deterministic, since it's essentially math; then generate sales talking points for pre-meeting preparation; then a tool for use during the meeting; and finally, after the meeting, automatically update the product proposal based on meeting notes. It's a complete feedback loop, about seven steps. This system allows the large asset manager to serve a much broader range of clients—a classic case.

Another one I find really interesting is the investment decision process itself. When doing due diligence, you typically have to run ten workstreams and hire lawyers and all kinds of advisors—commercial due diligence, environmental due diligence, and so on. Coordinating with all these people puts a lot of pressure on the investment team. We're building them a platform where they can interact with all their advisors, push questions to them, and automatically scan the entire data room. You can see a real-time interface tracking all advisors' progress, and you can also pull up questions this fund has raised in similar past deals, drawing on lessons learned from previous investments.

This way, they can leverage the firm's institutional knowledge while greatly streamlining communication with external advisors. And the final document output is automated too, which was itself a big burden. I've seen too many investors working weekends on this, so I'm glad to help them save that time.

There's also fund operations, like net asset value calculations or account reconciliation—every month, or even every day after market close, you have to confirm account balances are correct. My first job was bookkeeping, which was extremely time-consuming, but this process can be fully automated. OK, I've talked a lot—just hoping to show you some useful scenarios.

Yiwen: Based on what you just said, I actually want to know—do you think AI-era PE acquisitions have changed? Because in the past, when we talked about a PE firm acquiring a company, we might think of things like M&A between portfolio companies, some roll-up consolidation. Now it feels like we've almost entered a so-called "AI roll-up" era—on the surface you're buying these companies, but what you're actually buying is their workflows, and then we transform them into an AI-native company. Do you think this will change how PE itself operates?

Oliver: I've seen many different plays, but they mainly fall into two categories. The first type of investor says, any area where the AI disruption risk is too high, we won't invest in—that's actually many people's gut reaction. The second type is investors who more actively embrace this; they say this is actually a very interesting moment to create value through AI. The Amex GBT deal is a great example, and there are many similar deals.

Investors are acquiring companies that historically weren't very technology-intensive and aggressively empowering them with technology and AI. So this is clearly becoming an emerging playbook, and some leading GPs are already doing it. And I believe the scale of value they can create is real.

But actually creating that value is not a simple matter. One problem I see now is that there's a big gap between what many people imagine they can achieve and what can actually be delivered in reality. I want to emphasize a core point: a common mistake many people make is to view AI purely as a cost-reduction tool. In reality, AI's true value often lies in generating revenue and opening up new revenue opportunities.

So I often ask clients a question: if I gave you ten thousand college-educated employees for free right now, what would you do? What have you wanted to do in the past but couldn't? Because to some extent, that's the capability AI brings today. Like the two asset management company examples I just gave—for them, it means entering an entirely new customer or business segment, a market they simply couldn't reach before. So this isn't about cutting costs; it's about increasing revenue.

I think that's the direction many companies should really be heading. But right now, everyone is still very focused on the cost reductions AI brings, and I don't think that's the most powerful angle. What they should really be thinking about is: what could I not do before that I can do now—that is, revenue creation.

It can take many forms. Let me give you another example: we have a client that's a dairy company with many farms and cows. When we discussed with them, we asked: if you had ten thousand people, what would you do? They said, that's an interesting question—we would write reports for every account, because they wanted to reduce the time spent on these reports and spend more time keeping their cows healthy. So we helped them build a complete data integration and custom AI system to generate health reports for all their cows. That way, they could reclaim more time to genuinely maintain the health of their cows, which wasn't feasible in the past.

IV. How is AI changing consulting and enterprises themselves?

Yiwen: It sounds like what you're doing is quite similar to what consulting firms do, which is another topic I wanted to raise. Traditional consulting firms used to help clients handle areas they weren't familiar with, but now tech companies and AI companies like yours seem to be taking over that role, using AI to transform processes. Do you think the consulting industry will become obsolete because of this? Or will consulting itself turn into AI transformation consulting?

Oliver: I think over the next three to five years, consulting will see a wave of growth, because every enterprise talking about AI needs to rethink its business model. The simplest example is law firms—billing by the hour is getting harder and harder; if they switch to outcome-based billing, the entire incentive structure changes. For this kind of transformation, you need someone to talk to—you want to know how others have done it, whether there's experience to draw on, and you need someone to guide you through the journey. So I believe there will be a clear increase in demand for consulting over the next three to five years.

But the ones who truly unlock value are those who ultimately leave behind a transformed business. So I think AI labs and companies like ours—this model of finishing the work and leaving behind a transformed business—is the way to genuinely create value, rather than just talking about how to transform.

That said, there's a lot of uncertainty in the market right now, and everyone is in wait-and-see mode, so the demand for consulting is real—people don't know what to do or where to start. That's also a big part of my job: sitting down with clients to first figure out which things are worth doing first, and this has to be analyzed case by case.

Yiwen: Have you ever encountered a company that thought it could use AI to handle a certain workflow, but in reality it simply didn't work? For example, are people sometimes too optimistic about AI, or do they misunderstand how AI operates?

Oliver: The most common problem is wanting to AI-ify everything, but in reality that path doesn't work. You have to get a few things right. The most critical one is a good data platform, whose value compounds over time. No matter how smart AI is, without sufficient information and knowledge it can't do anything. We have a data module called Neuron that specifically helps you integrate data, map it clearly, and ensure the data is usable. This is the first hurdle for most companies, and it's not cheap, because they've never done anything like it before.

The second common mistake is: in a ten-step workflow, not every step should use AI. You can use AI to optimize the overall process, clarify the logic, and delineate which steps are deterministic, but not all steps should be handed to AI. For example, with financial processes like account reconciliation, you wouldn't want AI to do it—what you want is a deterministic result. So you can use AI to help you sort out the workflow logic, but many of the execution steps should be hard-coded, deterministic mathematical calculations. I think the two biggest pitfalls are, first, data, and second, letting AI do things that should be deterministic.
