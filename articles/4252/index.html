Silicon Valley's Hottest Job Is Here! Why Are Big Tech Companies All Hiring "Forward Deployed Engineers"?

Author: Cheng Cheng; Editor: Pan Yinru

The Forward Deployed Engineer (FDE), a role born over a decade ago, is now becoming one of the hottest jobs in Silicon Valley.

Data from online recruitment platform Indeed shows that in April 2026, the number of FDE job postings surged 729% year over year to 5,330, with Anthropic, OpenAI, Palantir, Stripe, and Google Cloud all hiring for this type of engineer. According to market intelligence and investment research search platform AlphaSense, in 2025, the number of listed companies mentioning this role on earnings calls jumped from 8 the previous year to 50.

Last month, OpenAI founded OpenAI Deployment with an initial investment of over $4 billion, dispatching FDEs deep into enterprises to provide customized AI building services, while also bringing about 150 FDEs to the new company through the acquisition of AI consulting firm Tomoro. Anthropic, meanwhile, partnered with Blackstone, Goldman Sachs, and others to establish an enterprise services joint venture valued at $1.5 billion, likewise adopting the FDE on-site model to drive deep deployment of the Claude model.

The role also comes with generous compensation. A 2026 survey by Perspective AI of 1,500 FDEs showed that the median total annual compensation for senior FDEs at frontier labs reached $485,000, rising as high as $725,000 at the senior staff level, with total compensation at top labs falling in the range of $350,000 to $550,000.

What signals does Silicon Valley's massive demand for FDEs send? On this question, Tan Yinliang, a professor of decision science and management information systems at the China Europe International Business School (CEIBS) who focuses primarily on AI economics and AI management research, told Yicai that this trend shows the competitive focus of large model companies is shifting from "whose model is stronger" to "who can turn models into productivity systems for enterprises." Technical leadership still matters, but it is becoming an entry ticket; what will truly determine the winner of the second half will be enterprise sales capability, industry solutions capability, engineering delivery capability, ecosystem partnership capability, and the ability to continuously demonstrate AI return on investment.

What do FDEs mainly do?

FDE is not an entirely new position; the American big data analytics and AI platform company Palantir pioneered this model. Wall Street analysts view FDEs as Palantir's "secret weapon." Dan Ives, managing director and senior analyst at Wedbush Securities, stated in a report sent to Yicai that the company's core value lies in the professional capabilities provided by FDEs — going deep into the field alongside customers, building products in real time, while observing customer pain points and seeing changes immediately.

Most software companies follow a process of gathering customer requirements, designing solutions, building products, and deploying at scale, but this approach hit obstacles with the defense and intelligence agency customers Palantir wanted to win. These institutional customers not only struggle to articulate their requirements clearly but also cannot publicly share their data, and even for similar counterterrorism projects, workflows differ greatly between agencies.

"We found that every customer wants something different. Rather than making a separate version for each one, or patching in a bunch of features that only apply to a specific customer, it's better to make the product a highly customizable platform. That way, you have to send employees on-site to deeply understand users and do localized adaptation." Bob McGrew, an early Palantir executive and now OpenAI's Chief Research Officer, revealed in a media interview last September that the company's CTO Sham Sankar invented the FDE strategy: having FDEs enter with the existing product to fill the gap between product capabilities and actual needs, first paving a "gravel road," while the backend product and engineering teams are responsible for rolling it out to 5 to 10 customers, turning that road into a "paved superhighway."

Internally, Palantir divides FDEs into two key roles: Echo teams and Delta teams. Echo teams are on-site analysts responsible for understanding customer needs, identifying which existing product features apply and which can be improved, while also managing customer relationships. The ideal candidate for this role needs domain expertise — for example, a former Army officer — as well as a rebellious spirit and the ability to identify shortcomings in existing practices. The other team is called Delta, composed of software engineers whose core capability is building "prototypes," with the ability to deliver in software form within extremely short timeframes rather than pursuing absolutely correct code. By 2016, the number of FDEs Palantir employed had already exceeded that of traditional software engineers.

Speaking about the difference between the standard SaaS (software as a service) model and the FDE model, McGrew said the former favors simple, repeatable contracts and pricing, and because the marginal cost of deployment is very low, SaaS teams are happy with small contracts. But under the FDE model, product requirements gradually become more complex, and contract sizes gradually grow.

However, some have raised new voices. Venture capital firm a16z founder Andreessen Horowitz (Marc Andreessen) called the invention of this role "title arbitrage" in a blog post — creating a new title to name a new force emerging within institutions and new capabilities under development. He argued that what Palantir is actually betting on is that "deep software integration with large enterprises and governments will be very important in the future. This work deserves a corresponding elevation in prominence."

Barry McCardel worked as an FDE at Palantir for about five years before founding the AI analytics platform Hex. In his view, it is not a glamorous job. "Doing this job means spending a lot of time on planes, sleeping in three-star hotels somewhere in the middle of America, and working in a dim, windowless conference room without enough power outlets." He told the media that the work itself is also extremely challenging: limited resources, small teams, yet having to solve an unprecedented problem under tight deadlines. McCardel added that customers often don't end up using the projects they spent time developing. "Extreme speed, high expectations for forward deployed engineering actions, and intensity are not for everyone," he said.

What signals does it send?

Notably, the FDE, which has existed for over a decade, is not a new role, but the timing of its explosion in Silicon Valley highly overlaps with the period from the birth of AI agents to their commercialization. What signals does this change send?

In terms of market demand, enterprises are seeking AI transformation, but purchasing AI does not equal completing AI transformation. The real difficulty lies in integrating AI into CRM, ERP, data warehouses, knowledge bases, permission systems, and approval workflows, and getting it actually used within the organization. When Google Cloud CEO Thomas Kurian posted a job listing for FDEs last month, he said the role is not new to Google Cloud, but customer and partner demand for Google's enterprise AI products and for Google engineers to help them embrace agent development is growing rapidly.

Meanwhile, large model companies are also targeting enterprise deployment. Almost simultaneous with the large-scale hiring of FDEs, OpenAI and Anthropic have also been poaching from software giant Salesforce. According to profiles on professional social platform LinkedIn, nearly a hundred Salesforce employees have moved to these two AI giants so far this year, mainly working in sales and marketing. Analysts say the two giants value these employees' deep connections with Fortune 500 companies and expect these veterans to help enterprises accelerate the deployment of their products.

Tan Yinliang believes that the popularity of the FDE role shows that AI commercialization is undergoing a shift from "selling model capabilities" to "selling business outcomes." In terms of business models, as models gradually become infrastructure, simply selling underlying API calls easily leads to price wars. If AI agents can be embedded into enterprise processes and directly improve sales, customer service, R&D, risk control, or operational efficiency, it becomes easier to build sustainable annual recurring revenue (ARR).

"Looking further ahead, AI companies are transforming from model companies into a new generation of enterprise software companies. The key to future competition lies not just in the strength of models, but in who possesses industry knowledge, customer relationships, sales networks, and delivery capabilities to turn AI from demos into enterprise production systems," he said.
