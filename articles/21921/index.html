Baseten Forward Deployed Engineer: What the Job Really Tests

FDE Times · 2026-10-24 · 6 min read

Summary

This article analyzes Baseten's Forward Deployed Engineer job posting and what it actually screens for. FDEs sit in engineering, not sales, and the first hiring criterion is exceptionally strong software engineering fundamentals, since Baseten believes empathy and communication can be trained. The team targets having 70% of FDE-built work integrated into the product, so code must be reusable. The core skill is converting vague customer goals into production services measured on quality, latency, and cost. The author offers preparation advice, including a portfolio project using Baseten's Truss, plus four common CV mistakes with rewrites.

In brief

  • Baseten's FDEs sit in engineering, not sales; strong software engineering fundamentals are the first hiring criterion.
  • The job is turning vague goals into specs, PoCs and services measured on quality, latency and cost.
  • The team aims for 70% of what FDEs build to be integrated into the product, so your code has to be reusable.

Baseten has set its forward deployed engineering team a hard target: 70% of what the team builds should be integrated into the product. The job posting adds a line that puzzles many applicants. Candidates are expected to be an engineer, a project manager and a product manager at once. The easy reflex is to fill a CV with phrases like “strong communicator” and “customer-facing experience”. That misses the point.

Baseten is explicit about where the role sits. According to the company’s blog post on its FDE team, FDEs sit within engineering and regularly contribute to the product. The posting for an engineering manager on the team is blunter still: FDE at Baseten is not a sales function.

So preparing for this role is not about polishing presentation skills. You need to show that you are an engineer who can take a hazy business goal and hand back a service that runs in production, is measurable, and is general enough for the company to turn into a feature.

What is the posting actually testing?

The team’s mission, in Baseten’s own words, has two halves: help customers see real value early, and build products and tooling that push the Baseten platform further. The second half is what separates an FDE here from an ordinary integration engineer.

The most concrete number sits in the team’s long-term goal: 70% of what is built within FDE should be integrated into the product. In day-to-day terms, if you write a latency benchmarking script for customer A, the team will want that script to work later for customers B and C, and then to become a tool in the product.

The first hiring criterion the blog names is exceptionally strong software engineering fundamentals. The post argues that empathy and communication can be trained in a technically strong engineer. The sensible reading: the technical side is what you must bring with you.

The second criterion, also from the blog, is curiosity at every layer of the stack, from ML down to infrastructure and networking.

The listing on Built In gets more specific: write production-quality code in a general-purpose language, and be familiar with AI/ML pipelines and the model development and deployment lifecycle.

The US version asks for at least two years of experience in a fast-growing environment; the London (EMEA) version asks for at least one. The door is open to early-career engineers, provided the application proves the right things.

From “faster, cheaper” to a spec with numbers

If you read only one line of the posting closely, make it this one: FDEs work directly with customers to turn ambiguous business goals into reliable, observable services with clear outcomes on quality, latency and cost.

The responsibilities section traces the whole arc: from vague goals to clear specs and tightly scoped PoCs, then on to deployment. FDEs are also brought in at the earliest stage of the customer journey, which means you usually receive a request when it is at its haziest.

Take a hypothetical case. A customer support company using an LLM to summarize tickets tells you: “The system is too slow, and the bill is too high.” That is not yet a technical requirement. Your first job is to break it down along the three axes the posting names.

Axis Discovery question Metric for the spec (illustrative)
Quality “What does a good summary look like? Who is grading it?” A set of 200 tickets with reference summaries; scores no lower than the current system
Latency “Which screen are users waiting on? How long before they give up?” p95 under a threshold agreed with the customer, measured at peak-hour traffic
Cost “Is the budget per request or per month?” Cost per 1,000 requests, compared with the current level

With that table in hand, the PoC becomes obvious: try a smaller model or a different serving configuration, run it on the same 200 tickets, report three numbers. If quality drops, you have evidence to go back and negotiate with the customer instead of arguing from gut feeling.

This is also where the project manager and product manager roles come in. You decide the order of experiments, agree with the customer on what “done” means, and ask yourself which parts of the work should become shared tooling. The script that runs evals on those 200 tickets, if parameterized from the start, is a prime candidate for the 70%.

How to prepare in a few weeks

Start with Baseten’s own tools. Truss is an open-source CLI for deploying and serving ML models on Baseten. A small project with it gives you two things at once: an understanding of the model lifecycle as the company actually handles it, and something concrete to talk about in interviews.

A good portfolio project should repeat the exercise above. Pick a real problem, write a one-page spec covering the three axes, package and deploy a model with Truss, measure latency at several load levels, estimate cost, then write a README explaining the trade-offs you made.

End the README with a short section: if this were work for a real customer, which parts would you extract into reusable tools?

Do not skip the lower layers, since Baseten’s blog names infrastructure and networking directly. Take a slow endpoint: the culprit could be the model, a cold start, the infrastructure configuration or the network path. Build the habit of asking “where is it slow?” and measuring each hop instead of guessing.

Four CV lines that send an application off course

Every bullet on your CV should follow the same frame: the original vague request, the metric you agreed, the measured result, and what became shared. The four mistakes below each depart from that frame, with a suggested rewrite (all examples are illustrative).

The most common mistake is positioning yourself as presales, a role Baseten has said plainly is not its FDE. Weak line: “Demoed the product to prospects and supported the sales team in closing deals.” Rewrite: “Built a text summarization PoC for a customer, wrote a spec with latency and per-request cost thresholds, then took the service to production.”

The second is knowing only one layer, when the criterion is curiosity at every layer of the stack. Weak line: “Fine-tuned a text classification model to high accuracy.” Rewrite: “Fine-tuned a classification model, packaged and deployed it as an endpoint myself, measured latency at each hop and identified cold start as the cause of slowness.”

The third is harder to spot: describing a “successful” PoC without a single number. Weak line: “Built a successful chatbot PoC; the customer was satisfied.” Rewrite: “Built a chatbot PoC and reported the quality score on a sample ticket set, p95 latency and cost per 1,000 requests against the old system.”

The last is describing one-off work for a single customer and stopping there, when the team’s goal is for 70% of what FDEs build to go into the product.

Weak line: “Wrote a benchmarking script for customer A’s project.” Rewrite: “Turned an internal team’s ‘cut costs’ request into a spec with latency and per-request cost thresholds, then packaged the measurement tool as a library for three other teams.”

Put the four rewrites next to the posting and they answer the question Baseten is asking: can this engineer turn a vague goal into a measurable service, and leave something behind for the product?
