Summary

Based on 7,408 engineer job listings received by Japan's デファクトリー over roughly four months (4,926 with stated rates), this article quantifies the realities of freelance engineering in Japan. Average monthly rate was ¥780,000 (median ¥750,000), with 19% of listings above ¥1 million. Rates vary by role: upstream/lead listings averaged ¥916,000 versus ¥667,000 for operations/maintenance, a gap of about ¥250,000. Take-home pay is estimated at 65–70% of revenue after taxes and insurance. The author argues most cited risks—income gaps, rate ceilings, lack of benefits—are manageable with preparation, and recommends independence only after 3+ years of experience, six months of savings, and diversified client channels. It briefly mentions FDE as an alternative to time-based SES contracts.

  • Average listing rate ¥780,000/month; median ¥750,000; 19% exceed ¥1 million.
  • Upstream/lead roles average ¥916,000, about ¥250,000 above operations roles.

The reality for freelance engineers is that while many projects average around ¥750,000 per month, the difference in role and preparation can create a gap of about ¥250,000 per month. Of the 7,408 engineer projects received by our company, DeFactory, the average of the 4,926 with listed rates was ¥789,000 per month, and the median (the middle value when rates are sorted from lowest) was ¥750,000 per month. Projects of ¥800,000 or more per month accounted for 42%, and those of ¥1,000,000 or more accounted for 19%. Projects with upstream/lead terms in the title averaged ¥916,000, while operations/maintenance/testing projects averaged ¥667,000 — a difference of about ¥250,000 overall, and a gap of ¥160,000–230,000 even within the same skill set. However, revenue does not translate directly into take-home pay; take-home pay is roughly 65–70% of revenue (Chapter 3), and income drops to zero in months when a project ends. Projects requiring 5–6 years of practical experience averaged ¥773,000 per month (5-4). Based on these figures and public statistics, this article systematically explains the reasons people say "don't do it" and the countermeasures. Detailed rate figures are summarized in the Rate Ranking by Skill.

Whether you can work freely is determined not by freelance status itself, but by the conditions of the project. Many projects require aligning working hours with the client, and it is common for compensation to be reduced if you work fewer hours than contracted.

  • "I can work whenever I like every day": Many projects require matching the client's working hours
  • "I can work from anywhere": Many projects prohibit remote work due to security policies
  • "I can freely adjust my workload": Contracts commonly reduce pay if hours fall below the contracted amount

Common working styles are three types: full-time quasi-delegation (paid for hours worked) of 5 days a week, 8 hours a day; juggling multiple projects 3-4 days a week; and a hybrid type that is fully remote with one office day per week. The more in-demand and highly rated your skills, the easier it is to negotiate working conditions.

As a guideline, take-home pay is roughly 65-70% of revenue. This is a rough estimate assuming taxes and insurance premiums total 30-35% of revenue, excluding expenses (the combined total of pension, national health insurance, income tax, resident tax, and individual business tax). This is because you pay the full social insurance premiums that your employer used to split with you as an employee, and you also pay income and resident taxes yourself.

The three most easily overlooked are the following.

  • National Health Insurance premiums: Vary greatly depending on income, municipality, and household composition. Check the amount on your municipality's estimate page
  • National Pension: A fixed amount, in the upper 10,000 yen range per month (about 215,000 yen per year). Check the latest amount on the Japan Pension Service website
  • Income tax and resident tax: The tax rate rises as income increases. Combined with national health insurance and pension, some people pay over 30% of their revenue

The advantages are rate and discretion; the disadvantages are income stability and security. Let's organize this by comparing with being a company employee.

The advantages can be summed up in two points: being able to grow your income yourself, and being able to design your own career.

  • Freedom in rate negotiation: You can raise your compensation according to your skills and market value
  • Juggling multiple projects: You can diversify your income sources
  • Tax savings through deductible expenses: Communication costs, equipment, books, seminar fees, and so on can be counted as expenses
  • Blue tax return special deduction: You can receive a deduction of up to 650,000 yen (conditions apply)
  • You can choose your environment: You can end contracts for projects that don't suit you without renewing
  • You can design your career: You can deliberately accumulate the experience you need next through project selection

The disadvantage is that you must provide for yourself the stability and security that a company once covered.

In terms of mental and practical aspects, there are the burdens of loneliness and self-management, of handling taxes, accounting, and contract management yourself, and of creating your own opportunities for skill improvement.

The hurdles can be divided into five: income gaps, rate plateaus, loneliness, age, and AI. For all of them, preparing before they occur is less burdensome than reacting after the fact.

If there is a gap between the end of one project and the start of the next, your income during that period is zero. Without financial reserves, you may be forced to accept unfavorable projects.

  • Secure savings of at least six months of living expenses before going independent
  • Start looking for your next project one to two months before the current one ends
  • Register with two or three agents to broaden your options
  • Consider splitting income sources, such as four days of work per week plus one day on a separate project

Rates tend to plateau when you keep working with the same technology in the same role. As seen in 1-2, even with the same Python, operations and maintenance roles average 735,000 yen while upstream and lead roles average 904,000 yen, a difference of about 170,000 yen by role.

According to METI's "Survey on IT Human Resource Supply and Demand" (2019), it is estimated that up to 790,000 IT professionals will be in short supply by 2030. Demand is analyzed to be especially high in advanced technology areas (AI, cloud, security, etc.) (Reference: METI Survey on IT Human Resource Supply and Demand).

  • Allocate part of your working hours (3 to 5 hours per week) to learning
  • Choose your next project based on whether demand will grow in 2 to 3 years and whether it moves you closer to upstream or lead roles
  • Once a year, take stock of your skills and reassess your market value

SES-type contracts based on man-month rates (working on-site at clients, compensated for time) are compensation for working hours, so the rate ceiling tends to be structurally fixed. As an alternative, there is also the role of FDE (Forward Deployed Engineer), which takes contribution to business outcomes as the axis of value.

If you want to exceed 900,000 yen per month, the following figures are a guide. Upstream and lead projects average 916,000 yen (median 850,000 yen), and projects requiring 7 or more years of experience average about 890,000 yen (median 800,000 yen, 143 listings) (5-4). For people around the 10-year mark, rather than improving development skills, the shortcut is to clearly state roles such as design, requirements definition, and team leadership in your resume, and apply only to projects seeking those roles.

In an environment where you work alone, not having anyone to consult with when you hit technical obstacles can be a burden. Building places to consult before going independent can reduce that burden.

  • Regularly participate in technical communities such as connpass, study groups, and Slack communities
  • Use coworking spaces to create an environment with other people working in the same place
  • Build relationships for exchanging information with fellow freelancers in the same position
  • Talk regularly with your agent's representative to get industry information

The age hurdle depends more on how you present your roles and experience than on age itself. Job listings sometimes state an age limit such as "up to 40s," so it needs to be checked before applying.

On the other hand, our aggregation showed that the longer the years of experience required by job listings, the higher the average rate. Among the 1,430 listings with rates that stated years of experience, projects requiring 1 to 2 years averaged 668,000 yen (262 listings), 3 to 4 years averaged 745,000 yen (591 listings), 5 to 6 years averaged 773,000 yen (434 listings), and 7 or more years averaged 888,000 yen (143 listings). Years were extracted from "X years or more" in the title and the first 1,000 characters of the body text, taking the maximum when multiple values appeared. This is an aggregation of only listings that stated years, showing the relationship between years required by listings and rates, not the age or experience of people actually hired. It also does not mean that accumulating experience will necessarily lead to these rates.

The conclusion is: it's possible, but going independent directly with no experience is not recommended. People who are stable make four things habits: understanding their market value, diversifying sources of projects, systematizing finances, and updating skills. All four can be imitated without special talent.

Every 6 months to a year, have interviews with multiple agents and ask about current market rates and which skills are in high demand. This prevents accepting work at undervalued rates and also guides what to learn next.

Having three channels—agents (multiple companies), posting on social media, and referrals from past clients and acquaintances—means if one dries up, the others can compensate.

For your first project, choose an entry point where you can get work even without a track record.

  • Through an agent: They handle everything up to interviews and project introductions. However, practical experience tends to be a prerequisite, and with 1-2 years of experience, the projects you are introduced to are limited. Register with 2-3 companies and compare conditions
  • Introductions from past workplaces or acquaintances: Since they know your track record, things often get decided easily
  • Building a track record through side work: If you have delivery experience, you can write it on your skill sheet

While you have 1-2 years of experience, prioritize experience of seeing projects through to delivery over being given a role. Taking on roles such as design or requirements definition from your third year onward makes it easier to raise your next rate.

The timing to go independent is when the following three conditions are met.

  • You have 3 or more years of practical experience (6-2)
  • You have savings of at least 6 months of living expenses
  • You have the prospect of a project you can start working on from day one of independence

Consider an example of savings. If your monthly living expenses are 220,000 yen, 6 months' worth is 1,320,000 yen. Since there is a gap before revenue comes in during your first year of independence, quit after keeping this amount in your account. In addition, set aside just over 30% of revenue separately for taxes and social insurance premiums to be paid the following year. With monthly revenue of 750,000 yen, that is about 220,000-260,000 yen per month (30-35% as in Chapter 3).

By starting with side work, you can try how to acquire projects and the delivery process while keeping your income from the company.

  • Check whether side work is permitted under your employer's work rules
  • Through introductions from acquaintances or side-work-friendly agents, take on small projects of about 5-10 hours per week. While doing side work as a company employee, if your income (revenue minus expenses) exceeds 200,000 yen in a year, you need to file a final income tax return (even at 200,000 yen or less, a separate resident tax declaration is required)
  • Experience delivery and invoicing for 1-2 projects, and write your track record on your skill sheet
  • Once your side work income is stable, decide when to go independent (9-4)
