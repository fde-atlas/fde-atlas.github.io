What is Palantir Gotham? Explaining Its Features, How It Works, and the Practical Work of FDEs

Original title: Palantir Gothamとは？機能・仕組みとFDEの実務を解説

FDE総研 · 2026-10-14 · 14 min read

Summary

This article explains Palantir Gotham, the analysis platform Palantir has sold to defense, intelligence, and law-enforcement agencies since its 2003 founding. Government work generated about $1.57 billion of Palantir's $2.87 billion in 2024 revenue. Gotham centers on an ontology that models people, organizations, places, and events, letting analysts test hypotheses across relationships, time, and location, with fine-grained access control and audit logs treated as core features. It differs from Foundry, the commercial product, mainly in mission type and security requirements rather than technology. The piece also describes how FDEs (internally called Delta) work with Deployment Strategists (Echo) through five phases, from mission understanding to operations, and argues Gotham's design ideas apply to regulated civilian sectors like finance and healthcare.

Key points

  • Gotham is Palantir's oldest product line, serving defense and intelligence agencies since 2003.
  • Government revenue was about $1.57B of $2.87B total in Palantir's 2024 fiscal year.
  • The ontology models real-world objects, analyzed across relationships, time, and location.
  • FDEs follow five phases: mission understanding, data connection, ontology design, workflow implementation, and operations.
  • Access-control design must be decided alongside the data model, not added later.

What is Palantir Gotham?

Palantir Gotham is Palantir's analytics platform for government, defense, and intelligence agencies that integrates scattered data and supports human decision-making.

Palantir Technologies was founded in 2003. Palantir Gotham is Palantir's oldest product line, provided to US intelligence and defense agencies since the company's early days. It was once internally called "Palantir Government." Until the commercial-focused Palantir Foundry appeared, Palantir Gotham was essentially the entirety of Palantir's business.

Who is Palantir Gotham for?

The primary users of Palantir Gotham are government agencies with missions in defense, intelligence, law enforcement, and public safety.

On Palantir's official website and in its annual reports (Form 10-K), Palantir Gotham is described as a platform for defense and intelligence customers to "achieve their mission objectives." Typical users include the following.

User segmentExamples of main usesRequired capabilities
Defense and militaryOperational planning, situational awareness, logistics trackingOperation on classified networks, responsiveness
Intelligence agenciesIntegration of multi-source information, relationship analysisStrict access control, audit logs
Law enforcement and public safetyInvestigation support, linking of incidentsEvidence management, legal accountability
Disaster response and public healthVisualization of damage, supplies, and personnelData sharing across different organizations

Let's also check the business scale. According to Palantir's FY2024 Form 10-K, of total revenue of approximately $2.87 billion, the government segment accounted for approximately $1.57 billion, more than half. These figures are as of FY2024 and are not Palantir Gotham's revenue alone. Still, it is clear that the government business that started with Palantir Gotham remains a pillar of Palantir's revenue.

Why is Palantir Gotham called an "analytics platform"?

Palantir Gotham is not a box for storing data, but a working environment for decision-making that supports the process of people forming and testing hypotheses.

The core of databases and BI tools is presenting aggregated numbers. In contrast, Palantir Gotham connects real-world entities such as people, organizations, places, and events as "objects," designed to be examined along three axes: relationships, time, and location. Palantir positions it as an "Operating System for Global Decision Making," supporting the very work of analysts tracing whether "this person is connected to that event."

PerspectiveTypical DB/BI toolsPalantir Gotham
Central rolePresenting aggregated numbersSupporting the work of forming and testing hypotheses
Unit of dataRows and columns of tablesObjects of people, organizations, places, and events
Axes of analysisAggregation of metricsThree axes: relationships, time, and location

From an FDE's perspective, the value of Palantir Gotham is not determined by the number of features. It is determined by whether field analysts can use it in the order they naturally think. This is likely why, during implementation, more time is spent on business interviews than on screen demos.

What is the difference between Palantir Gotham and Foundry?

Palantir Gotham is for government and defense analysis and operations, while Palantir Foundry is for enterprise-wide data integration and business operations.

Palantir Foundry is a product that Palantir fully launched into the commercial market in the mid-2010s, used by companies in manufacturing, finance, healthcare, and other industries. Both products center on a model of the real world called an "ontology," but their intended users and operating environments differ significantly. A detailed comparison by use case and customer is covered in Differences between Palantir Gotham and Foundry.

How do the target customers and use cases differ?

Palantir Gotham is for analysts and commanders working in classified environments. Palantir Foundry is used broadly, from field staff to executives.

Comparison axisPalantir GothamPalantir Foundry
Main customersGovernment agencies such as defense, intelligence, and law enforcementPrivate companies such as manufacturing, finance, and healthcare (also used by government agencies)
Core operationsIntelligence analysis, investigations, operational planning, situational awarenessSupply chain, production management, demand forecasting, business automation
Main usersAnalysts, investigators, commandersField operators, data engineers, business departments
Operating environmentClassified networks, on-premises, edge environmentsPrimarily commercial clouds (AWS, Azure, etc.)
Key prioritiesSeparation of privileges, auditing, high reliabilityCross-organizational data integration, speed of application development

Palantir Foundry also has government use cases such as the Ministry of Defence, so the boundary between the two products is not clear-cut. Rather than by customer industry, it is closer to reality to think of the choice between them in terms of the nature of the mission and the required security level.

What are the technical commonalities and differences?

The ontology is at the core of both products. On top of that, Palantir Gotham strengthens analysis and operations screens along with strict governance mechanisms.

In recent years, Palantir has offered Gotham, Foundry, AIP (Artificial Intelligence Platform), and Apollo (a deployment management platform) in combination. Palantir Apollo is a platform for continuously distributing and updating the same software, and its distribution targets include not only the cloud but also classified networks and edge environments with limited communications. It has been explained that Palantir Apollo plays a major role in enabling Palantir Gotham customers to keep using the software in a variety of environments (for details, see What is Palantir Apollo).

ComponentRole in Palantir GothamRole in Palantir Foundry
OntologyRepresents relationships between people, organizations, places, and eventsRepresents business objects such as products, equipment, and transactions
AIPSupports analysis and planning (use of LLMs)Automates business workflows and supports decision-making
ApolloDistribution and updates to classified and edge environmentsDistribution and updates across multi-cloud

It is often said that engineers with only Foundry experience who move to Gotham projects are first bewildered by "the weight of the procedures before you can touch the data." The first hurdle is not the technology stack but getting used to the operational rules.

What features and mechanisms does Palantir Gotham run on?

Palantir Gotham operates on four layers: data integration, ontology-based modeling, analysis applications, and access control.

Much of Palantir Gotham's internal structure is not publicly disclosed. The following is organized based on what is described in Palantir's official documentation and public materials.

LayerRoleRepresentative elements
Data integrationIngests and prepares data in various formatsIngestion, normalization and entity resolution, provenance attribution
OntologyModels real-world objectsObjects, properties, links
Analysis applicationsExamines along three axes: relationships, location, and timeGraph, Map/Gaia, Timeline, Dossier
Access controlControls viewing scope and records operationsMarkings, row- and field-level controls, audit logs

How is data integrated and modeled?

Palantir Gotham ingests data in different formats and integrates it by converting it into objects, links, and properties on the ontology.

The data handled by Palantir Gotham is wide-ranging: structured data (databases, tabular records), unstructured data (reports, communications records, images), and sensor and location data. Integration generally proceeds through the following steps.

  • Ingestion: Connects to existing systems, files, and streams to acquire data
  • Normalization and entity resolution: Cleans up variations in notation and duplicates, consolidating the same person or organization into a single object
  • Mapping to the ontology: Defines them as objects (people, places, vehicles, events), properties (attributes), and links (relationships)
  • Provenance attribution: Records where each piece of information came from so it can be verified later
  • Publication to analysis applications: Makes the data viewable from the various screens analysts use, according to permissions

In the government domain, analysis results whose sources cannot be explained cannot serve as a basis for decisions. This is why provenance management in step 4 is emphasized.

What screens do analysts use, and what do they do with them?

Analysts move between graph, map, and timeline screens, testing hypotheses along the three axes of relationships, location, and time.

Analysis axisRepresentative featuresQuestions analysts want to answer
Relationships (Graph)Link analysis, network visualizationWho is connected to whom, and through what paths
Location (Map/Gaia)Geospatial analysis, overlaying location dataWhat is happening where, and what is nearby
Time (Timeline)Chronological sorting, sequencing of eventsWhen, and in what order, things happened
Sharing (Dossier, etc.)Documenting analysis results, collaborative editingHow to hand off findings to whom

Gaia is a geospatial application introduced in Palantir's public materials, often discussed in the context of operations and situational awareness. In recent Palantir demos, uses such as querying in natural language and generating candidate courses of action in combination with Palantir AIP have also been shown. However, it has been repeatedly emphasized that the design keeps humans in the loop for final decisions (human-in-the-loop).

How are security and access control designed?

Palantir Gotham treats fine-grained, data-level access control and audit logs of all operations as being just as important as its analysis features.

In government and defense settings, showing only the right people the right scope of information is an absolute requirement. Palantir Gotham attaches markings (classification and handling designations) to data to control viewing at the row and field level, and records who viewed what and when. Palantir positions this as technology for protecting privacy and civil liberties (Privacy and Civil Liberties Engineering), and has maintained a dedicated in-house team to share information on it.

If you work on it as an FDE, it is safest to treat permission design not as a setting to add later but as a requirement decided alongside the data model. Postponing permission design can force a rebuild of the ontology.

What does an FDE do in a Palantir Gotham implementation?

The FDE embeds in the customer's operations and is responsible end to end, from understanding mission requirements through data integration to establishing ongoing operations.

At Palantir, engineers who are stationed on-site and work closely with customers to implement products have been called Forward Deployed Engineers (FDE; historically referred to internally as "Delta"). The FDE role originated from Gotham projects. The typical setup is for FDEs to work alongside Deployment Strategists (internally called "Echo"), who primarily handle relationship-building with customers and problem definition. An overview of the role is summarized in What is an FDE.

What are the specific duties of an FDE on a Gotham project?

An FDE's work on a Gotham project consists of five stages: mission understanding, data connection, ontology design, workflow implementation, and operational improvement.

PhaseMain FDE tasksImage of deliverables
1. Mission understandingInterviews with analysts and commanders, observation of current operationsDefinition of priority issues and target workflows
2. Data connectionIntegration with existing systems, data quality verificationIngestion pipelines
3. Ontology designDesign of objects, links, and permission markingsA data model aligned with the field's vocabulary
4. Workflow implementationBuilding analysis screens, dashboards, and alertsOperational apps the field uses every day
5. Operations and improvementObserving usage, incorporating requests, feedback to the product teamImprovement cycles and contributions back into product features

Palantir is said to be distinctive in its mechanism for feeding field insights gained in phase 5 back into product development. An FDE is not a contracted implementer but a role that connects the customer with the product. On Gotham projects, the speed of shuttling between the field and the product tends to determine mission success or failure, and FDEs accordingly tend to have considerable discretion.

What skills and constraints are required of an FDE on a Gotham project?

In addition to technical skills, an FDE on a Gotham project needs the operational resilience to work under the constraints of classified environments and an understanding of the defense and law enforcement domains.

The differences compared with Foundry projects and the general FDE role are as follows.

PerspectiveWhat is especially required on Gotham projectsBackground
Security clearanceIn some countries, obtaining the credential is a prerequisite for assignmentBecause access to classified data is required
Work environmentDevelopment in environments isolated from external networksBecause systems run on classified networks or edge environments
Domain knowledgeUnderstanding of defense, intelligence, and law enforcement terminology and proceduresBecause models must be built in the field's vocabulary
CommunicationAdapting to the decision-making structures of militaries and government agenciesBecause approval processes are multi-layered
Ethics and accountabilityBeing able to explain the legitimacy of data useBecause the impact on civil liberties and privacy is significant

Some US Palantir job postings list holding or being eligible for a security clearance (such as US citizenship) as a condition for government project positions. If you are considering applying, check the latest conditions in each job posting.

What can Japanese engineers learn from Palantir Gotham?

Opportunities for Japanese engineers to work directly on Gotham projects are limited. Even so, the design approach of connecting data to decisions while preserving permissions can be applied elsewhere.

The concept of "connecting highly sensitive data to field decision-making while preserving permissions" can be applied directly to FDE practice in heavily regulated private sectors such as finance, healthcare, and infrastructure.

Palantir Gotham design principlesApplications in private-sector FDE practice
Deciding permission design at the same time as the data modelSectors like finance and healthcare where controlling access scope is mandatory
Recording provenanceOperations with a duty to explain the basis of decisions
Building ontologies in the field's vocabularyDesigning apps that business departments use every day

If you are considering an FDE career, it is more useful to read Palantir Gotham not as "a product from the distant world of defense" but as the archetype of the FDE way of working. Enter the customer's field, understand the mission, and change decisions through data and software. This approach of Palantir's FDEs has been honed in Gotham deployments.

Who is suited—and not suited—to being an FDE on Palantir Gotham projects?

An FDE on a Gotham project suits people who want to change field decisions even in heavily procedural classified environments, and does not suit those who prioritize development speed above all.

Should you choose Gotham-style or private-sector FDE practice?

If you want to perform well in environments that emphasize accountability and control, Gotham-style work suits you; if you want to change operations quickly and broadly, private-sector FDE practice is a better fit.

FDE Soken comparison axisGotham-style (government/defense)Private-sector (Foundry projects, etc.)
Procedures before touching dataHeavy. Multiple layers of classification and approval processesRelatively light. Focus on cross-organizational integration
Timing of permission designAlways decided at the same time as the data modelEqually important in regulated industries, but can be more flexible otherwise
Work environmentEnvironments isolated from external networksCentered on commercial clouds (AWS, Azure, etc.)
Valued prioritiesSeparation of authority, auditing, and accountabilityScope of integration and speed of application development

The following types of people are likely to struggle in a Gotham-style FDE role.

  • People who see approvals and procedures as "waste" and prioritize development speed above all else
  • People who want to avoid explaining, in their own words, the legitimacy of data use and its impact on privacy
  • People who want to focus solely on technology rather than learning on-the-ground business terminology and procedures
