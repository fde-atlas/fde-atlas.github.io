The standard critique says embedded engineers destroy software margin. Palantir's went from 78% to 84%. Here is what that changes for a buyer.

TL;DR
  • Embedding engineers at customer sites is supposed to compress software margin into services margin. Across five years of doing exactly that, Palantir's gross margin rose from 78% to 84.1%, with net dollar retention at 150%.
  • The explanation is sequencing and accounting: a cheap value-proof phase separated from an expensive expansion phase, and engineer time that behaves like customer acquisition and R&D rather than cost of goods sold.
  • For the buyer that reframes the quote. The deployment line is priced against a 95% pilot failure rate, and there are four questions that tell you whether you are funding a product or an agency.

Six points of margin expansion across five years of putting engineers inside customer sites. The engineers were never the cost of serving a customer. They were the cost of acquiring one.

Every enterprise AI negotiation reaches the same awkward moment. The license is a number the buyer can live with. The deployment line next to it is three to seven times larger, and the vendor's explanation is some version of "our product is complex." Nobody is satisfied by that answer, and nobody should be.

I have written before about what the work itself looks like, in Forward Deployed Engineers Are the Real Discipline for B2B Revenue AI. This essay is about the invoice. What that shape of delivery costs, why the standard critique of it is wrong on the numbers, what the buyer is actually purchasing, and what the model costs you in dependence.

This essay is based on data from 200+ B2B AI pilots I have launched or rescued, plus public financial disclosure. All client cases anonymized to industry and size.

The counter-intuitive part: the margin went up

Here is the standard investor critique, and it is a reasonable one. Staffing senior engineers at customer sites is a services business. Services businesses carry low gross margin and do not scale. Any vendor doing forward deployment is therefore a consultancy wearing a software valuation, and the deployment line on your quote is the proof.

The data does not cooperate. Gross margin at the most services-heavy vendor in enterprise software expanded by six points over five years, from 78.0% in 2021 to 84.1% in March 2026, while net dollar retention reached 150% in the first quarter of 2026. Whatever embedded engineers do to a P&L, compressing it is not it.

Two things explain the number, and both matter to a buyer.

The first is sequencing. What used to be one expensive motion got split in two. A short bootcamp proves value on the customer's real data in one to five days, where a conventional pilot took one to three months. The expensive embedded work then arrives on a foundation somebody already validated, rather than trying to establish one. The cheap phase kills what was never going to fly. The expensive phase only runs where the effect is already visible. If your vendor wants six figures before anything has been proven on your data, they have skipped the phase that makes the model work.

The second is accounting. Book that engineer time as cost of goods sold and you have a low-margin services company. Book it as customer acquisition cost, which is what converting a proof into a multi-year contract is, plus R&D, which is what turning one customer's bespoke build into product capability is, and the same activity reads completely differently. Both treatments are arithmetically defensible. Most of the public argument about this model is really an argument about one line in a P&L.

What the buyer is actually paying for

Not engineer-hours. Risk removal, priced against a failure rate that is unusually well documented for an emerging category.

Source Finding
MIT, The GenAI Divide, 2025 95% of enterprise GenAI pilots produce no measurable P&L effect, against 30 to 40 billion dollars invested
Gartner 89% of AI agent pilots never reach production
Gartner, survey of 782 leaders, April 2026 Only 28% of AI use cases fully meet ROI expectations
Gartner 85% of AI project failures trace back to data quality
BCG, The Widening AI Value Gap, September 2025 60% of companies capture no material value despite continued investment

The detail inside the MIT finding is the one worth carrying into a vendor meeting. The failures are not model-quality failures. Generic tools work brilliantly for individuals precisely because they are flexible, and stall inside enterprises because they do not learn the organization's workflow. That gap is what an embedded team closes, and it is the same argument I made from the knowledge side in Customer Knowledge Architecture.

Four risks come off the table, and a competent vendor should be able to name which line of the quote covers which. Integration, because the data sits in closed and undocumented systems that no questionnaire will reveal. Data quality, the 85% number above. Adoption, the state where the thing is built, live, and unused. Mandate, the absence of which keeps a working system a departmental experiment forever.

Which makes "why does deployment cost more than the license" the wrong question. The right one is what it costs to land in the 95%.

The team is a pair, not a hero

The most portable lesson from how Palantir structures this is not who to hire. It is that the unit is two people, not one. The company names its teams after the NATO alphabet, and the two that matter here are Delta and Echo.

Role What it owns What breaks without it
Delta - the forward deployed engineer Pipelines, integrations, interface, deployment inside the customer environment Without it the engagement produces advice and slides, never a running system
Echo - the deployment strategist Stakeholder navigation, adoption, the internal politics of the customer organization Without it the system ships correctly and nobody uses it

Neither half works alone. The engineer needs customer context to build the right thing. The strategist needs enough technical literacy to promise something buildable. Founders standing up this motion almost always hire the Delta first and only, because that half looks like engineering while the other looks like overhead.

The outcome is predictable and I have watched it more times than any other failure in this category: a technically correct system with no users. It reports as a product problem and it is an org problem, which is the same mechanism behind why 93% of AI implementations in sales fail, and the reason the buying sequence in Sell B2B AI Middle-Out, Not Top-Down works the way it does. On the buyer side the same logic applies in reverse: if the vendor's deployment quote has no line for the adoption half, you are funding half a team.

What the vendors do not put in the deck

An honest piece has to include the bill, and the sharpest version of it comes from a competitor with an interest in saying so, which does not make it wrong.

Charlie Gottdiener, CEO of Anaplan, argues that forward deployed engineering is very good at producing a nice-looking dashboard, delivers limited durable capability, and manufactures dependency. His formulation is worth keeping in your head during a negotiation: "If I want to make a change, I've got to pay them for it." He calls it an effective sales strategy and a poor model for running software.

Manik Sharma, who leads Kinaxis and previously ran supply chain solutions at Palantir, adds an objection from inside the model rather than against it: putting 25-year-old engineers next to a customer is a problem, because they frequently lack domain expertise in the thing being solved. Technical fluency is not the same as knowing how the customer's industry breaks.

And the dependence risk has a public example at national scale. The NHS Federated Data Platform contract runs to 330 million pounds over seven years across up to 240 organizations. By April 2026, Parliament was describing dependence on a single foreign vendor for national health infrastructure as a weakness, with a break clause under discussion. That is the same exposure a private buyer takes on, rendered at a size where everyone can see it.

The practical response is not to avoid the model. It is to ask the transfer questions before signature rather than after. Which artifacts stay with us: models, configurations, documentation, trained internal people. Which changes can we make without raising a purchase order. What happens to the running system if the team leaves.

How to tell a real forward deployed team from an agency with a nicer landing page

a16z's teardown of the pattern lays out when the model is legitimate and when it collapses into a services trap. It converts cleanly into a buyer's checklist.

  • Deployments are time-boxed, with sprints to production rather than open-ended engagements.
  • There is an explicit cap on engineering headcount per unit of account revenue. Without one, the vendor's economics reward stretching the work.
  • Bespoke code is systematically refactored into reusable product configuration on a stated cadence.
  • The engineers sit inside product teams, not in a walled-off services unit with no path back into the roadmap.
  • The vendor is straightforward about its margin structure instead of dressing a services model as pure subscription.

And four questions for the meeting itself. Where does the general product end and customization for us begin. On comparable accounts, how long from signature to production. Do your mature customers require visibly less of your involvement than new ones. What exactly remains ours if we part ways.

The third question is the one that separates the two categories. In a genuine platform model, customer dependence decays as the bespoke work gets productized. In a services model dressed as software it cannot decay, because the dependence is the revenue. Vendors in the first category answer it immediately and with examples. Vendors in the second reach for the word "partnership."

One structural note for European buyers, because it changes what is negotiable. CISPE survey data puts 72% of European enterprise IT decision-makers citing data sovereignty as a factor in vendor selection, and regulatory pressure keeps pushing deployments on-premise or fully air-gapped. A SaaS motion cannot reach an air-gapped environment; an embedded engineer can. Where sovereignty binds, forward deployment stops being a go-to-market preference and becomes a structural requirement, which means the deployment line is not going to negotiate down to SaaS levels. What remains negotiable is the transfer terms, and that is where the attention should go.

Last thing. The model is not for everyone. It earns its price where the problem is mission-critical, the data is closed or fragmented, and the perimeter is non-negotiable. For standard use cases, run self-serve, measure on your own data, and buy the embedded team once you can say precisely what it is for. Buying FDE-shaped delivery for SaaS-shaped work is exactly as expensive as the reverse, and considerably more common.

Key Takeaways

01 Price the deployment against the failure rate, not against the license.

Comparing a deployment quote to a license fee is a category error. One is priced on software economics, the other on the probability of landing in the 95% of pilots that move no P&L number. Ask the vendor which risk each line removes.

02 If you are standing up this motion, hire the adoption half alongside the engineer.

Founders hire the Delta because it looks like engineering, and skip the Echo because it looks like overhead. The predictable result is a technically correct system with no users, which reads on the dashboard exactly like a product problem.

03 Ask whether the vendor's mature customers need visibly less of them each year.

In a genuine platform model, customer dependence decays as bespoke work gets productized. In consulting wearing a product logo it cannot decay, because the dependence is the revenue. That single question separates the two categories faster than any reference call.

FAQ

Why does enterprise AI deployment cost more than the software license?+

Because the license is priced on software economics and the deployment is priced on risk. MIT found that 95% of enterprise GenAI pilots produce no measurable P&L effect, and Gartner reports 89% of AI agent pilots never reach production. The deployment line buys integration into undocumented systems, data quality work, adoption, and executive mandate, which is where those projects actually die. It is also structurally expensive: senior engineers, on-site time, and one team covering one to three accounts rather than thousands of subscribers.

Does forward deployed engineering destroy software gross margin?+

The evidence says no, when the phases are sequenced correctly. Palantir's gross margin rose from 78% in 2021 to 84.1% in March 2026, with net dollar retention of 150% in Q1 2026, across the period it was best known for embedding engineers at customer sites. The mechanism was splitting a cheap value-proof phase, a bootcamp running one to five days on real customer data, from the expensive expansion phase that only starts once the effect is visible.

Is forward deployment just consulting with a software price tag?+

Sometimes it is, and there are tests that tell the difference. a16z frames them as: are deployments time-boxed to production, is there a cap on engineering headcount per unit of account revenue, is bespoke code systematically refactored into reusable product configuration, and do the engineers sit inside product teams rather than a walled-off services unit. Where those four hold, the model compounds into a product. Where they do not, you are buying an agency with a nicer landing page.

How should a buyer account for forward deployed engineering costs?+

Ask the vendor which line the work sits in on their side. Booked as cost of goods sold, it is the price of serving you and it should fall as the vendor matures. Booked as customer acquisition and R&D, it is the price of proving value and of harvesting your deployment into the product, which means part of what you fund benefits the next customer. Both treatments are defensible. The answer tells you whether your deployment is expected to make the product better or just to keep you running.

What is the vendor lock-in risk with a forward deployed team?+

A system custom-built inside your environment by a vendor's engineers can usually only be changed by those same engineers, at their rates. Anaplan's CEO Charlie Gottdiener summarizes it as: if I want to make a change, I have got to pay them for it. The public example at scale is the NHS Federated Data Platform, a 330 million pound seven-year contract where Parliament described single-vendor dependence on national health infrastructure as a weakness. Mitigate it in the contract: specify which models, configurations and documentation transfer to you, and which changes you can make unaided.

Why is data sovereignty pushing European buyers toward this model?+

Because sovereignty requirements make pure cloud delivery impossible for a growing set of buyers. CISPE survey data puts 72% of European enterprise IT decision-makers citing data sovereignty as a factor in vendor selection, and regulatory pressure pushes deployments on-premise or fully air-gapped. A SaaS motion cannot reach an air-gapped environment. An embedded engineer can. For those buyers forward deployment stops being a go-to-market preference and becomes a structural requirement, which also means the cost is not negotiable down to SaaS levels.

Does every company need a forward deployed team?+

No, and a vendor who says otherwise is selling. The model earns its price where the problem is mission-critical, the data is closed or fragmented across undocumented systems, and the environment has to stay inside the perimeter. For standard use cases, run self-serve first, measure on your own data, and bring in a deployment team once you know precisely what it is for. Buying FDE-shaped delivery for SaaS-shaped work is as expensive a mistake as the reverse.
