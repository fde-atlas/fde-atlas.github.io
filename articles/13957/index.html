OpenAI and Anthropic FDE hiring trends

Original title: OpenAI Anthropic FDE 招聘趋势

FDE Times · 2026-10-08 · 5 min read

Summary

Drawing on Epoch AI job-posting data, the article argues OpenAI and Anthropic are shifting hiring toward deployment roles—now 17% and 11% of postings respectively—because model capability is outpacing what enterprises can absorb. Both labs have placed their FDE operations into externally funded standalone companies, and OpenAI acquired Tomoro to gain roughly 150 FDEs on day one. Citing a16z, The Pragmatic Engineer, Decrypt, and Palantir's 10-K, the author identifies four drivers of FDE demand and advises developers to build resumes around concrete deployment stories rather than framework lists.

Key points

  • Deployment-focused roles rose to 17% at OpenAI and 11% at Anthropic per Epoch AI.
  • a16z argues enterprise AI products require heavier implementation than prior software.
  • Faster deployment accelerates revenue since unused tokens generate no income.
  • Palantir's 10-K describes FDEs as the front line for spotting R&D opportunities.
  • Job seekers should highlight production deployments in messy client environments.

Key Takeaways

  • The share of deployment-focused roles in hiring has risen from 5% to 11% at Anthropic and from 11% to 17% at OpenAI, suggesting customers cannot fully unlock the value of models on their own.
  • Both OpenAI and Anthropic have placed their FDE operations into separate companies that bring in outside capital; OpenAI's stated reason is to let the lab focus on building models.
  • For developers (including developers in Vietnam), experience getting systems running in messy customer environments is exactly the skill AI companies are now willing to pay for.

At Anthropic, the share of hiring devoted to driving deployment has risen from 5% to 11%; at OpenAI, that figure has gone from 11% to 17%. The two labs are still seen as competing on model quality, but Epoch AI's hiring data shows they are pouring more and more headcount into one thing: helping others actually use what they build.

Epoch's interpretation of these numbers is one the industry may not like to hear: customers struggle to fully realize the value of OpenAI's and Anthropic's products. Model progress has outpaced enterprises' ability to absorb it. The gap between the two is exactly where the Forward Deployed Engineer emerges.

If you are a developer considering a move into the FDE role, these numbers deserve more attention than any single new model release. They show that demand comes not from one company but from the structure of the market itself, and how the labs are responding is sketching out a fairly clear career path.

The deployment gap is not the customer's fault

It is easy to assume enterprises are slow because they lack talent. Joe Schmidt of a16z offers a different view: enterprise AI products require more implementation work than the previous generation of enterprise software. That is a property of the product itself, not a failure on the buyer's part. Large language models only become truly valuable once they are connected to real data, real processes, and the constraints that no one ever wrote down.

a16z's conclusion is therefore that building software that genuinely changes how an organization operates sometimes requires labor-intensive services. As for the rest, it puts it bluntly: at the best complex AI application companies, this role is often rebranded as a Forward Deployed Engineer or implementation specialist. In other words, the FDE title is largely a new label for familiar professional services work.

Decrypt describes the job with an apt metaphor: the FDE parachutes into the customer's organization and works amid a tangle of chaos. The model is borrowed from Palantir. The easiest scenario to imagine: a bank signs an API contract, and six months later still has no use case in production, because the data is scattered across three legacy systems and the compliance team has not signed off.

The person sitting next to them, untangling these knots one by one, is neither an account manager nor a researcher.

Money follows deployment speed

The Pragmatic Engineer points to a more pragmatic reason: for the labs, the faster AI solutions are deployed, the more revenue they generate. If a contract is signed but tokens are not consumed, no money is made. Every extra month a customer spends figuring things out is a month of delayed revenue, while the cost of training the models has already been paid. From this angle, the FDE is the person who shortens the distance between signing a contract and sending an invoice.

But if FDEs are so important, why have neither OpenAI nor Anthropic kept them inside the lab? According to The Pragmatic Engineer, OpenAI has moved this business into a separate legal entity backed by external private equity, and Anthropic has also set up an independent company with outside funding for its FDE consulting business. OpenAI's stated rationale is to free the lab to focus on building better models.

Regarding Anthropic, The Pragmatic Engineer only documented its structure: an independent company funded externally. Read through OpenAI's logic, the fact that both labs chose the same structure suggests they are solving the same problem: keeping research insulated from the pace of the services business.

The notable detail is speed. Decrypt reports that OpenAI acquired Tomoro, giving the new division roughly 150 FDEs from day one. If they believed they could hire gradually over a year, no one would buy an entire company for 150 people. The labs' choice to buy rather than build shows this skill is both scarce and urgently needed.

Four drivers, one conclusion

Put the four drivers side by side, and each one places a different skill demand on the FDE.

DriverWho pointed it outWhat it demands of the FDE
Customers are not fully realizing the value of modelsEpoch AI, based on hiring dataKnow the models well enough to spot what they can do that customers have not yet discovered
AI products require heavier implementation work than older softwarea16zIntegrate with messy data and processes
The faster the deployment, the earlier the revenueThe Pragmatic EngineerMeasure outcomes and prioritize the use cases that make money first
FDEs are the primary source of R&D ideasPalantir, 10-K annual reportCapture patterns from the front lines and bring them back to the product teams

The last row is the easiest to overlook. In its fiscal 2020 10-K, Palantir wrote that its FDEs are the first line for identifying R&D opportunities for the platform. In other words, this model does not just consume finished products; it feeds back into them. For the labs, the person sitting inside a bank or a factory is the earliest sensor for detecting where models fail in the real world.

What spinning out separate companies says about the job

Both labs have placed FDEs into separate legal entities, which carries two opposite implications for you. On one hand, it shows the FDE business is run as a services operation, kept separate from the rhythm of the research teams, which matches a16z's framing of "professional services with a new name." On the other hand, external capital plus 150 people in place from day one shows this is not a support function but a deliberately built business.

If your goal is a lab's deployment division, the most worthwhile investment is proving that you have gotten a system running in an environment it was not designed for. This advice comes directly from the picture Decrypt used to describe the job: parachuting into the customer's organization and living within its complexity.

Vietnamese developers working at outsourcing companies or building solutions for local businesses have an unexpected advantage here: they are already used to dirty data, manual processes, and customers who do not know what they want until they see a demo.

So your resume should pivot to a new axis. Instead of listing frameworks, tell a deployment story: what system, what obstacles you encountered on the customer's side, how you cleared them, and what measurable results followed. When reading job descriptions, watch for phrases like "customer environment," "production deployment," and "work on-site with clients" rather than lists of technologies. That is the language of people hiring FDEs.

As Epoch read from the hiring data, as long as customers still struggle to fully realize the value of models, the people standing at the intersection of labs and enterprises will be needed.

When a lab chooses to directly hire 150 engineers instead of waiting, the question in front of you is no longer whether this job actually exists, but whether you already have a deployment story you can tell.
