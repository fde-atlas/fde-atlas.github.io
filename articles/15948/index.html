What Really Separates an FDE from Outsourcing?

Original title: FDE 与外包的真正分界是什么？

FDEr.ai · 2026-07-31 · 3 min read

Summary

This article argues that FDE work is not simply premium outsourcing, despite surface similarities like on-site work at customers. It proposes three dividing lines: accountability for business outcomes rather than billable hours; a product feedback loop that turns field learnings into reusable platform capabilities; and asset reuse that improves delivery on subsequent projects. The author stresses that billing model and on-site presence are not valid criteria—contracts, acceptance evidence, production responsibility, and feedback mechanisms are. It also warns of 'FDE-washing' and offers questions and action items for enterprises evaluating AI delivery partners.

Key points

  • FDE differs from outsourcing by owning business results, not hours or deliverables.
  • A product feedback loop should convert field learnings into reusable capabilities.
  • Asset reuse must be proven by faster delivery on a second similar project.
  • Judge FDE claims via contracts, feedback mechanisms, and performance metrics.
  • Enterprises should first decide whether they need headcount or outcomes.

A frequently asked question

"Isn't an FDE just premium outsourcing?"

This question keeps coming up because, on the surface, FDEs and outsourcing do look similar: both go to the customer's site to work, and both solve the customer's problems. But if you look deeper at how the two operate and how their incentives are structured, you'll find they are completely different models.

Three core dividing lines

Dividing line one: accountability for outcomes vs. delivery of hours

DimensionOutsourcingFDE
DeliverablesCode, documentation, person-monthsBusiness outcomes or evidence of outcomes agreed by both parties
Acceptance criteriaFeatures completed, tests passingUsers actively using it, business metrics verified as agreed
IncentivesUsually billed by work packages, hours, or headcountBilling methods can vary, but accountability for outcomes must be written separately into the contract
Risk allocationAcceptance, changes, and risk responsibilities as specified in the contractClear agreements on production outcomes, boundaries, and risk allocation

Therefore, you cannot label a project based solely on "billed per person-day" or "on-site presence." What really needs to be examined is whether the contract clearly specifies business outcomes, acceptance evidence, production responsibility, scope of changes, and how risks are accepted. Billing methods may vary, but an FDE claim without outcome boundaries is hard to verify.

Dividing line two: product feedback loop vs. one-off delivery

A core mechanism of the FDE model that is often overlooked is the product feedback loop.

In some publicly documented FDE practices, requirements discovered at the customer site, pitfalls encountered, and tools developed are systematically fed back to the product or core engineering teams, in an attempt to turn them into general capabilities. For example:

  • A data format conversion a customer needs → becomes a general-purpose Adapter on the platform
  • A security compliance requirement in a particular industry → becomes a built-in compliance module in the product
  • An AI agent workflow pattern from a customer → becomes a reusable template

If the contract only requires delivering code to the customer, without mechanisms for product feedback, retrospectives, and asset ownership, this loop is unlikely to happen naturally. Whether a loop exists depends on actual processes and evidence, and cannot be inferred from a company's name.

Dividing line three: asset reuse vs. starting from scratch

Only when there are product feedback and asset governance mechanisms do FDE teams have the opportunity to reduce repeated exploration across similar projects. What can be reused, how much, and at what adaptation cost all need to be verified through change records, test results, and delivery times in a second scenario; fixed percentages cannot be promised in advance.

The gray zone: not everyone who calls themselves an FDE is one

It must be honestly acknowledged: there is indeed a trend of "FDE outsourcing" in the market. Some companies fly the FDE flag while doing nothing more than staff augmentation. To judge whether an FDE team is genuinely doing FDE work, you can ask these questions:

  • How is the delivery contract written?—Billed per person-day, or accepted based on outcomes?
  • Is there a product feedback mechanism?—Does what the FDE learns at the customer site become organizational capability?
  • Is there evidence of improved delivery efficiency for a second similar customer?—If everything starts from scratch every time, ask about asset, testing, and feedback mechanisms rather than jumping to conclusions.
  • How are FDEs evaluated?—By lines of code, or by customer business metrics?

FDEr.ai's take

The difference between FDE and outsourcing should not be judged by "whether someone is on-site" or by a single billing method. Being on-site is just a way of working; billing is just a commercial arrangement. The more reliable dividing line is: whether both parties jointly define evidence of outcomes, whether someone is accountable for production adoption, and whether on-site experience is consolidated into organizational assets after proper authorization, testing, and version management.

For enterprises evaluating how to deploy AI, the key is not choosing "FDE or outsourcing," but making sure your partner has the right incentive structure:

  • If you only need extra hands to complete well-defined tasks, outsourcing may be more suitable
  • If you need to explore uncertain AI scenarios and deliver business outcomes, the FDE model is a better fit
  • If the partner can also provide product capabilities (tools, templates, platforms), that is the full form of the FDE model

Action recommendations

  • When choosing an AI deployment partner, first clarify whether you need "manpower" or "outcomes"
  • Ask the partner to explain their acceptance methods, product feedback mechanisms, asset ownership, and evidence of reuse
  • Don't judge the model based solely on "billed per person-day" or "on-site presence"; first check whether production responsibility and business outcomes are written into the contract
