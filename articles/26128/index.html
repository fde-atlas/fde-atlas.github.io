What Is AI-Driven Development? Differences from Traditional Development, How to Proceed, and Risks Explained Through FDE Practice

Summary

This Japanese article explains AI-driven development, where AI handles entire development phases—from requirements drafting to implementation, testing, and review—while humans focus on deciding what to build and verifying outputs. It contrasts this with traditional development (where implementation effort was the bottleneck; now specification clarity and review capacity are), proposes a five-step cycle (define goals, specify, AI implements, verify, record), discusses prompt and context design, and applies the practice to FDE work: faster on-site prototyping and translating domain knowledge into specifications. It closes with four risk areas—quality, security, confidential data, and knowledge silos—and concrete countermeasures.

  • Key risks: plausible bugs, security flaws, confidential data leakage, and person-dependent practices.

What is AI-driven development?

AI-driven development is a development approach that incorporates AI into each stage of development, letting humans focus on judgment and verification. It covers the entire process from requirements gathering to implementation, testing, and review. It goes one step beyond partially using AI for code completion, restructuring the whole development process so that "AI does the work, and humans set the direction and verify it."

Treating AI as a "process owner," not just a "tool"

In AI-driven development, the difference from the past is that you don't just have AI assist—you hand over entire parts of the process to it.

Traditional AI use centered on "partial assistance," such as code completion in an editor or looking up the meaning of error messages. In AI-driven development, you delegate work to AI in units like the following:

  • Creating a draft spec document from requirements notes
  • Implementing changes across multiple files based on a spec
  • Writing and running test code, and fixing failures itself
  • Reviewing diffs and summarizing issues

The human's job shifts to deciding "what to build" and verifying whether the AI's output is correct. The amount of hands-on work decreases, but the weight of judgment increases.

The reason for the attention: coding agents becoming practical

The reason for the attention is that coding agents that can read a repository and autonomously carry out implementation and testing have become practical.

Tools like GitHub Copilot, Cursor, and Claude Code have evolved from completion features into agent-type tools that can handle terminal operations, editing multiple files, and running tests. As a result, the usage of "having AI write one function" has given way to the now-realistic usage of "having AI build one feature, with humans reviewing it."

From on-the-ground experience, results tend to differ less by tool performance itself and more by whether a team can restructure its workflow around AI.

How does AI-driven development differ from traditional development?

The biggest difference is that how people spend their time shifts from "writing" to "deciding and verifying." The order of the process itself doesn't change much. What changes is who does the hands-on work at each stage and where the bottleneck lies.

Differences in role division and bottlenecks

In traditional development, implementation effort was the bottleneck; in AI-driven development, spec clarity and review capacity become the constraints.

PerspectiveTraditional developmentAI-driven development
Main workerEngineers write the codeAI writes it; engineers direct and verify
BottleneckImplementation effortSpec clarity, review processing capacity
Role of spec documentsAgreements between humansDouble as input (context) for AI
Prototyping frequencyCostly and limited in numberCheap rebuilds, done repeatedly
Quality assuranceGuaranteed by the author and reviewersGuaranteed by combining test automation and human review
Required skillsImplementation ability, language/framework knowledgeDesign ability, articulation, verification, domain understanding

As the table shows, what gains value in AI-driven development is "the ability to turn vague requests into specs at a granularity the AI won't misinterpret."

Differences in deliverables and quality assurance

In AI-driven development, code is produced quickly and in large volumes, so quality is ensured through testing and review mechanisms.

With human-written code, the author can explain the intent. AI-written code tends to end up in a state of "it works, but no one can explain why it was written that way." For this reason, an approach that includes the following three items in deliverables works well:

  • Specs (why and what): Keep the instructions and design notes given to the AI in the repository
  • Tests (how it was verified): Prepare acceptance criteria as test code in advance
  • Review records (who approved it): Make the final responsible party clear

How do you carry out AI-driven development?

The basics are to break work into small specs, have the AI implement them, and run short cycles of verification through tests and review. Rather than delegating a large feature all at once, splitting it into easily verifiable units and iterating keeps the cost of failure down.

The basic 5 steps

Running the 5 steps of "define purpose → spec it out → AI implementation → verify → record" lets you scale to a team without depending on individuals.

StepWhat to doKey point
1. Define the purpose and completion criteriaWrite whose problem you are solving and what counts as doneState it definitively in 1-2 sentences
2. Prepare specs and contextGather related files, existing design policies, and constraintsAlso explicitly note libraries that must not be used, etc.
3. Have the AI implementPass the spec and context and request implementationKeep each request to a diff size you can review
4. Verify with tests and reviewRun automated tests and have a human confirm it matches the intentDon't stop at just passing the tests
5. Record learningsKeep track of points the AI misunderstoodReflect them in rule files or spec templates

Step 5, "recording," tends to be skipped. However, practical experience shows that whether or not you keep recording makes a real difference in overall team productivity.

Designing instructions and context determines the results

The quality of AI output depends not only on model performance but also heavily on the quality of the instructions and context you provide.

The differences between good and bad instructions can be summarized as follows.

ElementBad exampleGood example
Purpose"Build a login feature""Use the existing member DB to enable login with email + password"
ConstraintsNone"Use the existing authentication library. Do not add new dependencies"
Completion criteriaNone"Tests for the three cases—login success, failure, and lockout—must pass"
Reference materialNone"Refer to the existing implementation under auth/ and the design notes"

With many tools, you can have the AI constantly load a file containing project-wide rules (coding conventions, prohibited practices, etc.). Sharing this file across the team reduces variation in instructions from person to person.

How does AI-driven development prove valuable in FDE work?

For FDEs, AI-driven development is a weapon that speeds up the ability to show something that actually works on the spot, based on what they hear at the customer site. FDE (Forward Deployed Engineer) is a role established by Palantir, in which engineers embed themselves at customer sites and handle everything from problem discovery through implementation and adoption. The speed of the hypothesis-and-verification cycle in the field is the source of their value, making AI-driven development an excellent fit.

Rapid prototyping at customer sites

With AI-driven development, FDEs can lower the cost of prototyping and advance conversations with customers using "working software" rather than "documents."

Traditionally, the typical flow was to take the interview content back, design the solution, and show a first demo weeks later. With AI-driven development, you can get a rough version running the day after the interview—or sometimes on the spot.

  • You can confirm with the real thing: "Are these the fields to enter on this screen?"
  • You can surface the customer's "this isn't what I imagined" early on
  • Since the cost of rebuilding is low, you can compare multiple hypotheses side by side

However, be careful about situations where a prototype ends up being reused as-is in production. The FDE should draw a clear line between prototyping and production implementation.

The ability to translate domain knowledge into specifications becomes even more important

The more AI takes on implementation, the relatively heavier the FDE's role becomes in understanding the customer's business and translating it into correct specifications.

AI is good at writing generic code, but it doesn't know site-specific circumstances like "exception processing concentrates at month-end here" or "this approval flow cannot be skipped under company policy." Drawing out this tacit knowledge and translating it into specifications the AI can work with is the FDE's job.

For engineers aiming to become FDEs, the differentiating factors going forward can be organized as follows.

Implementation skill in a specific languageRelatively lowerBut a review-capable level is essential
Articulating requirements and formalizing specificationsHigherDetermines the quality of input to the AI
Understanding customer business and domainsHigherA source of information the AI doesn't have

What this means for companies considering adopting FDEs

By having FDEs skilled in AI-driven development, even a small team can consider a structure that runs everything from customer touchpoints to implementation end to end.

Traditionally, the assumption tended to be a structure with separate roles—interviewer, designer, implementer—with headcount to match. In a situation where most implementation can be delegated to AI, a model in which FDEs with domain understanding and verification skills handle both customer dialogue and development as one becomes a realistic option. Even then, it is a given that specifications, tests, and review records are kept as deliverables.

What are the risks and countermeasures to watch out for with AI-driven development?

The main risks are four: quality, security, confidential information, and over-reliance on individuals—all of which can be addressed with mechanisms. The common prerequisite is not to blindly trust AI output and to clarify the scope of human responsibility.

Quality and security risks

Since AI-generated code can contain plausible bugs and vulnerabilities, both automated testing and human review are necessary.

RiskConcrete exampleCountermeasure
Plausible errorsMissed boundary-value handling, calls to nonexistent APIsWrite acceptance tests first and always run them in CI
Introduction of vulnerabilitiesInsufficient input validation, flawed authentication handlingAutomate static analysis and dependency scanning, and have humans focus their review on authentication
Adding unnecessary dependenciesIntroducing unknown libraries on its ownProhibit new dependencies in the rules file and detect them via diffs
Breakdown of design consistencyCoding style varies from file to fileAlways provide design policies as context

In the field, people tend to conclude "the tests passed, so it's fine." However, if the AI also wrote the tests, a human must verify that the tests correctly represent the specification.

Confidential information and governance risks

In FDE work that handles customer data, the scope of information that may be passed to AI tools should be agreed upon with the customer in advance.

FDEs are in a position to directly access customer business data and systems. Getting the scope of information passed to AI tools wrong can lead to contract violations or information leaks. At minimum, confirm the following four points before starting work.

  • The AI tools to be used and the conditions for handling input data (whether used for training, retention period, etc.)
  • The classification of information that may and may not be passed to the AI
  • Ownership of generated output and the final person responsible for quality
  • How usage logs are stored and whether audits are possible

Risk of over-reliance on individuals

Don't keep AI usage know-how locked inside individual heads; keep instruction templates and rules files as team assets.

In a state where "the AI usage know-how exists only in a particular engineer's head," the quality of development drops the moment the person in charge changes. If you record and share instruction templates, rules files, and approaches that worked, development can continue at the same level even after personnel changes. Making the "record" step of the basic five steps a habit is itself a countermeasure against over-reliance on individuals.

AI-driven development is a method for reducing hands-on time and increasing time for thinking and engaging with customers. For FDEs, it is likely to become a core skill that accelerates delivering value in the field.
