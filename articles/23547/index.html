What Is a Forward Deployed Lawyer: Comparing Eudia's FDE and FDL Roles

Original title: 前线部署律师是什么：Eudia FDE 与 FDL 岗位对比

FDE Times · 2026-11-08 · 5 min read

Summary

This article examines how legal AI startup Eudia runs two distinct forward-deployed roles: FDEs under Engineering and Forward Deployed Lawyers (FDLs) under Product, the latter requiring at least four years of legal practice, with government roles based at DoW client sites and preferring candidates with security clearances. Because Eudia Counsel operates as a fixed-fee, AI-native law firm under Arizona's ABS program, some deliverables legally require a licensed attorney's sign-off. The author argues engineers cannot alone judge which errors matter, and the best FDEs convert expert judgments into evals and test cases rather than debating pass criteria.

Key points

  • Eudia places FDEs under Engineering and FDLs under Product, signaling FDLs supply product insight, not legal opinions.
  • FDL roles require 4+ years of independent practice; government postings prefer security-cleared candidates.
  • A 95% extraction accuracy is worthless if errors cluster in deal-breaking clauses like liability limits.
  • Strong FDEs turn each expert rejection into a labeled test case with documented reasoning.
  • Job seekers should ask who decides whether a result passes before joining an FDE team.

Eudia's careers page has two roles that both carry the "Forward Deployed" label, yet sit under different departments. The Forward Deployed Engineer (FDE) is listed under Engineering. The Forward Deployed Lawyer (FDL) is listed under Product, requires at least four years of practice experience, and must be able to handle matters independently without supervision.

Eudia is a legal AI startup founded in 2023. A job posting for its government business, published on June 30, 2026, requires the FDL to be embedded on-site at a client organization under the DoW. Eudia explicitly states that this is not a staff attorney position.

Engineers who are doing or want to do FDE work should pay attention to this detail. A company hiring both engineers and lawyers for customer-facing work means engineers are no longer the only ones bringing frontline knowledge back.

Engineers ensure the system runs; domain experts decide what counts as "correct." Whether you understand how these two roles complement each other determines whether you are seen as someone who solves problems or someone who merely wires up APIs.

Why do AI companies need real lawyers?

The answer lies in the business model. Eudia calls its platform "Augmented Intelligence": combining AI with human expertise to help legal practitioners work faster and better. The company has also acquired alternative legal service providers (ALSPs) such as Johnson Hana and Out-House.

Eudia went a step further and launched Eudia Counsel. According to CEO Omar Haroun, it is the first "AI-native" law firm, operating under Arizona's ABS program.

The firm handles due diligence for M&A transactions as well as contract work that must legally be handled by licensed attorneys. In other words, some deliverables cannot be signed off by software no matter how accurate it is.

Eudia Counsel also uses fixed fees rather than hourly billing. The price is set in advance, and every hour of attorney time that AI saves becomes Eudia's own profit.

As a result, the company has very concrete reasons to make its tools fit how lawyers actually work, and the people who understand that way of working best are not engineers.

Placing the FDL under Product rather than Legal makes it quite clear what Eudia wants from this lawyer: product requirements, not legal opinions for the company. The government role also requires the FDL to stay closely connected with the growth, product, and engineering teams, feeding frontline observations back into the platform.

The same batch of contracts, two ways of scoring

Imagine a hypothetical scenario. You are an FDE deploying a contract review AI agent for the legal team of an F500 company. The test set contains 200 contracts, and the AI agent correctly extracts 95% of the clauses. On the dashboard, that number looks great.

The lawyer sitting next to you will ask a different question: where is the wrong 5%? Suppose the errors are concentrated in limitation-of-liability clauses or change-of-control clauses, the kind of clauses that can make or break an M&A deal.

Then reviewers would have to reread everything, and 95% accuracy saves almost no attorney hours. Conversely, if the errors only appear in contact information, the customer might accept it on the spot.

This is exactly where each side has what the other lacks. Engineers cannot judge the severity of each type of legal error on their own, and lawyers cannot build eval sets, data pipelines, or logging systems on their own. The best evals come from lawyers assigning a risk level to each type of error, and engineers converting those levels into metrics that run on every deployment.

So the first thing to do after arriving at a customer site, before optimizing any metric, is to sit down with the domain experts for a conversation. Work with them to sort errors into two groups: "acceptable" and "invalidates the entire deliverable." That list is more valuable than any overall accuracy number.

When credentials and access matter more than skills

The government role adds one more criterion: preference for candidates who already hold a security clearance. This is not a hard requirement, but taken together with the four years of practice experience, it shows that in this market, credentials and access are far more than ordinary nice-to-haves.

Someone who has never handled a matter independently, or who lacks a security clearance, would likely find it hard to earn the trust needed to be embedded on-site at a defense customer.

You don't have to become a lawyer, but you need to understand why the person next to you has the authority to say "no." When a lawyer says a result "can't be used," that is usually a professional risk assessment, not a personal preference.

In this model, a good FDE turns that judgment into test cases rather than arguing about it. Every time an expert rejects a result, you add a case to the eval set along with the reason they gave. After a few weeks, the eval set has accumulated their domain expertise, and no one needs to re-explain the criteria in every meeting.

What does this mean for your career?

Eudia's logic is not limited to the legal industry. Any industry where outputs require licensed professionals to take responsibility may need engineers and domain experts working side by side in the same way. For those looking for FDE jobs, this is one of the criteria for evaluating a job description.

When reading a job posting, check whether the company has domain expert roles that collaborate with the FDE, and who each role reports to. Ask directly in the interview: who decides whether a result passes? The answer will tell you whether your time will go into building systems or into arguing about what "pass" means.

On a resume, "built a contract extraction data pipeline" says very little. Be specific about which expert you worked with to define the pass criteria, and which metric reflected the customer's real risk. If you don't yet have such a project, you can practice on your own: find a set of publicly available contract samples, write your own scoring criteria, and then ask an industry professional to help you refine them.

Eudia puts engineers in Engineering and lawyers in Product, yet expects both to bring frontline insights back into the product. In an industry where a single clause error can change an entire deal, the FDE who knows how to collaborate with the arbiters of "correctness" is the one customers want to keep around.
