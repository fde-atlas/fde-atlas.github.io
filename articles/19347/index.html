Cross-Analysis of AX Implementation Case Studies | Reduction Results by Business Type

Original title: AX導入事例の横断分析｜業務類型別の削減実績

FDX株式会社 · 2026-08-31 · 11 min read

Summary

FDX株式会社 cross-analyzed over 20 of its AX (AI transformation) cases by business type rather than industry, grouping them into four categories: generation, transcription, search/verification, and codifying expert judgment. It reports reduction rates for each (all self-estimated, client names undisclosed) and derives principles for implementation order and success conditions.

Key points

  • Transcription shows ~90% reduction; generation ~70–95%, the highest levels.
  • Search/verification averages ~70% because human source-checking remains a floor.
  • Codifying judgment yields ~80% cuts and biggest business impact, but requires recorded past decisions.
  • Successful cases preserved existing formats, approval flows, and tools.
  • Recommended order: transcription and generation first, then search, then judgment codification.

Even looking at AI adoption case studies individually, it is hard to see what could be useful for your own company. That is because if the industry differs, the work differs too. However, when you group work by its "type," patterns common across industries emerge.

In this article, we perform a cross-industry analysis of more than 20 AX (AI Transformation) cases implemented by FDX Inc., organized into 4 work categories. From actual results, we have organized the typical reduction rates for each category and the principles for deciding the order of implementation. Details of individual cases are listed in "Case Study List".

Note that all reduction rates in this article are our own estimates, and client names are kept confidential under non-disclosure obligations. Company sizes are described in bucketed form to avoid re-identification.

Who This Article Is For

  • Those in charge of AI initiatives looking for decision-making material on which of their company's work processes to start automating with AI
  • Those who need to attach "what kind of results other companies are achieving" to internal approval requests
  • Those who have looked at industry-specific case studies but feel unsure how to apply them to their own company

Work Falls into 4 Categories

When cases are classified not by industry but by the nature of the work, they converge into the following 4 categories.

CategoryNature of the WorkTypical Reduction RateImplementation Difficulty
1. GenerationCreating documents and content in fixed formatsAbout 70-95%Low to medium
2. TranscriptionMoving information from one place to anotherAbout 90%Low to medium
3. Search and verificationFinding needed information from accumulated materialsAbout 70%Medium
4. Codifying judgmentMaking expert judgment criteria explicit and reproducibleAbout 80%Medium to high

The highest reduction rates are in "transcription" and "generation." On the other hand, the greatest business impact often comes from "codifying judgment." The order of implementation should be decided based on these two axes.

What determines success in this category is whether existing formats can be maintained. In the ICT system equipment case, there was a constraint to strictly adhere to existing pptx/excel formats, but a system was built to fully automate generation without changing the format. Proposals that involve changing formats increase operational burden on the field and fail to take hold.

Category 2: Transcription

This is work that moves information from one place to another. It has the least inherent need for human involvement and the highest reduction rate.

Industry (Size)Target WorkResults
Digital talent development / consulting / recruitment (several dozen employees)Entering sales meeting records into CRMAbout 90% reduction

In the CRM entry case, meeting notes tools were linked with the CRM so that sales meeting content is automatically reflected in records. In this category, the secondary effect of "eliminating missed entries" is greater than the reduction effect itself. Sales data accuracy improves, and the foundation for analysis becomes consistent.

Category 3: Search and Verification

This is work that finds needed information from accumulated materials. Implementation centers on RAG (retrieval-augmented generation).

Industry (Size)Target WorkResults
Building materials distribution, major trading company group (annual revenue in the hundreds of billions of yen, several hundred employees)Searching literature and documentsAbout 70% reduction
Digital marketing / system developmentDocument creation and code analysisAbout 70% reduction
Housing construction (several dozen employees)Work around drawings and schedulesAbout 60-70% reduction on average across the domain

The reduction rate in this category is lower than generation and transcription because a step remains where humans verify the search results. In work where checking sources cannot be skipped, verification effort remains as a floor. Most generative AI incidents also arise from the omission of this verification step; details are covered in "Generative AI Incident Cases 2025-2026".

Category 4: Codifying Judgment

This is work that makes explicit the judgments experts make based on experience and makes them reproducible. Implementation difficulty is high, but business impact is the greatest.

Industry (Size)Target WorkResults
Water treatment machinery design, manufacturing, and construction / construction industry (several dozen employees)Calculating bid quotation amountsAbout 80% reduction
Specialty metals and rare metals trading company (multiple domestic and overseas locations)Purchase appraisalAbout 80% reduction

Beauty and cosmetics (several dozen employees)Evaluating customer service qualityQuantified evaluation criteria across 106 items

Quotations, appraisals, and screening have all been considered work that "only experienced people can do." The condition for this category to work is that past judgment results are retained as data. If they are not, you must first build a recording system, which extends the lead time.

Even within the same industry, the suitable category differs by department. Conversely, even across different industries, if the category is the same, the implementation approach is common.

Success Conditions Common to the Case Studies

The successful cases share the following characteristics.

  • They did not break existing operations: The design allows formats, approval flows, and tools to remain unchanged. The more change is required, the lower the adoption rate
  • They started with tasks whose effects can be measured numerically: Choose tasks first where the number of items and processing time can be recorded. If effects are measurable, the next investment decision can be made
  • On-site staff are involved in operations: There are people on-site who can make adjustments after implementation. If everything is left to external parties, operations stop when conditions change
  • Target tasks were identified in advance: They started from tasks, not from tools or platforms

In the case that achieved results company-wide, all six departments met their targets, with five reaching an S rating. The key factor was setting measurable metrics for each department, such as a monthly reduction of 720 minutes in routine HR tasks and 76 hours per month in creative submissions.

Principles for Order of Implementation

The order derived from the case studies is as follows.

  • Start with Type 2 (Transcription) and Type 1 (Generation): These have high reduction rates and low implementation difficulty, making it easy to create an initial success story
  • Introduce measurement mechanisms at the same time: Record the number of items and processing time. This becomes the basis for the next investment decision
  • Expand to Type 3 (Search and Verification): Start at the stage when the state of internal data organization becomes visible
  • Move on to Type 4 (Standardizing Judgment): Start only after confirming that past decision data is available

Following the reverse order tends to fail. Starting with standardizing judgment requires time for data preparation, initial results fail to appear, and internal support is lost. "For enterprise AI adoption of business processes, conduct a comprehensive assessment and start by automating high-ROI tasks" is the principle that can be stated from actual results.

Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ)

Q1. How are the stated reduction rates calculated?

A. All are our own estimates, calculated by comparing the number of items processed and processing time before and after implementation. Because the underlying task scope and measurement period differ by project, we do not guarantee that other companies will achieve the same results for the same tasks. When estimating for your own company, you need to calculate individually based on the nature (type) of the task and current processing time.

Q2. Can small and mid-sized companies achieve similar results?

A. Yes. The published cases include companies with only a few dozen employees, and their reduction rates are on par with companies generating hundreds of billions of yen in annual revenue. In fact, smaller organizations tend to have shorter lead times from implementation to adoption because approval flows are shorter and decision-making is faster. The difference lies in absolute reduction time, not reduction rate.

Q3. Which type offers the highest ROI?

A. In terms of reduction rate alone, Transcription (about 90%) and Generation (about 70-95%) are highest. However, since ROI is determined by "reduction rate × annual hours of the target task," tasks with a lower reduction rate but larger hours can yield greater savings. In practice, priorities are set by multiplying the reduction rate guideline for each type by the annual hours obtained from the task inventory.

Q4. How much data is needed for standardizing judgment?

A. There is no uniform standard, but the essential requirement is that past decision results have been recorded. For quotations, appraisals, and reviews, input conditions and outcomes must be accumulated in a traceable form. If no records exist, a process to build the recording mechanism must be added first, extending the lead time by several months. This assessment is made during the assessment phase.

Q5. What distinguishes these cases from those that stall without producing results?

A. The biggest difference is whether existing operations were forced to change. Systems premised on changing formats or approval flows increase the burden on staff and fall out of use. The next biggest factor is measurement: initiatives that cannot demonstrate results numerically fail to secure the next budget and end as one-off projects. For cases of choosing the wrong starting tasks, see "5 Structural Reasons AI PoCs Fail and How to Avoid Them".

Q6. What if I cannot determine which type my company's tasks fall into?

A. Breaking tasks down into "units of work that a person completes in one pass" reveals their types. A single task often mixes multiple types; for example, creating a quotation combines "searching past projects (Type 3)," "judging amounts (Type 4)," and "generating the quotation (Type 1)." After breaking tasks down, measuring which steps consume the most time determines the starting point.

Summary

  • When AX case studies are categorized by process type rather than industry, they converge into four patterns: generation, transcription, search/matching, and formalizing judgment
  • The reduction rates are approximately 90% for transcription, 70-95% for generation, 70% for search/matching, and 80% for formalizing judgment (all based on our own estimates)
  • The relatively lower reduction rate for search/matching is because the verification step of checking sources remains as a lower bound
  • Formalizing judgment has the greatest business impact, but its prerequisite is that past judgment results are retained as data
  • A common trait of successful cases is that they did not change existing formats, approval flows, or tools in use. The more changes are required, the lower the adoption rate
  • The order of implementation should be "transcription/generation → search/matching → formalizing judgment." Proceeding in reverse order requires time for data preparation and delays early results
  • Return on investment is determined by "reduction rate × annual hours." Do not choose processes based solely on high reduction rates
