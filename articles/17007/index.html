Summary

This article examines why AI projects stall at PoC or get abandoned after deployment, arguing the biggest loss is a finished model that never enters daily operations. It identifies five structural causes: data that doesn't reflect actual field conditions, undocumented tacit knowledge, poor UI, missing operational workflows, and waterfall development that delivers outdated requirements. Countermeasures include rapid prototypes, on-site observation, field-driven UI fixes, co-designed workflows, and continuous short improvement cycles. It presents FDE-style development, where engineers are embedded at the customer site and fix issues immediately, as a way to combine all five practices and make AI stick in real operations.

Key points

  • Most AI failures are organizational, not technical: completed models go unused.
  • Data quality and tacit knowledge are only visible through on-site observation.
  • Poor UI and absent operational workflows block adoption regardless of model quality.
  • Waterfall delivery often produces requirements that are obsolete by launch.
  • FDE engineers embedded on-site enable immediate fixes and fast feedback loops.

Insight 02

Why It Happens and How to Avoid It

Despite active investment in AI, on the ground it is common to see projects "stuck at PoC" or "deployed but never used." This article organizes the five structural reasons why this happens and how to avoid them.

The biggest failure companies face when adopting AI is not a technical problem. Millions are invested, the model itself is complete, yet it never gets integrated into frontline operations and falls into disuse—this "unused AI" is the greatest loss.

AI differs fundamentally from traditional business systems. Conventional systems were judged by whether they "worked as specified." AI, however, depends heavily on data quality, environmental conditions, business workflows, and UI details. Countless examples show systems that worked in PoC but never made it into real operations.

AI accuracy depends on data, yet core system data often fails to accurately reflect frontline realities. Input rules differ by department, outlier handling is decided implicitly, and the "back side" of the data is visible only on the ground. Even if you are told in a meeting room that "the data exists," it may not be usable as-is.

Expert judgment criteria—such as "this appearance means defective" or "demand rises in this season"—are rarely documented. This tacit knowledge, which never appears in requirements definitions, determines AI prediction accuracy. It cannot be captured through interviews alone; it requires observation on the ground.

"Input is cumbersome," "notifications are slow," "the screen is hard to read"—these small frustrations hinder AI adoption. No matter how excellent the AI model is, if the UI is something the frontline cannot or does not want to touch, it will not be used. UI is an area that can only be improved through real-world usage testing.

Integrating AI into business requires an operational flow: "who reviews the results, when decisions are made, and which system they are reflected in." Building only the model is not enough; without a business-side flow to receive the results, nothing moves. Many projects stall at exactly this point.

When AI projects follow a waterfall approach, by the time delivery arrives six months to a year later, conditions on the ground have changed and the "requirements of that era" no longer work. AI projects must not freeze specifications; they need to be updated while running alongside the frontline.

  • Ship small and fast: instead of specification documents, show the frontline a working prototype within days to weeks
  • Observe on the ground: data quality and tacit knowledge can only be understood by watching frontline work
  • Fix the UI on-site: usability is not decided in meeting rooms; adjust it to fit frontline workflows
  • Design the operational flow together with the business side: decide who does what and when, as a set
  • Keep improving in short cycles: assume requirements will change and build a structure for continuous improvement

The FDE (Forward Deployed Engineer) development model realizes all five of these practices within a single structure. FDEs are stationed not at development sites but at the customer's actual business frontline, adjusting data, tacit knowledge, UI, operational flows, and requirement changes in real time on-site.

Rather than answering in a meeting room with "we will address that in the next meeting," an FDE produces a revised version on the spot.
