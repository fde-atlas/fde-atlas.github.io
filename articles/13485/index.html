Forward Deployed Engineer Behavioural Interview Questions

Artificial Intelligence Uni (AIU.ac) · 2026-11-09 · 6 min read

Summary

This article argues that FDE behavioural interviews reduce to three hidden questions: what did you own, what did you refuse to build, and who ran the system after you left? Drawing on Glassdoor candidate reports, First Round Review, and Perspective AI, it notes behavioural questions open even technical rounds at Palantir and Google. It offers a three-part answer format for refusal questions and stresses that hand-back stories need concrete evidence, such as handover rehearsals where client mistakes drove documentation fixes. The author recommends preparing five core stories plus one refusal, told with deployment-specific detail, as Indeed postings for the role grew roughly 729% year over year by April 2026.

Key points

  • Behavioural questions test ownership, refusal, and hand-back, not generic teamwork.
  • Palantir's former FDE recruitment lead Shilpa Balaji asks: would you want this person in the trenches?
  • Refusal answers should name the system, the declined action, and what the user saw.
  • Handover rehearsals where clients make mistakes are stronger evidence than claims of training.

A forward deployed engineer behavioural interview asks three questions in disguise: what did you own, what did you refuse to build, and who ran the system after you left? Strong answers name a real deployment, a real refusal and a real handover. Vague stories about teamwork and managing expectations rarely survive the follow-ups.

Key takeaways

  • Which three themes sit behind most behavioural questions in this role, and why interviewers return to them
  • How to answer “what did you refuse to build?” with a specific system and a specific refusal
  • How to describe a handoff so it shows the customer’s team, not you, ran the system at the end

What a forward deployed engineer behavioural interview is testing

Behavioural prompts look generic, but they carry weight. According to Perspective AI, behavioural FDE questions probe ambiguity tolerance, ownership and cross-functional collaboration through your past experience. Candidate reports on Glassdoor suggest the format is common. One Glassdoor reviewer said every technical round at Palantir began with behavioural questions. Another described a Google recruiter screen heavily weighted to the behavioural portion.

The reason is structural. According to First Round Review, the FDE embeds directly with a customer to build the “last mile” of the product so it works in production. Nobody supervises that work minute by minute. The interviewer is checking what you do when nobody is directing you.

There is also a reputational reason. For years the model was dismissed as a consulting shop in software clothing, as KDnuggets notes. Questions about refusing work and handing it over are how an interviewer separates an engineer from a body-shop hire.

Forward Deployed Engineer interview questions and what each one tests

Ownership: what did you decide, and what happened next?

Ownership questions usually arrive in plain clothes. A Glassdoor reviewer reported being asked what project they were proudest of, and to describe a time they worked on a project end to end. The role invites this. The Pragmatic Engineer quotes Palantir comparing FDE responsibilities to those of a startup CTO, owning end to end execution of high stakes projects.

A strong answer is specific about the decisions that were yours. Take an illustrative case: an engineer deploys a document triage agent for a claims team, discovers the source data has three conflicting formats, and chooses to fix intake before touching the model. The story works because it names the choice, the cost of the choice and who disagreed.

Expect follow-ups such as: what was in your control, and what was not? What did you do without being asked? Perspective AI says interviewers look for specific, structured stories and honest reflection on failure. First Round quotes Palantir’s former FDE recruitment lead, Shilpa Balaji, describing her bar:

Would you want to be in the trenches with this person?

Honest accounts of a difficult deployment answer that better than a polished success.

Refusal: what did you refuse to build?

This is the question candidates prepare least and interviewers value most. Refusal is a form of ownership: you decided the customer’s request, as stated, would not produce the outcome they wanted.

The pattern shows up at company level too. Perspective AI’s analysis of the Palantir model lists refusing the systems integrator role among seven recurring lessons. It reports that Palantir walked away from contracts that wanted a systems integrator with better software. The interview question asks for the same judgement at engineer scale.

In smaller form, the prompt appears as a customer scenario. One Glassdoor reviewer was asked what they would do if a client proposed a solution they knew was not ideal. Perspective AI’s version: a customer demands a feature you know will not solve their actual problem.

Answering with the Refusal Test

The sharpest form of the question is the Refusal Test:

What did your system refuse to build, and what does the refusal look like?

Answer in three parts. Name the system. Name the specific thing it declined to do. Then describe what the refusal looked like to the person on the receiving end. An illustrative example: a customer-support agent that declined to issue refunds above a set value and instead routed the case to a named human with the evidence attached. The refusal was visible, explained and useful.

Then say what you offered instead, and how the customer responded. A bare “no” is a weak answer. So is a refusal that turned out to be wrong: if you were overruled, or later found you had misjudged it, say so. That candour is what the “trenches” bar is looking for.

Handoff: who runs it after you leave?

Candidates tend to spend their time on the build and skip the end. Interviewers notice. A system that only its original engineer can run is a dependency, not a deployment. AIU teaches the full sequence, which gives you a map for where handoff sits:

AIU teaches the deployment lifecycle in seven stages: post-sale handoff, discovery, scoping, build, deploy, iterate and hand-back.

Two of those stages matter most for behavioural answers. The first is post-sale handoff, where the engineer inherits promises made by someone else. A good story says what you found in the sales commitments and what you did about the gap. The last is hand-back, where the customer’s own team takes ownership. Questions here sound like: who runs this on the Monday after you leave? What did the customer’s team do the first time without you?

Hand-back is also where learning flows the other way. The Pragmatic Engineer notes that when a capability is missing that a customer needs, the FDE can add features and improvements to Palantir’s product. A candidate who can describe what they fed back to the product team shows the engagement did not end at the customer’s door.

Showing the handover worked

Claims of “we trained them” are cheap. A concrete answer is the handover rehearsal:

Five tasks the client team performs while the deployment engineers observe.

Describe which tasks the client team performed, what they got wrong, and what you changed in the documentation or the system afterwards. The mistakes are the evidence. A rehearsal where nothing went wrong tells the interviewer very little.

How to prepare your stories

Do not write fifty answers. Perspective AI suggests five core stories: cross-functional collaboration, handling ambiguity, a failed project, a technical disagreement and driving impact without authority. Add a refusal to that list, and make sure at least one story runs from handoff to hand-back. One full deployment can often serve several prompts, provided you retell it from a different angle each time.

Competition makes specificity matter. By April 2026, KDnuggets reports, Indeed postings for the role were about 729% higher than a year earlier. More postings means more candidates with similar titles, so the detail inside your stories is what separates you.

Practise out loud, with a colleague who interrupts. Ask them to push on every claim: what exactly did the system refuse, who saw it, who ran it afterwards? Candidates who want structured training in the role can look at the Certified Forward Deployed Engineer (CFDE)™, which AIU built as the first certification standard for the Forward Deployed Engineer role.

Frequently asked questions

What behavioural questions are asked in a forward deployed engineer interview?+

Expect questions on ownership, conflict, failure and customer pushback. Candidate reports on Glassdoor include being asked about a recent conflict at work, a project worked on end to end, and what to do if a client proposes a solution that is not ideal. Prepare real deployment stories for each.

How do you answer "what did you refuse to build?"+

Pick one specific system, name the thing it declined to do, and describe what that refusal looked like to the user or customer who met it. Then say what you offered instead and how the customer reacted. If you were overruled or later found you were wrong, say that too.

Is there a separate behavioural round in forward deployed engineer interviews?+

Often, yes. Perspective AI’s reported process includes a structured behavioural or values round of about 45 minutes, alongside coding, a technical deep dive and a case study. Glassdoor reports also describe behavioural questions opening technical rounds, so treat every round as a chance to tell a deployment story.
