How to Demonstrate Your Abilities in an FDE Job Search: How to Write a Project Deployment Retrospective

Key takeaways

  • A survey by Robert Half found that 67% of US HR leaders say reviewing AI-generated resumes slows down hiring, which makes verifiable evidence more valuable.
  • For a public project to carry weight, it needs a real deployment including APIs, authentication, and logging, plus a README that spells out the architecture and known limitations.
  • Personal accounts on Hacker News suggest that resumes are rarely read closely and referrals are no guarantee. A project write-up really works when someone has already started looking into you.

In March 2026, the staffing firm Robert Half published a number worth pondering for job seekers: 67% of US HR leaders said that reviewing AI-generated resumes slows down hiring. Employers are receiving more applications, yet taking longer to select candidates.

On Hacker News, a thread discussed: now that AI can write resumes, which hiring signals still matter? One commenter half-joked that a resume too perfect is most likely AI-generated. By that logic, a small mistake actually becomes a mark of a real human.

When a polished resume can be produced with almost no effort, polish itself loses its value. Robert Half's conclusion: employers increasingly need trustworthy humans to verify candidates' skills.

If you're targeting a Forward Deployed Engineer (FDE) role, this is the problem you face. The Pragmatic Engineer noted that a wave of FDE hiring began around early 2025; as of May 2026, it still describes demand as strong and growing, with Google, OpenAI, and Anthropic all hiring aggressively.

So, can writing publicly about your projects help you stand out from a sea of applications? Yes, but rarely by getting recruiters to come to you. An article about how a project went to production is most valuable at two moments: while you're still learning, and when someone already wants to verify your work.

What do FDE recruiters want to verify?

Gergely Orosz, author of The Pragmatic Engineer, points out that nearly all employers hiring FDEs value solid software engineering fundamentals. That's the baseline, and a clean GitHub repository can prove it.

Some companies demand more. Orosz mentions two companies, Commure and Matta, that value hands-on experience building and delivering projects end to end. The "delivery" part can't be fully told through code alone.

FDE Academy, a career guide for FDEs, advises candidates to present a project that has been fully developed and deployed, not just show code. Blockchain Council goes further: rather than building many small demos, build one complete public product to production standards.

According to that source, such a project should have a real cloud deployment, including API endpoints, basic authentication, and running logs. The accompanying README should include user stories, an architecture diagram, run instructions, and known limitations.

The last item is worth noting. Demos never show known limitations, but listing them tells the reader: you know where your system will break.

Two candidates, one project

Imagine two candidates, each of whom built an agent that reads customer support tickets and writes results into a CRM. Candidate A has a clean repository, a README with installation instructions, and a demo. Candidate B has the same repository, plus a long-form article.

In the article, B explains: why a queue was needed when the CRM's API rate-limited; how the messy fields users had entered manually over years were mapped; and how B explained mistakes the agent made to the support staff.

A has proven solid software engineering fundamentals. B has also proven they carried a project all the way from build to delivery, exactly what companies like Commure and Matta are looking for.

The difference is most visible in the interview itself. B's interviewer already has material to dig into: "What would you do if the client wouldn't let you set up a queue?" Every solid answer is evidence that AI couldn't have written for you.

A's interviewer has to start from scratch, at precisely the moment they have the most reason to suspect a resume that looks too perfect.

Nobody reads your blog, except at the key moment

Don't expect too much from that link on your resume. In the same Hacker News thread, one person said recruiters rarely read resumes; another argued that referrals are what really work, only to be answered that at their company even referrals are no longer prioritized.

These are personal accounts, not data, but they're enough to show there's no shortcut. So don't treat writing articles as a way to get recruiters to find you.

Shawn Wang (swyx) shows the other side. He says all of his public writing "got dug up," and that, in his words, is pretty much how he landed his job at AWS. Note the verb: the writing was "dug up," meaning someone found it while searching, not that it knocked on doors on its own.

swyx also points out a benefit you can get before you're hired. For him, public writing is a structured way to learn, because readers will point out where you're wrong. Having a stranger pick apart your queue design on a blog is far more comfortable than having it picked apart in an interview.

What can each signal prove?

The table below compares four types of signals FDE candidates typically have. The column contents are the editorial team's assessment based on the advice and personal accounts above, not measured data.

Resume bullet pointsExtremely easy to fakeProves almost nothing without verification; too much polish actually raises suspicionScreening stage, if anyone reads it
GitHub repository with code onlyModerateSoftware engineering fundamentalsWhen a recruiter is willing to click

No single signal is enough on its own. Referrals come earliest but prove nothing about ability, and by some people's experience, they're no longer a sure thing either.

Deployment write-ups are the most persuasive, but usually read last. A referral opens the door for you; the article gives the interviewer something to verify. Combining the two is the smartest strategy.

How to write an article that survives the "so what?"

Don't spread yourself across five projects at once; pick just one. Blockchain Council's advice to build one complete product makes sense: writing deeply around one real system gives an interviewer more to dig into than five demos.

You can structure the article around the sections that source lists for a README. Start with user stories. Using the ticket agent above as an example: support staff spend time manually copying results into the CRM, and the historical data is a mess.

The middle, and longest, section covers architecture and integration decisions. Draw a simple diagram: tickets come in, pass through the agent, enter a queue, and get written into the CRM. Then candidly write about one thing you got wrong, like calling the API directly the first time and getting blocked by rate limiting.

The final section covers known limitations: which types of tickets the agent gets wrong, and how you notify support staff when it does. Include links to the real deployment, including the API, authentication, and logs, so readers can verify it themselves instead of just taking your word for it.

Run a test before publishing: reread each paragraph and ask "so what?". Any paragraph where you can't answer, or that merely lists the technologies you used, should be rewritten around a specific decision and the reasoning behind it.

If your resume doesn't yet have a single line about a deployment for a real customer, this article is where you prove you can take a system from idea to stable operation on your own.

Every project on your resume should have a one-line description written in deployment language, such as "launched an agent for a rate-limited CRM that handles manually entered data", with a link to the article.

When reading job descriptions, watch for phrases like "end-to-end", "customer-facing", and "integration". These are exactly the places where your article can serve as evidence. Then ask someone you know in the industry to read it first and give feedback, before asking them for a referral.

When everyone can have a polished resume, the hardest thing to fake is the trace of a system that actually ran, and a story that holds up under the "so what?" question.
