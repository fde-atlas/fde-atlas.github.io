Summary

Shohei Tokumitsu, CEO of DeFactory, draws on projects where all KPIs were met but business impact never materialized, and proposes four principles for designing KPIs after commissioning an FDE consulting engagement. KPIs should connect to business outcomes rather than activity volume; leading indicators (e.g., interview counts, feedback cycle speed) should be tracked separately from lagging ones so problems surface early. The most important principle is documenting each KPI's measurement definition and pass criteria in writing, since verbal agreements break down when contacts change. Finally, reviews should occur at least monthly with decision-makers from both sides, ending in a concrete continue/adjust/exit decision. Best applied before contract signing or kickoff.

Key points

  • KPIs must link to business metrics like revenue, cost savings, or hours reduced, not just activity counts
  • Track leading indicators separately from lagging ones to enable early course correction
  • Most critical: document KPI definitions and pass conditions in writing, with a handover process for contact changes
  • Hold reviews at least monthly with decision-makers from both sides, deciding continue, adjust, or exit each time
  • Apply the principles before contract signing or kickoff; fixing them later is costly

Before commissioning an FDE consultant, you can vet the provider. However, what happens after the contract is signed cannot be prevented by vetting alone.

The "6 Principles for Vetting FDE Consultants" organized the warning signs to check before signing a contract. This article continues that discussion—how designing and agreeing on KPIs after the contract can determine the success or failure of the project.

I will introduce four principles, all of which come from experiences where I regretted not doing them. Rather than presenting them as procedures, I want you to first understand why they are necessary.

Shohei Tokumitsu

Representative Director of DeFactory. Based on experience leading multiple 0-to-1 new business developments in software development, both in-house and at other companies, and driving business growth, he currently works as an FDE field consultant/delivery lead and product manager (PdM), as well as handling recruitment (FDE, upstream SE, PM, PMO, CPO, CTO, consultants).

1. Principle 1: "Connection to Business Impact"

When setting KPIs, it is important to check where you are starting from.

Working hours, number of tasks completed, deployment frequency—these metrics are easy to measure and manage. However, if you keep pursuing them without seeing how they ultimately affect the business, you tend to end up in a state where "the numbers are there, but nothing feels like it has changed."

Looking back after a project ends, it is actually not uncommon to find that all the KPIs were met, yet almost no tangible business impact was generated. The frequent cause is that activity metrics and business outcome metrics were disconnected from the very beginning.

The starting question is: "What changes for the business when this KPI's value changes?" By confirming whether both the client and the vendor can answer this smoothly, you can notice before heading in the wrong direction.

When designing and agreeing on KPIs, check the following.

  • Whether the KPI is limited to activity volume alone, such as "working hours" or "number of tickets completed"
  • Whether the connection to business-side metrics, such as revenue, cost reduction, or reduced work hours, is explicitly stated
  • Whether both the client and the vendor can explain "why we are pursuing this KPI"

2. Principle 2: "Separating Leading and Lagging Indicators"

Only toward the end of a project do you realize "this doesn't look achievable"—I believe many people have had this experience.

One cause is that the metrics being tracked consist only of "results." Improvements in revenue or reductions in work hours are lagging indicators that emerge after a process has accumulated. If you track only those, it is hard to notice when something goes wrong along the way.

Leading indicators, as the name suggests, move ahead of results. "Weekly interview counts are only half of plan" or "the feedback cycle is behind the initial assumption"—if you can catch such changes early, you won't miss the timing to correct course.

To detect anomalies while a project is in progress, you need separate metrics that measure the process leading to results, not just the results themselves. Projects designed with lagging indicators alone often find that by the time problems surface, the available options are already limited.

When designing and agreeing on KPIs, check the following.

  • Whether final outcome metrics (lagging indicators) are set (e.g., 30% reduction in work hours, AI adoption rate of 80% or higher)
  • Whether process metrics (leading indicators) leading to them are also set separately (e.g., weekly interview counts, feedback cycle frequency)
  • Whether the design allows you to judge "whether course correction is needed" by watching the leading indicators' movements

3. Principle 3: "Clarifying Evaluation Criteria" ★Most Important

I feel this is the most important of the four principles. Looking back at projects I experienced that fell short, the most fundamental cause was here.

In that project, we discussed KPIs before signing the contract. "Improve accuracy in this area" and "reduce work hours for this task"—I thought we had agreed on the direction. However, what I realized later was that the definition of "what counts as that accuracy having improved" was nowhere documented.

When you proceed without judgment criteria, discrepancies in understanding arise when deciding "whether it was achieved." Even if we judged "we reached a sufficient level," the client felt "this isn't what we expected." This tends to surface toward the end of the project.

What is needed is to decide two things for each KPI: "what exactly will be used to measure this metric (definition)" and "what value or state must be reached to consider it achieved (clearance criteria)." Whether these two points are documented, not just verbal, makes a decisive difference later.

Also, agreeing on this between the people in charge alone is sometimes insufficient. If the point of contact changes and the agreed content is not handed over, what was decided becomes "a story nobody knows." You need to design it including who will leave what, and where.

When designing and agreeing on KPIs, check the following.

  • Whether the definition of what exactly will be used to measure this KPI is documented
  • Whether the clearance criteria of what value or state counts as "achieved" is documented
  • Whether agreement on these criteria is documented in writing (including meeting minutes and chat logs), not just verbally
  • If the point of contact changes, whether the person and procedure for handing over these agreements are decided

4. Principle 4: "Review Frequency and Structure"

Designing KPIs alone does not make them function. Without a place to regularly pause and compare against the current state, the meaning of the design fades.

What is important when designing review sessions is deciding from the start "when" and "who" participates. Unless it is a forum where the responsible parties from both the client and vendor are present, you get a situation of "reviews happened, but the decision-makers were not involved." When that happens, issues raised in reviews tend to fail to connect to the next action, and this repeats into the next review.

As for frequency, I recommend making once a month the minimum baseline. If the intervals are longer, you are more likely to miss the timing to take action before problems become serious.

Another thing to decide about the review sessions is whether they are designed so that you can make one of three decisions on the spot: "continue, adjust course, or withdraw." If the sessions repeatedly end with "let's wait and see," the project will not move forward.

Note that the framework of "who reviews and how" is still a work in progress. What is written here may also be revised through practice. I feel it is more honest to take the stance of refining things as you go rather than presenting an overly systematized framework.

When designing and agreeing on KPIs, check the following.

  • Is the review frequency designed to be at least once a month?
  • Is there a structure in place where the responsible parties from both the client side and the vendor side attend the review sessions?
  • Is the review designed so that one of "continue, adjust course, or withdraw" can be decided?
  • Are the review contents recorded and available for reference at the next review?

After checking the 4 principles

Even if there are items marked "needs confirmation" or "not set," that in itself is not a problem. If you notice them at this stage before placing the order, you have time to arrange a meeting and align on them.

However, if multiple items apply, I recommend sorting them out once before the project gets underway. In particular, with Principle 3 (clarifying judgment criteria), trying to revise it after the start incurs a large cost of realigning understandings that have already begun to take shape. This is the item where deciding at the entry point has the greatest value.

5. Frequently Asked Questions (Q&A)

5-1. When should I use the 4 Principles of KPI Design?

Before signing the contract, or before the kickoff meeting, is most effective. Even if you notice after the project has started that "the judgment criteria were ambiguous," the cost of realigning understandings that have already begun to take shape is large. In particular, Principle 3 (clarifying judgment criteria) is most effective when agreed upon at the entry point.

5-2. What is the difference between what to check before ordering FDE consulting and what to check after?

Checking before ordering is about evaluating "whom to ask." You confirm whether the provider is merely an order-taker, or whether they are selling time rather than results (see "6 Principles for Evaluating FDE Consultants" for details).

Checking after ordering, as covered in this article, is about designing "how to work together." Even if you choose a good partner, if you proceed with ambiguous KPI judgment criteria, gaps in understanding may surface in the final stages of the project.

Actual examples of projects that failed to meet targets are categorized and introduced in 【Case Study】Why 2 Out of 9 Projects Missed Their Targets.

5-3. I don't know what to set as leading indicators.

Leading indicators measure "the processes that must be passed through before final results appear." If reducing workload is a lagging indicator, candidates for leading indicators include "the number of weekly on-site interviews conducted," "the number of feedback cycles on prototypes (PoCs)," and "the usage rate of tools by on-site staff." A realistic starting point is to work with the FDE side to identify bottlenecks—"if this number doesn't move, the final results won't appear."

5-4. Are there criteria for deciding "continue, adjust course, or withdraw" at review sessions?

What you should decide in advance is the line for "what circumstances would trigger consideration of adjusting course or withdrawing." For example: "If leading indicators fall below 50% of target for two consecutive months, review the team structure," or "If information sharing from the client side stalls for three weeks, pause the project." Keeping this line in the contract or meeting minutes makes it function as a basis for decisions.

5-5. When the point of contact changes, agreed-upon content often fails to be carried over. Is there a countermeasure?

The most effective countermeasure is to leave things in writing in a form that does not depend on individual staff members. Meeting minutes and chat logs are also effective, but I recommend creating an "agreements list" as a project charter or contract appendix and storing it somewhere both parties can access. It will serve as the "first document to read" when a handover occurs.

Summary

  • Principle 1: Connection to business impact—measure with business metrics, not activity volume
  • Principle 2: Separating leading and lagging indicators—visualize not only results but also the process
  • Principle 3: Clarifying judgment criteria—agree in writing on the KPI's "definition" and "clearance conditions" (★ most important)
  • Principle 4: Review frequency and structure—review at least once a month, with decision-makers involved

Before ordering, evaluate the provider with the 6 evaluation principles; after ordering, align the yardstick for results with these 4 Principles of KPI Design. By operating these two as a set, I believe missed targets can be prevented.
