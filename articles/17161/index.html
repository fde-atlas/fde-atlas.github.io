FDE and the KJ Method | A procedure for turning field fragments into structure, and the boundary for dividing work with AI

Original title: FDEとKJ法｜現場の断片を構造に変える手順と、AIと分担する境界

TIMEWELL · 2026-09-15 · 13 min read

Summary

In the fourth article of TIMEWELL's FDE practice series, Hamamoto explains how engineers use the KJ method (developed by cultural anthropologist Kawakita Jiro) to turn hundreds of weekly field fragments into structure. The four steps—card-making, clustering with one-line labels, diagramming, and writing—avoid preset frameworks so unexpected categories emerge. The goal is labeling clusters as either company-specific 'branches' or cross-company 'patterns', returning patterns to the product. The author contrasts this with traditional requirements gathering, and defines which steps go to AI (card extraction, tentative sorting) versus humans (naming, diagramming).

Key points

  • KJ method has four steps: cards, clustering, diagramming, writing.
  • No preset frameworks; structure emerges from the fragments themselves.
  • Cluster labels split findings into product-worthy patterns vs. one-off branches.
  • AI handles card extraction and rough sorting; humans name clusters and draw links.
  • Done every Friday afternoon; weekly cadence keeps pattern/branch boundaries visible.

Hello, this is Hamamoto from TIMEWELL Inc.

This is the fourth article in the FDE practice series. In the previous three, I wrote about three techniques: listening, observing, and being present. All of them are techniques for gathering fragments from the field. The fragments you gather remain in your hands as a stack of verbatim transcripts, five-column observation records, and three-layer notebooks. In a week, that amounts to several hundred fragments.

The problem is that these fragments cannot be used for implementation as they are. What should you build first? Which issues are specific to this company, and which are patterns that exist at other companies too? Staring at the fragments will not give you the answer. You need a process to turn fragments into structure. What our engineers use in this process is the way of thinking behind the KJ method, created by cultural anthropologist Kawakita Jiro for field research. In this article, I will describe the procedure using field notes as material, the boundary between the steps delegated to AI and the steps kept by humans, and how this differs from requirements gathering in contracted development. If you want to first check how far your own team can turn field fragments into structure, please use the AI literacy diagnostic.

What is the KJ method? A "bottom-up from fragments" approach born from field research

The KJ method is a technique Kawakita Jiro developed to organize the vast number of fragments he collected during field research in the Himalayas and Nepal, and it became widely known through his book "Hassouhou" (Abduction of Ideas), published by Chuko Shinsho in 1967 1. The name comes from the initials of its inventor. It is a registered trademark of Kawakita Research Institute, and the official training and seminars are conducted by that company 2. What I write in this article is the practice our engineers use in the field, learning from its principles; it is not a substitute for that company's training. If you want to learn the essence of the method, please refer to the original book and the company's training.

The skeleton of the method, in my words, consists of four steps. Write each gathered fragment on a card, one phrase per card. Spread out the cards, group those with similar content into small bundles, and give each bundle a one-line name. Arrange the bundles spatially and draw their relationships as a diagram using lines and arrows. Finally, turn the diagram into text so it can be communicated to others. In a late-career interview, Kawakita himself stated that the core of this method lies not in sorting fragments into pre-existing frames, but in letting the fragments speak for themselves 3.

This is what distinguishes it from other organizing methods. Most organizing starts by creating frames first. You set up headings like "Issues," "Requests," and "Constraints," and put fragments into them. This is fast, but fragments that don't fit the frames get discarded. In the KJ method's approach, you don't create frames first. You look at the fragments, group similar ones together, and name the groups afterward. That is why unexpected frames emerge. For an FDE, it is these unexpected frames that hold value. Expected frames can be created in a conference room too.

This method has also been adopted in the field of quality control, where it is known as "affinity diagramming," one of the New Seven QC Tools. In the English-speaking world it is called the affinity diagram, and in user research practice it has become a standard method for turning interview fragments into structure 4. The names differ, but the principle of building up from fragments is the same.

An FDE's Friday. Turning a week's fragments into structure in four steps

Our engineers use this method in the field on Friday afternoons. It is the time to turn the fragments gathered from Monday to Thursday into decisions about what to build the following week. I will describe the procedure along with the materials.

There are three materials: the interview verbatim transcripts from the first article, the five-column observation records from the second, and the three-layer ethnography notebooks from the third. Together, that's roughly several hundred fragments per week.

The first step is making cards, one phrase per card. From the verbatim transcripts, cut out each person's statements, one card per distinct piece of content. "The section chief approves" and "But when things are urgent, the assistant manager pushes it through" are two cards. From the observation records, turn each item of discomfort from the fifth column into a card: "The explanation says section chief approval, but the screen shows the assistant manager's name." From the notebooks, capture the words that followed "usually" or "at our company." On each card, note the source—who, when, and in what situation—in small text. This is so you can later verify "is this bundle about sales only, or company-wide?"

The second step is grouping similar items. Spread out the cards and bring those with similar content together. What matters here is that the criterion for grouping is "meaning," not "words." If you collect cards containing the word "approval," talk about stamping and talk about screen operations get mixed together. Conversely, "the assistant manager pushes it through" and "the section chief doesn't look at details" use different words but have similar meanings. This closeness is something only someone who has been in the field can perceive. Keep bundles small: two to five cards, and split any bundle exceeding ten. Then give each bundle a one-line name. The name is not a summary; it is what the bundle is saying. "Approval in practice has two stages: the assistant manager's judgment and the section chief's stamp."

The third step is diagramming. Arrange the bundles on the desk and connect relationships with lines: cause and effect, opposition, sequence. If you can draw a line between the bundle "approval has two stages" and the bundle "urgent cases circulate on paper," that reveals the structure: "the on-screen approval flow doesn't accommodate urgent cases, so paper remains." The moment this structure becomes visible, what to build the following week is decided. Not fixing the on-screen approval, but creating an urgent-case path on the screen.

The fourth step is writing it up. Turn the diagram into text that can be communicated to the client contact and your own team. At our company, we write two kinds of documents. One is for the client contact: a single paragraph like "Next week, we will build an approval path for urgent cases on the screen. The reason is that the current paper path assumes the assistant manager's judgment." The other is for our own team: a judgment on "is the separate path for urgent cases a branch specific to this company, or a pattern that exists at other companies too?" This second one is the exit of the KJ method for an FDE.

Pattern or branch? Returning the bundle names to the product

Here, let me clarify the purpose for which an FDE uses this method. It is not to organize fragments. Organizing is the means; the purpose is to sort the bundle names into "branches specific to this company" and "patterns that exist at other companies too," and return the patterns to the product.

A person who worked as an FDE at Palantir for 8 years wrote that an FDE's job is to solve the problem in front of them, and generalization is the product team's job 5. Our company is small, so the same engineer does both. Then the question becomes where to make the generalization decision. You cannot decide while things are still fragments. Only when they become bundle names can you say "I've seen this at other companies too." "Approval has two stages" is a pattern we have seen at multiple companies. So on the product side, we make the number of approval stages and the approver at each stage configurable. "Urgent cases circulate on paper" is also a pattern. "That paper is held by Tanaka-san in accounting" is a branch. Branches are recorded but not put into the product.

In a January 2026 essay, a16z listed one test for identifying the FDE type as whether you can say "No" to customization 6. Saying "No" requires grounds. Grounds are the names of bundles. You can say "This is a branch unique to your company, so we won't put it in the product. Instead, we'll absorb it through configuration" because you built bundles on Friday, named them, and separated types from branches. If you take fragments home as-is, your only options are to build everything or refuse everything.

And this judgment is done weekly. If you do it once a month in bulk, the bundles grow too large and the boundary between types and branches becomes invisible. Once a week, turn hundreds of fragments into dozens of bundles, and split the bundle names into a few types and a few branches. This repetition is the substance of the reflux that returns field learnings to the product.

How this differs from requirements gathering in contracted development

Contracted development also has a process for organizing interview results. You create a requirements list, assign priorities, and feed it into estimates. In terms of outward behavior, organizing fragments is the same. The differences are shown in a table.

PerspectiveRequirements gathering in contracted developmentFDE structuring
FrameCreated first (feature list, issue list)Emerges from fragments
UnitRequirement (unit of functionality)Bundle name (unit of field reality)
JudgmentPriority and estimationType or branch
OutputRequirements specification, contractWhat to build next week, and types returned to the product
FrequencyOnce, during the requirements definition phaseEvery week

The biggest difference is whether the frame is created first. In contracted requirements gathering, a frame such as a feature list is created first, and interview fragments are sorted into it. Fragments that don't fit are dropped. "When it's urgent, the section chief pushes" doesn't fit anywhere in the feature list, so it doesn't survive into the requirements specification. Then in production, an urgent project stalls on the screen.

FDE structuring launches bundles from fragments without creating a frame first, so "when it's urgent, the section chief pushes" becomes a bundle, gets a name, and becomes something to build next week. A fragment that would be dropped in contracted work becomes the first thing built in FDE. This inversion is the reason for using a method that starts from fragments.

Another difference is the output. The output of contracted requirements gathering is the requirements specification and the contract, where things are finalized. The output of FDE structuring is what to build next week and types returned to the product, updated every week. Some may feel uneasy that nothing is finalized, but field reality changes every week, so I believe weekly updates are closer to reality.

Steps delegated to AI, and steps kept by humans

Of the four steps, what we delegate to AI is most of the first step and the first half of the second step.

In the first step, card making, we have AI read the verbatim transcripts and observation records and extract one card per piece of content. If a human extracted hundreds of fragments it would take half a day; a machine does it in minutes. Attaching sources is also machine work, as long as the transcripts retain speaker and timestamp. However, humans verify the granularity of extraction. AI tends to merge "approval is done by the manager. But when it's urgent, the section chief pushes" into one card. Whether to split it into two changes how bundles are built later, so humans check it.

The first half of the second step, grouping similar things—that is, the provisional sorting—is done by AI. We have it create a state where items are provisionally clustered by semantic proximity, and humans move things from there. Two things happen every time here. AI clusters things with the same words and separates things with different words but similar meanings. "The section chief pushes" and "the manager doesn't look at details" sit far apart. Humans bring them together. Also, AI wants to name bundles, but those names are summaries. "Statements about the approval process." That is not a name. Reducing what a bundle is saying to one line remains human work.

The third step, diagramming, and the fourth step, writing, are done by humans. Whether a line can be drawn between bundles can only be judged by someone who knows whether that line is truly connected in the field. If you have AI draw the diagram, you get a clean diagram, but the grounds for the lines become word proximity rather than the field.

Kawakita stated that the core is letting the fragments speak 3. AI arranges fragments quickly, but it is not letting the fragments speak. What speaks is the person who was there when the fragments were gathered. We maintain this boundary not so much out of respect for the method as because we have experience of getting next week's build wrong when we crossed it.

Our practice and its limits

I'll be honest. This method takes time. Friday afternoon disappears entirely. From an engineer's perspective, that time could be spent writing code. We keep doing it anyway because if we write code on Monday without building bundles on Friday, we find out on Wednesday that half of what we wrote was branches. Friday's half day protects a day and a half of the following week.

I'll also write the limits. This method cannot exceed the quality of the fragments collected. If the interviews are full of leading questions, the bundles are bundles of led answers. If the observation is only one day, the bundles are one day's story. That's why the techniques in the first through third articles come first. Also, bundle names depend on the eye level of the person naming them. From the same fragments, naming from a management height yields "inefficient business processes," while naming from a field height yields "the section chief pushes." Since what the FDE builds next week is the latter, names are given at field height. Language for management is written separately at the writing stage.

Finally, about the name. The KJ Method is a registered trademark of the Kawakita Research Institute, and our company is not certified by them. What we do is card-based structuring learned from the ideas in "Abduction: The Birth of Ideas"; formal training is conducted by the institute. If you want to learn the essence of the method, I recommend consulting the original texts and the institute's training. What we can write about is only how we connected it to field implementation.

Summary

Fragments gathered in the field cannot be used for implementation as-is. As a method for turning fragments into structure, we use the ideas of the KJ Method, which Jiro Kawakita created for field surveys. Four steps: card making with one phrase per card, grouping similar things by semantic proximity, diagramming that connects bundle relationships with lines, and writing addressed to the person in charge and to our own company. Because no frame is created first and things emerge from the fragments, frames you never anticipated arise.

The purpose of FDEs using this method is to split bundle names into "branches unique to this company" and "types that exist at other companies too," and return the types to the product. Fragments dropped in contracted requirements gathering become the first things built in FDE. AI handles card making and provisional sorting; bundle names and diagramming remain with humans.
