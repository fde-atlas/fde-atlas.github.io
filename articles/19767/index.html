What Are RPO and RTO: How to Calculate Backup Frequency and Data Loss

Original title: RPO 和 RTO 是什么：备份频率与数据丢失怎么算

FDE Times · 2026-10-08 · 5 min read

Summary

This guide explains how forward deployed engineers should answer a customer's question about how much data would be lost in an outage. The answer requires two numbers: RPO, which is determined by backup frequency rather than the mere existence of backups, and RTO, which must be measured through actual recovery drills. Drawing on AWS Well-Architected and Google Cloud guidance, the author shows that continuous replication cannot protect against accidental deletion or corruption, so promising zero data loss is an anti-pattern. A retail example with daily 02:00 snapshots yields a real RPO of up to 24 hours. The GitLab 2017 outage, lasting about 18 hours because backups were silently failing, illustrates paper-only recovery plans. The recommended workflow: inventory data stores, tier workloads by business impact, agree on RTO/RPO in writing, align backup frequency, run timed recovery tests, and alert on backup failures.

Key points

  • "How much data would we lose?" is an RPO question, determined by backup frequency.
  • Replication cannot stop accidental deletion or corruption, so never promise zero data loss.
  • RTO must be measured with real recovery drills, not assumed on paper.
  • Set RTO/RPO per workload tier by business impact instead of one company-wide number.
  • GitLab's 2017 outage (~18 hours) shows backups can fail silently without testing.

Key Takeaways

  • "How much data will we lose?" is asking about RPO. Real RPO depends on backup frequency, not on "whether backups exist."
  • When data is accidentally deleted or corrupted, replication won't save you, so never promise zero data loss.
  • A backup that has never been restored is not a recovery plan; RTO must be measured through real drills.

Right after a demo, the customer's operations lead asks a seemingly simple question: "If the system goes down, how much data will we lose?" Many engineers instinctively answer: "Don't worry, we have daily backups." That sounds reassuring, but it dodges the question.

The customer isn't asking whether you have backups; they want a number. Give two well-grounded numbers and be candid about their limitations, and you'll earn more trust than any architecture diagram. If you dodge, the customer will discover the real numbers on the worst possible day.

For a Forward Deployed Engineer (FDE), this capability comes with every deployment. The systems you build on-site at the customer touch their real data, so this question will come sooner or later, and it usually comes from the person who signs the contract.

One Question, Actually Two

AWS Well-Architected defines the recovery time objective (RTO) as the maximum acceptable delay between a service disruption and its restoration; the recovery point objective (RPO) is the maximum acceptable amount of time since the last data recovery point.

Google Cloud's disaster recovery (DR) guide puts RPO more bluntly: the longest period of data that can be lost in the event of a major incident.

A simple way to remember: RTO counts forward from the failure until service is restored; RPO counts backward until the last good copy. "How much data will we lose?" is an RPO question, but the person asking almost always cares about RTO too, even if they don't say it.

AWS requires setting both numbers for each workload separately, not one company-wide number. Later in this article we'll explain why this detail matters so much.

Backups at 2 AM: What's the RPO?

Suppose the customer is a retail chain whose order database takes a snapshot daily at 02:00. If a disk fails at 23:00, the best copy you have is the 02:00 one, so you lose 21 hours of orders.

Now the worst case: the incident happens at 01:59, one minute before the next snapshot. You lose almost a full 24 hours. The system's real RPO is 24 hours, and that's the number you should tell the customer.

AWS's DR whitepaper states explicitly that backup frequency determines the recovery point you can achieve. ISO 27001 training provider Advisera offers a practical rule: if the RPO is four hours, back up at least every four hours. If the customer can tolerate losing at most one hour of orders, daily snapshots fall far short, and backup frequency must be much higher.

RTO must be measured, not guessed. Restoring a large database, verifying integrity, pointing the application at the new database, clearing caches—every step takes time, and only running it for real tells you how long.

Why You Should Never Promise "No Data Loss"

At this point, many people immediately think of using continuous replication to drive RPO to zero. AWS acknowledges that continuous replication can bring backup lag close to zero, but it may not protect against data corruption or deliberate deletion.

The reason is simple: a buggy migration script drops a

orders

table, and the replica follows suit within seconds.

AWS writes that this holds even for multi-site active/active architectures: when data is corrupted, deleted, or scrambled beyond usability, recovery time is always greater than zero, and the recovery point is always at some moment before the corruption was discovered.

AWS goes further: even with all best practices in place, RTO and RPO remain greater than zero, meaning some availability and data will always be lost.

Well-Architected lists setting unrealistic targets like "zero data loss" as an anti-pattern. The same checklist includes another: setting targets so aggressive that costs rise while the business doesn't actually need it.

Ask Questions First, Then Propose Solutions

A good FDE doesn't jump straight to a solution. Several discovery questions recommended by AWS are worth borrowing: How much data can be lost before the business suffers unacceptable impact? Can that data be rebuilt from other sources?

The second question often changes the whole design. If online orders can be reconciled with the payment gateway, the RPO for the orders table might be relaxed; conversely, consultation records entered manually by staff are unrecoverable once lost.

Then tier the workloads. AWS recommends classifying workloads by business impact into critical, high, medium, and low, each with its own RTO/RPO. The table below is a hypothetical example for the retailer above, for illustration only:

Workload (hypothetical)TierSuggested RPOImplementation
Payments, ordersCriticalA few minutesContinuous log backups + periodic snapshots
InventoryHigh1 hourBack up at least hourly
BI reportsMedium24 hoursDaily snapshots, rebuilt from source data
AI data pipeline logsLowLoss acceptableRe-run from source data

RTO determines the DR pattern. AWS draws a clear distinction: a pilot light cannot handle requests without additional action, while warm standby can take traffic immediately, just at reduced capacity. For medium-tier workloads, pilot light or restore-from-backup is enough.

With these numbers, your answer in the meeting room can sound like this:

"For orders, in an infrastructure failure we lose at most a few minutes of data, and the system recovers within the time measured in our drills. If data is accidentally deleted, we restore to the most recent copy from before the problem was discovered. This is a limitation every system has; we've configured alerting to shorten the detection window."

Backups on Paper and GitLab's 18 Hours

GitLab's 2017 post-mortem is still cited by many people who run systems. GitLab.com was down for about 18 hours, and the GitLab team wrote themselves that they didn't know backups had been failing until it was too late. The backups existed, but only on paper.

Google Cloud recommends exactly the step GitLab skipped: once the DR plan is written, test it regularly, record every issue that arises, and adjust the plan accordingly. For an FDE, only by running recovery drills can the RTO you report to the customer be a measured number.

On your next deployment, follow this order. First, list every data store in the deployment, including the often-forgotten vector databases and object storage.

Then sit down with the customer to tier the workloads and agree on RTO/RPO in writing. Next, align the backup plan with the RPO, run a real recovery and time it, and configure alerting for backup job failures.

Four mistakes recur, and each one leads you to make a promise you can't keep:

  • Saying only "we have backups" without discussing RPO.
  • Treating replicas as backups.
  • Using one number for all systems.
  • Never testing recovery.

If you want to move into an FDE role, you can write this capability concretely on your resume: "Designed backup solutions for a 1-hour RPO, conducted quarterly recovery drills, with a measured RTO of X minutes." When reading job descriptions, watch for phrases like "disaster recovery," "on-call," or "customer-facing incidents."

When you see these terms, prepare answers to the question the operations lead asked, in case the interview touches on this topic.

The next time a customer asks "If the system goes down, how much will we lose?", you'll already have the answer from your most recent drill: run time and measured figures.
