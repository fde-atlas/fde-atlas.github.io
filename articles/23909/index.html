What's the Difference Between FDE and Solution Engineer? Comparing Roles, Responsibilities, and Careers

Original title: FDEとソリューションエンジニアの違いは？役割・成果責任・キャリアを比較

FDE総研 · 2026-09-25 · 9 min read

Summary

This Japanese article compares Forward Deployed Engineers (FDEs) with Solutions Engineers. FDEs, a role popularized by Palantir, embed at customer sites, write production-quality code, and are accountable for business outcomes after deployment; Solutions Engineers support sales through demos, proposals, and PoCs until the deal closes. The article contrasts their phases of involvement, daily work, skills, and KPIs, and notes rising demand for FDEs since around 2025 as OpenAI and Anthropic publicly recruit them and a16z highlights the role, driven by the difficulty of taking generative AI pilots to production. It also outlines how Solutions Engineers can transition to FDE roles and warns companies that renaming the title without changing metrics and authority will not work.

Key points

  • FDEs own post-contract delivery and business results; Solutions Engineers own pre-sale technical persuasion.
  • FDEs write production code on-site; Solutions Engineers mainly build demos and PoCs.
  • Generative AI's hard 'last mile' to production is driving FDE demand at OpenAI, Anthropic, and elsewhere.
  • Transitioning to FDE requires production implementation and data engineering experience.
  • Companies must change KPIs and grant implementation authority, not just retitle roles.

What's the difference between an FDE and a Solution Engineer?

FDEs work on-site with customers and take responsibility through production go-live and business outcomes. Solution Engineers support deal wins through pre-deployment technical proposals.

Both roles are often described as "engineers who understand technology and can talk to customers," making them hard to distinguish on job postings. However, they differ significantly in when they get involved and what constitutes completion of their work. FDE (Forward Deployed Engineer) is a role popularized by Palantir. In its official blog post "A Day in the Life of a Palantir Forward Deployed Software Engineer" and elsewhere, Palantir describes the role as one that embeds itself in customer organizations to define problems and build working systems on its own platform.

How do the definitions differ?

An FDE is "someone who writes software to solve customer problems on-site," while a Solution Engineer is "someone who demonstrates that problems can be solved with the product."

How far does each role's accountability extend?

The biggest difference is the scope of responsibility. Solution Engineers are accountable through the deal close; FDEs are accountable until customer business results are achieved.

A Solution Engineer's job centers on convincing the customer that "this product can meet your requirements" and moving the deal to contract. An FDE, on the other hand, is in a position where even building exactly to spec after signing may not count as success if the business itself doesn't change. From what I hear in the field, how much discretion an FDE is given to identify "the real problem beyond the stated requirements" and rebuild accordingly seems to determine how fulfilling the role feels.

Why is this difference getting attention now?

With the adoption of generative AI, the difficulty of moving from PoC to production has become apparent. More companies are seeking FDE-style structures.

Around 2025, AI companies such as OpenAI and Anthropic began publicly recruiting FDEs. The venture firm Andreessen Horowitz (a16z) has also highlighted the FDE as a notable role at startups. Systems built on LLMs often work well in demos but stall when they must handle real business data, permission management, and exception handling. FDEs are being sought, separately from Solution Engineers, as the talent that handles this "last mile."

How do the day-to-day jobs of FDEs and Solution Engineers differ?

Solution Engineers support many deals "broadly" in parallel, while FDEs work "deeply" with a small number of customers on implementation.

Even when working with the same customer, how they spend a week differs considerably. Here, we'll organize the comparison along two dimensions: which phase they own, and what they do day to day.

How are the phases divided?

Solution Engineers primarily handle sales meetings through PoC, while FDEs primarily handle production deployment and adoption.

PhaseSolution Engineer involvementFDE involvement
1. Requirements gatheringPrimary owner (as part of sales)May attend for large deals
2. Demos and proposalsPrimary ownerGenerally not involved
3. PoC (technical validation)Primary ownerParticipates when production deployment is the goal
4. ContractFinalizes technical conditionsConfirms feasibility of implementation
5. Production deploymentHands off and steps awayPrimary owner
6. Adoption and expansionRe-engages for upsell proposalsPrimary owner (creating new use cases)

The structure of "different people owning the PoC and the production build" is a point where information easily gets lost. In organizations with FDEs, it's common for the FDE to join from the PoC stage and continue through production.

How does the daily work differ?

Solution Engineers focus on external-facing work such as preparing demos and answering technical questions. FDEs repeatedly connect data, implement, and coordinate with the field.

A typical Solution Engineer's week is filled with tasks like the following.

  • Attending sales meetings alongside sales reps and answering technical questions
  • Preparing demo environments tailored to the customer's industry
  • Responding to security checklists and RFPs
  • Defining PoC scope and summarizing validation results

An FDE's week centers on tasks like the following.

  • Working with the customer's business staff to understand current workflows and data flows
  • Connecting to internal systems and data platforms and preparing the data
  • Implementing apps and workflows on top of the product and having the field try them out
  • Summarizing product gaps and communicating them to the internal product team

The fourth role, "feedback to the product," is often overlooked, but it is work that characterizes the FDE position. Even in Palantir's description of how FDEs work, the role of feeding insights gained in the field back into the product is emphasized.

How do the required skills and evaluation metrics differ?

Both roles require technical and communication skills. What differs is the weighting and the evaluation metrics: FDEs are judged on depth of implementation, while solution engineers are judged on persuasiveness and the breadth of deals they can handle.

How do the required skills differ?

For FDEs, production-quality coding and data processing skills are especially valued; for solution engineers, the ability to understand the product broadly and communicate it is especially valued.

How do the evaluation metrics (KPIs) differ?

Solution engineers are evaluated on closed deals and sales progress. FDEs are more often evaluated on post-deployment usage and customer outcomes.

Evaluation perspectiveCommon FDE metricsCommon solution engineer metrics
Main metricsNumber of use cases running in production, sustained adoptionWin rate on assigned deals, contribution to pipeline
Time horizonQuarterly to yearlyPer sales cycle
Customer-side outcomesReduced work hours, faster decision-making, etc.Resolution of technical concerns
Contribution to the companyProduct feedback, reusable componentsDemo assets, proposal templates

Different evaluation metrics also change day-to-day decisions. For solution engineers, "closing deals this quarter" matters; for FDEs, "whether it's still being used six months later" matters. If you're considering a career change, we recommend first checking whether this difference in time horizon suits you.

Who is suited to each role?

People who want to keep building hands-on in fast-changing environments are suited to FDE; people who want to keep engaging with many customers and industries are suited to solution engineering.

It's not a matter of which is better, but of where you find fulfillment in your work. FDEs work with a single customer over a long period, so they gain deep industry knowledge. On the other hand, the industries they handle tend to be skewed. Solution engineers can see many industries, but they may not get to see how what they proposed is actually used in production.

How do you transition from solution engineer to FDE?

Moving from solution engineer to FDE is entirely realistic. Your customer-facing experience carries over directly; what you need to fill in is experience with production implementation and data processing.

Which experience can be used as-is?

The ability to draw out customer problems, the ability to explain technology in terms the other party understands, and experience assembling PoCs will all be major strengths in FDE work as well.

In FDE hiring, many companies are said to conduct case interviews that break down customer problems, in addition to coding tests. People who have been through many sales meetings as solution engineers tend to be strong in these case interviews. There may even be situations where you're evaluated as "more accustomed to working with ambiguous requirements" than a pure backend engineer.

How do you fill the skill gaps?

What's missing is implementation experience that assumes production operation. The shortcut is to deliberately accumulate experience taking PoCs all the way to production.

  • Expand your PoC responsibilities at your current job: Don't stop at validation; try writing out the design for productionizing (authentication, error handling, monitoring)
  • Learn data processing hands-on: Practice SQL, Python, and ETL (extract, transform, load) flows with data close to real business use
  • Build generative AI implementation experience: Build RAG (retrieval-augmented generation) or agents, including evaluation, logging, and permission management
  • Learn to describe results in business metrics: Summarize your portfolio in terms of "what changed and how," not "what you built"
  • Try it through internal transfers or concurrent roles: Going through engineering roles in implementation support or customer success lets you experience the weight of accountability first

What should companies adopting FDEs watch out for?

Simply renaming the solution engineer title won't make FDEs work. You need to change the evaluation metrics and authority along with it.

What decision-makers considering FDE adoption should watch out for is the design of the evaluation system and discretion. If you just attach the FDE name while keeping deal-based KPIs, the people in the role will end up spending their time on PoCs and proposals. Only when you provide all three—authority for production implementation, a channel to propose to the product team, and a system that evaluates on long-term outcomes—do you approach a Palantir-style structure. In businesses like VACAN's, which deploy their own product into customers' operations, it's likely that the success of adoption depends on clearly separating the solution engineer and FDE roles and building a structure where one person can own a project from PoC to production.
