How does an FDE run weekly customer demos?

Original title: FDE 每周客户演示怎么做

FDE Times · 2026-11-09 · 5 min read

Summary

This guide argues that late-stage project surprises usually stem from weeks of silence between kickoff and delivery, not bad code. It proposes a fixed weekly 30-minute demo where the customer operates real software with their own data, ending with one documented change. Demos are checked against a signed requirements brief so scope drift surfaces immediately. The author traces the practice to the Agile Manifesto and Scrum Guide, walks through a minute-by-minute insurance-case example, prescribes a three-part meeting note sent within an hour, lists five ways demos become theater, and suggests turning this experience into resume and interview material.

Key points

  • Late surprises come from silence, not bad code.
  • Customers must drive real software with real data.
  • Every demo ends with one documented change.
  • Compare each demo to the signed requirements brief.
  • Send a three-part recap within an hour and relay feedback to product.

A demo only counts if the customer gets hands-on with real data and both sides agree on one change for the next week.

Key takeaways

  • Late-stage surprises on projects usually stem from silence between kickoff and delivery, rarely from bad code.
  • The weekly 30-minute demo must run on real software and end with one documented change.
  • Check every demo against the customer-signed requirements brief, so scope drift is caught on the spot.

It's week eight of the project. You share your screen and demo a nearly finished build. The customer's operations lead asks a question that silences the room: "Why can't it read attachments? Half our complaints are PDFs."

This usually doesn't mean your code is bad; it means nobody on the customer's side touched the software for seven straight weeks. During that time, every wrong assumption stacked on the last one, and by delivery day, the cost of starting over was already high.

For a Forward Deployed Engineer (FDE), this is a professional risk, not bad luck. You sit next to the customer, and requirements can change weekly, so the cheapest way to prevent surprises is a fixed rhythm: 30 minutes a week, with the customer hands-on using what you just built.

This practice predates the FDE role

The Agile Manifesto puts customer satisfaction first, through early and continuous delivery of valuable software. It recommends delivering working software every few weeks to a few months, with the shortest timescale preferred. It also treats working software, not documentation, as the primary measure of progress.

The Scrum Guide adds a point many teams skip: the Sprint Review exists to inspect the outcome and decide how to adjust going forward. The Guide also reminds teams it is a working session, and they should avoid turning it into a status report.

Put together, a real demo needs three things: real software, an engaged customer, and an ending that produces a change. Miss any one of them, and it's just a status report.

Why 30 minutes, and why weekly?

The Scrum Guide caps the review for a one-month Sprint at four hours. Scaled down, a one-week cycle needs roughly a quarter of that. 30 minutes is even lighter: small enough to fit in the customer's calendar, with no one needing a manager's approval.

The Agile Manifesto wants business people and developers working together daily throughout the project. On-site with a customer, that's often impossible, because real users are busy with their day jobs. The weekly demo is the minimum contact pinned to the calendar: even in a week with zero overlap, you still get one round of feedback.

The rhythm works for teams with longer Sprints too. PostHog's FDE team runs two-week Sprints, from Monday of one week to Friday of the next. A weekly demo gives you a mid-Sprint check, while there's still time to adjust course, plus another check at Sprint's end.

A sample demo, minute by minute

Suppose you're deploying an agent that triages complaint emails for an insurance company's customer service department. Before writing the first line of code, follow PostHog's FDE team's practice: write the scope as a short requirements brief, send it to the customer for sign-off, then start investing. That brief becomes the yardstick for every demo that follows.

MinuteContentWho holds the mouse
0–3Restate this week's goal and last week's decisions against the requirements briefFDE
3–18Customer runs 5 real emails from their own inboxCustomer-side user
18–25Questions, flagging errors, prioritizing togetherBoth sides
25–30Read out decisions, lock in next week's workFDE

The critical stretch is minutes 3 to 18. You don't perform; you say: "Could you pick the five hardest emails from this week and paste them in?" When the agent misroutes one, don't rush to explain—ask: "If this one were yours, which category would you put it in? What told you that?"

The answer to that question is the most valuable data of the week. If she says "you can tell from the attachment," then the problem in the opening scenario that surfaced in week eight, you caught in week two.

An FDE quoted by Insight Partners says one demo beats many conversations. These 15 minutes show why.

The last five minutes are for reading out decisions: "Next week: add a step to read PDFs, drop the 'Other' category since nobody uses it, and push the CRM integration back." Anyone who disagrees raises it on the spot, not by email later.

Feedback only counts when it flows

Within an hour of the demo, send a three-part meeting note: what the customer saw, what both sides decided, and what happens next week. If any decision deviates from the original requirements brief, state it explicitly and ask the customer to reconfirm in writing. Small changes not recorded today easily become surprises on delivery day.

Feedback also has to reach your own product team. PostHog's FDE team sees itself as the fastest feedback loop between how customers actually use the product and the product roadmap.

Insight Partners likewise stresses that the feedback loop to product must be deliberate. Appending one line to each weekly report—"the insurance customer needs to read PDF attachments"—is what deliberate looks like.

Five mistakes that turn demos into box-checking

The most common mistake is showing slides instead of software. Slides show what you plan to do; working software is the evidence of progress. The second mistake is demoing with self-made sample data: that data was written to run smoothly in the first place, which conveniently hides the tricky cases the customer cares about most.

The third mistake is inviting only the project sponsor, not the people who will use the system every day. Sponsors approve direction; users are the ones who find bugs.

The fourth mistake is hiding the parts that aren't fixed yet. Showing the customer a half-finished feature beats canceling with "nothing to show this week," because one cancellation easily becomes a habit.

The last one is the sneakiest: the demo goes smoothly but produces no decisions. If the "decided" column in your notes is empty three weeks in a row, you're reporting to the customer, not working with them.

Exercise: write the meeting notes for the sample demo

Back to the insurance company demo. Suppose the results were: the team lead ran 5 emails, the agent classified 3 correctly and 2 incorrectly, and both wrong ones had complaints inside PDF attachments; nobody picked the "Other" category; and she said the CRM integration wasn't urgent. Set a 10-minute timer, write the three-part notes first, then keep reading.

A passing version looks roughly like this. Saw: 3/5 emails classified correctly; both errors were because the complaint was in a PDF. Decided: add a step to read PDFs, drop the "Other" category, push CRM back. Next week: rerun these 5 emails, plus 5 new ones with attachments.

Then score your version with three questions. Which decision deviated from the requirements brief? Is there a line asking the customer to reconfirm? Which line should go to the product team? Could someone who missed the demo understand what's being shown next week?

Turn the demo rhythm into a job-hunting advantage

If you want to prove you can do more than write code—that you can iterate quickly with customers—don't write "strong customer communication skills" on your resume.

Instead, write one line that says what you did and what it produced, for example: "Ran 30-minute demos with end users weekly; caught a scope change in week two and reconfirmed the requirements brief in writing."

Before interviews, prepare a specific story: a demo whose feedback made you change course. When reading a job description, look for sections about working directly with customers: that's exactly where this story belongs.

You don't need anyone's approval to start this. Set up a recurring 30-minute weekly meeting, invite the people who will eventually use this system, and let them hold the mouse. The PDF question will come sooner or later—it's much better to hear it in week two.
