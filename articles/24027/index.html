How Does an FDE Engage Customers? Four Practices That Build Trust and How to Avoid Being a Mere Order-Taker

Original title: FDEは顧客をどう巻き込む？信頼を生む4つの作法と御用聞きにならない方法

FDE総研 · 2026-07-23 · 9 min read

Summary

This FDE総研 article explains how Forward Deployed Engineers (FDEs) build customer trust and engagement through hands-on accompaniment rather than acting as pure consultants or outsourced developers. Its central principle is that working deliverables, not words or slide decks, create trust: a rough but functional product shown early resolves doubts through experience and shifts discussion from abstraction to specifics. The author outlines four practices—entering the field, structuring the true problem behind stated requests, building something runnable quickly, and staying until the solution is actually adopted. The article also warns against becoming an order-taker: FDEs should verify which problem a request solves before building, citing VACAN Technologies' positioning and Anthropic's job postings that require discovery skills. Conflicts with customers are resolved by returning to the structured problem as shared ground.

Key points

  • FDEs are stakeholders in the field, distinct from consultants and SIer-style vendors.
  • Trust grows fastest from early, rough, working deliverables rather than proposals.
  • Four practices: enter the field, structure true problems, build fast, ensure adoption.
  • Avoid order-taking by checking which problem each request actually solves.
  • Disagreements are resolved by returning to the structured problem and judging via experience.

How do FDEs engage customers?

FDEs are neither advisors nor vendors—they work hands-on alongside the customer's business, building trust through working deliverables to engage customers. The core of engagement lies not in "words" but in "usable deliverables," and how the relationship itself is structured is the starting point of engagement.

What does FDE "hands-on accompaniment" mean?

FDE hands-on accompaniment means going into the field, running alongside customers from problem discovery through resolution and adoption, and treating business outcomes as one's own concern.

FDE (Forward Deployed Engineer) is a role popularized by Palantir—engineers who embed themselves in the customer's field and take responsibility for outcomes. Unlike contracted development, where requirements are received in a meeting room and taken back to build, FDEs position themselves where the work actually happens and run alongside customers from problem discovery through resolution and adoption.

VACAN Technologies positions itself this way: "Not merely offering advice, and not merely building systems. A company that can work hands-on alongside the customer's business" (Wantedly interview).

Accompaniment is also a declaration that the work doesn't end with handing over a deliverable. FDEs treat the customer's business outcomes as their own concern, extending their scope to the point where what they build is actually used and generates value. The reason FDE customer engagement is discussed as "professional practice" rather than "sales technique" lies in how this scope of responsibility is defined.

How does this differ from pure consulting or pure system delivery?

Consulting alone leaves the field unmoved; delivery alone cuts the work off from the field's context. FDEs integrate both on-site.

The FDE position may seem to sit between consulting and SIers, but it differs from both. The differences among the three can be summarized as follows.

What remainsUnderstanding of the problemSomething that works to specA deliverable continuously used in the field

Pure advisory deepens understanding of the problem, but the field doesn't move. Pure spec-driven building leaves something that works, but whether it truly solved the right problem is never validated. FDEs integrate both roles by going into the field, getting hands dirty, and building agreement with customers through working artifacts.

This single point—"working hands-on alongside the customer's business"—is the starting point of how FDEs engage customers. By becoming neither a detached advisor nor a vendor, but a stakeholder facing the same problem in the same field, the customer shifts from "the one who commissions" to "the one who advances things together."

Where does FDE trust come from?

FDE trust grows quickly not from the volume of proposals, but from showing something rough yet actually working, early. Once customers use it, the next problems can be discussed in the same words.

Why does a "working output" work better than documents?

Working artifacts resolve doubts like "will this really work?" through experience, and instantly move discussion from abstraction to specifics.

VACAN Technologies describes itself as "a technology accompaniment firm that solves field problems with working outputs" (official website). The key phrase here is "working outputs." Rather than piling up thick proposal documents and perfect design specs, handing over something rough but tangible early advances customer understanding and trust faster.

Even when told verbally "this is what it will become," customers can't be certain it will truly solve their problems. Working artifacts dramatically reduce that uncertainty. By touching, using, and experiencing it, the discussion shifts from abstraction to specifics. The lingering doubt of "will this really work?"—which no amount of careful explanation in documents can eliminate—is answered by the experience itself when a working deliverable is in front of them. This is why VACAN Technologies places "working outputs" at the center of trust building.

How does the relationship change once customers start using it?

Once customers use it, requests and pain points that only surface in the field emerge, and the next problems can be discussed by customer and FDE in the same words.

Once a working artifact is used, the relationship with the customer moves to the next stage. Voices that only emerge in the field come out: "We'd like this to work more like this," or "Actually, we're also struggling in this situation." The first deliverable doesn't need to be a finished product—it's enough if it serves as a shared foundation to move the dialogue forward.

In this way, starting from a working output, problems are articulated one after another, and FDE and customer iterate on improvements while looking in the same direction. Trust is born not from one big proposal, but from the accumulation of small agreements made through working artifacts. Conversely, the slower the first move, the slower customer engagement gets off the ground.

What are the practices FDEs use to engage customers?

FDE customer engagement advances through four practices: go to the field, structure the true problem, move fast, and drive adoption together. Each concretizes the principle of "deliverables over words."

How do you enter the field and structure the true problem?

By not treating requests as the problem itself, but digging out the essence from facts observed in the workplace, organizing them, and presenting them as a structure.

Tamaki describes his aim as "going deep into the customer's workplace, capturing the true problem, organizing and structuring it, and moving forward while also leveraging generative AI" (Wantedly). What customers voice as requests is often not the problem itself, merely one interpretation of it.

If you build exactly what was heard in a meeting room, surface complaints may be resolved but the essential problem remains. That's why FDEs start by entering the field and re-capturing what the real bottleneck is as a structure. A structured problem also becomes a shared language that the customer and FDE can repeatedly return to during subsequent consensus-building.

How do you move fast and embed it together?

By building something small and working against the structured problem, gaining buy-in through experience, and staying alongside until it's in continuous use in the field.

The latter half of the method is the phase where deliverables are used as "material for judgment." First, build something small and working, and gain agreement by having the customer experience it. Once agreement is reached, stay alongside until it becomes embedded in the field and continuously used. Deliverables that run but go unused don't count as customer-engagement outcomes.

At VACAN Technologies, this flow of "enter the field → capture the true problem → move fast → embed it together" functions as a series of practices for engaging customers. It's not about building and handing over; seeing it through to adoption builds the next layer of trust and creates a relationship where the next problem is tackled together.

How does an FDE avoid becoming a mere errand-runner?

The key to avoiding becoming an errand-runner is not taking requests at face value, but re-capturing the true problem in the field and proposing something that works. This single point is what decisively differs from a relationship of merely offering advice.

What's the criterion for not taking requests at face value?

The criterion is to verify through structure "which problem this request solves," and never start building without an answer.

Going deep into the field carries the risk of falling into being an "errand-runner" who takes every customer request. But FDEs don't build requests as-is; they re-capture the problem in the field and propose. By structuring the essence behind the request and showing with something that works "isn't this what really needs to be solved," they maintain a relationship as equal stakeholders, not subordinates.

Customer-facing skill is a core FDE competency, and Anthropic's job postings list discovery capability with customers as a requirement (Anthropic official job listing). It's precisely because FDEs have discovery—the ability to dig out the true problem—that they can go deeper into the issues behind requests.

How do you proceed when opinions conflict with the customer?

Instead of a standoff, return to the shared foundation of the structured problem and redefine together what to solve, turning conflict into progress.

Conflict usually arises when the discussion has become about "how to solve." By pulling the agenda back from the solution to the problem itself and re-laying it out as a structure, you can see where understanding diverges. Add a working output there, and priorities can be judged through experience rather than opinions.

The third path—neither errand-runner nor imposer—stands on the shared language of working outputs and structured problems. Claiming equality without these two means the argument gets settled by the strength of positions.

Where does the engagement method fit within the overall project?

The customer-engagement method functions as part of the overall project approach, running consistently from requirements definition through adoption.

The project approach is explained in How does an FDE's project proceed?, and the overall role in What is an FDE?. Customer engagement is not a technique only at the start of a project; it's repeatedly needed when finalizing requirements, when placing what was built into the field, and when handing over to operations.

Conversely, projects where engagement fails have reverted to "words-only exchanges" at some phase. Checking which of the four practices—field, structuring, working outputs, adoption—is missing is the shortest path to recovery.
