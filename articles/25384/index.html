Secondary Damage from GMO Information Disclosure Incidents and Prevention of Recurrence | Measures Companies Should Implement Now

Original title: GMO情報公開事案の二次被害と再発防止｜企業が今すぐ実装すべき対策

FDE総研 · 2026-10-28 · 17 min read

Summary

Based on GMO Internet Group's official reports (No. 9–15) and major Japanese media coverage, this article analyzes what may follow the public leak of customer data from the お名前.com Desktop Cloud breach. The editors foresee three risks: continued staged releases via new domains despite takedowns, secondary fraud such as phishing and impersonation using leaked personal data, and direct threats against affected individuals, as happened in Finland's Vastaamo case. Comparing Vastaamo, Medibank, Optus, and KADOKAWA, the article identifies three recurring patterns: drip-style releases, pressure shifting to individuals, and unverifiable data deletion. GMO's distinctive responses—tiered consolation payments by data category and a bounty capped at 1 million USD—are noted. Recommended measures include avoiding leak sites, changing passwords, enabling two-factor authentication, centralizing compensation channels, legal action against spreaders, banning plaintext credential storage, minimizing privileges, retaining logs, and management-approved remediation deadlines. The article closes by discussing how FDEs can help implement fixes, while noting their limits.

Key points

  • Editors predict continued leaks, secondary fraud, and direct threats to individuals.
  • Comparison with four past incidents reveals drip releases, personal targeting, and unverifiable deletion.
  • GMO offers tiered consolation payments and a bounty capped at 1 million USD.
  • Key defenses: no plaintext credentials, least privilege, logs, and remediation SLAs.
  • FDEs can bridge findings to implementation, but prioritization remains management's job.

What could happen next in the GMO data disclosure incident?

According to FDE Research Institute's assessment, three things could happen next in the GMO data disclosure incident: continued publication threats, secondary damage, and direct threats against individuals.

This article is based on GMO Internet Group's official announcements (Reports 9 through 14) as of October 28, 2026, 18:20, and reporting by Nikkei, Asahi Shimbun, Mainichi Shimbun, and Security Measures Lab. Report 15 (October 29) and the November 2 press conference are reflected based on reporting as of November 5. As the incident is ongoing, figures and circumstances may change going forward. If further updates emerge, we will update this article with the revision date noted.

In the unauthorized access to GMO Internet Group's "onamae.com Desktop Cloud," on October 27, an apparent attacker published what they claimed was "information related to GMO and its customers" (GMO confirmed the publication in Report 10 the same day). On October 28, the attacker's site domain was suspended, and GMO has established a reward program. The sequence of events from discovery to publication is summarized in our previous article, "GMO Unauthorized Access and the White Hacker's Warnings." This article focuses on "what could happen next" and "what companies should implement on the ground to prevent the same situation."

Below, we separate sourced facts from the outlook and opinions of the FDE Research Institute editorial team. VACAN Technologies, which operates FDE Research Institute, is not a party to this incident.

The daily-noon publication threat and the takedown cat-and-mouse game

The domain was suspended and the publication site is no longer viewable, but this does not mean the attacker has been identified or eliminated.

In Report 13, GMO reported that it had worked with related businesses and others to suspend the attacker's site domain. If a new site is launched, the policy is to move toward takedown as soon as it is confirmed. Asahi Shimbun reported that ICANN lookups confirmed records showing the registrar had taken suspension measures. Meanwhile, blogs have pointed out that "the publication site merely becoming unviewable does not mean the attacker has been identified or eliminated." GMO is reported to have explained at the November 2 press conference that contact from the attacker had ceased after the night of October 23. Even so, the editorial team believes that if the attacker intends to continue daily publications, the possibility of launching a site on a new domain remains.

Secondary damage and the possibility of direct threats against individuals

FDE Research Institute is particularly wary of two things: fraud using the published information and threats directly targeting victims themselves.

According to reports, the data claimed to be published customer information included names, dates of birth, addresses, phone numbers, and websites the individuals regularly browse (authenticity unverified). With this much information available, it becomes easier to create convincing phishing attempts and impersonation calls using the person's real name. In the Vastaamo case, discussed later, the attacker, after the company refused to pay, reportedly emailed patients directly demanding money. We believe the GMO incident should also be prepared for the same pattern.

Root cause investigation and the criminal investigation are still ongoing

While the timing of the intrusion was explained, details of the entry route, the full extent of the damage, and the progress of the investigation have not yet been made clear, so it is important not to jump to conclusions about the cause based on speculation.

GMO has reported that, within the scope of its analysis of the devices entrusted to it, no signs of malware infection or ransomware attacks were found (Report 14). At the November 2 press conference, GMO is reported to have explained that the attacker infiltrated a customer's server environment on August 5, 2026, reached the administrator server, and that GMO confirmed this on October 20. The full picture, including the amount of damage, is said to be under investigation. If the "browsed websites" mentioned in reports were included in the published information, it is possible that device-side information was stolen. However, GMO is still investigating where the information was taken from, so no definitive conclusion can be drawn. It is likely that whether active cyber defense was invoked will not be disclosed (for details, see "What Is Active Cyber Defense? Corporate Preemptive Measures and How to Use FDE").

What can we learn by comparing with past extortion and data disclosure incidents?

In past incidents, attackers who were denied payment kept applying pressure through piecemeal releases and threats against individuals, and data spread did not stop even after deletion.

Comparison table of Vastaamo, Medibank, Optus, KADOKAWA, and GMO

When arranged along three axes—"demands," "method of release," and "corporate response"—the similarities and differences between the GMO incident and the four past incidents become visible.

For the GMO incident's demand amount, GMO's official figure of "about 8 billion yen" and the media-reported "1 million dollars" appear separately. The relationship between the two has not been clarified, and it is unknown which is the final demand.

Three patterns common to past incidents

What they share are three things: refusing payment prolongs the pressure, the focus shifts to individuals, and the data does not disappear.

  • Piecemeal release (drip-feeding): Medibank's information was released in multiple batches. GMO's "every day at noon" announcement is close to this pattern.
  • The focus shifts to individuals: In the Vastaamo case, more than 24,000 people reportedly filed reports that they received threats directed at them personally.
  • "Deletion" cannot be verified: The Optus attacker apologized, saying the data had been deleted, but others had already obtained copies. This shows that even removing domains and posts does not stop the spread.

How GMO's response differs from past cases

What differs from past cases in GMO's response is that it established special condolence payments by category of information and a reward system.

GMO established special condolence payments for each category of released information, and also prepared a reward capped at a total of 1 million US dollars. No such measures appear in the comparison table of the four past incidents. On the other hand, its stance of refusing payment is the same as Medibank's and Optus's. Considering the comparison table, the risk of shifting to drip-feeding releases or threats against individuals should be seen as remaining in the GMO incident as well.

What should companies and users do to prevent secondary damage?

The basics are: users should avoid touching the released sites and be suspicious of any communications they receive, and companies should consolidate compensation channels and take legal action against those who spread the data.

What users should do (by situation)

Users should not access the released sites, and should thoroughly change passwords and verify whether communications they receive are genuine.

User situationWhat to do
All usersDo not access the released sites. Do not share reposts or links either (GMO has warned that information of those who access the sites could be taken by the attacker)
Those whose trading account IDs or passwords were stored on the serverChange passwords immediately and enable two-factor authentication
Those who received emails, SMS, or calls claiming recovery of losses or compensationDo not respond until verified on the official website

What companies should do

FDE Soken believes that consolidating compensation and warnings through official channels reduces the room for impersonation to slip in.

GMO's compensation has three tiers. In its fourth report, it announced a flat 5,000-yen condolence payment to all users, compensation for losses from unauthorized transactions, and six months of free alternative services. In its twelfth report, it separately established special condolence payments paid by category of released information.

Category of released informationSpecial condolence payment
Basic information (name, contact details, etc.)10,000 yen
Financial and authentication-related information (trading account IDs, passwords, etc.)15,000 yen
Sensitive information and information requiring special care, etc.25,000 yen

If multiple categories apply, the total amount is paid, and GMO has set "a cap of 3 million yen per release."

Legal measures against spreading and repeated communication to customers

It is important to respond to those spreading the data with warnings and criminal complaints, and to repeatedly tell customers that "compensation communications come only from official channels."

GMO has sent a cumulative 20 warnings to related posts on X and indicated a policy of taking legal action, including criminal complaints. For companies, repeatedly telling customers that "compensation communications come only from official channels" is a basic anti-impersonation measure.

What measures should companies implement to prevent the same situation from happening again?

For prevention of recurrence, it is important to decide, at a level of detail that can be implemented on the ground, four points: where credentials are kept, permissions, logs, and deadlines for fixing identified issues.

Review the storage of credentials and permissions

The top priorities are not storing credentials in plaintext and minimizing permissions; the scale of damage in the event of a leak is determined by these two points.

In the GMO incident, login information for trading accounts stored on the server was targeted (second and fourth reports). As a general rule, the following measures are effective.

  • Do not store credentials in plaintext on servers or devices
  • Avoid saving passwords in browsers and sharing the same password among multiple people
  • Minimize permissions, and use read-only credentials for processes that only need to reference data
  • Make two-factor authentication mandatory
  • Prepare in advance a procedure to forcibly terminate active login sessions

Inventory of externally exposed surfaces and log retention

Closing unused entry points and collecting logs in one place for a long period reduces opportunities for intrusion and speeds up investigations.

Identify externally visible services, accounts, and admin panels (control panels), and shut down any that are not in use. Retain logs for a long period and manage them in one place, so that in the event of an intrusion you can trace back through them.

Set remediation deadlines for "findings," with management deciding priorities

Vulnerability findings only gain real force once management has approved remediation deadlines for each severity level.

GMO's representative explained that although the company had received reports from in-house white hat hackers, it came under attack while still facing challenges in the depth and speed of its response. It was reported that at the press conference on November 2, he acknowledged that in April 2024 an expert from a group company had pointed out the issue, and although the company judged it could be addressed through emergency maintenance, the response proved insufficient. From a practitioner's perspective, findings tend to be left unaddressed in situations where "fixing it will stop the service." Whether availability or security takes priority is something management must decide in advance.

What to decideOwnerKey points
Severity classificationSecurity officerKeep the rationale for each classification decision
Remediation deadlines (SLA) per classificationApproved by managementAutomatically escalate to a higher role when a deadline is exceeded
Decisions on fixes that involve service downtimeManagementDocument the priority between availability and security in advance

Priorities that vary by company size and structure

The fewer dedicated security staff a company has, the more it can achieve results with minimal effort by starting with credential storage and two-factor authentication.

The following is the FDE Soken editorial team's summary. For small organizations without dedicated staff, it is realistic to first move ahead with eliminating plaintext storage and making two-factor authentication mandatory, and to start with a simple remediation deadline table. In organizations where the security officer and development team are separate, the priority is to decide remediation deadlines and reporting rules first, so that findings do not stall between the two.

What is the playbook for responding to extortion threats, and what are the statutory reporting deadlines?

Decide your payment policy, contact sequence, and compensation framework in peacetime, and build a structure that can act by working backward from the reporting deadlines under the Act on the Protection of Personal Information.

Six items in the extortion response playbook

Since it is too late to start discussions after receiving a threat, the following six items should be documented in peacetime.

  • Payment policy: GMO has declared it will "never comply." The reasons are that payment does not guarantee deletion of the information or prevention of misuse, and that it amounts to funding criminal organizations.
  • Contact sequence: The order in which to contact the police, the Personal Information Protection Commission, and business partners, and the person in charge of each
  • Internal rule against touching the perpetrator's site: Accessing it may result in the viewer's information being captured.
  • 24-hour contact point: Communication structure for PR, legal, and customer response
  • Compensation framework: As GMO did, one approach is to set amounts for each category of leaked information.
  • Response to those who spread the information: Preserve evidence, then file disclosure requests for sender information or press charges.

Reporting deadlines under the Act on the Protection of Personal Information

For leaks likely to harm individuals' rights and interests, reporting to the Commission and notifying affected individuals are mandatory, with the preliminary report due within roughly 3 to 5 days.

ProcedureDeadline (per Personal Information Protection Commission guidance)
Preliminary reportWithin roughly 3 to 5 days of becoming aware of the incident
Final reportWithin 30 days
Final report (when there is a risk of leakage for malicious purposes, etc.)Within 60 days
Notification to affected individualsMandatory along with the report

Initial-response tabletop exercises and handling of customer devices

Conduct tabletop exercises on initial response immediately after receiving a threat email at least once a year, and set rules for examining customers' devices.

GMO collected devices from customers across the country and had its white hat team analyze them. When examining customers' devices, if you do not decide in advance how to obtain consent, how to store the devices, return procedures, and how to handle data, staff on the ground will be unsure how to proceed.

What role can FDEs play in preventing recurrence?

FDEs can shorten the distance between a finding and its implementation and adoption in the field, but it cannot be said that "having an FDE would have prevented this."

Shortening the distance between "finding" and "adoption"

The strength of an FDE is that a single person can consistently handle everything from identifying issues to implementation and adoption in the field.

FDE (Forward Deployed Engineer) is a role popularized by Palantir. FDEs embed themselves in customers' operations and consistently handle everything from identifying issues to implementation and adoption. The issue at stake in this case is that the white hat hackers' findings never reached implementation. FDE Soken believes that the situation where the person who reports and the person who fixes are separate, and prioritization stalls between them, can occur in many organizations. Changes that go deep into business workflows—such as changing how credentials are stored, separating permissions, and building a log infrastructure—progress more smoothly when someone who understands the realities of the field handles them through implementation.

How do FDEs, IT consultants, and SIers differ in preventing recurrence?

FDE Soken's comparison axis is "who takes responsibility through to adoption of the implementation," and FDEs differ from other roles in that they take on that full scope.

The following is the FDE Soken editorial team's summary of typical roles. Actual contract scopes vary by project.

RoleMain role in preventing recurrenceLevel of involvement through adoption
FDEImplements findings to fit field workflows and embeds them in operationsInvolved through post-implementation operations
IT consultantOrganizes issues and proposes countermeasure policiesMainly policy-making; implementation tends to be handled by others
SIerBuilds and modifies systems according to requirementsMainly implementation within the contractually defined scope

Differences between roles are compared in detail in "Differences between FDEs and consultants" and "Differences between FDEs and SIers."

What cannot be delegated to FDEs

Approving payment deadlines and deciding whether to refuse payment are management's responsibility, and the FDE cannot take on these duties on their behalf.

The FDE is not a universal solution. We believe the FDE can take on the part of translating management's decisions into on-the-ground implementation and making them stick. The overall role of the FDE and how to build an organization for its introduction are explained in "What is an FDE?" and "How to Introduce an FDE".
