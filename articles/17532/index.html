An urgent request that was not urgent

FDE Interview Prep · 2026 · 2 min read

Summary

This is a practice prompt for FDE interviews about handling a customer request that arrives flagged urgent. Citing Leo Mehr, Director of Engineering at Ramp, it argues that when a sales rep demands an integration urgently, a good engineer first diagnoses what is driving the urgency, who will actually use it, and whether workarounds were exhausted. The article lays out a six-part answer structure: the request as received, questions asked before building, confirming the real driver, the smaller alternative shipped instead, communication order (account executive first, then requester), and the outcome. It warns against stories that make the customer look foolish, and against diagnosing so long that the requester only hears a delay. It lists likely follow-up questions and includes a two-minute illustrative answer set at a fictional warehouse software vendor.

Key points

  • Core advice: spend most of the answer on diagnosis, not building.
  • Cite Leo Mehr of Ramp: ask what drives urgency before coding.
  • Six-part structure from request as received to final outcome.
  • Tell the account executive the plan first, then the requester in their terms.
  • Failure modes: sounding like you doubt every request, or diagnosing too long.

How to answer

Leo Mehr, Director of Engineering at Ramp, said in an AI Engineer talk published in July 2026 that when a sales rep urgently demands an integration, a well-trained pauses to ask what is driving the urgency, who will use it and whether all workarounds have been exhausted. Source 1How Forward Deployed Engineering is done at Ramp — Leo Mehr (YouTube auto-generated English captions)PublisherAI Engineer, YouTubeSource typerecorded talk or interview Give the diagnosis most of your time. Pick a story where the request wasn’t urgent but something behind it was, often on a different date than the one in the email. A story where nothing was urgent at all makes the customer the fool and you the skeptic.

  • The request as it arrived. Who sent it, through whom, the deadline, and the pressure attached. “Forwarded by our account executive on a Thursday, flagged urgent, with a line about the contract.”
  • What you did before building. The questions you asked, and of whom. The strongest are about time and events: what happens if this lands a month later, and what happened recently to make it urgent now.
  • The driver, and how you confirmed it. Check it against the data or the person closest to the work, not only the requester.
  • What you did instead. The smaller thing that met the real driver, and when it shipped.
  • How you told your side, then the requester. The account executive hears the plan first, so nobody on your side is surprised on the call. Then the requester, in their terms: their real deadline, what will be live by then, and the full request still on the table.
  • The result, and what became of the original request.

Quote the question that broke it open, word for word; it is the line in the story you can use again.

Set a clock on the diagnosis, hours or a day, and tell the requester when they will hear back. Know what would have made you start that day.

Follow-ups

What the interviewer may ask next, once your first answer is on the table.

  • What would have told you the request really was urgent?
  • How did the account executive react when you didn’t start building straight away?
  • How long did the diagnosis take, and who was waiting on you while it happened?
  • What would you have done if the VP had said no and insisted on the full feed by month-end?

Where answers go wrong

  • Tells it as the customer being wrong and the speaker being clever, which reads as someone who second-guesses every request.
  • Diagnoses for days before replying, so the requester hears a delay instead of a faster, smaller fix.

Answer this in two minutes

Write the answer you would say out loud. The clock starts with your first word.

Illustrative answer about a fictional project

I was the deployed engineer for a warehouse software vendor. On a Thursday our account executive forwarded an email from a customer’s VP of operations: they needed a real-time inventory feed to their online store “by end of month”, flagged urgent, with a line about reviewing the contract.
