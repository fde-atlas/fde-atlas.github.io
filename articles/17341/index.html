AI-allowed coding interviews: how you're graded

FDE Times · 2026-11-04 · 6 min read

Summary

Anthropic, Meta and some OpenAI FDE rounds allow AI tools. See why the score sits in verification: reading, predicting and testing code before you trust it.

In brief

  • Every new Claude model forces Anthropic to redesign its hiring test. The brief now states that candidates may use AI tools.
  • In AI-enabled rounds at Meta and Google, most of the score sits in verification: whether you read the code before running it, and whether you grow suspicious when the answer looks fine.
  • According to one interview guide, in OpenAI FDE rounds that use Codex, candidates must reason aloud and explain the time and memory costs of the code the tool writes.

Every new Claude model forces Anthropic to redesign its engineering hiring test, Tristan Hume wrote on the company’s engineering blog.

The reason is that the model performs on a par with the strongest candidates. Even so, Anthropic has not banned AI. Its performance engineering take-home states plainly that candidates may use AI tools, just as they would on the job.

This is not confined to one lab. Meta and Google also run coding rounds that allow AI. According to one interview guide, so do some of OpenAI’s Forward Deployed Engineer rounds. In these rounds, the weight of the grade is shifting from the ability to write code to the ability to judge it.

The evidence from Anthropic and Meta concerns engineering hiring in general, not FDEs specifically. For anyone who works as an FDE or wants to, though, it cuts closer, because the job is to put code into a customer’s real systems and run it on real data.

There, a piece of agent-written code that looks right but is wrong has real consequences, well beyond a lost interview point.

When the machine can write, what is left to grade?

An analysis on techinterview.org explains why companies have changed how they score. Interviewers have moved the bar to the one task a model cannot do for you: deciding whether its output is any good.

A Meta speaker, in a piece published on LeadDev, described the company’s new round as an “AI-native” coding test that measures adaptability rather than memory. In this account, the skill being tested has moved from knowing frameworks to catching AI hallucinations.

Knowing a lot is therefore no longer the biggest advantage. The advantage lies in noticing when what you are looking at is wrong, even when it looks entirely convincing.

The behavior being graded is simpler than you think

According to techinterview.org, Meta calls this behavior “critical verification”. Observing it is straightforward: do you read AI-generated code before you run it?

Discussing the AI-enabled coding rounds at Meta and Google, the same site argues that verification accounts for most of the score. The moment you paste an AI suggestion into the editor, the interviewer watches what you do next.

The article describes strong candidates as those who treat the AI like a fast, confident intern who is sometimes wrong. Catching its mistakes during the interview counts as a good signal. The senior-level signal these rounds look for is a candidate who becomes more skeptical precisely when the answer seems fine.

Each company describes it differently, but set the rounds side by side and they grade the same skill from different angles:

OpenAI’s FDE rounds want to hear you state the cost

An OpenAI FDE interview guide on Aced (formerly Exponent) says some coding rounds allow AI. What is assessed is whether you can reason about and verify the code you write with it.

In screens that use Codex, candidates share their screen, reason aloud and explain the time and memory costs of everything the tool produces.

To see what that demands, take a hypothetical exercise. A logistics client hands you an order file of 2 million rows and asks you to merge duplicate customer records by email. The agent immediately writes a function with two nested for loops that compares every pair. You test it on 10 sample rows and the result is entirely correct.

A candidate who only knows how to press run stops there. A candidate with judgment reads the code and names two problems.

The first is that comparing every pair across 2 million rows costs roughly two trillion comparisons. At O(n²), the function will not finish in any acceptable time.

The second is that the email comparison is case-sensitive, so “[email protected]” and “[email protected]” are treated as two different customers.

The fix is not hard. Normalize each email by stripping whitespace and lowercasing it, then group records with a hash map. Time drops to O(n); in exchange, memory is also O(n), because every key must be held in memory.

That is the “explain the cost” part the guide describes. The example shows one more thing: 10 sample rows are usually clean, and a client’s data rarely is, so green tests on a sample prove little.

Anthropic also grades how you choose your tools

According to Hume’s post, Anthropic treats how candidates decide to invest in tooling as part of the hiring signal. Do you stop to write a benchmarking script or a small test suite before letting the agent optimize, or do you let it make blind edits and hope the numbers improve?

FDEs face exactly this question in their first week on a client site. Time is short, and you must choose between doing the work by hand now and building a tool that makes verification faster for the month ahead. Seen this way, Anthropic’s take-home is far closer to real work than a LeetCode problem.

That the test has to change with each new model is also a hint: whatever skill a machine can perform is likely to drop off the grading sheet. Investing in the ability to tell right from wrong is a safer bet than memorizing another framework.

How to practice before your next interview

The most effective practice targets exactly what the interviewer observes.

Whenever an agent produces code, take five steps before trusting it: read every line before running it, say aloud what the code is meant to do, actively look for inputs that break it, state its time and memory costs, then decide to keep, fix or discard it based on evidence from tests or measurements.

Do not press run until the first four steps are done. The fifth is where you show the interviewer what your decision rests on.

When reading FDE job descriptions or interview prep material, watch for phrases such as AI-enabled coding round, Copilot or Codex allowed, or share your screen and explain your reasoning. They signal that the company will grade judgment, and that your time is better spent practicing coding while talking aloud than grinding problems.

On a CV, “used AI to boost productivity” tells a recruiter almost nothing. A stronger line describes one specific occasion: what code the agent generated, what bug you caught before it reached production, and which test or measurement you used to verify it.

The new interview rounds are not looking for people who trust AI, nor for people who reject it. They want people who know to doubt an agent’s code at the moment it looks most perfect, and on a client site, that is also when you are most valuable.
