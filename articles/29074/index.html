What Is Brooks's Law: The Mythical Man-Month and FDE Project Delays

Original title: 布鲁克斯定律是什么：《人月神话》与 FDE 项目延期

FDE Times · 2026-10-28 · 4 min read

Summary

This article uses the 1995 anniversary edition of The Mythical Man-Month to explain why Brooks's Law applies to FDE deployments. Adding people to a late project backfires because new hires consume senior engineers' time while communication channels grow quadratically: a four-person team has 6 channels, six people have 15. The author recommends cutting scope, protecting the engineer who understands the full integration, and watching for the second-system effect after a successful deployment. Cutting scope only works if the client agrees to new milestones; the law also shouldn't be used to reject all help. Suggested reading order and interview advice are included.

Key points

  • Brooks's Law: adding people to a late project delays it further.
  • Communication channels grow quadratically: 6 channels for 4 engineers, 15 for 6.
  • The surgical team model says protect your strongest engineer's focus.
  • The second-system effect threatens post-launch versions with feature bloat.
  • Cutting scope requires renegotiated milestones with the client.

Key Takeaways

  • Brooks's Law: adding people to a delayed software project only makes it later, because newcomers need time to get up to speed and the number of communication channels grows quadratically
  • Recommended reading: the 1995 twentieth-anniversary edition of The Mythical Man-Month, which includes all the original essays, new essays, and "No Silver Bullet"
  • For an FDE, rescuing a delayed deployment usually means cutting scope and letting the people who know the project best focus on the core work, not asking for more people

The most-quoted line from this book: adding manpower to a late software project makes it later. Fred Brooks drew this lesson from his experience managing System/360 and OS/360 at IBM.

Fifty years later, the line still holds where FDEs often find themselves: in a client's conference room, the deadline has already been missed, and someone suggests "sending two more people over."

As a Forward Deployed Engineer (FDE), you will sooner or later sit in such a meeting. The deployment is three weeks late, the client is impatient, and the client's team wants to add people as a show of good faith. The Mythical Man-Month explains why this instinct often backfires, and gives you the numbers you need to push back.

The recommended edition is the Anniversary Edition (Addison-Wesley, 1995). According to Pearson, this edition preserves the essays from the original 1975 edition unchanged, adds new essays re-examining software project management, and includes "No Silver Bullet" (1986).

Brooks's Law: there are two costs, not one

Brooks identified two reasons. The first is easy to see: newcomers need time to get up to speed, and during that time they consume the hours of the very people who know the project best. The second is less obvious: every additional person adds several communication lines.

The book gives the formula n(n − 1)/2 for the number of communication channels in a team. Try it on a mid-sized project: four engineers have 4 × 3 / 2 = 6 channels. Add two more to make six, and it becomes 6 × 5 / 2 = 15. Headcount grows by 50%, but channels grow to 2.5 times. With 50 programmers, the number is 1,225.

Now put this math into a hypothetical scenario. You are deploying a data pipeline for a client; the project is three weeks late, and the company sends two new engineers.

In the first week, you sit there explaining the client's schema, the process for requesting permissions, and why one particular table must never be touched. In the second week, they start writing code, but every decision now has to travel through 15 channels instead of 6.

By the third week, the project hasn't recovered a single day.

So at the client's site, the first thing to do is bring this math back into the meeting and propose an alternative: cut whatever the next milestone doesn't need, and keep the existing team focused.

The Surgical Team: concentrate force around the strongest person

The second idea worth remembering is the "surgical team." One highly skilled chief programmer personally writes the critical parts, while everyone else plays a supporting role. Brooks pairs this model with a blunt observation: "great" programmers are five to ten times more productive than average ones.

Many FDE projects already work this way, just without anyone naming it: one person holds the full picture of the client's context and the integration architecture. Brooks's lesson is: protect that person.

The five-to-ten-times figure is a general observation about the spread between programmers, not a measurement of any individual; but if the chief engineer spends half the day repeatedly explaining things to newcomers, what's being wasted is precisely the person the whole project depends on.

The Second-System Effect: the second version is the most dangerous

The third idea applies after go-live. Brooks warns that an architect's second system tends to be stuffed with all the features deferred from the first. He calls it the most dangerous system of an designer's career.

For an FDE, this trap usually appears right after the first successful deployment. The client is happy, your confidence is up, and suddenly the "next version" list becomes the scope of the next version. Re-read that list and ask, item by item: does it solve the client's problem, or is it just emotional debt left over from last time?

Cutting scope can also go wrong

Once people understand Brooks's Law, many stumble at the next step. The most common mistake is cutting scope internally without agreeing on new milestones with the client. The team thinks it did the right thing, but the client still expects all the features by the original date, and by delivery day, the trust is gone.

The second mistake is using the law as an excuse to reject all help. Brooks was talking about newcomers who need ramp-up time and added communication channels, so the question to ask is: will the people being sent add to either of those?

The safe approach is to write it down: what gets cut, what the new milestone dates are, get the client to confirm, and only then adjust the team's plan.

Who should read it, and in what order?

The book suits engineers with two or more years of experience, especially those starting to own projects or needing to discuss timelines with clients. InformIT says few books in software project management have been so influential and enduring. Even so, don't treat it as a textbook; many of its examples come from the mainframe era.

Recommended order: first read the essay on the law and the communication formula, then the surgical team, then the second-system effect. Save "No Silver Bullet" and the new 1995 material for last, to see how Brooks reassessed his own work.

When preparing for an FDE interview, don't just quote Brooks's Law. Tell a story about a time you refused more people and cut scope instead: how late the project was, what you cut, and which milestone you hit. Put all three points in your resume, with concrete numbers for the weeks of delay and the milestone achieved.

A formula from 1975 won't make the client's project go faster. But it gives you ample reason not to make it slower.
