DS vs FDE | An 8-Axis Comparison of Roles, Responsibilities, and Success Definitions, and Why You Should Use Them Together

Summary

This FDX Corporation article compares Deployment Strategists (DS) and Forward Deployed Engineers (FDE) across eight axes: purpose, deliverables, KPIs, skills, contract type, engagement period, reporting line, and exit criteria. DS decides what to build and owns investment decisions; FDE implements working systems. Drawing on Palantir, OpenAI, Anthropic, and Scale AI practices, it argues the two roles should generally be staffed separately, hired DS-first, and bundled in paired contracts to avoid scattered pilots and wasted budgets.

Key points

  • DS defines what to build; FDE implements how to build it — complementary, not competing roles.
  • Eight-axis comparison covers purpose, KPIs, skills, contracts, reporting lines, and exit conditions.
  • Palantir, OpenAI, and Anthropic separate the roles; Scale AI uses a hybrid due to homogeneous products.
  • Hire DS first: FDE-only staffing leads to scattered PoCs and no exit judgment.
  • Three common misconceptions: DS as PM, FDE as seconded SE, and one person doing both.

Summary (100 words): The DS decides "what to build," while the FDE implements "how to build it." The two roles are complementary, not interchangeable—adopting only one makes AI implementation almost certain to fail. This article clarifies the differences between the two roles through an 8-axis comparison and organizes how to combine them.

Who this article is for

  • Executives and HR leaders making hiring decisions on AI implementation partners
  • AX promotion leads considering whether to hire a DS or an FDE first, or both
  • Corporate planners wondering whether existing PMs or former strategy consultants could cover the role
  • CTO offices and corporate strategy teams looking to reference the go-to-market organization design of overseas startups

Reading "What is a Deployment Strategist (DS)?" and "What is a Forward Deployed Engineer (FDE)?" beforehand will deepen your understanding.

Conclusion: the DS handles "what to build," the FDE handles "how to build it"

  • DS and FDE are complementary roles, not an adversarial structure. The DS owns the investment decisions and success definitions—"why build it" and "what counts as results"—while the FDE implements them into a working system.
  • The two roles are separated because strategy decision-making and technical implementation require very different skill sets. If one person tries to handle both, either executive dialogue or implementation will end up under-resourced.
  • The hiring order should, in principle, be DS first, then FDE. Deploying only an FDE without a DS leads to mass production of implementations that never fit the business, burning through budget without any ability to make exit decisions.

Each axis is explained in turn below.

Axis 1: Primary purpose

The DS's primary purpose is achieving ROI on AI investment and driving decision-making. The DS creates the material that lets executives decide "go / no-go" and "scale up / exit," and drives decisions to a conclusion in decision meetings.

The FDE's primary purpose is getting software that fits the business up and running. Merely working to spec is not enough; the deliverable is a state where the frontline actually uses it in daily operations.

Axis 2: Definition of results

The DS's results are KPI improvements that can be translated into an ROI story, with the production go-live decision and completion of in-house handover as milestones.

The FDE's results are a working system, production operation, and a self-sustaining handover. Writing code alone does not count as a result.

Axis 3: KPIs

The DS's evaluation metrics are magnitude of business KPI improvement and accuracy of exit decisions. The improvement magnitude varies by target business—for example, reducing average response time by X% in customer support, or cutting defect rates by Y% in manufacturing. Accuracy of exit decisions is measured by whether projects that should be exited are exited, and projects that should be scaled are scaled.

The FDE's evaluation metrics are utilization rate / business adoption rate / modification lead time. Whether the deployed system is actually used in the field, whether end users touch it daily, and how short the time from a modification request to production deployment is.

Axis 4: Skill requirements

The DS spans three areas: business understanding × KPI design × executive dialogue. Business understanding means domain experience (finance / manufacturing / healthcare, etc.), KPI design means the issue-framing skills of a strategy consultant, and executive dialogue means experience discussing logically with executives. Coding is not required, but the DS should be able to read code.

The FDE spans three areas: business understanding × technical implementation × operations design. Business understanding can be somewhat shallower than the DS's, but technical implementation must be at a level of writing Python / TypeScript / SQL intensively in production work. Operations design requires the ability to design monitoring, alerting, and SLI / SLO (see: Integrating FDEs into your organization | Skill map and evaluation criteria).

Axis 5: Contract type

The DS is usually engaged under a hybrid contract of outcome-linked (KPI achievement) + fixed monthly fee. Success fees vary with the degree of KPI achievement. This is because the DS is a role accountable for "delivering results," which doesn't fit an hourly billing model.

The FDE is usually engaged under fixed monthly fee + outcome incentives. Incentives are added per milestone, such as achieving production go-live or completing the self-sustaining handover. It is monthly-fee based, but not the "per person-month rate" of an SES-style arrangement.

Axis 6: Engagement period

Both roles run 6–18 months, but the peak of engagement differs. The DS is heavier in the early phase (Define / Decompose) and the late phase (Decentralize). The FDE is heavier in the middle phase (Build / Run). See the diagram below.

Axis 7: Reporting line

The DS reports directly to the client's executives / business owners. The monthly executive report is chaired by the DS.

The FDE reports to the client's frontline leaders / in-house team leads. Weekly steering is run between the FDE and the frontline leader.

If the boundary between the two becomes blurred, a double disconnect occurs: frontline voices fail to reach executives, and executive decisions fail to reach the frontline.

Axis 8: Exit conditions

The DS's exit conditions are KPI achievement + completion of in-house decision-maker development. You cannot withdraw unless the customer side has developed the "next DS."

The FDE's exit conditions are completion of maintenance and extension by in-house engineers. The achievement criterion is a state where the customer's engineers can independently handle extension and modification.

Role division at Palantir / OpenAI / Anthropic

Palantir: the birthplace of DS and FDE

At Palantir, DS and FDE are paired on a single project. Since its founding, the company has championed the "Forward Deployed Team" that is deeply tied to clients. With products like Foundry / Gotham—general-purpose packages requiring customization—sales are impossible unless both the DS's "translation ability" and the FDE's "implementation ability" work in tandem.

Both roles have a culture of physically stationing at client offices, and in some projects the DS's on-site time is longer than the FDE's.

OpenAI: Deployment / GTM Engineering

At OpenAI, similar roles exist under the names Deployment Engineering / GTM Engineering, supporting business integration of ChatGPT Enterprise / API for enterprise customers. DS-equivalent roles are sometimes operated under titles like Customer Engineer or Solutions Architect.

Anthropic: Deployment Strategist job postings

Anthropic explicitly posts job openings under the role name "Deployment Strategist." It is a role that leads the business integration of Claude on the customer side, designed to run alongside Forward Deployed Engineers / Customer Engineers.

Scale AI: Forward Deployed Solutions Engineer

Scale AI employs an integrated role called Forward Deployed Solutions Engineer, expressing the DS + FDE hybrid talent as a single person. This is because Scale AI's product characteristics (a data labeling platform) are relatively homogeneous, and per-project customization is shallower than at other companies, so there is little benefit in separating the roles.

For detailed overseas examples, see "The Reality of the FDE Model as Seen in Palantir / OpenAI / Scale AI".

Three patterns Japanese companies tend to confuse

Confusion 1: mistaking "DS = a really good PM"

A PM is a role that advances a set plan on schedule. A DS is a role that creates the premises of the plan and has the authority to change the plan if necessary. The subject of decision-making is different.

To convert a PM-background person into a DS, you need to reinforce issue-framing ability and experience in executive dialogue. Conversely, placing a strategy consulting background person as a PM leads to failures where excessive issue-raising stalls project progress.

Confusion 2: mistaking "FDE = seconded SE"

SES and seconded SEs implement according to specifications. An FDE is a role that creates the specifications themselves through dialogue with the business. Because they get involved on-site before requirements are even fixed, both the volume of dialogue with business stakeholders and the depth of understanding of the business domain are incomparable to SES.

The FDE skill set is a fusion of "senior engineer + product manager + domain expert," and the standard salary range is 2–3 times that of SES (see: FDE vs SES vs SI vs Strategy Consulting | A Thorough Comparison of 4 Models).

Confusion 3: mistaking "DS and FDE can be done by the same person"

At Palantir / Anthropic / OpenAI, DS and FDE are in principle separated. The reason is competition for time. If you spend time on dialogue with executives, you cannot concentrate on implementation; if you immerse yourself in implementation, monthly executive reporting gets neglected. The more you try to do both, the more half-baked each becomes.

The exception is companies selling homogeneous products like Scale AI, but in enterprise AI implementation, separation is almost always the right answer.

Examples of contract structures combining both

Pattern A: DS + FDE pair placement (Palantir type)

  • 1 DS + 2–3 FDEs provided as one set
  • Contract period of 6–12 months
  • Fixed monthly fee + KPI achievement incentive
  • Exit conditions explicitly stated in the contract

The most costly, but with the highest success rate. The mainstream for enterprises.

Pattern B: DS-only placement + spot FDE

  • 1 DS placed for 12–18 months
  • FDEs procured on a spot basis from multiple companies only during the implementation phase
  • The DS handles FDE selection and oversight

For mid-sized projects. The DS's workload is heavy, and a capable DS is a prerequisite.

Pattern C: spot DS + in-house FDE

  • External DS deployed intensively for 3–6 months
  • FDEs are covered by in-house engineers
  • The DS mentors in-house FDE candidates while leading KPI design

For companies with a strong in-house development orientation. It can accelerate turning in-house engineers into DS / FDEs, but the ramp-up is slow.

FAQ

Q1. Can one person serve as both DS and FDE?

It is possible in exceptional cases, but not recommended. The reason nearly all leading companies separate the two roles is as explained in the previous chapter's "3 patterns Japanese companies tend to confuse" (confusion pattern 3): the time allocation between executive dialogue and implementation simply cannot be reconciled.

Only when a small startup faces severe budget constraints may a senior person take on both roles. Even then, it rarely lasts more than 3 months.

Q2. Who does the DS report to?

On the customer side, the business owner (CXO / head of the business division). On the delivery side (the implementation partner, such as FDX), a senior DS in the role of Engagement Lead or Practice Director oversees them. An FDE field lead never becomes the DS's manager.

Q3. Is there a career path from FDE to DS?

It exists, but only for a few. Moving from FDE to DS requires strengthening executive dialogue skills and issue-framing skills, along with 12 to 24 months of deliberate training. The reverse, DS becoming FDE, almost never happens (the skill sets do not match).

Palantir has a promotion path of FDE → Engagement Lead → DS. A similar design can work at Japanese companies as well.

Q4. Which should be hired first, DS or FDE?

The DS should be hired first. Hiring only an FDE without a DS leaves "what to build" undecided and PoCs proliferate. Hiring the DS first makes KPI design and production-readiness decisions function, and FDE hiring decisions also become more accurate.

However, in specific domains where what to build is already clear (e.g., RPA-izing routine tasks), starting with an FDE can also work.

Q5. How large is the compensation gap between the two?

At foreign AI companies, the DS is somewhat higher paid than the FDE (base salary + performance-linked pay). In Japanese in-house models, the ratio is roughly DS : FDE = 1.3 to 1.5 : 1. This reflects the difference in scarcity of the skill sets.

However, senior FDEs (Principal FDEs) can sometimes earn compensation equal to or above that of a DS.

Q6. How should a combination of in-house and outsourced be designed?

Representative examples are as follows.

  • External DS (full-time) + external FDE (full-time) → Pattern A
  • External DS (full-time) + in-house FDE (part-time) → launch phase
  • In-house DS (full-time) + external FDE (per project) → in-house maturity phase

Full in-housing is often achieved only 24 months or more after the start of AX initiatives. Aiming for full in-housing from the start makes the ramp-up far too slow.

Q7. How should reviews of the DS / FDE be designed?

For the DS, a quarterly ROI review (business KPI improvement / accuracy of exit decisions / in-housing progress). For the FDE, a monthly operations review (uptime / business adoption rate / incident response). If both are judged on the same evaluation axis, one will inevitably look worse.

FDX's DS + FDE hybrid offering

FDX Inc. offers the DS + FDE pair placement (Pattern A) as its standard offering.

  • Team composition of 1 DS + 2 to 3 FDEs
  • Monthly executive reporting (led by the DS) + weekly steering (led by the FDE)
  • Performance-based compensation linked to KPI achievement
  • Handover to in-house operations in 6 to 12 months → contract ends
  • Exit conditions explicitly stated in the contract (self-sufficiency completed / review triggered by missed KPIs)

Ordering "DS only" or "FDE only" under separate contracts weakens collaboration between the two and makes results harder to achieve. Bundling them as a pair into a single contract package clarifies accountability.
