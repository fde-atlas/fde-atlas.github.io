Practical Techniques to Avoid Failure in FDE Requirements Definition

Original title: FDE要件定義で失敗しない実践テクニック

日本Fde協会 · 2026-08-01 · 5 min read

Summary

This article by the Japan FDE Association argues that failures in requirements definition for forward deployed engineers stem mostly from how engineers ask questions and run projects, not from technical skill. It describes common failure patterns—building exactly what customers ask for, and waiting for a perfect spec document—and shares field-tested techniques: challenging whether requirements are truly needed, using prototypes to surface unstated assumptions, probing the reasons behind workflows, and incorporating access control, audit trails, and documentation that survives staff turnover into requirements from the start.

Key points

  • Stated customer requirements are often pre-baked solutions; verify the underlying problem first.
  • Show a working prototype early instead of perfecting a written spec before building.
  • Cutting requirements is part of the job; back decisions with ROI, cost, and effort.
  • Design permissions and work logging during requirements definition to avoid rework.
  • Write documents anyone can understand so systems survive personnel changes.

"We created a requirements document, but the finished product never gets used in the field"—failures like this are not uncommon. We, the Japan FDE Association, are an organization of practitioners of FDE (Forward Deployed Engineer—engineers who embed themselves in customers' sites and solve problems from both technical and business perspectives). By reading this article, you will learn the common failure patterns in FDE requirements definition and the practical techniques we actually use in the field to avoid them, so you can apply them to your own projects right away. Rather than steering you toward specific tools or vendors, we plainly share the mindset you will need no matter whom you work with.

Common Failure Patterns in FDE Requirements Definition

Failures in requirements definition are almost always caused not by a lack of technical skill, but by "how you ask" and "how you proceed." Let's start by covering three typical patterns.

Building exactly what was asked for

When a customer says "we want this kind of feature," it is often already the customer's own solution. If you build it without confirming the real problem behind it, you end up with a system that works but is never used. When our Representative Director Maekawa moved into the e-commerce business, he had technical skills but lacked marketing knowledge, and sales were nearly zero for the first six months. His experience of growing the business only after abandoning the assumption that "a good product will sell itself" and conducting thorough customer interviews underpins our stance of never taking requirements at face value.

Waiting for a perfect requirements document

If you try to nail down every requirement on paper before starting to build, misalignments in understanding won't surface until the very end. In FDE practice, we value an approach of showing a working product as quickly as possible to gather feedback. A screen you can actually touch exposes misunderstandings far faster than specifications agreed upon in writing.

Practical Requirements Definition Techniques Used by FDEs

Here we introduce concrete methods that have proven effective in actually running projects.

Start with "Do you really need that?"

Reducing requirements is also part of requirements definition. A common reaction we hear from people who work with FDEs is: "It was eye-opening when they asked me right at the start, 'Do you really need that?' They stopped us from building something unnecessary." Deciding not to build is a choice to direct limited resources toward what will truly make an impact. Backing proposals with numbers—ROI, cost, and effort—keeps this line-drawing rational.

Getting agreement through prototypes

At a manufacturing client's site, showing a single simple screen surfaced a flood of feedback like "this doesn't match how our operations actually work," far more effectively than hammering out shipping and receiving forms on paper specifications. When people get to touch something that works, implicit rules that were never verbalized come to the surface. It helps to think of a prototype not as a finished product but as a tool for aligning understanding. For concrete methods, also see FDE Prototyping Techniques.

How to ask questions that draw out the background of a problem

We sometimes receive reactions like "they know our business better than our own employees." This is not a special talent—it simply comes from checking each step of the workflow one by one and digging into why each procedure exists. We have organized our interview framework in detail in FDE Interview Techniques.

The easily overlooked requirements of "governance and person-dependency"

Typical market explanations of requirements definition stop at features and business flows. However, what gets scrutinized in a production rollout is what lies beyond that.

Decide who can access what from the very beginning

If permission design and how work records are kept are not built into the requirements definition stage, you will end up rebuilding later. Especially in systems that span departments, being careful not to break existing permission structures builds trust. When talking with SaaS company executives, we often hear that while they can build features, they lack people who can design to withstand this kind of audit.

Design so things keep running even when the person in charge changes

Requirements documents tend to become understandable only to the person who wrote them. From experience at a startup: systems that depend on one person during the founding phase grind to a halt during handover the moment the team grows. Leaving behind documentation that anyone can read and make decisions from is also work that should be included in requirements definition. We believe that whether you can incorporate this perspective of governance and preventing person-dependency into requirements definition is what separates strong FDEs from the rest. We have summarized the basic framework of requirements definition in Avoiding Failure in Requirements Definition | 5 Things FDEs Do.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q. Shouldn't I create a perfect requirements document first?

A. Rather than aiming for perfection, it is more reliable to show a working product early and eliminate misalignments in understanding. We recommend keeping the specification document as a record of agreements while solidifying requirements through prototype-based validation.

Q. Can't governance and permission requirements be added later?

A. This is an area where retrofitting tends to lead to rework. Including who can access what and how work records are kept in the requirements at the same time as feature design reduces rework.

Q. What should we do if we have no FDE-like talent in-house?

A. Both training and external support are options. You can use the FDE certification as a learning goal for existing members, and we also accept inquiries about project-based support and corporate training.
