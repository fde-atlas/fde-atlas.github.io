What does "FDE 50% travel" mean? Forward Deployed Engineer travel requirements

Original title: FDE 出差 50% 是什么意思？前线部署工程师出差要求

FDE Times · 2026-11-11 · 5 min read

Summary

This article explains what travel percentages in FDE job postings actually mean. Citing Bloomberry data from about 1,000 postings (68% mention travel, commonly 25%-50%), it argues the figure is never spread evenly across weeks but clusters around project phases, especially build and integration, which can mean consecutive weeks on-site. It distinguishes local embedded roles, like Palantir's four-to-five-days-per-week postings in Colorado Springs, from multi-city travel roles, and offers a method: convert percentages into per-week schedules and ask interviewers about the longest recent deployment rather than policy. It also notes build-focused FDEs still spend 70%-90% of time coding.

Key points

  • 68% of FDE postings mention travel; 25%-50% is common, higher in government, defense, healthcare, and industrial sectors.
  • Travel clusters in the build and integration phase, sometimes three consecutive weeks away.
  • Local embedded roles and multi-city travel roles differ; a posting without a percentage may require more on-site time.
  • Convert percentages into day counts per project phase before accepting an offer.
  • Build-focused FDEs spend 70%-90% of time coding while traveling 30%-50%.

Key takeaways

  • Travel for FDE roles typically runs around 25%-50%, and is higher in government, defense, healthcare, and industrial sectors.
  • Travel clusters by project phase, with the longest stretch usually falling in the build and integration phase.
  • Being on-site four to five days a week in the same region is not the same as flying to different locations; keep the two apart when reading a job posting.

You open an FDE job posting and see a line saying "up to 50% travel." Your first reaction is probably: does that mean two and a half days away from home every week? In reality, almost never. That percentage is just an extremely uneven work schedule compressed into a single line.

Misreading that line hurts you in both directions. Some people accept an offer, only to discover they'll be at a client's factory for three straight weeks.

Others miss out on a great role because they assume "50%" means half their life on the road. What's needed here is a concrete skill: reading the travel requirements, converting them into days, and asking the right questions before signing.

How much travel is normal?

Bloomberry analyzed roughly 1,000 FDE job postings and found that 68% of roles involve travel, with 50%-75% travel ratios fairly common. By comparison, the equivalent figure for Sales Engineers is only about 20%-40%. FDE Academy puts the numbers lower, at roughly 25% to 50% overall, depending on the company and industry.

Specific postings fall within this range too. According to Uvik's FDE index, OpenAI's roles list travel at up to 50%, Vercel at 25%-40%, Striim at up to 50%, and GWI at around 30%-50%. FDE Academy also notes that deployment projects serving government, defense, healthcare, and industrial clients tend to sit at the high end.

One caveat, though. Uvik's conclusion is that travel is not a universal feature of FDE roles; only about a third of the postings it surveyed listed travel as a significant factor.

That's far from Bloomberry's 68%, and the reason is that the two measure different things.

So a posting that "mentions travel" isn't necessarily a travel-centric role. Telling the two apart requires careful reading.

On-site work and travel are two different things

Palantir's posting for Forward Deployed Engineers (FDE) for its US government business is a typical example. It requires candidates to work at the client's location four to five days a week, mainly in and around Colorado Springs. Palantir also writes that you'll work with clients, often directly on-site, to understand and solve their problems.

Measured by time spent with the client, this is nearly a full-time on-site role. But because the client is concentrated in one region, you may still get home every night.

The same posting only vaguely says you need to be willing to travel as needed, without giving any percentage. In other words, a role with no stated travel ratio may keep you at the client's site far more than one that says "40% travel."

Conversely, that 40% role might involve fewer days at the client's site, but require you to fly between several cities and stay in hotels.

So when reading a job posting, separate two dimensions: how long you'll be at the client's site, and how far that site is from your home.

Travel comes in bursts, not weekly averages

FDE Academy suggests understanding travel as high-intensity concentrated periods tied to project phases. The longest on-site stretches usually occur during the build and integration phase, lasting anywhere from a few days to several weeks.

The reason is simple: this is the phase where you need access to real systems, real data, and real operators.

Bloomberry describes FDEs as long-term embedded partners with clients, often staying on-site for weeks at a time. It also offers a simple test for distinguishing FDEs from adjacent roles: if you spent six weeks at a client fixing production bugs, you're a Forward Deployed Engineer.

In other words, long stretches at the client's site aren't a side effect of the job. For many companies, that is the job.

Converting "40%" into a real schedule

Suppose you take on a 12-week project with a 40% travel requirement. Twelve weeks is 60 working days, and 40% means 24 days at the client. The table below shows one hypothetical allocation, based on the rhythm FDE Academy describes.

WeekPhaseLocationOn-site days
1Requirements researchClient site4
2-3Building a first version with sample dataRemote0
4-6Building and integrating with real systemsClient site15
7-10Polishing, evals, bug fixesRemote0
11LaunchClient site3
12HandoverClient site2

That totals 24 days, exactly 40%. But what actually affects your life lies elsewhere: weeks 4 through 6 are three consecutive weeks away from home, while weeks 2-3 and 7-10 you're fully at home.

If you have young children, or evening classes, what you need to negotiate is those three consecutive weeks; the 40% figure tells you nothing about that.

Do this conversion for every posting that interests you. If the posting doesn't say how long a project lasts, that's the first question to bring into the interview.

Step by step: reading and clarifying travel requirements

First, highlight three pieces of information in the posting: the ratio or number of days, the client's location, and the industry. If the role is in government, defense, healthcare, or industrial sectors, prepare for the high end. Next, assume a project of average length and convert the ratio into days, as in the table above.

Then, go into the interview with questions about actual practice, not policy. "What was the team's longest on-site stretch last quarter?" The answer you get will be far more honest than "Is there a lot of travel?"

Also ask how long a project typically lasts, and which phases require the whole team on-site.

Finally, your resume. Show that you've delivered results in front of clients, not just vaguely "collaborated with clients." A line like "On-site for three weeks, integrated a data pipeline into the client's ERP system, resolved production bugs alongside the ops team" carries far more information.

Bloomberry notes that "builder FDEs" spend 70%-90% of their time writing code while traveling 30%-50%, meaning travel doesn't replace coding. You still write code; you just write it sitting next to the user.

Common mistakes

The most common mistake is spreading the percentage evenly across weeks and planning your personal life around that. The second is interpreting "travel as needed" as rarely traveling. Palantir's posting says exactly that, while the same posting requires four to five days a week at the client's site.

The third mistake is conflating a local on-site role with a remote-friendly role that only requires an occasional flight. The fourth is being afraid to ask about travel for fear of seeming uncommitted. The opposite is true: candidates who ask specifically about project rhythm show they understand how the job works.

The travel number in a job posting is just the starting point of the conversation. Someone who knows the field will convert it into a week-by-week schedule, and then decide whether to take the job.
