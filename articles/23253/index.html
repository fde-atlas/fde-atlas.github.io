Summary

This article by BizDev Tech (run by Japan's DeFactory) examines why warnings against becoming a freelance engineer are valid. It acknowledges real risks—income gaps between contracts, weak social credit for loans and housing, heavy taxes and insurance costs, skill obsolescence, and isolation—illustrated through three failure cases: a Java engineer stranded in legacy work, a developer bankrupted by unpaid taxes, and one hospitalized from overwork. Calculations show a freelancer earning ¥8.8M keeps about ¥5.8M, roughly matching a salaried employee making ¥6.5–7M, so revenue should be 1.5x prior salary. Success factors include a business-owner mindset, choosing agents with shallow supply chains, and updating skills toward AI and cloud. The piece ends by promoting BizDev Tech's direct-client, high-rate placements.

Key points

  • Confirms freelance risks: income gaps, poor credit access, heavy taxes, skill decay, isolation.
  • Three failure cases: legacy-tech obsolescence, tax-driven bankruptcy, overwork breakdown.
  • ¥8.8M revenue yields ~¥5.8M take-home, equal to a ¥6.5–7M salaried employee.
  • Rule of thumb: freelance revenue must be 1.5x previous salary to justify the risk.
  • Concludes with promotion of BizDev Tech's direct-client placements and free consultations.

"I want to become a freelance engineer. But there are so many voices saying 'don't do it' that my legs freeze up." "What if I go independent, fail, can't find re-employment, and end up in a miserable situation..."

If you have just typed "freelance engineer, don't do it" into your smartphone's search bar and landed on this article, first let me commend your excellent risk management skills.

That's because far too many engineers go independent on impulse without thinking things through and end up in truly irreversible situations.

The internet is flooded with negative information about freelancing. "Projects can suddenly disappear." "You'll go bankrupt from taxes." "Your social credibility drops to zero." To state the conclusion upfront: all of this information is fact, and these are things that can actually happen.

However, at the same time, "don't go to the battlefield unprepared and unarmed" is also the honest truth from those of us who support engineers.

Conversely, with a correct understanding of the market, a survival strategy, and a trustworthy partner, being a freelance engineer can become "the ultimate career path," offering a level of income and work freedom unimaginable during your days as a company employee.

In this article, "BizDev Tech," which has supported the careers of countless engineers, will explain the following truths without holding anything back, from the professional perspective of someone who knows the industry's inner workings.

- Why do people say "don't do it"? 5 harsh realities - A concrete documentary of the "miserable endings" caused by lack of preparation - [Independent estimate] What's the take-home pay on a 10-million-yen annual income? The cold numbers of taxes and insurance - The decisive "fork in the road" between engineers who succeed and those who fail - A concrete roadmap for minimizing risk as close to zero as possible

By the time you finish reading this article, your vague "anxiety" should have transformed into a "strategy" you can start executing tomorrow.

There are clear structural reasons why the keyword "freelance engineer, don't do it" flies around on search engines and social media.

This is not merely one person's opinion; it is the true nature of the "risks" inherent in freelancing.

Let's start by knowing the enemy.

High-rate, direct-contract project information

The greatest advantage of being a company employee is that "if you show up at the office tomorrow, there's work, and you get paid."

However, the moment you become a freelancer, this guarantee disappears.

The most frightening moment is when a project ends (contract expiration). If your next project isn't lined up, next month's income will mercilessly drop to "zero."

In particular, engineers with weak sales skills who fail to choose the right agent face the reality of only being able to land "deeply layered projects (multi-tier subcontracted projects)" where they are bought out at undercut rates.

As a result, they suffer from the contradiction of "being busier than when I was a company employee, yet taking home less pay."

Japan's financial and real estate systems are still designed to favor "full-time employees." Compare "a freelancer earning 10 million yen a year" with "a full-time employee earning 4 million yen a year." In mortgage screening, the overwhelming majority of banks trust the latter.

- Getting rejected for credit cards - Being denied in rental apartment screening - Being unable to get a mortgage, or facing higher interest rates

Even after going independent, when engineers decide "I want to buy a house" and are turned away at the door, regretting "I shouldn't have done it," the cases never stop coming.

Once you become a freelancer, you must fully bear the costs of the "employees' pension" and "health insurance" that your company used to split with you (National Pension and National Health Insurance). On top of that, there are no paid holidays and no sickness benefits.

Also, the annual "tax filing" season that arrives every February to March is a major source of stress for engineers. With the introduction of the invoice system, administrative work has become even more complicated. The burden of handling accounting, tax, and legal matters all by yourself, on top of your actual development work, is heavier than you can imagine. The lament "my hourly rate ended up lower than a part-timer's" arises precisely because these "hidden costs" weren't factored in.

Companies invest in education for their own employees as "investment in the future," encouraging them to learn new technologies. However, what they demand from freelancers is only "immediate, ready-to-use capability."

So what happens as a result? Freelancers tend to end up selling off slices of "what they can do right now." If you join a Java 7 maintenance project, you'll face old code day after day. Unless you proactively keep up with the latest technologies (Go, Rust, AWS, AI, etc.), your skill set will become obsolete, and in three years you'll be weeded out as "an engineer with no market value."

When trouble strikes, there's no boss to protect you. If you introduce a bug that causes damage, you alone stand on the front line of responsibility. Moreover, now that remote work is widespread, an environment where you silently write code all day without speaking to anyone amplifies feelings of isolation. It is by no means rare for engineers to suffer mental health breakdowns and quit due to the stress of "having no one to consult with" and "being unable to share vague anxieties about the future."

Here, to help you visualize this more deeply and realistically, we present "failure cases" we have actually witnessed in the industry, in documentary form. These were not especially "incompetent people." They started out smoothly and full of confidence. Just a slight bit of "carelessness" and "lack of preparation" threw their gears out of alignment.

[At the Start of Independence: A Smooth Takeoff] Mr. A (then 35) worked at a mid-tier SIer developing business systems in Java. His diligent character was well regarded, and after going independent he joined a maintenance and operations project at a monthly rate of 700,000 yen. Delighted at "nearly double his take-home pay as a company employee," he believed he had succeeded.

[Signs of the Fall: Sinking into Lukewarm Water] The project he joined was technically outdated (legacy frameworks like Struts) and unstimulating, but the working relationships were good and overtime was nearly zero. "This is fine as it is." Thinking this, Mr. A stopped learning new technologies and stayed on the same project for five years. Meanwhile, the world had shifted dramatically toward cloud and modern web development.

[The End: Sudden Contract Termination and Rejection by the Market] One day when Mr. A turned 40, the client told him: "We are completely overhauling the system and rebuilding it with AWS and Go. Accordingly, the current maintenance team will be disbanded at the end of this month." Pale-faced, Mr. A hurriedly contacted his agent, but the reply was despairing. "With your background (legacy Java only, no leadership experience), honestly, it's difficult to find projects we can introduce you to." Even after dropping his rate to 500,000 yen, he couldn't even get interviews. His savings ran out, and when he swallowed his pride and looked for regular employment, he was turned away at the door: "With that skill set in your 40s..." He has now left the IT industry and makes a living working part-time in delivery.

Analysis: What Went Wrong? Mr. A's failure was "staying on the same project too long." For a freelancer, remaining with the same technology and environment for more than two years means the death of your market value. He needed a strategy of periodically changing projects and forcibly updating his skills.

[At the Start of Independence: The Illusion of a High-Rise Condo and a Luxury Car] Mr. B (26) spent two years at a web production company before going freelance. Luckily, he landed a project with a shallow intermediary chain, and his monthly income reached 850,000 yen—equivalent to an annual income of 10 million yen. "I'm a winner." Having made big money young, he moved into a high-rise condo in central Tokyo, bought a luxury watch on credit, and went out drinking nearly every night.

[Signs of the Fall: Cash Flow Collapse Born of Ignorance] 850,000 yen was deposited into his account every month. He mistakenly believed all of it was "money he could spend." He knew intellectually that freelancers are obligated to pay "taxes" and "insurance premiums" out of revenue, but he had never truly internalized it. He filed his tax return carelessly and didn't even hire an accountant.

[The End: The Red Notice (Demand Letter) and a Hell of Debt] June of his second year of independence. Seeing the resident tax notice that arrived in his mailbox, Mr. B collapsed to his knees. Then came the national health insurance premium and estimated tax prepayment notices, piling on further. The total billed came to several million yen. But only a few hundred thousand yen remained in his account. The worst kind of hand-to-mouth operation began: "borrowing on a card loan to pay taxes." Chasing money distracted him from work, mistakes piled up on the job, he lost trust and had his contract terminated. All that remained was several million yen in debt and the reality of personal bankruptcy.

Analysis: What Went Wrong? "Deposit amount = salary" is not true. At least 30-40% of deposits needed to be set aside in a separate account for next year's taxes. Taxes come later, when you've forgotten all about them, without mercy.

[At the Start of Independence: High Workload Born of a Sense of Responsibility] Ms. C (32) was an excellent engineer specializing in React/Vue.js. After going independent, she joined a new development project at a startup. The client trusted her deeply, and tasks came her way one after another: "We'd like Ms. C to handle this." Trying to live up to expectations, she kept writing code through weekends as well.

[Signs of the Fall: A Battlefield Without the Protection of Labor Law] As a company employee, alarms go off over excessive work. But a freelancer is a sole proprietor, and there are malicious clients who think they can "make you work as much as they want." Even when specification changes came down late at night, Ms. C couldn't refuse, saying "it's in the contract." Insomnia, loss of appetite, trembling hands. She masked her body's SOS signals with caffeine.

[The End: A Doctor's Order and the Terror of Zero Income] One morning, she couldn't get out of bed. The diagnosis: "adjustment disorder" and "severe autonomic nervous dysfunction." The doctor ordered immediate rest. But the moment she rested, her income dropped to zero. There was no sickness benefit either. The rent on her apartment, where she lived alone, and medical expenses ate away at her savings. She repeatedly tried to return to work too soon and relapsed, ultimately moving back to her parents' home. Her career was completely severed.

Analysis: What Went Wrong? She lacked "the courage to say no" and "self-protection through contracts." Also, rather than shouldering everything alone, she should have negotiated workload adjustments through an agent. A freelancer's body is their one and only capital, not a consumable.

【The High-Income Trap】 Even with revenue of 13.2 million yen, only 8.35 million yen remains in your hands. Nearly 5 million yen disappears into taxes and insurance premiums. This is the reality of Japan's progressive taxation and social insurance.

For freelance engineers to truly feel "richer" than they were as salaried employees, remember the following rule.

Freelance revenue target = gross annual salary as a salaried employee × 1.5

Someone who earned 6 million yen a year as a salaried employee only gets a return commensurate with the risk once their freelance revenue exceeds 9 million yen. In other words, absolutely do not go independent at a rate that is "the same as your current salary, or only slightly better." This is the conclusion proven by the numbers.

You have now seen frightening examples and cold, hard numbers. Some of you may be thinking, "Maybe I should give up after all..." But wait. Even in this harsh environment, there are definitely "successful people" who keep a take-home income of over 10 million yen a year, work wherever they like, and build assets while enjoying technology.

What makes them different from those who fail? Here are three decisive "turning points."

Successful freelancers see themselves as the CEO of a company called "Me, Inc." They don't just "write code"; they also have a business owner's perspective, such as the following.

- Marketing mindset: In which industry and with which language (market) can my skills (product) sell at the highest price? Go over Java? Entertainment over finance? - Financial management ability: Can you predict taxes as in the simulation above and manage cash flow? - Risk hedging: Do you avoid depending on a single client, build relationships with multiple agents, and always have a "next move" ready? - Acting like an employee replacement: By working in a role like an employee rather than on a task (issue) basis, you can work not as a PG but in an SE-like capacity.

Also, as one criterion for joining projects at 900,000 yen or more per person-month (annual revenue of 10.8 million yen):

- Experience starting from requirements definition - Full-stack (front-end and back-end) experience, or back-end experience of 5+ years in a specific language/framework

is required.

They don't have the mindset of "receiving work"; instead, they think, "I provide technical expertise as a business partner and receive compensation in return."

This is the most concrete turning point, and the one that determines your survival rate. Many freelancers who fail (like the people in Case A and B) try to do sales on their own to avoid paying fees, or casually look for projects on low-rate platforms like crowdsourcing. They also register with random agents they find through web searches and get stuck with projects with heavy middleman markups.

Successful people, on the other hand, understand that "my time should be focused on development and skill improvement." They completely outsource sales activities, rate negotiations, and handling contract-related issues to "agents," the professionals in that field. However, they choose their agents carefully.

【Criteria Successful People Use to Choose Agents】

  • Shallow supply chain (direct to end client or prime contractor): No middleman exploitation, and a high payout rate to engineers.
  • Account managers who understand technology: They don't deal with sales reps who "can't tell the difference between Java and JavaScript."
  • Strong negotiation skills: They have the strength to firmly push for rate increases and improved working conditions with clients.

They have an excellent sense of balance between "what I want to do" and "what is in demand." They don't cling to familiar old technologies like the engineer in Case A. For example, today they would keenly recognize that experience implementing "generative AI (LLM)" and cloud-native (AWS/GCP) development is highly valued in the market. If necessary, they will study on their own time, or even "strategically lower their rate" to dive into projects that let them take on unexperienced challenges, in order to gain the "track record" of real-world experience.

They constantly check "how much their skills sell for in the market" like a stock price, and rebalance their portfolio in line with the changing times.

Here is a concrete action plan to avoid the risks behind the "don't do it" warnings and achieve your ideal career. Whether you can execute it will determine your future.

Do not submit your resignation impulsively out of a negative motive like "I hate my company." While you still have the powerful "credibility" of being a salaried employee, thoroughly complete the following preparations.

- Getting credit cards and signing loan contracts: In your first year as a freelancer, your social credibility is equivalent to being "unemployed." Complete all necessary contracts while you are still employed. - Securing a financial safety net: Save at least 6 months' worth of living expenses, ideally 1 year's worth. This directly translates into "the courage to turn down unpleasant projects." - Test marketing through side work: Use weekends and weekday evenings to do side work and find out "whether your skills work outside the company" and "what it feels like to earn money on your own."

In the freelance market, the treatment of engineers with no experience or little experience is unforgiving. If you currently have less than one year of professional experience, I'll be blunt: build up at least two years (ideally three) of hands-on experience, whether at your current company or by changing jobs. In particular, whether you have experienced the full development cycle of "requirements definition, design, implementation, testing, and release" makes a world of difference in the projects available to you and your rates. Also, once you join projects as a freelancer, it can be difficult to accumulate knowledge and experience in infrastructure, so gaining infrastructure experience while you are still a full-time employee will make it easier to build up your skills as a full-stack engineer.

Once you've decided to go independent, you choose a partner to help you find projects. What you must absolutely avoid here are agents or brokers with deep layers of subcontracting, where you can't tell how many tiers down they are. They skim 20% or even 30% off your compensation without doing anything.

Choose an agent like "BizDev Tech," which has direct contracts with end clients and prime vendors. The benefits of a shallow contracting chain are immeasurable. - High rates: Middleman margins are eliminated, and the savings are reflected directly in your compensation. - Information transparency: You get an accurate picture of the team atmosphere and the skills required. - Control: It's easier to negotiate working conditions and schedules.

In the era ahead, the value of engineers who can merely write code will plummet. With GitHub Copilot and ChatGPT, anyone can write code at a certain level.

However, the value of "engineers who can leverage AI to boost productivity tenfold" and "engineers who can build AI itself into systems" will skyrocket going forward.
