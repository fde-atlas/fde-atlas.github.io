Labs — Interactive AI & Engineering Games, in Order

Vibe Engines · 2026-09-03 · 6 min read

Summary

Vibe Engines' Labs turns 61 AI and engineering concepts into small browser games organized into 14 ordered ladders, where play order is part of the pedagogy. Players act on instinct, fail, then see the correct method and formal theory. Topics span model training, evaluation, transformers, inference cost, agents and prompt injection, distributed systems, networking, and storage. A dedicated section simulates forward deployed engineer work: scoping under deadlines, domain ramp-ups, data mapping, integration reliability, deployment targets, rollout blast radius, evals, security reviews, and simulated interview rounds. All labs are free, browser-based, and require no account or math background.

Key points

  • 61 interactive labs arranged as 14 ordered ladders; order changes what each lab teaches.
  • Training ladder has you draw a decision boundary by hand before gradient descent.
  • Agent labs cover the loop, MCP tools, and attempting prompt injection yourself.
  • FDE simulations include scoping, data mapping, rollout blast radius, and client roleplay.
  • Everything is free, browser-based, keyboard-playable, and needs no background.

Labs — frequently asked questions

What is interactive, game-based learning for AI?

It's learning a concept by playing it instead of reading about it. Each lab here is a small browser game: you drive the idea yourself and fail on instinct, then watch the real method do it right, followed by the formal theory, a worked trace and a quiz. For abstract AI ideas like gradient descent, learning rates or retrieval, feeling the mechanic first — then formalizing it — makes it stick far better than a lecture or a wall of math.

Which lab should I start with?

Gradient Descent (The Descent) — rung 4 of the Training a network ladder, and the single most foundational idea in machine learning, since every neural network and large language model is trained by following the gradient downhill. If you want the three rungs under it first, the ladder opens by having you draw a decision boundary by hand.

Why are the labs in ladders instead of tags?

Because play order changes what a lab teaches, more than reading order does for a written page. The training ladder has you draw a boundary by hand, bend it, look at the terrain it lives on, and only then walk downhill — played the other way round, the walk is a demo rather than a discovery. A tag list cannot express that; an ordered ladder can, so each rung is built assuming the one below it.

Do I need math or a machine-learning background?

No. The labs lead with a visual, hands-on mechanic and a concrete story, not proofs. You'll meet the real terms — loss, gradient, learning rate, local minima, momentum — and see the update rule, but the understanding comes from playing the system: nudging it, tuning it, and breaking it.

How are labs different from the algorithm games or the tools?

The algorithm games teach classic data-structures-and-algorithms topics (shortest paths, sorting, search). Labs cover AI and engineering concepts — how models learn, how retrieval works, how a request flows through a system. The free tools are utilities you reach for while building (calculators and visualizers); labs are guided, game-first lessons that teach a concept end to end.

Are the labs free?

Yes — every lab is completely free, runs entirely in your browser with no account or sign-up, is keyboard-playable, and respects reduced-motion settings.
