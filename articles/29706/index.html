[Lens Quick Take] Frontline answers to seven questions about FDE

What exactly is an FDE? Why are five giants betting on three different playbooks?

In the same quarter, five giants poured money into the same type of role, but the core tension is not about who invested more—it's that the FDE naturally morphs with a company's stage and business model. At Cresta, a 500-person company, Zhong Qianjie added one word: Product. His team spends a third of their time modifying product code—spotting a problem at the customer site in the morning, submitting a PR in the afternoon, shipping the next day. At the 10-person Ventus AI, Lu Xiaopeng's FDEs follow through from pre-sales to post-sales, carry business metrics, and are expected to do the work of ten people. As for model companies doing FDE, Zhong Qianjie's take is blunt: essentially they want to sell more tokens, and the model is precisely the layer most easily replaced. The fragmentation of the definition is itself a signal: FDE is a role that grows on demand, and trying to force it into a single standard definition is doomed to miss the mark.

Where exactly is the line between FDE and outsourcing, implementation, and Solutions Architect?

When engineers in China hear FDE, their first instinct is to map it to on-site engineers, outsourcing, or Solutions Architects. Zhong Qianjie draws a hard line: anything you can teach the customer is not FDE—SAP implementation can be taught to customers, but AI agent hallucinations and latency simply cannot. Lu Xiaopeng is more honest about the 10-person Ventus: in the early days, FDE is solutions plus implementation, following through from pre-sales to post-sales, one person doing the work of ten. The disagreement is not about the name but about company stage: Cresta has dedicated SAs handling legacy systems like Five9 and NICE, so FDEs focus on AI agents; Ventus still bundles everything into one role. An FDE with no product to improve, stacking headcount and selling time, is no different from outsourcing.

From demo to production—why is it not just the "last mile"?

A single voice AI agent may run 20 models simultaneously—from ASR, interruption detection, and noise isolation to RAG, Tool Call, and multi-model concurrent Guard Rails—plus PCI or HIPAA compliance audits that easily take six months to a year. Zhong Qianjie offered a very concrete ratio: spend a week getting end-to-end working, then spend a month writing thousands or tens of thousands of tests. These are not traditional unit tests—you need to use historical calls to train small models to simulate impatient customers or patients with missing teeth speaking unclearly. This complexity explains a core fact: it's not that customers lack engineers—it's that the complexity of AI agents exceeds the experience boundaries of traditional engineering teams.

Why is "distillation" the prerequisite for the FDE business model to work?

If an FDE finishes one project and the next starts from scratch, it's no different from outsourcing with stacked headcount—or even worse than outsourcing. Lu Xiaopeng made a precise distinction: experience scattered in individual heads is not accumulation; only when it converges into a concrete knowledge base or Skill does it count. Zhong Qianjie's team has already taken the next step: packaging internally distilled artifacts (Markdown, CLI, scripts) into agentic tools—when a customer says what they want to do, best practices are automatically invoked to attempt it. After an FDE has built five or six insurance agents, industry vocabulary and processes become second nature; jumping into a completely new vendor, the technical side may still be fine, but the customer easily perceives you as an outsider. With large models and Skills, distilling and reusing experience becomes possible, and FDE transforms from a human services offering into a business model.

Distillable reuse is the watershed that turns FDE from human services into a business model

Palantir has been doing FDE for over a decade—is it still the same species today?

Same name, but the species has changed. Ten years ago, Palantir's FDEs struggled to accumulate knowledge or influence the product, and many who left complained they had no say over Foundry—"If you don't know how to use it yourself, go read the docs." Today, AI coding has turned the sound of artillery into PRs: those who hear the pain points can boldly modify the repository. Another shift is in supply and demand: the last wave relied on business relationships to sell headcount, signing multi-year contracts and delivering slowly; this wave, internal push at large customers has risen exponentially while products are not yet 100% there, making FDEs the ones who fill the gap. But model companies doing FDE and SaaS companies doing FDE still have different motives—the former burns more tokens, the latter makes the product stronger. Cresta also has a 20-plus-person FDPM team, with mini-CTOs and mini-CEOs dividing the work. Palantir deserves respect, but copying Palantir is not the same as copying success.

What kind of company and boss can actually cross the last mile?

The two guests, from both the supply and demand sides, confirmed the same judgment: the only common trait of companies that can push AI forward is that the boss must be AI native themselves. Among the bosses Lu Xiaopeng served in the healthcare industry, at most 5% had this mindset. His strategy in six words: win the lighthouse customer first, then push downward. Zhong Qianjie saw the same shift from the supply side: last year he still had to spend effort persuading major clients to adopt AI, but this year there has been an explosion—no one wants to be in the last batch to be eliminated. Resistance from the middle layer comes from conflicting interests: once you distill everyone's capabilities, that person no longer has much reason to exist. Without C-level support, it's almost impossible to push through. This means a window of opportunity for companies deploying AI: while models keep getting stronger but aren't yet foolproof, the high ground you seize now will be hard for others to take later.

95% vs 5%: The Real Filter for Enterprise AI Adoption

Can the FDE model work in China? Is this path still worth pursuing for engineers?

High deal size is a prerequisite, but more fundamentally it's about payment habits and awareness. Domestic bosses often think "two or three people tinkering around could build this too"; clients even ask directly: "If you give me two people to accompany me, why can't I just hire two people myself and evaluate them?" A quote from Zhong Qianjie that was repeatedly cited in the discussion: "The most dangerous illusion is the boss having illusions." Lu Xiaopeng believes the business environment will gradually improve, but what FDE solves is how to actually get standard solutions into enterprises. The same discussion also came down to people: Zhong Qianjie rates his team's AI nativeness 9 out of 10, and in interviews he looks at where you push back against AI in Claude Code; Lu Xiaopeng rates it 1—each person is 10x more efficient, but the whole enterprise may only improve by one or two times. 7,000 resumes, twenty-some offers, an acceptance rate of less than 0.3%. FDE is the escape hatch out of the sealed room, provided you're fiercer than AI.

I. The Definition of FDE and Different Bets on Models

One Job Title, Three Completely Different Operating Logics

Yu Yi: OK, let's start with a definition, or a delineation of this role. As Mr. Wang Peng said, for many people in China, FDE is a new concept, but globally Palantir has been doing it for over a decade. That said, the role's current popularity should also be tied to this wave of AI. Regarding the translation question everyone mentioned, I've already seen several versions: Forward Deployed Engineer, Forward Deployed Engineer... What stays constant is probably the "engineer" and "deployed" parts. So I'd like to hear from Zhong Qianjie and Lu Xiaopeng: how do you define this role? Are there actually different types of positions within FDE in the market today? I've also seen that there seem to be three types of FDEs now. Please introduce your understanding, as well as the current state of and recent changes in the FDE role in the market.

Zhong Qianjie: I think the main thing is just not to call it "Frontend Deployed Engineer", because the word "frontend" is too easily confused with front-end development.

Let me go through this in order. Since this is an interactive discussion, I don't want to talk for five or ten minutes in one big block. Whether you're a front-end engineer or a back-end engineer, you probably feel some confusion these days, because AI is getting stronger and stronger. Let me start with a conclusion: FDE really is an escape hatch out of the closed room. Especially if you're very confident in your engineering skills, confident in your problem-solving ability, and not afraid of interacting with people (though if you'd rather hide in a room writing code, that's a different story), but if you have strong technical skills and are willing to use them to make things happen, FDE is a great way out. And I don't think there will be a so-called AI version of the FDE in the short term—one, two, or three years—because there's so much human element involved.

To make a long story short, my definition is Forward Deployed Product Engineer (FDE): not only do you land the product and build the solution, but you also bring frontline experience back into the product to make it better. Of course, this varies from company to company. Our company's characteristic is that we're fundamentally a SaaS with a very complex feature set—possibly thousands of APIs and tens of thousands of features. If customers had to learn all these features on their own after paying for them, it might take three months or six months and they still might not use them well. Especially now, the complexity isn't just in software features; the complexity of AI is enormous too—are you on OpenAI's 5.1, 5.2, or 5.3, and what are the subtle differences between them? Is your prompt more effective if you write the corresponding table as a Markdown table, or as several sections? There are many details that aren't obvious at all. Even if a customer has an engineering team of hundreds or thousands of people, they may not be as strong in AI expertise as our team.

So this complexity is right there—whether it's the complexity of the software itself or the complexity that AI brings—and if customers have to learn it themselves or stumble through the pitfalls slowly, it takes far too long. Nowadays we often finish a project in two or three months, and the customer gets an AI version that can replace a call center. When people call in, they don't have to wait half an hour or an hour listening to music; they can come right in and say what they need, give their order number, ask whether they can change their booking, and roughly how much extra it would cost—all these boring things can be handled by AI.

For this kind of AI deployment, FDE is a very good model. But at least for me, my approach is: these engineers deployed to the front lines take the information they gather there (what works well in practice, what products have problems or defects) and bring it back into the product, making the changes directly. This is a crucial point. Including myself and my team, we probably spend a third of our time continuously improving the product itself. Because we're not just hearing the gunfire—the shells are exploding right next to us—we know exactly what works and what doesn't. And these people are hired to a very high standard, very full stack, almost like mini CTOs. So once we know where the problem is and how to improve it, with today's AI it's easy to work on several repos at once, get things moving, and then do some manual testing.

Because AI coding is so powerful now, those of us on the front lines dealing with real deployments know where changes should go, then come back and have AI generate PRs to make the changes ourselves, so the product keeps getting stronger, and other FDEs can work on more advanced, harder, newer things. This iteration makes the platform stronger and stronger. Of course, there will never be a day when customers can just mindlessly drag and drop (the world keeps moving forward), but we're always making the platform more powerful and the quality on the customer side very high. We don't just walk away, we don't sell headcount, and we don't do consulting—we have a strong product mindset. So I think this is a fairly effective approach.

Of course, including the conference I attended in San Francisco last week—it was full of people doing professional services and selling customer services. They were very interested in whether they could generate revenue through the FDE model, charging by the hour or by headcount. I personally don't agree with that, because I don't carry a quota, and our FDEs don't charge customers directly. We're part of the product—it's like buying a car with a driver included, or buying a coffee machine with someone who makes the coffee for you. II. The Divide Between FDE and Traditional Roles

Yu Yi: If there's nothing to add, let me add to Wang Peng's question. Indeed, whenever FDE comes up, both we ourselves and outsiders have a lot of confusion. This role is a new one in the AI field, but previously in SaaS—including the on-site engineers and outsourced engineers that Chinese companies are particularly good at, as well as Solutions Architects and customer success engineers—there seems to be some overlap with FDE. What do you two think is the most unique aspect of the FDE?

Zhong Qianjie: My provocative take is: anything you can teach the customer is not FDE work.

Yu Yi: So only what you can't teach the customer is FDE work? OK, is that the distinction?

Zhong Qianjie: Right. If you can teach the customer, then the complexity of that thing itself is limited. For example, if you previously did SAP implementation, some people think FDEs are just the suit-wearing SAP implementers from ten or twenty years ago. But with SAP, there's the opportunity to teach the customer or do it for them, so that's traditional implementation engineering or consulting.

With FDEs—especially FDEs for AI agents—it's because AI itself looks easy but is actually very hard. So what if you have 500 or 1,000 engineers? You still don't know how to build a good AI agent. I have too many examples: a company first thinks it's strong, even fights internal political battles to carve out a path, and finally builds an agent, only to find it's still not good enough—waiting three or five seconds for a question to be answered, prone to hallucinations, and even eliminating the last few percent of hallucinations is very hard.

So this is something that either can't be taught, or combined with a specific platform like Cresta, you can hardly expect the customer to learn. At that point, the FDE approach is especially effective, because the complexity is hidden away, and there are many magical parts the customer doesn't need to know about. So FDEs doing data engineering, FDEs doing security, or FDEs without any product accumulation—those don't count as real FDEs to me.

Yu Yi: So the product is the most important thing—there has to be something that lands in the product. OK, Lu Xiaopeng, since you've worked in both China and the US and are familiar with this role in both places, I'd especially like to hear your perspective from the front lines on both sides.

Lu Xiaopeng: I think this really has to be viewed from the stage of the company, because any role exists to serve commercial success. From what I've heard, Cresta should be the big brother, probably our next stage—we have things to learn from you.

Our company is at the stage of moving toward Series A, with a size of about 10 people. At this stage, how do we define the FDE, or what kind of person fits the need when hiring? First, they need to be able to communicate with customers, similar to half a customer success manager. Why? Because there are many customer needs right now, and we need someone to follow through from beginning to end (starting maybe a few months before the sale, through the sale, and after it). So the FDE has to cover that entire line.

Second, they need to understand the user's scenario and actually get the thing done. As I just said, they have to bridge the gap: the company's internal product covers 80%, and the remaining 20% is the customer's own needs—like how to get data in, how to export it, how to integrate with the customer's systems—and that's what the FDE needs to do.

So in an early-stage company, the FDE's definition is rather vague—you almost have to use one person as ten. At this stage, they first need to take on the customer success role, doing weekly meetups with customers, understanding new requirements and whether timelines can be met. That's the most important thing. Second, based on the timeline, break it down into milestones and deliver them. With these two lines covered, you can call someone a qualified FDE.

Wang Peng: From what I've heard, I get a sense: as the product form evolves (from fairly pure base models, to harnesses with some complexity but relatively thin—like context—to deeper industry-specific SaaS), the FDE's role is actually differentiating along the way. At first it might be relatively general—both telling users how to operate in an AI-native way and how to develop; later, if your SaaS specialization is already fairly high, it becomes product deployment or solving users' specific problems. This may indeed be related to the evolution of the product form. When I did research inside Tencent, I got the same feeling—the FDEs on our own internal teams are completely different in nature.

Zhong Qianjie: Right. And given our company's current state, I think it's quite fortunate for me and my team—we focus more on the AI agent itself. The company also has SEs who mostly handle demos, and SAs who mostly handle integrations with call center systems, like Five9, NICE, and so on—roughly 10 to 15 very complex systems on the market, some of which came out 20 or 30 years ago. The integrations are very technical but not AI. We have dedicated Solutions Architects who handle these telephony system integrations, knowing how data flows back and forth, how conversation history gets written to tickets or placed into Snowflake. These data engineering tasks are done by other teams.

I think this is a luxury and a stroke of luck—my team can spend more time on the AI agent itself. Of course, ultimately our FDEs are responsible end to end; technically speaking, they're like a mini CTO—we can talk with customers and understand the business situation. But we influence the whole deal and the whole process more through technical means, making changes.

Because the company is neither particularly small nor particularly large, there are dedicated people with different responsibilities to handle fancy demos or large system integrations. That way, our group focuses more on tackling hard problems: how to eliminate hallucinations, how to reduce latency, how to provide a smoother communication experience. These AI-related things are often hard to keep up with—best practices may need to be overhauled every three or five weeks—and we can devote a lot of energy there. So it's mostly luck. Different companies, different forms, different markets—FDEs will operate differently.

A typical FDE's weekly time allocation

Wang Peng: Please continue.

Lu Xiaopeng: Because for mid-level employees, they and AI are sometimes mutually exclusive—if you distill everyone's capabilities, then the person themselves no longer has much reason to exist. This is very challenging. Without C-level leadership support, it's indeed hard to push forward.

Wang Peng: A follow-up question. You talk about AI native, but this is hard to pin down. I often see a problem: it's precisely the boss who hasn't figured it out. The common logic of custom development is "I'll keep doing things the way I always have, and you use your tools to enhance what I already do"—that's a "+AI" approach. Another approach is "we tell them how other companies in their industry are doing this in an AI native way with our help"—that means you need to persuade them, and you need to understand their industry even better than they do. Which of these two situations do you think is more common, or how do you weigh them?

Lu Xiaopeng: In the healthcare industry we serve, 95% of people are in the first category: I have this org structure, all the software I buy serves that org structure, and AI is always just a pilot—seeing which part of the process can save some headcount, so they bolt on tools.

The second kind is more AI native. How do you define AI native? I think it means never assuming you know better than AI on anything—whether at the org structure level or any small execution level, never assume you know better than AI. Instead, first ask AI "from your perspective, how do you see this?" and then keep iterating with AI. That's an AI native mindset and starting point.

But very few bosses have this mindset—maybe at most 5% in our industry. Yet these people tend to be the ones whose businesses are already doing well, genuinely leading in the industry.

Wang Peng: So even then, you haven't persuaded that 95% to move in this direction?

Lu Xiaopeng: You can't persuade them; you can only filter. You can only sell to them in language they can understand.

Zhong Qianjie: Half-jokingly, there's now a term called "AI housekeeping"—not buying a robot to do chores at home, but a company bringing in a group of people like FDEs to tidy up the house: pour out what should be poured out, throw away what should be thrown away.

On the topic of AI native, we're very focused on AI agents for customer experience—not research agents, coding agents, or video generation agents. So for stakeholders it's very clear: can the original 1,000 people become 20% of that, with that 20% focusing more on serving high-net-worth customers or handling tricky issues, while simple, boring tasks like credit card loss reporting and replacement or address changes can be handled by AI.

Because of this focus, we spend a lot of time making sure we're familiar with voice models and do tool calls and API calls well. On API calls, we also have a rough notion of which companies are AI ready (say, if they use a lot of Salesforce or ServiceNow, their APIs are fairly standardized, accessible over the public internet, reasonably secure, and their documentation and knowledge bases are fairly complete)—these customers are comfortable to serve. Other customers' homes are already a complete mess: their API might have been built five years ago by an Indian outsourcing company that no longer exists; sending the same parameters to the API lands in different places—it's like paper mache. Sometimes, without outsiders coming in to help sort things out, people just let things rot for all kinds of reasons.

So sometimes an FDE or an AI company, as an outside force, can clean up a company's messy, dirty, and shabby stuff. Of course, this also requires boss-level support. It's often not suitable for us to talk only with the head of the call center, because this has risen to the level of customer experience and even brand identity. The people we usually talk to, if not the CEO, are teams in charge of Customer Experience or Digital Experience—these people often report to the CEO, control a lot of budget, and have strong motivation around modernizing the brand image, increasing stickiness, and upselling more products. Some even come in with big ambitions to make the company fully AI-driven, believing that without AI you fall behind, and after much vetting they pick Cresta as one of the key players to achieve that.

That makes the push very different. Because, as Vincent said, sometimes we inevitably step on some people's toes—some people were living comfortably, doing simple tasks every day and leaving at five to go cycling. Those repetitive jobs will be replaced by AI. If you go talk to them about "what you do every day and how to say it most effectively," they might throw up smoke screens, because they don't want to be distilled away. So you need to find a good stakeholder to help push this forward. Ideally the CEO; if you can find an executive, and you frame it not as cost savings but as increasing customer stickiness and standing out from competitors, the push will be much stronger. Along the way, someone will inevitably get hurt or resist.

The so-called last mile is sometimes not purely a technical or knowledge base problem—it needs some catalysts. We are people who understand AI better than ordinary engineers and can fill in the last mile more easily. For example, we're familiar with healthcare and with talk tracks, and we may do more than the people inside the customer's company. If you've already done 10 projects in healthcare, compared to the healthcare people at the customer's company—who have a narrower view and don't know what better solutions exist now—they're more willing to trust our recommendations.

Filling in the last mile is arguably an art, but you definitely have to dig in deep. That's also why today's large model vendors—even ones like Databricks with a huge platform business—are eager to do FDE work: it lets them see the most real-world difficulties and friction, and whether by themselves or co-creating with customers, once the gap is filled and customers see real business value, they'll keep using your product and won't easily switch around, which benefits everyone.

Wang Peng: I'm thinking of an ideal model. You said maybe 5% of customers are AI native—could you work out a good model with those customers and then show it to the 95%, so they accept it more readily? Within deep engagement in one industry, do you think this is an effective flywheel or closed-loop model?

Lu Xiaopeng: Yes, that's exactly the model we're pursuing now. For traditional enterprises, what's the decision path of 95% of bosses? Their main line is, say, operating a chain of clinics (how do I get better doctors, improve patient satisfaction, expand and buy more clinics)—that's the main line. They have no understanding of AI at all. When does it prompt them to sign an AI deal? Most of the time it's "everyone else is using it, so I will too." It's hard for them to compare three or four different AI companies; instead it's "the big player in my industry is using it, so I have to keep up"—95% of people think this way.

So our current strategy is to first find that 5% (basically who we serve now), serve them well as lighthouse references, and then push horizontally or downward—it becomes much easier to push. Because most people's decision path isn't "I understand AI deeply and want to try three or four vendors," but rather "everyone else is using it, and if I don't I'll fall behind, so I have to use it." That's just how it is in this industry.

Zhong Qianjie: On our side, we observed that last year we had to spend a lot of breath encouraging large customers to AI-ify some use cases. Some were persuaded to give it a try, but back then models' instruction following wasn't great, they made frequent mistakes, voice models would drift off mid-conversation. There was still a lot of friction. But this year it's truly exploding.

As Lu Xiaopeng said, many people are willing to follow the crowd and don't want to be the first to try something new, but no one wants to be in the last batch to be eliminated. When you see everyone around you doing it and there are good examples, the pressure on you not to do it is huge. In North America, the customer experience space is actually quite bad: you often wait an hour or two on the phone to book a doctor's appointment, in-home installation is a hassle, and banks are a big problem too. So we spend a lot of time landing big logos. If two of the five banks you regularly use are running very modern AI agents, the other three will get anxious and come looking for us or other vendors. At that point, pushing becomes easy—people believe what they see with their own eyes, not flashy things in videos. There's a real sense of pressure.

For enterprise companies like us doing AI deployment, this is a calculator problem: while demand is hot and models are getting stronger but not yet foolproof, we should do more use cases and seize more high ground. Once you've taken the high ground, it's very hard to be dislodged.

IX. Can Big-Tech KA Experience Be Replicated to Industry Verticals

Wang Peng: Right, but I want to raise an issue: at big companies, the KA industry lines and the regular service lines are actually separate, and there's some competition between them. You actually finish with KA and then replicate into industries, but for us, KA is KA and industry is industry—replication from regular services has a gap, at big companies. Do you have any suggestions?

Zhong Qianjie: Sorry, I don't quite understand. What's the difference between big companies and industries, in the Chinese context?

Wang Peng: For example, at Chinese big companies, our industry line serves KAs, and there's also an ecosystem team for a broad range of industry customers. There's a certain competitive relationship between them—whoever names the customer first gets to own them. KA may serve the high-end heads of industries, while the teams serving regular industry customers felt in their research that there's this problem: KA finishes a project at very high cost, but because they're two separate teams, the experience is hard to pass on to the regular customer teams.

Zhong Qianjie: Is this because of the human factor? Not something technology can solve.

Lu Xiaopeng: It just requires organizational change. Maybe Tencent needs to go AI native internally first, right? It feels like this is an internal organizational relationship problem.

Zhong Qianjie: We're trying something like that too. For sales, there will still be one group for large customers and another group for mid-market enterprise, and even a split between East and West—sales has a strong regional orientation, because in the end it's a scaling problem: how many calls to make, how many people to visit.

But for those of us doing the actual technical work (especially on my side, FDE), I haven't yet reached the point of splitting into East and West regions. On my side, I deliberately cultivate specialists: payment specialists, database specialists, insurance and aviation specialists. It's hard to say these people must be in the East or the West. In a team of ours—500, 600, under 1,000 people—doing this is relatively easy. But when a company gets big, everyone sets up their own fiefdoms and easily steps on each other's toes, so there must be some fine-grained division, and some places become awkward.

10. Who to hire: picking 20 out of 7,000 resumes

7,000 resumes, 20 offers

Yu Yi: The livestream time is up, but I still have some questions I'd like to ask you both—could I take another 15 to 20 minutes? The livestream side also just told me the numbers are very good right now and suggested we keep chatting.

Lu Xiaopeng: OK, no problem.

Yu Yi: Actually, I'd like to ask some more specific questions. I saw you posted an FDE position and received over 4,000 applications. We've previously dug through Silicon Valley FDE job descriptions too. I'd like to ask you both: for FDE hiring, what qualities are you focusing on now, and how do you screen and verify candidates?

Zhong Qianjie: Simply put, we're hiring mini CTOs, and the hands-on kind—people who can write code, can push back with clients, and are extremely reliable. I really identify with the word "reliable." If you're a reliable person, you'll learn what you don't know and break through obstacles when you encounter them. That's why I really like hiring people who have started their own businesses. If someone has previously worked at a so-called founding engineer role or an early-stage startup, or even been a founder, and is also capable, has good product sense, and is reliable, then they can make things happen.

Because on my side it's often one or two people running a project, interfacing directly with the client's technical lead. Taking on many APIs and building a really good AI agent is genuinely hard. So technical ability, client communication skills, and that kind of drive (carrying things end to end, mowing down whatever stands in the way) require strong capability and initiative. You definitely can't wait for a PM to file a ticket before you act—you need very strong initiative.

On the other hand, everyone is in a hurry and racing to claim territory—I need to go from 10 people to 100—so we can't wait for an ordinary backend engineer to be trained into an AI agent FDE. I require the people I interview to have actually built AI agent things, like having used LangChain, AutoGen, and the like, having built some AI agents themselves and tested them. It's not enough to call yourself an AI engineer—everyone is an AI engineer. You must have actually built AI agents for me to even put your resume in the pile to review.

We've already received 7,000 resumes and have only hired twenty-some people so far—the ratio is staggering. Of course there's a lot of noise in between; these days making a resume is too easy. You can completely use AI to produce a beautiful resume tailored to a JD, even fake LinkedIn profiles, homepages, GitHub accounts. Many people want to do AI but have actually never had the chance to build it themselves—they've only used it. Those people are rather awkward for me. So going from 7,000 people to issuing twenty-some offers is a very large funnel, and the recruiting workload is heavy.

But many problems can only be solved through hiring—it's not realistic for one person to run seven or eight projects. You have to fish for good people worldwide, which is also why there's an advantage in the US: you can draw talent from all over the world. We have Chinese people, Indians, people from all kinds of places. As long as the ability is there and they have that founder mode, founder mindset—whether they're currently an IC or a manager, or will go start their own company later—they'll appreciate this work environment: lots of autonomy, being able to make the right decisions, and growing very fast.

Hiring still comes down to luck. That's also why I spend a lot of time winning awards externally and building brand effect, so that people who are hesitant or very talented can keep an open mind and give the FDE role a try.

Wang Peng: A lot of companies now say they're recruiting FDEs from high school students. Do you think this is a future trend? That there's no need for a university education to do this work.

Zhong Qianjie: I wouldn't dare. I know Palantir claims to recruit people directly from high school. I think they must have really strong connections to pull that off with high schoolers.

Lu Xiaopeng: We currently have two people: one just graduated from high school and is about to start undergrad at Harvard, and the other studied at NYU for two years and then joined us. But I think there are still some issues. If it's about being an engineer, I think it's totally fine—being a software engineer is completely fine. But doing the FDE role at our company right now falls a bit short. The main reason is that we're quite early-stage and the role requires taking on part of the client communication. Also, project management and controlling project timelines—without having stepped in a few potholes yourself, it's hard to develop project management awareness. That's also a very difficult thing.

Wang Peng: Because I do research in education. Some high schools now already have project-based learning, where students have communication skills and some development ability by high school. I don't think it's necessarily true that you must have an undergraduate education. I also have friends who received US undergraduate offers saying, "Once I get this, I can use it to find a job—I don't need to actually get the diploma." Is that a future trend?

Lu Xiaopeng: I think education needs to be completely restructured. The current education system simply cannot handle this wave of AI. What are we learning? Testing how much book knowledge the brain can memorize—that in itself is already meaningless, because no matter how much we memorize, we can't out-memorize AI. It actually runs counter to the AI native direction.

If the education system were restructured, with more project management taught, and exposure at a younger age to how to plan projects, understand user needs, and use AI tools to do all of this, it would be entirely possible. But doing this now is more like panning for gold: 1,000 high schoolers come, and you pan out the one who becomes the next founder. That's very difficult.

Let me also reflect: this may be why hiring is so hard for us. We want to hire people who have started companies or really want to, who understand technology and can communicate—and in the Bay Area such people are genuinely scarce. The education system can't keep up.

11. The appeal and career prospects of the FDE role

Yu Yi: Since you brought this up—basically, both from what you've said and what I've seen in the JD descriptions, this is a startup-prep role. And the compensation seems to have a median in the $200,000 to $300,000 range, not a particularly high-paying position. So I'm curious: for people willing to join this role with that all-around, hexagon-warrior level of ability, what's the incentive to join and to stay?

Zhong Qianjie: One part is that everyone realistically needs to support their families, so the salary can't be too low. There's a gradual trend working in our favor—FDEs seem to get paid slightly more than other backend engineers, because the work really is hard and demanding.

On the other hand, Lu Xiaopeng and I are both at startups. Although our ARR recently reached 100 million, we're still early-stage, with a long road ahead and lots to look forward to. Beyond the base salary, you get some company equity, and who knows what might happen. For those of us putting in enormous effort and making many sacrifices, there's still hope: on one hand, you get to work on very exciting, challenging AI problems and engage with many excellent companies, and the potential returns are very promising.

And frankly, some of my cases were internal transfers—people who were doing full stack work and saying "in three years I want to start my own company," and when they saw the company hiring FDEs, they wanted to move over to better prepare themselves for entrepreneurship. That's fine too; I don't expect anyone to stay here for ten years. If they grow well over these three or four years, accomplish a lot for us, and become senior themselves, then whether they want to become a manager or leave later, we can talk about it then.

For capable people who embrace challenges, FDE is an ideal job.

Lu Xiaopeng: For smart people, FDE seems like a very high-value role. Once all technologies are equalized, what's left? What's left is traffic and resources. The people who can access customer resources are the ones with the most say. For all engineers, when Codex and Claude Code are this powerful, how should you think about your career development? If you have more ambition and want to start a company later, which skills do you need to strengthen?

I think it's actually knowing how to communicate with customers, solve concrete problems, and calculate how much value solving that problem generates—this is a scarce resource, and increasingly so. Because for all other technical capabilities, AI is rapidly catching up.

Zhong Qianjie: Sometimes an FDE may even be more valuable than a PM, because traditional PMs may not be that familiar with technology. Of course, this inevitably steps on the toes of some PM colleagues. But because FDEs are on the front line, haggling with customers all day, they discover new business models our company could build that customers are willing to pay more for, or that features we were once proud of simply don't work—FDEs are extremely sensitive to these pain points. Because you're on the front line, you can push for change internally in a very loud voice, or even just make the change directly without notifying anyone, without first going to a PM or arranging things with others. And ultimately, engineering-wise, it's all online, so it's easy to make very concrete changes.

So there will be many opportunities for traditional engineers to transition into FDE roles. Of course, there will always be top-tier talent working on databases and very low-level things. But FDE will be a good choice. Including meetings with customers—once the meeting ends and everyone is having coffee or a meal and chatting about this and that, you might pick up a lot of information (like "who had they already approached before coming to us, why did their own attempts fail, what were they dissatisfied with").

That's also why I think Vincent's side should try on-site work—being on-site brings many surprises. Sometimes I'll even deliberately have a newly hired FDE sit in on a project they're not involved in, just to get a feel for how much information being on-site brings. You can then just type up or dictate that information to your AI agent afterward. We FDEs can access the real world and feed it back to AI. AI can't do that itself, so we're very valuable.

Twelve. AI native scoring: 9 points vs 1 point

9 vs 1: Behind two extreme scores lies the same understanding

Wang Peng: Do you think FDE is a transitional role that exists because AI isn't yet AGI, or will there be more and more of them in the future? There's even a saying that future education and talent demand may shift toward the FDE direction. How do you understand the capability model for using AI well?

Zhong Qianjie: I don't know if it's the ultimate form, but I think it will be a mainstay for a long time. A company with good salespeople—sales are also hard to replace. Simple cold emails and phone calls can be AI-ified, but truly great salespeople are hard to replace with AI. FDEs are very down-to-earth people with engineering skills who leverage great AI, and that part is also hard to AI-ify. You have some decision-making authority—roughly speaking, that's what it takes.

You don't necessarily need that many engineers doing low-level component library development or CI/CD. You just need to build what you want to say—Codex and Claude Code can do it very well for you. The bar for pure engineers will keep rising; you have to become an expert to have specific value. The CRUD engineers in the middle will be in an awkward position: either become very strong and become a technical expert, or get closer to the business and become an FDE.

Lu Xiaopeng: I think FDE will increasingly lean toward customer success. Since the technical requirements are getting lower, how do you out-compete other companies and provide better value? It comes down to providing emotional value, better service, and better understanding of needs. I think FDEs in the future will lean more toward customer success.

Yu Yi: Let's wrap up with one last, fairly general question. We've recently been doing research on AI native organizations (the "AI Leap-takers Survey") and have already spoken with quite a few domestic companies. One fixed question from that survey I'd like to ask you both: Using AI native as the standard, how closely does your team work with AI, on a scale of 1 to 10? And why that score?

Zhong Qianjie: For the FDE team, I'd say we can reach 9. We're probably the most aggressive AI users in the company. Look, I'm currently managing 30 people directly, with no managers in between. With so many one-on-ones, so much project scheduling, everyone's leave requests—my AI is my assistant, my EA, or my junior manager.

Our FDEs also use AI heavily—I won't even mention how many tokens we burn in a day. We're almost the most aggressive AI users in the company, and we often train salespeople and even other engineers on "new ways to use AI lately." Because if you don't use AI, you'll die a miserable death. Everyone is already so busy—without AI for writing code, doing research, or even reviewing flags, doing it manually just isn't feasible. And sometimes you need to impress the other side's CPO or CTO, doing lots of research and saying the right things—all of this requires using AI in various ways. Even making slides is done with AI.

A common approach for us now is: say what you want to do, don't tell the AI how to do it—the AI may understand how better than you do. This is a mindset issue. Of course, we don't blindly follow AI's suggestions; AI is more for gathering information or doing simple work, and ultimately a human still needs to be in the loop. But you definitely have to be tougher than the AI—you need to have value. That's also why sometimes in interviews I look for: you use Claude Code's code well, but during the process, what did you push back on with the AI, proving you could be better than it? That's how you have value—otherwise I could just hire a middle schooler who uses AI all day. You must be tougher than the AI. But I'm fairly confident in our FDE team.

Lu Xiaopeng: Right, right. Because I think you should never feel you're already using AI enough, or that you understand things better than AI. So I'll give it a 1—there's still a lot of room for improvement.

Think about this question: what are the things only humans can do that AI can't? Where AI beats humans is that as long as it has electricity, it can keep burning tokens—that's an energy problem. For humans, it's a brain capacity problem.

I think what's missing right now is the internal observability layer within enterprises. If you ask every CEO or CTO "which of your employees' tasks can be AI-ified and which can't," no one can answer. First of all, there's no such observability layer, so there's no way to answer how AI native a company or a person really is.

Essentially, it's still a continuously looping, evolving relationship. The ultimate measure actually comes down to how replaceable each employee is. If a person can define an input and an output, like an API, then they are the most replaceable. But if there's a segment between "input → output" that AI can't handle and requires a human, then at that point that person is 100% AI native. This is an extremely extreme way of thinking.

But what I want to express is: although everyone is using a lot of AI, within the overall organizational structure, the complexity of the organization itself creates a lot of man-made noise. Even though everyone feels they're using AI and their personal efficiency has increased 10x, the efficiency of the entire enterprise may have only improved by one or two times. Fundamentally, observability is still too poor—we don't know which things must be done by humans. There's still a long way to go.

Yu Yi: I usually also follow up with this question: since it's on a 10-point scale, what would a 10-point AI native organization or team look like in your minds?

Lu Xiaopeng: I don't think such an organization exists. If it did, it would surely first appear at companies that master intelligence.

Yu Yi: So Anthropic and OpenAI are more likely to become such organizations.

Lu Xiaopeng: Right, the most native companies within each region under geopolitical dynamics.

Zhong Qianjie: I'm also curious what a 10-point company would look like. I believe wherever there are people, there's politics, so I don't think OpenAI is an ideal utopia either. There will still be a strong human element (even I find it hard to fully AI-ify project assignments). After all, humans hold a lot of extra information, and even have intuition and a sense of aesthetics.

So we don't necessarily need to pursue pure AI nativeness; it's more about a place of symbiosis. How to hand the boring things and the hard things to AI, but people still need to retain their humanity. For example, I spend a lot of time doing one-on-one talks—as an employee, if you're unhappy, you don't want to talk to an AI; you still want to talk to a person, vent some complaints, or even prove that you deserve a promotion. Unless eventually all managers in management are AI, but as long as there are people judging who has more potential, who has more influence, and who can take on the big deals, these decisions are still made by humans, so there's no need for us to pursue pure nativeness.
