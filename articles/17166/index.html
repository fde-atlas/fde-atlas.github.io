Should You Cancel Expensive SaaS and Build In-House? 7 Decision Criteria for the Generative AI Era

Original title: 高額SaaSを解約して内製化すべきか｜生成AI時代の判断基準7つ

TIMEWELL · 2026-09-29 · 28 min read

Summary

TIMEWELL's Ryuta Hamamoto proposes seven criteria for deciding whether to cancel expensive SaaS and rebuild in-house. Rising prices and yen depreciation inflate SaaS costs, yet official Japanese guidance (METI's DX Report 2, Digital Agency's DS-310) still favors SaaS, and AI coding speed comes with caveats on stability and security. He recommends screening first on regulatory complexity and maintenance staffing, then weighing cost, difficulty, and timing. The Klarna case shows consolidation of data, not AI-generated screens, is what makes dropping SaaS feasible.

Key points

  • A major vendor raised Japan prices 20% in April 2024; the yen weakened over 40% versus the dollar since 2020.
  • JUAS's 2026 survey found cost reduction (41.3%) became the top expected benefit of in-house development.
  • METR's randomized trial found AI made experienced developers 19% slower, though developers believed they were faster.
  • Klarna's CEO clarified it retired ~1,200 SaaS tools after unifying data with Neo4j, not by replacing SaaS with LLMs.
  • Recommended order: screen on criteria 4 and 6, then assess cost (1-2), difficulty (3, 5), and timing (7).

Hello, I am Ryuta Hamamoto of TIMEWELL Corporation.

When you open a SaaS renewal quote and see that the amount has gone up from last year, have you ever thought, "Wouldn't it be cheaper to build it ourselves?" Now that the cost of writing code with generative AI has dropped, that feeling feels more realistic than before.

Let me state my position up front. I believe that for most SaaS, it is better not to cancel. For operations bound by laws and regulations, such as accounting and payroll, leaving them to SaaS is still the rational choice. However, for operations that are charged in proportion to the number of users, whose features evolve slowly, and whose mechanisms are simple, rebuilding them has become worthwhile. The hard part is deciding where to draw the line for that "only in those cases."

This article compiles seven criteria for deciding whether you should cancel an expensive SaaS you are already paying for and build your own instead. It is the first of a five-part series; how to select candidates, estimating the five-year total cost, the practicalities of cancellation and migration, and designing for hosting on your own cloud are each explored in separate articles. I wrote about the broader question of how much of AI usage to keep in-house in How much of AI internalization should your company do itself?. This article focuses narrowly on the single question of "should you cancel the SaaS you are paying for now?"

Why people start to feel that "building it would be cheaper"

There are two main reasons SaaS costs look like they are ballooning: rising prices themselves, and the weak yen.

Here is one example of a price increase. The Japanese subsidiary of a major overseas software company raised its yen-denominated prices for enterprise software and cloud services by a flat 20% starting April 1, 2024. It also stated that going forward, it may adjust prices in local currency as part of regular price reviews twice a year, taking into account exchange rate fluctuations against the US dollar 1. In other words, the yen price of the SaaS you pay for moves through two channels: revisions to the dollar list price, and reviews of the yen-denominated price.

Let's also look at the yen exchange rate. Averaging the Bank of Japan's published monthly averages by calendar year, the rate was 106.78 yen per dollar in 2020, 151.50 yen in 2024, and 158.78 yen from January to August 2026 (calculated by me from BOJ statistics) 2. That is more than 40% yen depreciation compared with 2020. For services priced in dollars, the yen invoice amount keeps rising even if you do nothing.

Companies are feeling this too. In the 2026 edition of the survey by the Japan Users Association of Information Systems (JUAS), the second most common reason for IT budget increases was "the impact of the weak yen, rising labor costs, and vendor price increases," cited by 46.6% of companies in their FY2025 plans 3. In the same survey, the top expected benefit of internalizing system development was "reducing development costs" (41.3%), overtaking the previous year's top answer, "improving development capability through knowledge accumulation within the company." The report surmises that, in response to steep vendor price hikes, "the priority of immediate cost reduction has risen rapidly" 3.

Then generative AI arrived. In a survey of 817 of its own users conducted by an overseas development tools company (published February 2026), 35% answered that they had replaced at least one SaaS with a self-built tool. However, since the respondents were people already using tools for building things, they are presumably skewed toward building compared with the general population.

The emblematic story is Klarna, the Swedish payments company. It made headlines for dropping a major CRM, and the interpretation spread that AI had replaced SaaS. Yet CEO Sebastian Siemiatkowski himself stated in a March 2025 post on X (formerly Twitter) that, according to internal estimates, the company had shut down about 1,200 SaaS subscriptions, while making clear that it had not replaced SaaS with LLMs, and that there are limits to storing CRM data in an LLM 4. What it actually did was build an internal technical foundation, using Neo4j and other tools, to organize data into knowledge. He also wrote, "Will every company do what Klarna did? I doubt it" 5.

I find this explanation the most instructive. Klarna was able to drop SaaS not because AI built its screens for it, but because it first consolidated data that had been scattered across disparate SaaS products. Screens can be built afterward, as many as you like. If you cancel SaaS while the data remains unconsolidated, you may end up merely adding more in-house tools that do the same work.

Even so, continuing to use SaaS is usually the right call

Let me lay out the evidence that weakens my argument first. Hiding it would mislead readers' judgment.

First, official guidance clearly leans toward SaaS. METI's DX Report 2 (December 2020) states that for collaborative domains, companies should "reject self-reliance," promote standardization of business processes, use SaaS and packages, and restrain IT investment budgets and staffing 6. DS-310 (May 2025), established by the Digital Agency for government information systems, likewise calls for broadly prioritizing the use of SaaS because it can reduce the amount of development 7.

The reality of Japanese companies points the same way. According to IPA's "DX Trends 2025," for procuring systems in non-core, non-competitive domains, "introducing packaged software" was 33.8%, "introducing SaaS" was 24.4%, and "in-house development" was 16.6%. Companies "progressing" with internalizing system development were 22.3% in Japan versus 46.4% in the US. And 82.3% of Japanese companies progressing with internalization cite "difficulty securing and training talent" as a challenge 8.

The claim that generative AI speeds up development also comes with caveats. In a randomized controlled trial by the US research organization METR with 16 experienced open-source developers and 246 real tasks, the condition with AI access took 19% longer to complete the work. The developers themselves had predicted beforehand that they would be 24% faster, and even after finishing, they felt they had been 20% faster 9. In a February 2026 follow-up, an estimate using tools from late 2025 found an 18% reduction in time required, but the confidence interval ranged from -38% to +9%, crossing zero 10. DORA's 2025 survey of about 5,000 people reported that AI use is positively associated with software delivery throughput but continues to show a negative association with delivery stability 11. In a survey by security vendor Veracode, 45% of code generated by over 100 LLMs failed security tests 12.

Building quickly and running safely for a long time are two different things. SaaS subscription fees include the cost of that "running for a long time."

The reason DS-310 itself writes an exception is that it makes one. The same document states that while SaaS use is strongly recommended, it is not unconditional, and in cases such as when the number of users grows in stages and fees become high at the operational stage, it asks you to carefully evaluate, from a lifecycle cost perspective, whether it truly reduces costs. It also says that if similar functionality to the SaaS can be achieved with another managed service, both approaches should be compared and evaluated. For SaaS that charges per account or expensive SaaS, it reiterates elsewhere to pay close attention to trends in the number of accounts in use 7.

The claims in this article stay within the scope of that exception. This is not a story about quitting SaaS. It is a story about identifying what falls under the exception and considering rebuilding only those things that do.

Seven criteria for deciding whether cancellation is worth considering

Here is a table of the seven criteria I use, shown up front. They are not meant to decide on any single one, but to be viewed side by side.

① Annual cost and future price increases

The first thing to look at is how much you are paying now and how much it is likely to become. It sounds obvious, but I feel that not many companies, even while watching the annual invoice amount, line up the revision history of the past five years alongside currency effects.

There are three numbers to look at: the current annual cost, the frequency and magnitude of past revisions, and, for services priced in dollars, sensitivity to exchange rates. As in the earlier example, for a service that has announced it will review prices twice a year, calculating how much the annual cost changes when the dollar moves by 10 yen makes the discussion in internal approvals concrete.

Conversely, canceling a SaaS costing a few hundred thousand yen per year and rebuilding it is almost never worth it. Add up the cost of building it, the maintainer's time, and server costs, and it easily exceeds what you save by canceling. In practice, this criterion is best used not to show "you may cancel," but to filter out things "not worth considering." A concrete method for estimating the five-year total is covered in the third piece of the series, Estimating the Five-Year Total Cost of SaaS.

② User growth and per-account billing

This is the criterion DS-310 explicitly calls attention to 7. For SaaS billed at a monthly rate per user, the more the company grows and users increase, the more the bill grows even though the features stay the same.

Let's calculate with hypothetical numbers. If 300 people use a SaaS costing 3,000 yen per user per month, the annual cost is 10.8 million yen. Assuming users grow 20% per year and the unit price rises 5% per year, in year five there are about 620 users and the annual cost is about 27.2 million yen, with the five-year total reaching about 90.4 million yen. If both headcount and unit price stayed flat, the five-year total would be 54 million yen, so the difference is about 36 million yen (all figures are calculations based on our assumptions; actual unit prices and growth rates differ by company).

The cost of building it yourself does not double when the number of users doubles. Server costs do increase, but the way they grow is completely different from per-user billing. The more a tool touches every employee—internal portals, request workflows, ledgers—the more this difference matters. According to the Ministry of Internal Affairs and Communications' Reiwa 7 survey, 83.5% of companies use cloud services at least in part, and the second most common use was "internal information sharing and portals" (61.4%) 13. The tools everyone touches are precisely the ones being covered by cloud services.

For companies where users barely grow, or flat-rate contracts, this criterion does not apply. Please check the user count forecast alongside your hiring plan.

③ Whether data and features change slowly

The third criterion is how often the system changes. Since this is easy to misunderstand, let's check the original source.

The concept of pace layering, presented by Gartner in 2012, divides a company's systems into three layers by speed of change. Regarding the slowest layer, "Systems of Record," the original explains that these are existing packages and systems that manage core transaction processing and critical master data, with established processes common across many organizations and often subject to regulatory requirements, so their rate of change is slow 14. In other words, the original source's position is that the slower something changes, the more it should fundamentally be held as a standard package. Simply saying "it changes slowly, so it's fine to build it yourself" says the opposite of the original.

The reason slow change is still included as a criterion is that it becomes useful when combined with another axis. That axis is the next criterion, ④, complexity arising from laws and accounting rules. Something that changes slowly and is also simple in structure. Operations such as internal ledgers, FAQs, internal portals, request intake, and quote ledgers barely change in fields or procedures for years once built. For such operations, much of the SaaS subscription fee ends up funding the development of new features your company never uses.

The same source also notes that even for the same type of application, the layer it belongs to can differ by company 14. What is a mere ledger for one company may be a source of competitive advantage for another. How to identify candidate business processes is explored in depth in the second installment of the series, How to Identify Business Systems Worth Building In-House, using our own perspective based on data update frequency. Note that we could not find any public framework that distinguishes build vs. buy along the axis of data update frequency, at least within the scope of our research. Please read it strictly as our own perspective.

④ Complexity of laws, regulations, and accounting rules

Even if change appears slow, processes bound by laws and regulations should be treated separately. These are processes such as accounting, payroll, tax filing, and attendance management that must keep up with tax reforms and changes in accounting standards.

Here is a concrete example. Under the 2024 fixed-amount tax cut, the reduction for salaried workers was, in principle, applied by deducting it from the withholding tax on salaries and similar payments made on or after June 1, 2024, with any amount not fully deducted in June to be deducted sequentially from salaries later in the same year 15. If a company had built its own payroll calculation, it would have needed to implement and verify this mechanism within a few months of the tax reform and confirm everything through year-end adjustment. SaaS subscription fees include the cost of keeping up with such changes.

Even with generative AI speeding up code writing, the work of reading the details of each amendment, interpreting them correctly, and identifying exceptions remains. Moreover, mistakes directly affect employees' salaries and tax filings. I believe this kind of process should continue to rely on SaaS or packaged software.

The guideline for drawing the line is: "Does the company itself set the rules of the process, or do laws and regulations?" Internal approval routes for applications are set by the company. The calculation of payroll withholding is set by law. The former can be a candidate, but the latter should, in principle, be excluded from consideration.

However, what you exclude is only the source of truth and legally mandated processing. Even within accounting or HR/labor SaaS, application screens and aggregations touched by all employees are parts the company itself decides. If you build those in-house and pass data to the SaaS via API, you may be able to reduce the number of SaaS seats needed for accounting and HR staff. This is a tactic that works well with criterion ②, account-based billing. I described the way to divide things and the contract clauses to check in advance in the second installment of the series, How to Identify Business Systems Worth Building In-House.

⑤ How many integrations with other systems are needed

The fifth criterion is the number of integrations. This is not an official guideline but my own view: what most determines the maintenance burden after building is, I feel, the connections to other systems.

Many SaaS products come with components for connecting to single sign-on, accounting, HR master data, chat, email, BI, and so on, and the vendor takes on the work of keeping up with changes in the specifications of those integration targets. If you build in-house, all of that follow-up work becomes your company's responsibility. Think of each additional integration target as one more place that can break.

Counting is simple. List all the systems that feed data into the SaaS and all the systems that receive data from it. If the numbers are large and synchronization is bidirectional, the cost of rebuilding should be dominated by developing and maintaining integrations rather than developing screens. If the only entry and exit points are employees typing into screens and a CSV export once a month, the difficulty drops considerably.

Recall the Klarna story. The reason they were able to cancel was that they consolidated their data first 4. If you want to cancel an integration-heavy SaaS, you need a design that consolidates where data lives beforehand. That design is covered in the fifth installment of the series, Designing to Host Business Applications on Your Own Cloud.

⑥ Who will maintain it after it is built

Of the seven criteria, this is the one I weight most heavily. Because sustaining and fixing what has been built is far harder than building it.

The domestic numbers are sobering. In the IPA survey mentioned earlier, 82.3% of companies pursuing in-house development cited securing or training talent as a challenge, and 19.5% answered that "migrating externally developed systems to in-house development is difficult" 8. Even in IPA's 2026 edition, 85.5% of companies in total answered that the supply of personnel driving DX was "somewhat insufficient" or "significantly insufficient" 16.

Overseas, too, maintenance is a challenge that remains to the end. Fred Turner, CEO of the US health insurance company Curative, said he canceled a $600,000-per-year CRM contract and built an in-house CRM in two months, while also stating that maintenance was "definitely one of the hardest challenges," according to reports 17. It could be said that as building has gotten faster, the weight of maintenance has become more visible.

There are three questions to check under this criterion. Can you name a specific person responsible for fixing the built system? Can you explicitly secure a set number of hours per week for that person? Is there a reliable accompanying partner for when the person in charge cannot handle changes or incidents alone? If you cannot answer all three, I judge that it is still too early to cancel.

The accompanying partner referred to here is not an outsourcer who builds and delivers on your behalf. It is someone who builds alongside you until your internal staff can fix things themselves. The DX Report 2 also states that demand will grow for vendors to support the transition to in-house development and transfer skills while working alongside user companies, for technologies that internal talent at user companies cannot immediately handle 6.

⑦ Conditions for cancellation and migration

The last criterion is contract terms. Even if all other conditions are met, mishandling the cancellation procedure or the timing of migration can extend the period of double payment or result in data loss.

The difficulty of switching is also reflected in the numbers. In a fact-finding survey on the cloud sector published by the Japan Fair Trade Commission in 2022, only 15.7% of businesses (86 out of 548) had experience switching cloud providers in the past 10 years. Among those who gave a clear answer, only about 14.1% said they would switch if the service they were using raised prices by 5-10% 18. While the survey mainly targeted IaaS and PaaS users, the tendency that it is hard to leave a cloud once you start using it is clear.

The items to verify start from those listed by IPA's "Guide to the Safe Use of Cloud Services for SMEs" (June 2026 edition) as points to confirm at the end of use: return or download of all data, data compatibility and portability, complete deletion of remaining data, and assurance that other users cannot reuse it 19. DS-310 also calls for avoiding vendor lock-in by choosing services that guarantee data portability and publish reasonable pricing structures 7.

In the contract, be sure to check three points: the auto-renewal conditions and the notice deadline for cancellation; how unit pricing is handled when you reduce the number of licenses and keep only part of them; and how many days after contract termination you can still extract your data. In the master agreements of major overseas SaaS vendors that we reviewed (September 2026 edition), it was stipulated that, unless the order form states otherwise, the contract auto-renews on a one-year basis unless notice is given at least 30 days before expiration; that renewing with reduced quantity or term triggers repricing regardless of the previous unit price; and that the vendor has no obligation to retain data unless requested within 30 days after termination. Keeping only part of it does not necessarily make it cheaper. The cancellation process is written as a schedule working backward from the renewal date in the fourth article of the series, SaaS Cancellation and Migration in Practice.

How to use the seven criteria

Adding up all seven with equal weight leads to poor decisions. I use them in the following order.

First, screen candidates using criteria ④ and ⑥. Operations tightly bound by laws or accounting rules are excluded from consideration no matter how much the other criteria point toward cancellation. Operations that cannot name a specific person responsible for maintenance are also excluded for now. These two carry large losses when they fail, and the problems only surface after the system has been built.

For those that pass the screening, look at the money using criteria ① and ②. How much will you likely pay over five years, and what share of that is account-based billing growth? If the difference compared to building it yourself is small, there is no reason to cancel. Only those with a large difference move on to the next step.

Next, assess difficulty using criteria ③ and ⑤. The slower the pace of change and the fewer the integrations, the easier the rebuild and the lighter the ongoing maintenance. Finally, use criterion ⑦ to decide the timing. Even if something becomes a candidate, if only three months remain until the notice deadline, it is safer to skip that renewal and prepare for the next one.

Narrow the candidates down to one. Trying to cancel multiple SaaS products at the same time causes migration work to overlap, and the burden on operations concentrates during the parallel-running period. It is realistic to learn the process with the first one, confirm that maintenance is manageable, and then move on to the second.

SaaS cancellation and in-housing with an FDE

Now, about our company. Using the FDE (Forward Deployed Engineer) approach, we advance this whole process together: from the decision, through rebuilding and migration, to self-sufficiency within your organization. An FDE is an engineer who works on-site with the customer, sitting alongside them to handle development and implementation. I wrote in detail about what this work involves in What Is an FDE?

What our FDEs can do

First, we take inventory of the SaaS products you currently use and narrow the candidates down to one using the seven criteria in this article. We compile the annual cost, billing units, user count trends, integration targets, and contract renewal dates and notice deadlines into a list, and honestly tell you "you should keep this one" for anything that fails the screening.

Once a candidate is chosen, within the first week we show you a working prototype running on real data with personal information and the like masked. Without waiting for a finalized specification, we let the people on the ground try it, ask "how does it differ from your current work," and fix it the next day. Through this back-and-forth, it becomes clear which SaaS features are actually used and which are not.

We then set the parallel-running period, migrate the data, and calculate backward from the renewal date when to send the cancellation notice. We start with a period of 45 to 90 days, and define the end condition as "the in-house staff can make changes themselves" before beginning.

There is also one commitment about how we use AI. We select only cloud services and AI models for processing that do not use customer data for training. Where processing happens is designed case by case, balancing requirements and cost. For our information security management system, we are certified under ISO/IEC 27001 (registration scope: planning, development, and provision of SaaS products leveraging AI technology).

Details of our approach and structure are summarized on the FDE service page.

How this differs from contract development

What happens if you cancel a SaaS product and rebuild its replacement through contract development? Only the contractor knows the internals of the built system, and every change requires an estimate and an order. You want to avoid the outcome of escaping SaaS lock-in only to find yourself in another lock-in under the contractor's maintenance contract.

Our FDE model is structured differently from contract development in this respect. First, the meaning of hours. In contract development, hours are revenue, so the longer the engagement, the better. We position FDE hours as an investment, and consider it good when the hours per customer decrease over time. Second, where the know-how goes. Common mechanisms usable by other companies are returned as features of our own products, used to make the next engagement faster and cheaper. Company-specific procedures and business terminology remain with that company. Third, how it ends. We do not leave at acceptance; we step away once the in-house staff can make changes themselves. This means that cultivating the "person responsible for maintenance" from criterion ⑥ within the company during the build process is part of the job.

Our practice and its limits

To be honest, this approach does not fit every company or every operation.

For SaaS products governing operations bound by law, such as accounting, payroll, and tax filing, even if you consult us about cancellation, we do not recommend rebuilding. As per criterion ④, once you include the cost of keeping up with regulatory changes, SaaS is cheaper and safer. The same applies to things like the core of an ERP, with high transaction volume and connections to many systems.

If you cannot place a maintenance owner in-house, we will not take on the work. Since we are structured on the premise of leaving once the system can run on its own in-house, building it without an owner only leaves behind a system no one can fix. We recommend either delaying the start until an owner can be assigned, or deciding to keep using the SaaS.

For SaaS products with small annual costs, the build cost and maintenance labor tend to exceed the savings from cancellation, so they rarely qualify. If the notice deadline is approaching, we propose starting with preparation for the next renewal.

I will also write out our own line-drawing. We build and operate our own employee portal (daily reports, goal management, internal training, etc.), but for accounting we continue to use an external cloud accounting service. The reason we drew the line this way is precisely criteria ④ and ⑥.

Finally, a word about our capacity. We are a small company, and our management team works directly in the field. The number of projects we can take on simultaneously is limited, and we do not hide this cap.

Summary

  • Behind the apparent growth in SaaS costs lies a double movement of price increases and the weak yen, and in JUAS's survey, reducing development costs ranked first among the expected benefits of in-house development
  • Even so, the basic principle of public guidelines remains SaaS-first, and the speed of building with generative AI comes with reservations about stability and safety
  • The exceptions are written by DS-310 itself. For expensive SaaS that inflates through account-based billing, or for things that can be built with managed services offering equivalent functionality, compare on lifecycle cost
  • Decisions are made using seven criteria. ④ legal and accounting rules and ⑥ maintenance structure act as gatekeepers, ① and ② look at cost, ③ and ⑤ at difficulty, and ⑦ at timing
  • Narrow candidates down to one, organize the data first, and then build the screens

The lesson I took from the Klarna example is that whether you can stop using SaaS is not determined by how smart your AI is. It is determined by whether you can hold the data and maintenance in your own hands; cancellation is merely the result.
