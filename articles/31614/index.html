FDE Visa Sponsorship and the $100,000 H-1B Fee Explained

Original title: FDE 签证担保与 H-1B 10 万美元费用解读

FDE Times · 2026-10-27 · 6 min read

Summary

This article explains what visa sponsorship questions on FDE job applications actually reveal, and analyzes the new $100,000 H-1B fee. The fee applies to new petitions filed after September 21, 2025 for candidates outside the US, while change-of-status petitions for people already in the country are exempt. A California court has issued a preliminary injunction blocking collection, but USCIS says it will charge the fee if the injunctions are lifted. The author advises candidates to state their visa situation in the first recruiter call and ask role-specific sponsorship questions, treating the conversation as an early discovery exercise.

Key points

  • Application screening questions classify candidates; they do not indicate whether a company sponsors visas.
  • The $100,000 fee covers only new H-1B petitions for candidates abroad, not change-of-status filings.
  • Courts currently block the fee, but USCIS plans to collect it if injunctions are lifted.
  • F-1 OPT candidates should answer yes to needing future sponsorship to avoid wasted interview rounds.
  • Ask about sponsorship for the specific role, and about alternatives, in the first recruiter call.

Key takeaways

  • The question "Do you now or in the future require visa sponsorship?" is a screening criterion for the company; it does not indicate whether the company offers sponsorship.
  • The $100,000 fee applies to new H-1B petitions filed for individuals outside the US, not to change-of-status petitions filed within the US. Courts are currently blocking collection of the fee, but USCIS says it will collect it once the injunction is lifted.
  • State your visa status clearly in your first call with the recruiter, and ask questions specific to the role you're applying for.

FieldAI is hiring Forward Deployed Engineers (FDEs) in Irvine, California, and its application form places two questions side by side. The first asks whether you are legally eligible to work. The second asks whether you now or in the future require employer sponsorship for a work visa or work authorization. Neither question tells you whether FieldAI offers sponsorship.

Anthropic's FDE job posting in Munich is very direct: "We do sponsor visas!" Immediately after comes a caveat: the company cannot successfully secure sponsorship for every role and every candidate.

If you're an engineer who needs paperwork to work in another country, you'll encounter every variation of wording between these two styles. This year, reading these phrases correctly has become far more important. A new fee splits H-1B candidates into two categories with very different costs, even when their skills are identical.

What is the screening question actually asking?

The FieldAI-style double question has two parts. The first asks whether you can legally work in the location. The second asks whether you now or in the future require sponsorship. The word "future" targets people who can work now but hold only temporary work authorization and will need to file another application later.

A student in the US on an F-1 visa doing Optional Practical Training (OPT) can answer "yes" to the first question but still has to answer "yes" to the second.

Don't check "no" on the second question just because you can work right now. If you answer incorrectly here, it will surface sooner or later when the company prepares its filings, by which point both sides will have wasted their time.

What matters more is understanding the purpose of the question. It gives the company data to categorize your application. It says nothing about whether the company has a sponsorship policy.

Who does the $100,000 fee fall on?

According to Boston University's international student office's summary of US Citizenship and Immigration Services (USCIS) guidance, the $100,000 fee applies to new H-1B petitions filed after September 21, 2025 for employees outside the US — that is, people who need to obtain a visa at a consulate.

Petitions filed before that date are not charged the fee. Employers must attach a pay.gov payment receipt or proof of exemption, or USCIS will reject the petition.

The law firm DLA Piper adds two points. The fee is charged once per petition, not annually. Amendments, extensions, and change-of-status petitions filed for people already inside the US are also outside the fee's scope.

Imagine two candidates applying for the same FDE role. The first is on OPT in Boston, and the company only needs to file a change of status within the US. The second is an engineer in Bangalore, and the company must file a new H-1B petition so he can get a visa at a consulate.

Both might take the same take-home assessment and score identically. But only the second candidate's application is affected by the $100,000 fee: the fee is currently blocked by the courts, but it could still be collected once the injunction is lifted.

Which category you fall into changes your conversation with the recruiter. So before you click submit, you need to know exactly whether your situation is a change of status or a new petition.

Courts are blocking the fee, but the fee hasn't gone away

The fee's legal status is still shifting. According to Envoy Global, on September 30, 2026, a court in California issued a preliminary injunction striking down the policy used to collect the fee.

Envoy Global also reported that the First Circuit Court of Appeals declined to stay a June ruling pending appeal. Even so, the presidential proclamation itself has not been overturned.

According to Business Today, as of October 25, 2026, USCIS has begun approving some petitions without the fee being paid. The agency says it will comply with court orders, but will collect the fee if those orders are lifted.

For job seekers, the key point is that USCIS may still collect the fee later. A fee that is suspended but could be retroactively collected remains a risk that hiring teams must factor into headcount planning.

Don't use court news to argue with recruiters that the fee no longer exists. What you should do is clearly explain which type of petition your situation requires, and let them assess the risk correctly themselves.

Which signals in job postings are real?

Anthropic's line is a good example of policy put into writing, even though that posting targets Germany and has nothing to do with the H-1B. It has all three elements: a clear stance, a stated limitation, and a commitment to reasonable efforts after an offer is extended.

The company itself advises candidates to ask recruiters about the specific role they're applying for. Read alongside FieldAI's screening questions, it shows how much the information these two styles give you can differ.

The last column is something you have to bring to the call yourself. No job posting will answer these questions for you — not even the most clearly written one.

Ask in the first round, and ask specifically

Imagine passing several rounds of interviews, each requiring a week of preparation, only to learn in the final round that the role offers no sponsorship for people outside the country. All that time wasted. So raise the visa question in your very first call with the recruiter, before anyone has invested much effort.

Open with facts, not a request for accommodation. If you're on OPT, you can state that starting next year you'll need an H-1B through a change of status within the US.

If you're outside the US, say directly that the company would need to file a new H-1B petition. That one sentence tells the recruiter which of the categories above you fall into.

Then ask exactly what Anthropic's posting admits needs to be asked: does this specific role offer sponsorship? Then ask whether the company is currently filing petitions for candidates outside the US.

The third question targets the worst case: if the H-1B isn't feasible, does the company hire FDEs in other offices? Anthropic has FDE roles in Munich, so this question isn't far-fetched.

This is itself an FDE skill

Treat this call as a small-scale customer needs survey: ask the hardest questions early, before either side has invested too much. By stating your visa status clearly in the first round and asking about policy directly, the recruiter also gets to see how you ask questions.

Put a brief line about your work eligibility at the very top of your resume, using precise terminology, such as "F-1 OPT, requires H-1B change of status". Don't make the reader guess. This advice applies whether you're applying in Munich, London, or any other city that requires work authorization.

Fee rules will continue to change with each ruling. But your immigration situation, and the questions you ask recruiters, remain in your own hands from the very first call.
