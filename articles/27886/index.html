Factory AI FDE: Aitomatic, TSMC and skills to learn

FDE Times · 2026-10-31 · 7 min read

Summary

Drawing on Aitomatic's public materials and TSMC's AI Forward Deployed Engineer posting in Phoenix, the article argues that in factory AI the hardest FDE work lies outside the model: extracting expert knowledge from process and equipment engineers, joining production data across systems, and measuring errors by defect class. Aitomatic's self-reported 94% accuracy on 2,400 defects per shift could still mean 144 misclassifications, so where errors fall matters more than the headline number. The author also notes that software engineering fundamentals remain the entry ticket, and that deployment work may increasingly flow through integrators like FPT and ITOCHU Techno-Solutions.

  • The bottleneck is capturing expert knowledge on site, not fine-tuning models.
  • TSMC's Phoenix FDE posting still requires solid software engineering and agile skills.
  • 94% accuracy can hide 144 misclassifications per shift; measure by defect class.
  • Design a hand-off branch where uncertain cases go to a human.
  • Look beyond AI companies' job boards to integrators and manufacturers for FDE work.

“A confident wrong answer is dangerous on a fab floor.” The line does not come from a workplace safety manual. It sits on the homepage of Aitomatic, a company selling what it calls “Digital Engineers” for physical industry.

For anyone who wants to work as an FDE, that sentence says more than most job descriptions. No public FDE posting from Aitomatic has turned up, so any picture of what the role looks like at the company should be treated as inference.

Still, the company’s public materials point in a fairly clear direction. In factory AI, the hard part of a deployment most likely sits outside the model: getting the knowledge out of engineers’ heads, connecting it to production data, and then proving the system knows when it might be wrong. If this is the field you are aiming for, those are the things to practice.

An open foundation model is only part of the story

SemiKong, Aitomatic’s open-source model for semiconductors, is built on Llama 3.1 70B, according to a Meta case study. That detail alone does not say where the hard work lies, since fine-tuning a foundation model for semiconductors can be grueling too.

The clearer clue is in how the company is described. Meta writes that Aitomatic helps semiconductor companies capture and scale their deep expertise through Domain-Expert Agents (DXA).

A 2024 case analysis on the Zilliz blog describes the OpenSSA architecture, a set of small specialized agents, with a process that begins by capturing knowledge from process engineers and equipment experts.

Plenty of people can download an open foundation model. The expertise in a factory’s engineers’ heads has to be gathered on site. That is the reason to infer that the hard part of the deployer’s job lies here.

So the first task is most likely a conversation rather than a line of code. If you have never deliberately practiced this skill, start here.

Say you ask an equipment engineer: “When do you stop the machine?” He replies: “When this signal looks a bit off.” That answer is not usable yet, and asking more about the general procedure rarely helps.

Ask about the last three times he stopped the machine instead. Which signal looked off, off compared with what, was there a time it looked off and he kept running, and who had the authority to decide. Each of those stories gives you a draft rule along with a test case drawn from real life.

Phoenix shows where the bottleneck is

Why go to such lengths over expert knowledge? The Zilliz post uses TSMC as an example: the same technology still produces different results across fab projects, and the fab in Phoenix, Arizona has struggled for lack of local expertise.

The bottleneck is not confined to the US. VnEconomy, in a piece published on March 13, 2025 around AISC 2025, summarized the view of Aitomatic’s CEO that Vietnam still lacks AI experts and highly skilled semiconductor engineers. The two settings are very different, but both turn on people with expertise.

The telling detail is TSMC Arizona’s posting for an AI Forward Deployed Engineer, also in Phoenix. Don’t rush to treat the two facts as cause and effect. What is worth reading is one requirement in the ad: a solid grasp of software engineering fundamentals and agile development methods.

One line does not capture an entire job posting, but it is enough to draw a lesson. To get into factory AI, there is no need to abandon your trade and relearn semiconductors from scratch. Software engineering remains the ticket in; knowledge of the factory is what you must pick up fast enough to talk with the people running the machines.

Six items on a homepage make a data exercise

Aitomatic’s homepage describes the context of semiconductor manufacturing in six terms: equipment, recipe, lot, process state, quality and yield. Read with an engineer’s eye, this is close to the brief for a first week at a client.

Take a lot whose yield drops, and an engineer who asks the agent why. To answer, the system has to know which equipment the lot passed through, which recipe it ran, what the process state was at the time, and which run each quality result belongs to.

Now suppose the lot ID in the quality system does not match the one in the equipment log. However good the model, the answer then rests on two wrongly joined records, delivered in a very confident voice.

So the first thing to do is pick a single real question, map the data path for that question, and check every join before touching the prompt.

94% can still mean 144 errors a shift

Aitomatic reports one result in semiconductors: 2,400 defects classified per shift at 94% accuracy. This is the company’s own claim, with no independent evaluation, so treat it as a place to start asking questions, not a conclusion.

The arithmetic is simple: if that rate applies across the shift, 6% of 2,400 is 144 misclassifications. The 94% figure says nothing about where those 144 fall.

Suppose the shift includes 100 defects of a type that can ruin an entire lot. In theory, the system still scores 94% even if it misclassifies all 100 of them, and it also scores 94% if all 144 errors land on harmless defects.

The same number can describe a serious risk or a good tool, and only analysis by defect class can tell the two apart.

At the client, this is where your work begins. Ask the engineers which defect types they cannot afford to miss, measure those separately, and design a branch where the system hands uncertain cases to a person instead of guessing. The warning about the fab floor at the top of this article, written as code, looks exactly like that branch.

The jobs may not be only at Aitomatic

In February 2025, Aitomatic announced that its DXA Factory platform had been chosen by ITOCHU Techno-Solutions and FPT for global deployment, with FPT targeting industrial AI. If this model spreads, much of the deployment work may flow through systems integrators.

This is still inference, but it suggests a wider way to look for work. Don’t search only for the title “FDE” on an AI company’s careers page.

Read postings from integrators and manufacturers as well, and look for the real signs: direct work with process or equipment engineers, who maintains the system after go-live, and whether production data falls within the scope of the job.

The same announcement describes the final stage of the DXA lifecycle as production deployment with continuous optimization. Use that phrase as a template for your CV. A line that only says “built agents for factories” says very little; a line stating whom you extracted rules from, which defect class you measured and what you fixed after going to production tells the whole story of the work.

In an interview, you can also turn the tables with one question: “The last time your system gave a confidently wrong answer, what happened next?” How they answer tells you where the real work is.

A demo can win on smooth answers. On a fab floor the test is much harder: the first time your system says “not sure,” the engineer on shift still trusts it.
