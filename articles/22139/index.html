The FDE That AI Giants Are Fighting Over: What Exactly Makes It So Valuable?

Original title: AI巨头争抢的FDE，到底值钱在哪里?

腾讯新闻 · 2026-10-28 · 6 min read

Summary

Since the start of this year, tech companies worldwide have announced layoffs of nearly 200,000 people. Meanwhile, hiring demand for one AI role is exploding: in the US, job postings have grown more than 30-fold in three years, with a median salary of about $195,000; in China, postings for related positions have surged 21-fold in a year.

OpenAI, Anthropic, Microsoft, Amazon, and Google have together poured nearly $10 billion into the effort—some launching new companies, others building new departments—all competing for the same kind of talent. The most counterintuitive part: the scarcest skill for this role is not writing code.

Anthropic: Training 10,000 "people who bring AI into enterprises"

On October 2, Anthropic, the developer of Claude, announced a $100 million investment to establish the "Claude Frontier Academy," with the goal of training 10,000 "Forward Deployed Engineers" by the end of 2027.

The first cohort consists of working engineers selected from partners, including Accenture, Morgan Stanley, Novo Nordisk, and several top consulting firms. The training has two phases: first, a 4-day in-person intensive that begins with a "simulated enterprise deployment"; after passing the assessment, participants return to their own companies and spend 12 weeks working hands-on on a real project. The first certifications are expected to be completed in early 2027.

In Anthropic's view, the bottleneck to enterprise AI adoption is not the models, but the lack of people who can drive change. Steve Corfield, the executive in charge of the program, said that what customers lack now are people who both understand how the business operates and can personally take AI from an idea to a deployed system.

This is only the latest example in this year's AI talent war among tech giants.

Pouring billions in, the giants are all competing for the same kind of talent

In May this year, OpenAI established "OpenAI Deployed," a company dedicated to helping enterprises adopt AI, led by TPG with a first funding round of over $4 billion. The same month, Anthropic jointly founded an AI services company with Blackstone, Hellman & Friedman, and Goldman Sachs, with an initial investment of about $1.5 billion from all parties.

From late June to early July, Amazon AWS and Microsoft made moves in succession: AWS invested $1 billion to build its own deployment engineering team; Microsoft invested $2.5 billion to establish a new department, stationing about 6,000 engineering experts at customer enterprises. In September, Google Cloud and Accenture formed a joint business unit, planning to train up to 1,000 Accenture engineers to be deployed on-site at customers.

In the past, these companies competed on whose models were stronger, whose parameters were bigger, and whose benchmark scores were higher; now they are all pouring money into the same direction: sending people into customer companies to actually put AI to use.

This type of talent has a unified name in the industry: FDE (Forward Deployed Engineer), commonly translated into Chinese as "前沿部署工程师."

Job postings up 30-fold in three years, big companies offering million-yuan packages

Readers familiar with US stocks will not be unfamiliar with the origin of this term. More than a decade ago, Palantir systematically pioneered this approach: instead of sitting at headquarters writing generic products, engineers are stationed on-site with customers, working with the same data and carrying the same business metrics, gradually turning vague requirements and scattered data into usable systems. Palantir CEO Alex Karp has a judgment repeatedly cited in the industry: large models are certainly important, but for at least the next few years, the value lies in implementation.

Today, this practice is becoming a hot profession. According to labor market analytics firm Lightcast, there were fewer than 200 FDE job postings nationwide in the US in 2023, but more than 7,300 in the first nine months of 2026—a more than 30-fold increase in three years. The median salary listed in these postings is about $195,000, more than three times the median salary of all US job postings. Among the employers posting the most FDE jobs, consulting firms like Deloitte and Accenture rank ahead of tech giants like Google. Clearly, FDE is no longer just a technical role at AI companies; anywhere AI needs to be applied to business needs such people.

The FDE wave has already reached China. Data from the Maimai platform shows that in the first half of 2026, postings for FDE-related positions on the platform surged 21-fold year over year. ByteDance's "Doubao AI Large Model FDE" role offers an annual package of up to about 1.05 million yuan, while Ant Digital Technologies, Zhipu, and others have also offered packages above 900,000 yuan. But even with high salaries on offer, suitable candidates remain hard to find: according to Liepin Big Data Research Institute, from September 2025 to August 2026, the supply-demand ratio for FDE talent in China was only 0.28—meaning nearly 4 positions competing for 1 person.

This gap is being amplified by policy. At the end of August, the Ministry of Industry and Information Technology issued a notice encouraging AI application service providers to build FDE teams and root themselves at user sites; Wuhan Optics Valley proposed cultivating 1,000 FDEs in three years, and Shanghai proposed establishing an FDE engineer reserve of over 1,000 people. Enterprises, service providers, and local governments are all demanding talent, and qualified FDEs are far from enough.

AI adoption is hard on the business side, and what's scarcest is precisely people who understand the business

Seeing this, many people might think: this is an opportunity for young programmers—I don't write code, and I'm past the age of switching careers. But consider how Anthropic describes a good FDE: familiar with agent systems, skilled at problem-solving, good at communication, with engineering experience. Half of these four criteria lie outside technology.

The hardest part of AI adoption was never in the code. Over the past two years, almost every enterprise has bought AI products and run pilots, but far fewer have actually made them part of daily business. An S&P Global survey shows that the share of companies abandoning most of their AI projects rose from 17% to 42% within a year; a 2025 report from IDC and Lenovo was even more direct: 88% of AI proof-of-concept projects ultimately failed to reach production.

Everything goes smoothly in demos, but once in real business, you run into dirty data, legacy systems, permission approvals, employee habits, and that unavoidable question: who is responsible when things go wrong. This is also the difference between an FDE and on-site outsourcing: outsourcing does whatever the client says; an FDE must first judge whether AI should be used at all, which model to use, how much to spend, and how to prove effectiveness—before deploying.

So the truly valuable half of an FDE's capabilities is understanding the business. Which clauses in a contract matter, which number in a report could cause trouble, where an approval process gets stuck, which task handed to a machine would keep the boss up at night—AI cannot replace these, and people who have worked in an industry for ten or twenty years understand them best.

Zhang Yu, Vice President of Tencent Cloud, recently said something that struck a nerve with many enterprises: in the past, companies paid big money for external teams to do projects, and "once the project was done, the team left, taking the capabilities with them." More and more enterprises realize that rather than waiting for external teams to come and go, it's better to let their own business-savvy people develop this capability.

If you have been deeply engaged in an industry for years, what you lack has never been experience, but the step of handing that experience to AI to execute: knowing what today's AI can and cannot do, how to build it, how much it will cost, and how to handle failures. Our upcoming training camp is designed precisely for this step.

Covering the last mile of AI adoption in two days

Wallstreetcn's first "FDE Practical Training Camp" will open in Shanghai on November 14-15. This is an in-person, hands-on camp limited to 50 seats. Over two days, it will guide you to build your first AI agent with your own hands, walking through the entire process from model selection and cost calculation to evals and deployment.

First, let's talk about what an agent is. Many people's impression of AI is still stuck on chat that "answers one question at a time," but an agent is different: you hand it a task, and it will look up information, open software, read and write files, and deliver results on its own—and if it makes mistakes, it can redo the work itself. When the giants send FDEs into enterprises, this is exactly the technology they are deploying.

This two-day bootcamp covers 12 modules, from how to choose models and calculate pricing all the way to evals, cost optimization, and secure deployment—all methods that major companies actually use to put AI into production. Lead instructor Right is a former Senior Algorithm Engineer at Tencent, where he led the algorithm design and large-scale rollout of a core WeChat AI agent project, with deep expertise in agent system design, model training, and Harness architecture. He has also published multiple agent-related papers at top conferences such as NeurIPS and ACL, and his work has been cited by Google DeepMind.

Who is this course for

  • Business owners and managers: those who want to understand exactly what AI can do for their company, how much each task costs, and where the risks lie
  • Professionals in finance, legal, accounting, operations, consulting, and other fields: those with lots of repetitive work who want to build a tool themselves and hand it over to AI
  • Anyone who wants to add a hard skill to their toolkit for the AI era

Thirty years ago, the people who learned Excel first saved themselves countless late nights over the following two decades.

Today, tech giants are spending billions of dollars competing for people who understand business and know how to use AI—and you already have half of that. The other half can be filled in over these two days.
