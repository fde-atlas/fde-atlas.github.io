What's the difference between simple outsourcing and FDEs? AWS's strategy of embedding AI teams in financial institutions

Original title: 単なる外注とFDEは何が違う？ AWSが金融機関にAI部隊を常駐させる狙い

TechTargetジャパン · 2026-09-09 · 7 min read

Summary

This TechTarget Japan article examines AWS's roughly $1 billion Forward Deployed Engineering program, which embeds generative AI specialists inside major financial institutions such as JPMorgan Chase, Goldman Sachs, Barclays and HSBC. AWS's John Cain argues FDE differs from outsourcing: teams leave behind AI infrastructure and processes so customers can scale independently rather than depending on one-off projects. A case study covers Danske Bank, which uses generative tools to rebuild legacy applications from extracted business requirements. Cain says development-to-production cycles have shortened by 70%. The article also notes regulatory concerns: the UK has designated AWS, Google Cloud, Microsoft and Oracle as critical third parties, regulated by the Bank of England and FCA from July 2026, with a 24-hour outage potentially costing £650m–£1bn.

Key points

  • AWS invests about $1 billion in Forward Deployed Engineering, embedding generative AI experts at financial clients.
  • FDE differs from outsourcing by leaving infrastructure and processes that let customers scale on their own.
  • Danske Bank rebuilds legacy apps from business requirements using generative AI tools, not simple code translation.
  • AWS claims development-to-production cycles shortened by 70% for clients.
  • UK regulators designated AWS, Google Cloud, Microsoft and Oracle as critical third parties, regulated from July 2026.

AWS is investing heavily in a new strategy that goes beyond simply providing infrastructure, sending generative AI specialists directly to major financial firms. This article examines the full picture of this hands-on support, which cuts development time by 70%, as well as the moves by regulators who are increasingly worried about dependence on giant cloud providers.

Amazon Web Services (AWS) began its serious push into the financial services sector 10 years ago and has evolved its offerings in step with changes in the industry.

About 10 years ago, offering unlimited processing capacity on a pay-as-you-go basis was a major differentiator. AWS and a handful of other hyperscalers delivered on this, but today AWS is far more than just a pipe connecting to customers' system infrastructure.

The company embedded itself with some of the world's largest banks early on. "We focused on large enterprises early on because of the complexity of their operations," says John Cain, Global Head of Financial Services Market Development at AWS. "Focusing on large enterprises set a high bar for our ability to operate globally in regulated markets."

Companies such as JPMorgan Chase, Goldman Sachs, Barclays, and HSBC are now referred to in press releases not as customers but as "partners." That word is a fitting description of the track record both sides have built together.

According to Cain, when financial institutions adopted AWS technology to gain the flexibility and processing power they needed, they had concerns about security, compliance, and governance. "We have taken a careful approach to this industry," he adds.

AWS embedded deep into financial services

A decade on, AWS is using a $1 billion-scale investment in what it calls "Forward Deployed Engineering" (FDE) to station generative AI specialists on-site at financial services customers.

At first glance this may seem like traditional outsourcing, but Cain rejects that characterization. This program "helps customers build the technical foundation they need in the era of generative AI," he says. "There are an enormous number of customers who need support to drive generative AI innovation in-house."

FDE teams are cross-functional, bringing together experts in technology, science, and financial business. Cain himself started his career as an engineer before moving to the business side, and describes the financial industry as "a perfect fit."

"I started my career as an engineer and gradually shifted my focus to the business side. One of the great things about financial services is that it is a technology-driven industry. If you understand the technology, the business is easier to understand," says Cain.

This may sound like a description of the typical outsourcing industry, but Cain insists there is a crucial difference. "FDE teams not only help solve business problems; rather than ending with a one-off project, they leave behind a foundation of generative AI infrastructure and processes so customers can accelerate their own growth on top of it."

FDE is delivered through professional services contracts, but the relationship changes as customers become more self-sufficient, Cain explains. "FDE teams support customers in becoming self-sufficient and scaling on their own."

Today it is not just major banks: payment system operators, securities exchanges, and other industry leaders are also working closely with AWS to adopt cutting-edge technology.

The right time and the right place

Ten years ago, AWS offered enterprises on-demand cloud processing capacity on a pay-as-you-go basis, positioning itself at the starting point of the financial industry's transformation. That effort laid the groundwork for the AI-driven technological innovation now underway.

"It is hard to imagine that enterprises could have adopted generative AI this quickly without the benefits of cloud infrastructure like AWS," Cain says.

At the same time, major tech companies like AWS have gone beyond simply providing computing resources, offering practical experience and support to customers adopting the latest technologies such as AI through programs like FDE.

"Considering the investment required to build foundation models, set up inference environments, and deploy them at scale while keeping pace with the industry's pace of innovation, even the largest customers would find it difficult to do it alone," says Cain.

One of the results AWS has delivered to customers is a dramatic improvement in development team efficiency. Cain calls the gains in developer productivity "remarkable," noting that the time from business idea to code to production has been cut by 70% compared with before.

Beyond new development: modernizing legacy systems

AI technology is also helping enterprises modernize aging legacy systems without compromising existing functionality. One bank benefiting from this is Denmark's Danske Bank. As part of its strategy "Forward '28", which includes a digital transformation (DX) plan, the bank began working with AWS in 2024.

In the first phase of the project, the bank worked with AWS to build a platform for migrating legacy applications to the cloud. In the recent expansion of its efforts, it is using AWS's cloud, AI, and engineering resources to develop services for digital customers.

"Danske Bank is a great example of using generative tools not simply to translate code, but to explain what the code does from a business logic perspective," Cain says. "The bank extracts its business requirements, feeds them into modern generative AI coding tools, and builds new applications based on business requirements rather than on the legacy code."

As dependence on vendors such as AWS, Microsoft, and Google grows, the question arises of how financial institutions can maintain a competitive edge with their own internal resources. In an environment where every company has similar access to the major vendors' technology, Cain emphasizes that each financial institution's "data" and "corporate culture" are the differentiating factors.

"In leveraging these new technologies, it is not just about technical modernization; the business side needs to think about how quickly it can develop products and bring them to market. That is the real transformation," says Cain.

The growing presence of AWS and similar providers in the financial sector has not escaped the attention of financial regulators around the world. In the UK, there are calls for stricter regulation to be applied to companies like Amazon. According to a report by the UK Cyber Monitoring Centre (CMC), a 24-hour system outage at a major AWS or Microsoft Azure cloud region in the UK, Ireland, Europe, or the eastern US could cause severe economic disruption across the UK, resulting in direct revenue losses of £650 million to £1 billion. Given such potential impact, the move toward tighter regulation is hardly surprising.

UK financial regulators are currently monitoring the IT vendors on which the financial sector depends for its operations. As initial regulatory targets, the UK subsidiaries of AWS, Google Cloud, Microsoft, and Oracle were designated as "critical third parties" (CTPs).

These four companies, which provide critical infrastructure supporting the UK financial sector, including cloud services, have been subject to regulation by the Bank of England and the Financial Conduct Authority (FCA) since July 2026. More companies may become subject to similar regulation in the future.

Cain accepts the fact that AWS is under regulatory scrutiny. "As AWS has grown in scale, I understand that we will face tighter scrutiny. We have been working on regulatory compliance even before any direct regulatory orders were issued," said Cain.
