Origin: The Palantir Model · OpenFDE

Original title: 起源：Palantir 模式

OpenFDE · 2026-10-16 · 4 min read

Summary

This article traces how the FDE model originated at Palantir out of necessity: intelligence-agency customers wouldn't reveal their needs or trust Silicon Valley, so founders ran demo-driven product discovery and turned on-site deployment into a structured practice. Field teams pair Echo (domain experts managing accounts) with Delta (rapid-prototyping engineers). Bob McGrew's "gravel road versus paved superhighway" metaphor captures how field customizations get generalized by headquarters, with Ontology as the flagship abstraction. The author argues AI agents, lacking ready-made products, make FDE the PMF playbook of this era; Palantir's alumni network has spawned 350+ startups.

Key points

  • Palantir invented FDE practices out of necessity with secretive intelligence clients.
  • Field teams pair Echo domain experts with Delta prototyping engineers.
  • Gravel roads (field work) get paved into highways (generalized product).
  • Ontology abstracts semantics into objects, properties, and links.
  • AI agents need product discovery, reviving the FDE model today.

The classic archetype of the FDE comes from Palantir. It was not a carefully designed role, but a set of tactics forced out of a startup in a desperate situation where "no one was willing to tell you the requirements, and no one believed Silicon Valley could do defense." Once you understand why Palantir had to do this back then, you understand why AI companies today have rediscovered it all over again.

It all started with a demo

When Palantir was founded, it set out to build software for intelligence agencies. The difficulty was: the team didn't know anyone in intelligence, and even if they found someone, that person wouldn't tell them "what I actually do every day." So founder Stefan Cohen took an approach that was highly unusual at the time—build a demo first and show it to potential customers. The customer would say "this thing is terrible, it has nothing to do with our work," and Cohen would follow up with "then how would you want it to be different?", write down all the feedback, and go back to iterate.

Under the classic PMF paradigm, startups can do things that don't scale early on, but after finding PMF they should pull away, focus on scaling, and treat all customers equally. But Palantir's market didn't allow it—intelligence, law enforcement, the military, even counter-proliferation and counterterrorism all have vastly different workflows. Early member Shyam Sankar simply turned this logic upside down.

Rather than building separate versions for each customer and piling up features that only fit one customer, it's better to make the product a highly customizable platform, then station engineers on-site to deeply understand users. By conventional thinking, all of this counts as "services" and should be minimized; but Shyam had FDEs turn on-site work into a process of product discovery.

Echo and Delta: the two roles of the field team

Palantir's field teams are not lone heroes, but pairs of two completely different types of people fighting together.

Stationed at the customer site, talking with users to figure out which demo or use case is truly valuable for that scenario, while also serving as the account manager managing the customer relationship. The ideal profile is a domain expert (say, a former Army officer or a senior medical practitioner) with a "rebel" quality—Shyam even calls them "heretics": they not only understand the existing way of doing things, but can also see where it falls short. Palantir Blog↗

Is a software engineer, extremely good at writing code quickly, turning ideas into working products, and deploying them for customers. The wrong hire for Delta is the craftsman-type engineer who pursues abstract perfection and hopes the code won't need changes for ten years; what you want is someone who can figure out how to get the product built, even if the first version is rough and has to be torn down and rebuilt. Prototyping ability is the core skill of this team. Dev vs Delta↗

Internally, Palantir also splits the engineering role into Dev and Delta: Dev is "one capability serving many customers," while Delta / FDSE is "one customer needing many capabilities." The boundary between the two can blur, but the division of labor is clear—one fixes the platform, the other goes into the field. Dev vs Delta↗

Gravel roads and superhighways

The most refined metaphor for the FDE model comes from Bob McGrew—Palantir's second engineer and later Chief Research Officer at OpenAI.

The FDE comes in with the existing product, fills the gap between product capability and actual needs, and paves the road into a "gravel road." The product and engineering teams at headquarters then abstract and generalize these field practices into a "paved superhighway" that can serve the next five to ten customers. This is really doing things that don't scale at scale. YC Library↗

The most classic case of abstracting field work into a general product is Palantir Ontology. The initial instinct was to build one table for "people" and one table for "funds," but they quickly found this couldn't generalize across institutions. So they raised the level of abstraction: the bottom layer keeps only extremely general primitives—objects, properties, media, and links—and leaves the specific semantics of "this is a person," "this is a ship," "this is a flow of funds" to the Ontology to encode in each customer's scenario. This is the other half of the FDE model—the general foundation and on-site customization are equally important, otherwise it really becomes consulting.

Why it is the PMF paradigm for the Agent era

Bob's judgment is that the FDE model differs from standard SaaS in one fundamental way. SaaS pursues "doing less and less for each customer while contract size stays the same"; the FDE model is the opposite—contract size grows larger and larger, because you are delivering increasingly valuable outcomes for the customer. The KPI for measuring it is not how much custom code was written, but the value of the delivered outcomes, and whether product leverage is growing over time.

Focus on one of the top five priorities of the customer's leadership, and genuinely land a specific high-impact problem from within.

As you stay longer at the customer, the product fits the needs better and better through product discovery, cost per value falls, and margins turn from negative to positive.

Abstract the field practices into capabilities that can serve the next batch of customers, then "land and expand" from one market segment to the next.

Why are AI agents today replaying this scene? Bob's answer: AI agents have no ready-made product. No one knows exactly what "building an agent" means; there is a massive amount of product discovery work, and it can only be done starting from business practice inside enterprises—which is precisely the home turf of the FDE model. He even offered an analogy: OpenAI is like the headquarters product team, while the countless startups out there are the FDEs figuring out how to get research results adopted. OpenAI↗

FDE training is essentially founder training—you're learning all the skills of startup founders, just with a powerful product lever in hand. This explains why Palantir has incubated 350+ tech companies: Anduril, ElevenLabs (whose co-founder is a former Palantir FDE), Hex, Ironclad, Blend, Sourcegraph, and more all come from this alumni network.

Palantir's classic combination is "strong platform + FDSE + Deployment Strategist," and most AI companies today are replicating or adapting this structure.
