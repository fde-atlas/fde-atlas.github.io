Consultants vs. FDEs: Solving Problems with Technology

Original title: コンサルとFDEの違い｜技術で解く選択肢

日本Fde協会 · 2026-08-04 · 5 min read

Summary

This article by the Japan FDE Association contrasts FDEs with consultants. Both solve customer problems, but consultants typically end at proposals and strategy documents, while FDEs design and build working systems themselves, covering requirements, implementation, AI agents, and operations. The article highlights an often-overlooked FDE responsibility: governance, including access control, logging, audit trails, documentation, and even halting unnecessary development. It advises choosing consulting for strategy-level work and FDE for end-to-end ownership, noting the two roles are complementary rather than opposed, and mentions the association's private FDE certification.

Key points

  • Consultants deliver proposals; FDEs build and run working systems.
  • FDEs must handle access control, logs, audit trails, and documentation.
  • FDEs also make judgment calls, stopping development that shouldn't happen.
  • Career advice: strategy-minded people fit consulting; builders fit FDE.
  • The two roles are complementary, chosen by project phase.

"How is that different from being a consultant?" — Whenever we explain what an FDE (Forward Deployed Engineer) is, this is almost always the first question we get. As the Japan FDE Association, we are not in the business of steering people toward any particular service, so we can answer this question neutrally from the standpoint of practitioners who have actually worked as FDEs. After reading this article, you will be able to explain the difference between FDEs and consultants in your own words, and judge which path better fits your career or the challenges you currently face.

The Decisive Difference Between FDEs and Consultants: Whether They Take Responsibility Through Implementation

Consultants and FDEs share one thing in common: both "solve customer problems." The difference becomes clearest after the problem has been sorted out.

Ending with a Proposal, or Building Something That Works

A consultant's deliverables are, in most cases, "proposals" and "strategy documents." They articulate what should be done and support decision-making — and that carries great value. FDEs, on the other hand, go a step further. Beyond interviewing stakeholders and organizing the issues, they actually design business applications and workflows, build AI agents and data integrations, and create a working operational setup with their own hands.

"Doing It Yourself" Is the Core of FDE

Representative Director Maekawa, during his time as a PM at a major engineering firm, managed a development team of eight and more than twenty stakeholders while handling both interdepartmental coordination and reporting to management himself. For a shipping and receiving management system for an oil refinery complex, he worked hands-on through the entire process, from requirements definition to implementation and operations/maintenance. Not purely technical, not purely business — he has experienced firsthand the value of having someone on-site who understands both. This experience underpins our definition of the FDE not as someone who "proposes" but as someone who "solves."

Comparing the Scope of FDEs and Consultants in a Table

When you place the two roles side by side, the boundary becomes visible. This is not about which is superior; they simply cover different ranges of responsibility.

PerspectiveConsultantFDE
Main deliverablesProposals, strategy, analysisWorking systems and established operations
Endpoint of involvementSupporting decision-makingEstablishing operations and continuous improvement
Technical implementationGenerally outsourcedHands-on, done by oneself
Measure of successQuality of the proposalWhether it actually works in the field

When we receive consultations from SaaS company executives, we often encounter situations where impressive strategy documents exist but nothing is moving on the ground. We see the FDE's role as personally filling the "implementation gap" between documents and the field.

The Often-Overlooked Ability to Handle Governance and Audits

Most explanations of FDE on the market stop at "an engineer who embeds themselves in the customer's operations." But what is truly tested in the field goes beyond that.

Areas That Matter After the Build

Once AI or systems are embedded into business operations, questions inevitably arise: "Who can access which data?" "Are operation logs being kept?" "Are job responsibilities appropriately divided?" This includes access control and log design, securing audit-ready evidence, and documentation and handover to prevent over-reliance on a single person. Whether an FDE can cover all of this determines whether an implementation endures or remains a one-off.

Also Making the Call to "Stop" Things in the Field

In manufacturing client environments, governance considerations carry particular weight. Representative Director Maekawa often cites the reaction: "I was shocked when I was first told, 'Do we really need this?'" Being able to build something is not the same as it being worth building. Stopping unnecessary development and preemptively addressing governance risks is also part of an FDE's job. This scope — extending into the "after the build" phase — is fundamentally different in nature from consulting, whose primary work is making proposals.

Which One Should You Choose?

In terms of career, you can frame it like this: if you find value in designing overall strategy and supporting decision-making, and want to make high-level problem structuring your strength, the consultant path suits you. On the other hand, if you want to build working things with your own hands and see them through to adoption in the field — based on our startup experience, people who find joy in that feeling of "owning it all the way to the end" are well suited to FDE.

Choosing Whom to Consult for Problem-Solving

If you are deciding whom to engage, the criterion is simple: if you want help organizing what should be done, choose a consultant; if you want end-to-end responsibility from structuring through implementation, operations, and governance compliance, choose an FDE. The two are not opposing concepts — you use them according to the phase. For the difference between FDEs and SEs/SIers, see this article; for how to start the career, see How to Become an FDE: A Roadmap from Zero Experience.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q. Can you be both an FDE and a consultant?

A. In practice, the boundaries sometimes overlap. Working in a consulting manner up through problem structuring, then personally handling everything from implementation to operational adoption, is how FDEs work.

Q. Is FDE a more technical role than consulting?

A. While FDE is grounded in technology, business-side perspectives — such as business interviews and attention to governance and audits — are also essential. Its defining feature is combining both technical and business capabilities.

Q. Is there a way to demonstrate FDE practical skills?

A. There is the FDE Certification, a private examination operated by the Japan FDE Association. While it is not a national qualification, it can serve as a learning goal and a benchmark of practical skill. For details, see the certification page.
