Summary

This long-form Japanese guide by Shohei Tokumitsu, CEO of DeFactory, targets engineers considering a career as a Forward Deployed Engineer (FDE). It defines the FDE as an engineer-consultant hybrid who embeds at customer sites and owns outcomes from problem discovery through AI implementation and adoption, tracing the role's origins at Palantir. Drawing on DeFactory's placement data and bloomberry's analysis of 1,000 FDE job postings, it covers three FDE archetypes, required skills (Python, SQL, LLM work), compensation (¥10-25M salaried; ¥800K-2M+/month freelance), common field obstacles, and three typical transition paths from SES engineering.

Key points

  • FDEs originated at Palantir and take end-to-end responsibility for customer business outcomes, unlike SEs who deliver to spec.
  • Three archetypes exist: Builder (~90% coding), Sales Engineer (~40% coding), and Internal Tools Builder.
  • Japan salaries range ¥10-25M; freelance rates average ¥1.35M/month per DeFactory's 11 closed deals.
  • Core skills: Python, SQL on DWHs, and LLM implementation (required in 31% of postings); fast learning beats exhaustive knowledge.
  • Field challenges include user resistance, dirty data, and legacy integration; SES engineers can transition via three documented routes.

An FDE (Forward Deployed Engineer) is a hybrid "engineer × consultant" role who works on-site at client companies and is responsible for everything from AI implementation to embedding it into business operations.

For the definition of FDE and its origins at US-based Palantir, see What is an FDE? for a detailed explanation.

In addition, this article provides a systematic explanation for everyone considering a career as an FDE engineer, covering the job responsibilities, three types, required tech stacks, salary differences between full-time and freelance roles, challenges faced in the field and their solutions, and how to transition from an SES engineer, all from the practical perspective of DeFactory, which has supported the launch of FDE organizations.

An FDE engineer is defined as "an engineer who embeds themselves at the client site, adapts their company's products and technology to the client's business context, and drives the engagement until results are achieved." It is neither SES (quasi-delegated contracts) that provide resources, nor fixed-price contracts that deliver deliverables; the goal is "solving the client's business challenges with technology."

FDE project information - exclusively for currently active FDEs (Forward Deployed Engineers)

This is a posting by DeFactory itself, where a pair of at least two people - a consultant (PdM) and an engineer - join a client company and handle everything from identifying challenges to implementation, embedding within the organization, and internalization. The hourly rate is 5,500 yen and up for the engineer role and 6,000 yen and up for the PdM role, with fixed billing (hourly rate × estimated utilization).

Bob McGrew, former Chief Research Officer at OpenAI, describes FDE engineers as "productized consulting." While SES engineers work by "providing instructed tasks as man-hours," the value of FDE engineers lies in "proactively discovering the client's business challenges and implementing solutions with technology." The nature of this "productized consulting" and the changing role in the AI era are explained in detail in What is FDE Consulting? Explaining the New Roles, Skills, and Future Potential in the AI Era.

The origin of FDE engineers lies with the US data analytics company Palantir. Most of the company's key clients - government agencies and financial institutions - could rarely use off-the-shelf software as-is, with inconsistent data formats, complex business workflows, and undocumented tacit knowledge present on-site. To address this challenge, FDE (internally titled FDSE at Palantir, referred to as Delta) was born as a model in which engineers are deployed directly to client sites, repeatedly observing, experimenting, and building on the spot.

The main responsibilities of FDE engineers are as follows.

  • Requirements gathering at client sites: Interview executives, business owners, and on-site staff to extract the true challenges
  • Data integration and pipeline construction: Connect heterogeneous data from core systems, SaaS, spreadsheets, and more
  • Product customization and extension: Develop and implement missing features in the company's product on the spot
  • Workflow design and business integration: Design operations that naturally embed the product into business processes
  • Support from PoC to production operation: Provide consistent support from technical validation through production operation and scaling
  • Feedback to the product team: Feed insights gained at client sites back into product development at headquarters

In other words, an FDE engineer is neither "just a customer success" role nor "just an implementation support engineer," but a "junction between the field and the product" - someone who writes what is needed on the spot and evolves the product based on feedback from the field.

The way FDE engineers work is not uniform; they are broadly classified into three types depending on the proportion of development work.

① Builder FDE: Builds AI systems at client sites and deploys them to production

A type where about 90% of working hours are spent on development tasks (coding, etc.). Areas of responsibility include PoC design, minimal implementation through productionization (MVP development), AI workflow and agent construction, LLM algorithm tuning, rapid demo implementation, and more.

② Sales Engineer FDE: Technical consulting and architecture selection proposals

A type where about 40% of working hours are spent on development, with the rest devoted to technology selection and client negotiations. Responsible for validating technical feasibility from the sales stage and designing the implementation approach after winning the contract.

③ Internal Tools Builder FDE: Development for business improvement (e.g., internal DX)

A type that carries out practical work based on internal customer challenges. Rather than external clients, they treat business improvement within their own company or group as the "customer."

Note that in the Japanese market, models like DeFactory, which offer an FDE-style approach as a lab-type team without a specific product, have also emerged, and the FDE concept is evolving into diverse forms.

The work of FDE engineers rarely consists solely of coding time; it is characterized by alternating between design reviews, client presentations, business observation, and alignment meetings with the in-house product team.

Based on "A Day in the Life of a Palantir Forward Deployed Software Engineer" published by Palantir, a typical day for an FDE engineer is structured as follows.

  • Morning: Stand-up meeting with the customer's on-site staff
  • Late morning: Implementation work such as isolating and fixing the previous day's data pipeline failure
  • Afternoon: Sitting alongside the customer's operations department to directly observe business processes
  • Evening: Meeting with the in-house product team to share "generalization candidates observed in the field"

Palantir's job postings explicitly state "up to approximately 25% travel and on-site stays expected," showing that this is a different working style from typical engineering roles that assume full remote work.

The technology stack is merely a foundation. DeFactory Inc. defines professionals who can deliver results as FDE engineers along the following two axes.

Requirement 1: Ability to independently raise questions and identify problems from a business perspective

Rather than simply "building the features you were asked for," this is the ability to understand the customer's business structure and workflows, independently determine "what problem truly needs to be solved," and frame it as a question. Rather than acting after being handed requirements, whether you can stand on the side that defines the requirements itself is the first turning point in being evaluated as an FDE engineer.

Requirement 2: Ability to solve problems through development technology, including AI

Even if you can identify a problem, if you cannot turn it into an actual technical solution, only half of your value as an FDE engineer is realized. You need the ability to use technologies such as AI and data infrastructure to implement identified problems into solutions that work in the field.

These two requirements are not a simple addition of engineering skills and consulting skills, but a "multiplication" skill set where the two are integrated. If either one is missing, it becomes difficult to provide the essential value of an FDE engineer.

The typical workflow of an FDE engineer is not a waterfall model of "requirements definition → design → implementation → delivery," but an agile-style cycle integrated with the field. The standard approach is organized into six phases.

  • ① Discovery (problem identification: 1-2 weeks): Interviews with executives, business owners, and field staff, investigation of current business and data flows, and building consensus on the "problem to truly solve" and "success metrics (KPIs)"
  • ② Design (design: 1-2 weeks): Design of the solution architecture, phased roadmap planning (PoC → expansion → production operation → scaling), and return on investment (ROI) estimation
  • ③ Build MVP (minimum viable product: 2-4 weeks): Focus on the 1-2 business areas with the highest value to build an MVP and provide a "usable" experience in the field
  • ④ Iterate (iterative improvement: 2-8 weeks): Collect weekly feedback from field users, implement high-priority improvements, and quantitatively monitor usage rates and business KPIs
  • ⑤ Scale (production rollout: 1-3 months): Gradually expand target departments and business processes, build operational frameworks, and establish governance (access control, logging, auditing)
  • ⑥ Handover (internalization support: 1-2 months): Transfer knowledge to the customer's in-house operations team, prepare documentation, and conduct training

Running this cycle 1-2 times over 6-12 months is the standard pace for FDE projects.

Whether you're suited to be an FDE engineer can be judged more by your attitude toward work than by your technical skill.

Four traits of people who are a good fit

  • Feeling intellectual excitement about hacking into customers' business operations: People who find joy in the process of entering a new industry or organization and deciphering how things work on the ground
  • Not wanting to move away from writing code: People with a strong motivation to not just stop at design, but to build something that actually works from start to finish
  • Welcoming ambiguous specifications as "problems to solve": People who can see situations without clean specification documents as opportunities rather than stress
  • Wanting to make decisions with ownership and take responsibility through to results: People who can make both technical and business decisions themselves and own the outcomes

Four traits of people who are not a good fit

  • People who want to go deep in one technical area and become a specialist: For specialists who want to master distributed systems or compilers, other technical positions are a better fit
  • People who want to implement given specifications with high quality: For craftsman types who want to implement specifications to the highest quality exactly as written, product engineering is a better fit
  • People who are not good at customer interactions or external communication: Since dialogue with a wide range of people, from VP-level executives to frontline staff, is essential, the burden becomes heavy
  • People who dislike change or travel: Palantir assumes up to 25% travel, which can be exhausting for those who want to work with the same team for a long time

FDE engineers frequently face four walls: "frontline resistance," "the dilemma between customization and productization," "data quality issues," and "legacy integration." Overcoming these requires not only technical skill but also the ability to move people through dialogue and architectural design skills.

A very common failure pattern is when a system deployed by an FDE engineer enters the operational phase and immediately goes "unused" or "becomes a mere formality." Most of the causes stem not from technical problems but from organizational and human issues. Below is a summary of typical friction patterns and countermeasures.

Friction patternCauseCountermeasures taken by professional FDEs
Resistance such as "I don't want my work processes to change"Attachment to existing workflows and anxiety about changeGet key people (those with strong influence over operations) on your side first
Abandonment such as "it's a hassle to use"Confusing UI/UX or too many stepsUI design that blends into frontline workflows, embedding into existing tools
"In the end, we go back to Excel"Data updates are a hassle and the sense of management is lostDesign flows that automatically fetch data so users can focus only on judgment and approval
Criticism such as "the data can't be trusted"Data quality issues and mismatched update timingVisualize data quality monitoring and publish reliability metrics daily
Conflict between "frontline departments vs. IT departments"Blame-shifting and siloed organizationsEstablish a project sponsor structure involving management and put decision-making in writing

What professional FDE engineers particularly emphasize is creating "the first small success experience." If you can give frontline users the experience of "actually saving time" within the first 2-4 weeks of a project, the subsequent cooperation changes dramatically. This is a strategy called "Minimum Viable Wins" and is considered a fundamental FDE practice.

A structural conflict FDE engineers face is the dilemma that "the more you build custom solutions for the customer in front of you, the more product standardization falls behind." There are five main design principles for overcoming this dilemma.

  • Separate the "core" from the "periphery": Extract highly reusable processing into the product itself, and customer-specific processing into a thin external layer
  • Thoroughly design things to be "changeable via configuration": Don't hardcode specific requirements; build a structure where they can be switched via configuration files and parameters
  • Standardize by the third customer: Have a decision rule to immediately incorporate into the product itself when the same kind of request comes from a third customer
  • Regular feedback with the headquarters product team: Provide weekly feedback on "features needed in the field" and "wasteful features"
  • Handle customer extensions via "Plugins" or "Workflows": Make it possible to implement customer-specific processing in the No-Code layer

Particularly important is the "standardize by the third customer" rule. The rule of thumb that if you implement the same feature custom for three customers, it should be a standard feature of the product itself, is widely shared in the industry.

What you inevitably face early in an FDE project is "dirty data." It's not uncommon for 60-70% of the effort in a data integration project to be spent on cleansing. Below is a summary of representative patterns and how to handle them.

Data problem patternSpecific exampleHow to handle it
Notational inconsistenciesA mix of "株式会社," "(株)," and "カブシキガイシャ" (variants of "Co., Ltd.")Build normalization dictionaries and record-matching rules
Missing valuesImportant ID fields are NULLConfirm with the business side and decide on completion rules
InconsistenciesThe relationship "sales > total > profit" is brokenAutomate business rule checks and introduce anomaly detection

Professional FDE engineers prioritize "not trying to solve data quality problems perfectly right away." The standard approach is to "focus on the business area with the highest value and improve data quality only within that scope."

Many enterprise customers still run mainframes, core systems, and ERPs from 20-30 years ago, and extracting data from these is a major hurdle for FDE projects. There are five main success patterns.

  • Leveraging CDC (Change Data Capture): Capturing database changes in near real time and processing them as streams
  • Hardening file-based integration: Stabilizing regular exports of CSV/fixed-length files and enhancing error handling
  • Wrapping with an API Gateway: Adding a thin API layer over legacy systems to integrate with modern products
  • Leveraging iPaaS: Connecting legacy systems and SaaS with no-code tools such as Workato, Boomi, and Mulesoft
  • Integration via a data lake: Using a data lake such as S3/GCS as a hub, with each system loosely coupled

In particular, "integration via a data lake" is the most recommended modern pattern in recent years, as it enables modern analytics and AI use while minimizing legacy system modifications.

When SES engineers or SEs transition to FDE engineering, they are not judged solely by technical tests right away. DeFactory's actual track record of supporting such transitions includes three typical patterns.

A route where, after about 3 years of implementation experience with Java, Python, etc. at an SES, the person first goes independent as a freelancer (around ¥650,000/month), gains experience in upstream processes such as requirements definition and design (around ¥900,000/month), and then transitions to FDE engineering (around ¥1,300,000/month). Because it proceeds in stages, rates can be raised steadily while keeping risk low.

A route where someone who has gained experience as a project management assistant or in PMO at an SES goes independent as a freelance PM (around ¥1,000,000/month), then adds technical implementation skills and transitions to FDE engineering (around ¥1,400,000/month). Since they already have business acumen, added technical skills tend to translate directly into higher rates.

Engineers at SES companies who worked on AI-related projects such as Python and LLM implementation can sometimes join FDE engineering projects directly without going through freelance independence (around ¥1,100,000/month). For those with both technical skills and business thinking, this is the fastest route to becoming an FDE engineer.

When working as an FDE engineer, what you gain differs greatly depending on whether you are a full-time employee or a freelancer. If you value stability and organizational growth, full-time employment suits you; if you value income ceiling and flexibility, freelancing is the better fit. Job market trends and required skills for those considering a move to a full-time FDE role are covered in detail in a separate article.

The biggest advantages of being a full-time FDE engineer are the opportunity to receive stock options and access to organizational training and evaluation systems. If you build experience as an FDE engineer at fast-growing companies like LayerX or AI Shift, career paths to PdM, VPoE, or AI consultant also open up.

On the other hand, the drawback is being tied to a single company's context for a long period. Experience concentrated at one company makes it harder to gain diverse industry experience compared to freelance FDE engineers.

The biggest advantages of being a freelance FDE engineer are no income ceiling and the freedom to work across multiple industries and companies. By running engagements at two companies in parallel at 3-4 days per week each, some have achieved over ¥2,000,000 per month.

The drawbacks are the lack of long-term incentives such as stock options and the need to self-manage the risk of income loss when projects end.

To win year-long contracts as a freelance FDE engineer, paradoxically, an attitude of "building systems that run without you" is effective. FDE engineers who proactively propose internalization support, documentation, and knowledge transfer earn high trust from clients, which in turn leads to continued and expanded contracts as clients want to "ask them for the next project too." Rather than gatekeeping engineers, engineers with a hand-over mindset are chosen in the long run.

Before working as an FDE engineer, understanding the basics of standard working styles, contract types, and taxation helps you avoid stumbling blocks in job hunting and contract negotiations.

As of 2026, the standard working style for freelance FDE engineering projects is 3-4 days per week, fully remote or hybrid (1-3 on-site visits per month). Full-time, five-day on-site work like SES is almost nonexistent, and an increasing number of projects can be completed entirely remotely. For full-time FDE engineers as well, travel is generally capped at around 25%, as in Palantir's example.

FDE engineers' contracts generally come in two forms: full-time employment or outsourcing (quasi-mandate contracts). Freelance engagements are mainly quasi-mandate contracts, since this arrangement, which does not hold the contractor responsible for delivering a finished product, suits the dynamic requirement changes typical of AI projects. Some short-term, intensive projects use fixed-price (contract-for-work) agreements, but these fit poorly with the scope of an FDE engineer's duties (from problem definition to adoption support) and remain a minority.

Freelance FDE engineers command high rates, so it is worth considering incorporation early. As a rule of thumb, once your monthly rate exceeds 1 million yen (annual income of 12 million yen), the tax savings from incorporation (partial expensing of social insurance premiums, consumption tax treatment, etc.) start to become significant.
