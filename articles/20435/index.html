FDE Agent Workflow Control: How to Choose Between HITL and HOTL

Original title: FDE 智能体流程控制：HITL 与 HOTL 怎么选

FDE Times · 2026-11-07 · 5 min read

Summary

Using CrossCountry Consulting's forward deployed engineer role as a case study, this article explains how to design human oversight for AI agent workflows in finance. It argues clients ask who is accountable when an agent errs before asking what it can do, so FDEs must assign automation, HOTL, or HITL controls to each workflow step based on risk and reversibility from week one. A vendor invoice example shows the riskiest steps are simple irreversible actions like changing bank details, not where models err most. Control tables need sign-off from 5-7 client stakeholders, and the article lists three common design mistakes plus resume advice.

Key points

  • Finance clients ask about accountability before agent capabilities.
  • Each workflow step gets automation, HOTL, or HITL based on risk and reversibility.
  • Irreversible steps like bank detail changes require mandatory HITL with dual approval.
  • Control tables need buy-in from 5-7 client stakeholders.
  • Common mistake: rating risk by technical difficulty instead of business impact.

CrossCountry Consulting requires Forward Deployed Engineers (FDEs) to pair every process step with an approval or monitoring control based on risk and materiality, starting from week one.

Key Takeaways

  • In finance, clients first ask "who is responsible when the Agent makes a mistake" before asking what the Agent can do.
  • Every step of a workflow has its own control level: automation, HOTL, or HITL, depending on that step's risk and materiality.
  • A control design needs sign-off from 5-7 client-side stakeholders, so the control table matters as much as the code.

Imagine your first week at a client site. The invoice-reading Agent already runs successfully on sample data. Then the accounting supervisor asks a question that has nothing to do with the model: "If it posts an incorrect journal entry, who signs off?"

In finance projects, this kind of question determines whether an Agent can go into production. CrossCountry Consulting positions itself as a transformation partner for the office of the CFO (i.e., the corporate finance function).

As a result, the company's Forward Deployed Engineers (FDEs) work mainly with clients' finance and operations teams. The job posting for its Forward Deployed Engineer & Agentic Workflow Engineer role sets out a very specific requirement: design HITL/HOTL control points according to the risk and materiality of each workflow step.

This skill is not about prompt writing or model selection; it is about knowing where to place humans in processes that involve money and audits. It can be learned by practicing on concrete workflows.

Clients Ask About Accountability First, Models Second

CrossCountry's approach to AI is "process before technology," and its services cover the full journey from pilot to production, including multi-agent orchestration. This ordering makes sense. In a finance department, the real product is a process that auditors can accept; the Agent is only one part of it.

Time pressure is equally real. The job posting requires FDEs to deliver an AI Jumpstart solution within the first week of a project. These deployments are small and fast, deliver immediate value, and require neither large upfront investment nor complex infrastructure changes.

With only one week, there is no time to wait for auditors to approve code line by line. The control design must be in place from day one, and clear enough for people outside the software industry to understand.

HITL Makes the Process Wait, HOTL Lets It Continue

In HITL mode, the workflow stops completely and waits. The Agent proposes, a human clicks approve, and only then does the next step execute. In HOTL mode, the Agent keeps running on its own. Humans observe via dashboards or alerts and have the authority to halt it when something looks wrong.

The costs of the two differ. HITL is safe but labor-intensive and slows the process down. If every step is HITL, you have merely added a few clicks to the original manual process. HOTL is fast, but only works where errors can be detected and corrected before damage is done.

So for every step, ask two questions: how severe are the consequences of an error (materiality)? Can it be corrected before the money leaves the account or the number enters the reports?

Scoring Every Step of an Accounts Payable Process

Take a hypothetical scenario every finance team knows: processing vendor invoices. First break the workflow into steps, then score each one against the two questions above.

This table shows two things. First, the control level within the same step can vary with transaction amount, and that threshold must be set by the client's controller, not by you. Second, the highest-risk step is not necessarily where AI is most error-prone.

Reading invoices is where the model makes the most mistakes, but errors there can be corrected. Changing a bank account is a simple operation, yet once it goes wrong, the money is gone for good.

Every HITL control point also needs two things, or auditors will not accept it. First, the reviewer must see sufficient evidence: the original invoice, the corresponding purchase order lines, and the Agent's reasoning for its suggestion.

Second, a log recording who approved, when, and what they saw. As the number of Agents grows, CrossCountry uses its own CrossCoreAI platform to orchestrate, monitor, and govern these Agents. Whatever tool you use, you need to think through what to monitor beforehand.

Five to Seven People Signing One Control Table

According to CrossCountry, AI governance requires 5-7 stakeholders working together. For an FDE, that number means the table above must pass through several people with different concerns: finance wants speed, IT worries about system access, audit needs an audit trail.

The effective approach is to show them the control table before the demo. The "control level" column sparks the most debate, and the earlier that debate happens, the better. If audit wants the matching step changed from HOTL to HITL, you only need to change one row in the table, not rewrite the Agent.

The role also requires being on site. The job posting notes 20%-40% domestic travel depending on the project stage. Watching an accountant process invoices for an afternoon teaches you more about real risks than any process document.

Three Mistakes That Get Designs Rejected

The most common mistake is assessing risk by technical difficulty rather than business consequence. Engineers tend to place controls where the model is weak, while overlooking simple but irreversible operations like payments or modifying vendor master data.

The second mistake is setting the materiality threshold yourself. That threshold belongs to the client's internal control policy. Your job is to make it a configurable parameter and then ask the right person for the number.

The third mistake is treating HITL as an "OK" button. If reviewers only see a one-line summary, they will reflexively approve everything, and the control exists only on paper. A good HITL control point gives reviewers enough information to be able to say "no."

How to Put This Skill on Your Resume

CrossCountry may hire for this role at the Manager or Associate Director level. That means it is a senior position, and the hiring team wants to see judgment, not just code.

When reading FDE job postings in finance, insurance, or healthcare, watch for phrases like "human-in-the-loop," "governance," and "materiality." When you see them, prepare a control design case study to bring to the interview.

On your resume, don't just write "built an invoice-processing Agent"; spell out how many steps the workflow had, how many control points you set, which steps moved from manual to HOTL, and who on the client side signed off. Bring a table like the one above to the interview and explain clearly why you placed HITL on the payment row rather than the invoice-reading row.

In the finance department, an AI agent earns trust not because it never makes mistakes, but because everyone knows where errors will be caught if one occurs.
