FDE: A Practical Guide to Putting AI into Production — An Operations Manual for Getting AI into Real Business Settings

Original title: 《FDE：AI落地实战指南》：把 AI 塞进业务现场的操作手册

AI Fundamentals · 2026-09-16 · 6 min read

Summary

This piece reviews Fan Zhongkai's book "FDE: AI落地实战指南" (FDE: A Practical Guide to AI Deployment), positioning it as an operations manual for forward deployed engineers in the Chinese context. The author is a chief architect at Baidu who led the Wenxin App's engineering rollout. The book has seven parts and thirty chapters, deliberately keeping the technical section to five "good enough" chapters while focusing on requirements discovery, demo-driven delivery, and organizational adoption. The review highlights five common failure modes of enterprise AI projects (four of them organizational), interview techniques that surface real pain points, "work fossils" as demand signals, and a week-by-week insurance claims case study in chapter 22. Appendices offer seven practical tools, and the reviewer suggests different reading paths for career switchers versus active project owners.

Key points

  • Author Fan Zhongkai is a Baidu chief architect behind Wenxin App.
  • Seven parts, thirty chapters; only five cover technical basics.
  • Four of five AI project failure modes are organizational, not technical.
  • Chapter 22 offers a week-by-week retrospective of an insurance claims project.
  • Appendices include interview templates, a 60-question FDE question bank, and checklists.

Our earlier piece, “Models Are No Longer Scarce; What's Scarce Are People Who Can Put Them into Business”, made this judgment: models are no longer scarce; what's scarce are people who can embed models into business. The article walked through the 80/95/99 rule, three cost thresholds lowered by AI, the ontology layer, and three levels of replication leverage, explaining what this role is and why it suddenly became scarce in 2026.

After reading it, you know which direction to head in, but not where to plant your first step. Fan Zhongkai's *FDE: A Practical Guide to Putting AI to Work* picks up exactly that question.

I. Who Wrote This Book

Fan Zhongkai is a Principal Architect at Baidu and technical lead for ERNIE applications. He led the ERNIE App through the entire journey from technical pre-research to engineering deployment, and inside Baidu he led a team that broke down the boundaries between algorithms, engineering, applications, and product.

This background shapes the nature of the book. Among Chinese-language materials, several works already exist under the FDE topic, and they don't overlap with one another:

The first three explain why the FDE exists, what it looks like, and what doing the job feels like. The fourth lands on the operational level: what to do on day one after entering the scene, how to surface real requirements, how good a Demo needs to be, and what keeps a system alive after launch. It writes about projects in a Chinese context, facing domestic organizations, real approval chains, and reporting cycles—territory the first three materials don't cover.

II. Seven Parts, Thirty Chapters

The book has seven parts and thirty chapters, plus a conclusion and appendices.

Two choices in this structure are deliberate.

The technology part is only five chapters, and all of it takes a "good enough" approach. Chapter 13 is literally titled "Foundational Understanding of Large Models: A 'Good Enough' Mental Model." The book has no intention of turning readers into algorithm engineers; the goal of the technical portion is to enable an FDE to judge "can AI solve this problem, and which path should it take." This matches the actual makeup of the FDE role: writing code takes up only a third of the time.

The mindset part comes first, and Chapter 4 already discusses "a day and a year." Most technical books start with environment setup; this one first shows readers where the time in this job goes and what the rhythm looks like, before discussing methods. For someone considering a career change, this order is far more useful than the reverse.

III. Three Things to Read First

Five Ways Projects Die

The book groups the common ways enterprise AI projects die into five categories:

  • Demo-type: the project exists from day one only for the presentation three months later; after the warm applause, the system is never opened again
  • Data-type: the model choice is good, but the documents fed to the system have three versions and two sets of definitions
  • Process-type: the AI works, but the business process isn't changed one bit; employees have to open an extra web page, and two weeks later everyone returns to the old way
  • Trust-type: the system is overall decent, but one day it confidently gives a wrong answer that happens to be seen by an influential person, and trust collapses instantly
  • Ownership-type: the system launches, the vendor leaves, no one inside is clearly responsible, and it slowly fails with no one mourning it

The author's own annotation: of the five, only one has any technical flavor; the other four are all organizational problems.

This judgment is the same point as in our long article—"in the 80-to-99 range, most problems don't lie in model capability"—only here it's recast as five concrete patterns you can use for self-inspection. Counting how many of these your own project hits is more direct than reading a methodology.

Ask Differently

Asking a client "What are your pain points?" never surfaces real pain. The book suggests different questions:

  • "Could you walk me through the case you actually handled yesterday?"
  • "When was the last time something went wrong? What happened?"
  • "Do you keep any spreadsheets of your own to help you track these things?"

The book includes an interview case: a claims adjuster casually remarked, "When something goes wrong, it's my name on the line," and that single sentence changed the project's direction. The client had originally wanted fully automated review; it ended up as AI pre-reading plus human decision-making.

That complaint mattered because it exposed a constraint no interview question could surface: the reviewer personally bears signature-level accountability, and fully automated review would erase exactly that accountable party. Constraints like this never make it into requirement documents; you can only see them on the ground.

Work Fossils

The same section contains another signal: at almost every enterprise client, scattered around are small tools employees built themselves—an Excel sheet maintained for five years, an auto-fill macro cobbled together.

The reasoning: no one sacrifices their after-hours time to build a spreadsheet for a problem that doesn't hurt.

These "work fossils" are the most honest signal of demand, because they bypass every interviewing technique and directly show which steps genuinely cause pain. The FDE's value is turning these private self-rescue tools into enterprise-grade productivity systems.

IV. Chapter 22: A Project Reviewed Week by Week

Of the six case chapters, Chapter 22, "Full Retrospective: The Ocean Insurance Claims Intelligent Assistant," proceeds week by week:

Week 0: the situation at entry → Weeks 1–2: the lurking period → Week 3: a meeting that changed direction → Weeks 4–6: the first Demo → Weeks 7–16: ten weeks of productionization → Weeks 17–30: the adoption period → Week 31 onward: consolidation and exit

It's followed by three sections: "The Numbers Retrospective," "Three Moments It Nearly Derailed," and "Five Things This Project Got Wrong."

This style is rare in Chinese technical books. Most case write-ups stop at the results, while these three sections preserve the most replicable material: where things nearly derailed, and which decisions turned out to be wrong in hindsight.

V. Appendices and How to Read It

The appendix contains seven ready-to-use tools: an FDE capability self-assessment checklist, a customer interview template, an AI project requirements analysis template, a demo design template, an FDE interview question bank (60 questions), an enterprise AI implementation checklist, and a glossary (75 terms).

Given the book's positioning, here are two suggested ways to read it.

If you are considering a career change, start with Chapter 4, "A Day and a Year in the Life of an FDE," and Chapter 28, "The FDE Growth Path" (covering three starting points, a 90-day plan, four rungs, salary benchmarks, four kinds of costs, and "Will AI replace FDEs?"), then go back and read the capability chapters. Deciding whether to take this path matters more than figuring out how to walk it.

If you currently have an AI project on your hands, start with the five ways projects die and the full retrospective in Chapter 22, run a health check on your own project against them, then consult the methodology and delivery chapters as needed.

The division of labor between this book and our long article is clear: the article provides a cognitive map, placing the FDE within the industrial structure of AI implementation to explain it; this book provides an operations manual, dealing with "what to bring when you show up tomorrow, what question to ask first, and what counts as done."
