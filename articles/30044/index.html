How to Set AI Evals Pass Rates: pass@k and pass^k

Original title: AI 评测通过率怎么定：pass@k 与 pass^k

FDE Times · 2026-10-08 · 5 min read

Summary

Drawing on Anthropic, Hamel Husain, and Andrew Ng, this article argues that evals are a core FDE skill. Because AI agents produce nondeterministic output, deciding how accurate is accurate enough is a product decision that the engineer closest to the customer must make, then translate into runnable test sets. The piece explains the difference between pass@k (at least one success in k runs) and pass^k (all k runs succeed), showing how business tolerance for error determines which metric applies. It also stresses reading real traces daily, calibrating LLM-as-judge against domain experts, and advises job seekers to showcase concrete eval work rather than prompt engineering buzzwords.

Key points

  • Pass rate is a product decision that the FDE should negotiate with the customer.
  • Teams without evals end up reactively fixing bugs in production.
  • pass@k measures at least one success; pass^k requires all k runs to succeed.
  • Reading real traces daily and aligning LLM-as-judge with users is core FDE work.
  • Resumes should cite eval set size and agreed pass rates, not prompt engineering.

Key Takeaways

  • An agent's output is uncertain, so "correct" must be defined by someone. Anthropic believes the person closest to product requirements and users is best suited to do this. On deployment projects, that person is the FDE.
  • Anthropic warns that teams without evals end up passively fixing bugs in production. Hamel Husain found that failed LLM products share a common root cause: a robust evaluation system was never established.
  • Pass rate is a product decision. The FDE agrees on a pass rate with the customer, then converts it into metrics (pass@k or pass^k) and a runnable eval set.

"Pass rate is a product decision." That sentence comes from Hamel Husain, and Simon Willison cited it in a blog post. Engineers used to writing unit tests find it awkward: the pass rate of unit tests is simply 100%, nothing to discuss.

But this very sentence explains why evals have become a core skill for the Forward Deployed Engineer (FDE) working in AI. An AI system's output differs from run to run, so "correct" is no longer a purely technical question. Someone has to decide how correct is correct enough, and that decision must be written as something runnable.

Anthropic is explicit about who should do it: the person closest to product requirements and users is best positioned to define what success means.

On AI deployment projects, that person is not a researcher at headquarters, but an engineer sitting in the customer's office. This is exactly the FDE Andrew Ng described in The Batch: an engineer embedded in the customer's organization.

Without evals, who pays the price?

Anthropic's definition of evals is just a few words: give an AI system an input, then use scoring logic to judge its output. It is so simple that many teams skip it entirely, and Anthropic points out why: the cost of evals is visible from the start, while the value accumulates slowly and is easily overlooked.

Anthropic notes that the result is teams stuck in a cycle of passively fixing bugs, discovering problems only after the system reaches production. Hamel Husain observed that failed LLM products usually share a common root cause: a robust evaluation system was never established.

For an FDE, none of this is abstract. When an agent gives a customer's employee a wrong answer, you're the one taking the call, not the model team. The cost of missing evals is borne by the FDE, and the compounding effect Anthropic describes benefits the FDE most.

Hamel calls it a flywheel: the eval system forms a closed loop that lets you iterate very quickly. Every time a customer reports a bug, you turn it into a new test case, so next time you won't have to wait for someone else to report it.

Three levels of evaluation—which one belongs to the FDE

Hamel divides evaluation into three levels: level one is unit tests, level two is model and human evals (including debugging), and level three is A/B testing. At first glance this looks like an org-wide division of labor, but look closer and you'll see that every level needs something the FDE is uniquely positioned to provide.

LevelQuestion it answersWhat the FDE brings
Level 1: Unit testsDoes the output violate hard rules?Business rules heard directly from users in the field
Level 2: Model and human evalsBy the customer's standards, is the output "good"?Relationships with domain experts, used to calibrate LLM-as-judge
Level 3: A/B testingWhich version delivers better business outcomes?Access to real workflows, enabling actual measurement

Level 2 best showcases the FDE's capabilities. Using an LLM to score outputs is cheap and fast, which is appealing, but Hamel warns that the model must be aligned with human judgment, because "correct" is subjective.

The "humans" here are not you, but the accountants, support agents, or maintenance engineers who use the system every day. Only the FDE is close enough to them to ask: "How would you score this, and why?"

Hamel also has a principle: remove all friction from looking at the data. Reading real traces one by one is core work, not a side task. The FDE who opens traces every morning discovers failures before the customer does; the FDE who only stares at dashboards finds out after the customer is already unhappy.

pass@k or pass^k: customers don't know which question they need to answer

Back to Hamel's sentence: pass rate is a product decision. The first step in making that decision is choosing the right metric. Anthropic distinguishes two metrics that differ greatly for agents: pass@k measures the probability of at least one success in k runs, while pass^k measures the probability that all k runs succeed.

For the same agent, these two numbers can be far apart, because the agent's output is uncertain.

Suppose you're deploying an invoice-reading agent for a finance department. If their process allows someone to review results and rerun, pass@k is a reasonable metric. If the output goes straight into the books with no one checking, what they really need is pass^k, and that number will be much lower.

Customers rarely raise this question themselves; they just say "it must be accurate." Translating "it must be accurate" into "on these 200 test cases, pass^5 must exceed threshold X," and then negotiating X, is the FDE's job.

"Negotiate" here is meant literally. Customers often want 100%, which systems with uncertain output rarely achieve. Assume each additional percentage point costs more than the last, and tell the customer this from the start.

Only the engineer caught in the middle holds both pieces of information: on one side, the system's capability limits; on the other, the business's real tolerance for errors. Hand this decision to sales or the model team, and the result is often one side over-promising while the other tests something nobody needs.

What if the evals engineer becomes a standalone role?

Andrew Ng speculates that AI engineering may split into more specialized roles, such as AI FDE, LLMOps engineer, or Evals Engineer. He may be right. But even if someone specializes in building eval infrastructure, the hardest part cannot be handed off: defining success.

Scoring infrastructure can be built centrally, but understanding which errors each customer's users consider serious cannot.

So for developers in Vietnam or anywhere else looking to transition into an FDE role, evals are the most worthwhile skill to invest in right now: it's both technical work and proof that you understand the business. And you don't need a big project to practice.

Pick an AI workflow you've built, assemble a small eval set from real traces, ask someone who knows the domain to score it by hand, then measure how far your LLM-as-judge deviates from them. Walk through this loop once, and you'll have a concrete story to tell in interviews.

Don't write "familiar with prompt engineering" on your resume; write how many test cases your eval set has, how you scored them, and what pass rate you agreed on with users.

When reading job descriptions, watch for terms like evaluation, trace, observability, or "jointly define success metrics with customers." They signal that the company understands what an FDE does, rather than just wanting someone to install software.

Models will change, frameworks will change, and the prompts you write today may be thrown away next quarter. What remains is the eval set you built together with the customer, and the answer to a question only someone close enough can ask: how correct is correct enough?
