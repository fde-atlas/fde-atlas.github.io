FDE Readiness Checker — Do You Clear the Published Bars?

RECATOOLS · 2026-07-31 · 6 min read

Summary

RECATOOLS' FDE Readiness Checker tests your background against published entry criteria only — two employer postings, seven FDE training programmes, and one public apprenticeship — quoting each bar verbatim with a read date. The finding: all six programmes that publish a technical bar require you to already be an engineer, and Palantir never mentions domain expertise. Domain knowledge may help on the job, but on the written criteria it does not get you through the door.

  • IIT Roorkee's certificate screens on degree discipline plus a 50% minimum mark.
  • Palantir lists strong engineering background as a preference only; domain expertise is never mentioned.
  • Anthropic requires 4+ years in a technical customer-facing role, with 25–50% travel.

How to Use the FDE Readiness Checker

Separate your three experience figures

Years of domain expertise, years as an engineer, and years in a technical customer-facing role are counted separately. Several gates score only one of the three, so collapsing them hides the answer.

Rate your tooling honestly

Python, Git, Docker, cloud and SQL. Choose “Strong / production” only if you have shipped with it — AIU states plainly that these are tools used throughout its programme, “not taught from scratch”.

Add education and logistics

Degree discipline decides the one university-badged programme, and both employers publish a travel figure that is easy to overlook when reading a posting quickly.

Read the gates, not just the score

Every bar you clear or miss is quoted in the provider’s own words, with a link and the date the page was read, so you can check any of it yourself.

What the Published Bars Actually Say

Forward Deployed Engineer is the role of the moment, and the discourse around it is unusually noisy. A great deal of it says the job is ideal for people who deeply understand an industry — that the scarce ingredient is domain knowledge rather than engineering, and that a seasoned banker or clinician or logistics manager is exactly who these teams need. It is an attractive claim, and it is worth testing against what the people doing the hiring and the training actually write down. That is all this tool does: it ignores commentary entirely and reads only published entry criteria, then tells you which of them you clear. Ten gates are covered — two employer postings, seven FDE-specific training programmes, and one public apprenticeship — and each bar is quoted verbatim rather than paraphrased, because paraphrase is precisely where this subject goes wrong.

The pattern that emerges is consistent and slightly uncomfortable. Every FDE-specific programme that publishes a technical bar expects you to be an engineer already: six of the six that publish one. The single university-badged certificate, from IIT Roorkee’s Continuing Education Centre, screens on degree discipline and a minimum mark — which is the one credential a mid-career domain expert cannot retroactively acquire. Even the free, community-run roadmap, which has no gate to enter at all, opens by telling you that FDE “is not a beginner role”. And Palantir, the company that invented the job title, asks for a strong engineering background as a preference and does not mention industry or domain expertise anywhere in its requirements. Only one gate of the ten publishes no technical bar whatsoever, and it is a twelve-week vendor traineeship selecting on portfolio and interview.

Two gates are handled differently from the rest, and the reason is worth stating plainly because it affects how much you should trust any tool of this kind. Job postings are withdrawn the moment a role is filled — the Anthropic posting quoted here had already been taken down within a day of being read. Employer entries are therefore presented as dated snapshots rather than live vacancies, and they link the employer’s stable careers index rather than a permalink that has already rotted. Programme pages persist, so those are cited live and directly. Every quote on the results page carries the date it was read, so you can judge its freshness yourself instead of trusting that someone maintained it. One further absence is reported rather than hidden: three certifications could not be read from their own pages, so they are excluded and named as excluded, because a count that quietly includes unverified entries is worse than a smaller honest one.

Domain expertise may well make you better at this job. On the published criteria, it does not get you through the door — and that gap is the whole finding.

Four of seven programmes will not publish a price

Six of the six FDE training programmes that publish a technical bar require you to already be an engineer.

The only university-badged programme, from IIT Roorkee's Continuing Education Centre, screens on degree discipline and a minimum of 50% marks.

roadmap.sh — free, community-run, with no gate to enter — still opens by stating that FDE "is not a beginner role".

Palantir asks for a "strong engineering background", but only as a preference; it never mentions industry or domain expertise.

Both employers publish a travel requirement: up to 25% at Palantir, and 25–50% at Anthropic.

Supervity is the one programme publishing no technical bar at all — selection is a portfolio, an aptitude brief and a 15-minute interview.

AIU's course is open to anyone, but its certification needs 2+ years of relevant experience for Foundation and 4+ for Professional.

AI Singapore's apprenticeship pays SGD 4,000/month, yet every named outcome is a model-building role rather than customer-facing deployment.

Anthropic asks for 4+ years in a technical, customer-facing role — experience as a pure back-end engineer does not satisfy it.

Four of the seven training programmes do not publish a price at all, which is itself a finding about how young this market is.
