Product development is the process of planning and designing "what products or services to deliver to the world" based on market and customer needs, and consistently driving the actual development through to release.

"Product" here broadly includes digital products (web services, smartphone apps, SaaS products, etc.) as well as physical goods, but in the modern business context, it almost always refers to IT and digital products.

What fundamentally distinguishes product development from other types of development is that it starts from the very question of "what should we build?" The essence of product development is starting from an uncertain state where no answer has been determined, forming hypotheses, and shaping the product through repeated validation.

The three terms "product development," "software development," and "system development" are often confused, but each has clear differences. The table below summarizes them.

Comparison itemDescription
Product developmentStarts from "what to build." Market and user needs are the starting point. Involves repeated hypothesis validation. Aimed at creating new businesses and services.
Software developmentThe work of translating defined requirements into code. Technical implementation is the starting point. Aimed at completing the work as designed.
System developmentBuilding systems for business efficiency and problem-solving. Starts from requirements definition. Aimed at improving existing business operations.

Simply put, if software development and system development are acts of "taking the shortest route to a fixed goal," product development is an act of "moving forward while exploring where to go."

This difference is the biggest reason why the skills and attitudes required of engineers involved in product development differ. Not only coding skills, but also the ability to think from a business and user perspective is required.

Product development is a team activity involving multiple roles working together. The main related roles are summarized below.

  • Product Manager (PdM): Defines the product vision, strategy, and roadmap. The navigator of development.
  • Engineers (frontend/backend/full-stack): Responsible for implementation. In product development projects, they often join from the requirements definition stage.
  • UX designers/UI designers: Responsible for designing user experiences and screen design.
  • Data analysts: Propose directions for improvement based on analysis of user behavior data.
  • Marketers: Responsible for market research, competitive analysis, and post-release growth strategies.

When freelance engineers join product development projects, they are evaluated not only on their implementation skills as engineers, but also on their communication skills for collaborating with the roles above and their ability to think from a business perspective.

Why is product development essential for companies to continue growing over the long term? Let's explore this in depth from three perspectives.

The foundation of running a company is providing products and services to generate revenue and profit. For continuous growth, companies must constantly create products and services that meet user needs.

Product development starts from market needs and solving user problems. Solving the problems users are truly struggling with directly leads to increased company revenue and profit—this is why product development is the engine of corporate growth.

Conversely, if you build a "product made only from the creator's perspective" that is detached from user needs, it won't be accepted by the market no matter how technically excellent it is. "Market-need mismatch," the most commonly cited cause of startup failure, arises precisely from this problem.

Thoroughly maintaining a user perspective in product development can be said to be a fundamental activity for protecting a company's sustainable growth.

In today's rapidly changing market environment, it is difficult to keep beating competitors with existing products and services alone. Especially in the digital services space, new players keep entering the market, intensifying the competitive environment.

Through continuous product development, companies can constantly keep providing new value to the market. Developing features that anticipate users' latent needs, designing unique experiences that competitors don't have, and continuous data-driven improvement—all of these can only be achieved through ongoing product development.

Moreover, the know-how, user data, and technical infrastructure accumulated through product development become "barriers to entry" that competitors cannot easily imitate. In this sense, continuous investment in product development directly leads to building long-term market advantage.

Through product development, companies can also gain the byproduct of talent development. The process of tackling uncertain challenges, forming hypotheses, and testing them dramatically improves team members' thinking skills, execution ability, and problem-solving capabilities.

Launching a new business and making it succeed is extremely difficult, and it is said that most products fail once they reach the market. However, that experience of failure becomes a valuable asset for the organization. The accumulation of lessons learned—such as "what went wrong" and "how to improve next time"—raises the capabilities of the entire organization.

Furthermore, an environment where employees can take on product development challenges also contributes to recruiting and retaining excellent engineers and designers. For talented engineers who seek to "build a business together" rather than simply "write code as instructed," opportunities to participate in product development are a major attraction.

While there is no fixed "correct procedure" for product development, there is a basic flow common to many success stories. Please refer to the following six steps.

The starting point of product development is discovering "the problem to solve." What is important at this stage is to focus not on "what you want to build" but on "what users are genuinely struggling with."

The main methods for problem discovery are as follows.

  • Market research and competitive analysis: Understand market size, the strengths and weaknesses of competing products, and market trends
  • User interviews: Listen to the candid voices of target users and uncover latent problems
  • Review analysis of existing services: Extract problems from App Store and Google Play reviews and social media commentary
  • Leveraging internal and industry knowledge: Start from problems specific to your company or industry

By using frameworks such as the Lean Canvas, you can organize problems, solutions, customer segments, revenue models, and KPIs on a single sheet, quickly visualizing the overall picture of the business. The depth of insight at this stage determines the direction of subsequent development.

Once a problem has been identified, the next step is to specifically define the target users who have that problem. With a vague target definition like "businesspeople in their 20s to 40s," the product you develop will also end up vague.

A persona is a concrete representation of the typical target user, defined as a specific individual. For example, "32 years old, an engineer at an IT company, interested in side jobs but unsure where to start"—described realistically as if the person actually existed.

Clarifying the persona brings consistency to all decision-making, including feature prioritization, UI/UX design, and marketing messages. In product development, persona design is one of the most important early tasks.

Before shaping an idea into reality, conduct interviews with target users to validate your hypotheses. There is often a significant gap between the problems the development team assumes and the problems users actually face.

The key point to keep in mind during interviews is "don't lead the interviewee." If you ask "Would you like to use this service?", most people will answer "yes." Instead, it is important to dig deeper into users' behavior and feelings with questions such as "What problems are you currently experiencing?", "How are you solving them?", and "What frustrations do you have with that approach?"

Also, interviews are not something you do just once; they should be conducted continuously throughout the development phase. Checking reactions by showing prototypes, conducting user interviews after release—creating a culture of always listening to users' voices is the source of great products.

Before building a full-spec product, use landing pages, prototypes, or mockups to gauge market response. By testing actual purchase intent and willingness to pay at this stage, you can significantly reduce development risk.

Here are some representative test marketing methods.

  • Smoke test: Publish a landing page (LP) before the service is actually complete and gauge demand from "number of registrations" and "click-through rate"
  • Crowdfunding: Attempt fundraising before development and quantitatively measure market response
  • Pre-sale: Accept advance purchases before release to confirm actual purchase intent
  • Ad testing: Run A/B tests with multiple catchphrases and appeals to identify the most resonant message

Rather than thinking "let's make the business succeed," repeatedly testing from the perspective of "how much can we lower the probability of failure" is the shortcut to product development success. If the hypothesis turns out to be significantly off at this stage, it is important to pivot (change direction) without hesitation.

Once market response has been confirmed, it is finally time to begin actual development. What is important here is the concept of the "MVP (Minimum Viable Product: a product with the minimum set of features)."

An MVP is a product that implements only the minimum features necessary to deliver value to users. The goal is not to build a perfect product, but to launch with minimal functionality and obtain real feedback from the market.

The benefits of releasing an MVP first are as follows.

  • Development costs and time can be minimized
  • Market response can be obtained early, allowing course corrections
  • Wasteful feature development can be prevented
  • Feedback from users can determine the next development priorities

By adopting agile development methods (Scrum, Kanban, etc.), you can iterate improvements in short cycles (sprints) and respond flexibly to market changes. The attitude of "fail fastest, learn fastest" is especially important in MVP development.

Freelance engineers also have many opportunities to participate in this MVP development phase. Engineers with agile development experience and skills as Scrum Masters or tech leads are especially highly valued in product development projects.

A product release is not the goal; it is the true starting point. Continuous improvement is essential until achieving PMF (Product Market Fit: the state where the product truly fits the market).

The main activities to carry out after release are organized below.

  • Collecting and analyzing user feedback: Support inquiries, app store reviews, user interviews, etc.
  • Quantitative analysis: Setting and measuring KPIs such as DAU/MAU, retention rate, LTV, and churn rate
  • Feature improvement and deprecation: Use data to determine which features to improve and which to remove
  • Growth hacking: Improving NPS (Net Promoter Score), viral initiatives, SEO/ASO measures, and more

If you treat a release as the finish line, your product will quickly become obsolete. Continuously running an improvement cycle (Build → Measure → Learn) is the key to long-term product growth.

The biggest thing to avoid in product development is building a service that no one uses. This not only wastes development effort and cost, but also deals a major blow to team morale and organizational trust. By always keeping the following points in mind, you can greatly reduce that risk.

The most common pitfall in product development is prioritizing the assumptions of developers and planners, or "what they want to build." Engineers in particular tend to fall into the mindset of "we could build something interesting with this technology."

Taking a user-centric perspective is not simply about "listening to user requests." It is a creative activity of uncovering latent issues that users themselves are unaware of and proposing the best solutions to address them.

What is required is to identify not only the visible requests, but also the issues users fundamentally want to solve and the goals they want to achieve. It is important to eliminate your own assumptions and biases, and to maintain an attitude of constantly listening to data and user feedback.

In today's world, where the pace of market change is accelerating, development cycle speed directly translates into competitive advantage. By delegating decision-making authority to the development team and simplifying approval processes, you can achieve a rapid hypothesis-testing cycle.

One reason product development often fails at large companies is slow decision-making. With a structure where "we can't move without checking with our boss" or "executive approval is required," you cannot respond quickly to market changes.

It is essential to give product development teams a certain level of budget and decision-making authority, creating an environment where they can act autonomously. Even on projects with freelance engineers, checking this point in advance lets you gauge how smoothly the project will run.

In product development, data-driven decision-making is important, rather than relying on "intuition" or "rules of thumb." A hypothesis like "changing this button's color should make it easier to use" remains just a hypothesis until it is verified through A/B testing.

You cannot improve what you cannot measure. In product development, setting appropriate KPIs and building a measurement environment is one of an engineer's key responsibilities. Use data analytics tools such as Google Analytics, Amplitude, and Mixpanel to establish a system for continuously measuring and analyzing user behavior.

Data-driven decision-making also helps reduce subjective disagreements within the team. An environment where discussions are based on facts—"the data says this"—greatly boosts the productivity of a product development team.

Product development is a battle against uncertainty. No matter how carefully you conduct market research and user interviews, it is not uncommon for things to go differently than expected once you release.

What matters is not "avoiding failure" but "learning from failure as fast as possible." Organizations that postpone releases out of fear of failure lose learning opportunities and fall behind competitors. On the other hand, organizations that experiment on a small scale, fail, learn from it, and apply those lessons to their next initiatives can grow over the long term.

This "failure-assumed" mindset needs to become embedded not just in individual engineers, but in the organizational culture itself. Not blaming failure, sharing lessons learned, and applying them to the next challenge—an environment with this kind of psychological safety is a common trait of excellent product development teams.

In this section, we will explain practical skills and career strategies for excelling in product development projects, aimed at freelance engineers—and engineers aspiring to go freelance—reading BizDevTech.

For freelance engineers, product development projects are a highly attractive area in terms of both high rates and high growth.

Product development projects have the following characteristics.

  • Higher monthly rates (around 800,000–1,500,000 yen): Engineers who can handle upstream processes from requirements definition through design to implementation command especially high rates
  • Many long-term ongoing projects: Since continuous improvement and growth phases continue after release, contracts tend to extend over the long term
  • Many fully remote and flexible projects: Abundant remote-work-friendly projects, especially at startups and IT companies
  • Many opportunities to improve skills: You can learn the latest technologies and development methods on the job
  • High impact on the business: Your development directly affects the product's KPIs, making the work highly rewarding

As of 2026, demand for launching new products—such as AI development, MVP development, and SaaS development—continues to expand. In particular, projects involving AI feature integration and LLM-based product development are rapidly increasing, and demand is extremely high for freelance engineers with these skills.

The skills required to join a product development project fall broadly into two categories: "technical skills" and "business and communication skills."

Technical skills vary depending on the type of project, but the following skills are especially valued in product development projects.

  • Frontend: React, Next.js, TypeScript (the standard tech stack for modern product development projects)
  • Backend: Node.js, Python (FastAPI/Django), Ruby on Rails, Go (heavily used at startups)
  • Cloud infrastructure: AWS, GCP, and Azure setup and operations skills (including Amplify, Vercel, Firebase, etc.)
  • Database design: PostgreSQL, MySQL, NoSQL (DynamoDB, Firestore, etc.)
  • CI/CD and DevOps: Automation and infrastructure-as-code with GitHub Actions, CircleCI, Docker, Terraform, and more
  • AI/ML integration: LLM integration skills with the OpenAI API, Anthropic API, and more (demand surging since 2024)

In product development projects, the following skills are valued in addition to pure technical ability.

  • Requirements definition: The ability to define "what should be built"—the capacity to translate business problems into technical requirements
  • Agile development experience: Hands-on experience with agile methods such as Scrum and Kanban
  • Proposal skills: Able to proactively make improvement suggestions such as "this could be done better"
  • Documentation skills: Able to properly create and update specifications and design documents
  • Team leadership: Able to lead a small team and drive development (tech lead / lead engineer)

So, what career strategy should you adopt to actually land product development projects? Let's walk through it step by step.

Rather than simply listing achievements like "I developed XX," create a portfolio that shows "what you developed to solve what problem" and "how KPIs improved after development."

Clients for product development projects are looking not just for "people who can write code" but for "people who can understand business challenges and build products." A portfolio that reflects this mindset directly leads to winning projects.

If you have no product development experience, the fastest path is to start by taking on small MVP development projects or side-gig-level startup support projects to build a track record.

Even with zero track record, developing and releasing your own service is itself a portfolio. Publish a repository on GitHub and make your achievements visible in the form of "I built this service to solve this problem. It has XX MAUs, and I've iterated on improvements like this."

Engineers who can join not only the implementation phase but also the requirements definition and design phases are especially highly valued in product development projects. Communicate actively with clients and show a willingness to contribute to discussions about "what should be built." To participate in upstream processes, documentation skills, presentation skills, and a sense of cost and scheduling are also important. For engineers aiming for tech lead or EM positions, acquiring these skills is an effective mid- to long-term career strategy.

Rather than searching for product development projects on your own, using an agent specializing in IT freelancing allows you to land high-rate projects more efficiently. Agents understand your skills and experience and introduce projects with a high match rate, so mismatches are less likely—another advantage.

When registering with a freelance agent, the content of your skill sheet is extremely important. Describe the overview of each project you handled, your scope of responsibility, the technologies used, and your results in concrete terms to accurately convey your market value.

Demand is expected to keep growing for engineers with experience in AI development, MVP development, and SaaS development who can practice agile development. Honing your product development skills and experience can be called the best investment for raising your market value as a freelance engineer over the long term.

In this article, we comprehensively covered everything from the basic concepts of product development, to why it is essential for a company's sustainable growth, the concrete development flow (6 steps), keys to success, and career strategies for freelance engineers.

While product development is an essential endeavor for a company's sustainable growth, in reality many products end up never being accepted by the market. However, that is not because product development itself is wrong—it is because the processes for "reducing the probability of failure" are missing.

Conduct careful user interviews, validate hypotheses through test marketing, and release an MVP to learn from the market—if you can root this "reduce the probability of failure" philosophy in your organization, product development will eventually become a company's most powerful growth engine.

For freelance engineers, deepening your knowledge and skills in product development is a highly effective investment both for winning high-rate projects and for building a long-term career. Aim to evolve from "an engineer who writes code" into "an engineer who builds businesses."
