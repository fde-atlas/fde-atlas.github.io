For enterprise AI to make money, who should be the first role you hire? OpenAI's former Chief Research Officer gives the answer

Original title: 企业 AI 要赚钱，第一个岗位先招谁？OpenAI 前首席研究官给答案

腾讯新闻 · 2025-09-09 · 9 min read

Summary

This article distills Bob McGrew's interview on YC's Lightcone podcast. McGrew, former chief research officer at OpenAI who led GPT-4 development, argues that enterprise AI fails not because models are weak but because tools go unused after launch. His answer: hire Forward Deployed Engineers first—people who embed with customers, solve novel problems on-site, and turn demos into deliverable products within months. Drawing on his Palantir experience, he explains how FDEs pave the way for product teams to generalize solutions, why AI agents need this model more than SaaS did, and how delivering early results builds the trust that turns pilots into large contracts.

Key points

  • FDEs work on-site with customers, fixing products in real time rather than writing specs or demos.
  • AI agents, unlike standardized SaaS, require per-customer iteration because workflows and data differ everywhere.
  • Palantir split the role into Echo (domain-savvy scouts) and Delta (fast, pragmatic prototype builders).
  • FDE deployments may lose money initially but lower costs over time as solutions generalize.
  • Over 100 YC startups now recruit FDEs, a role barely known three years ago.

Full text

(Highlights from the YC Lightcone interview with Bob McGrew on the FDE model)

What do enterprises fear most when adopting AI?

It's not immature technology, nor an unaccepting market. It's this: the tool launches, but nobody uses it.

Bob McGrew, a key developer of ChatGPT and former Chief Research Officer at OpenAI, has deep insight into this. He led the development of key models like GPT-4 and O1, and today his advice is:

Don't build the platform first, don't hire architects first—first find people who can deliver results quickly.

He's not talking about product managers, nor consultants,

but an emerging role that hundreds of YC companies are scrambling to hire:

FDE—Forward Deployed Engineer

On September 8, 2025, McGrew explained this role in full on YC's Lightcone podcast. He also published a post on X (formerly Twitter) emphasizing his core methodology:

"Doing things that don't scale—at scale."

The message is simple: build people and processes first, not technology.

Success stories like Claude, Castle, and Palantir all have such "implementation experts" behind them.

At this juncture, we must ask again:

For enterprise AI to make money, what exactly should the first step be?

Why not the CTO, not the product director, but this new role that has never appeared in org charts?

Section 1 | Not a consultant, not sales: Who exactly is an FDE?

This role is called the FDE. It sounds like a technical position, but it's not an engineer.

Its full name is "Forward Deployed Engineer." Bob McGrew summed it up in one sentence:

"An FDE doesn't do demos for you or sit through requirements meetings with you. They go directly into the field and solve the problem together with the customer."

✅ FDEs aren't engineers?

If you think of an FDE as a consultant, you're wrong.

Consultants stand on the sidelines, produce a plan, and let others execute it. FDEs are the ones who get their hands dirty, who make the software actually usable.

McGrew stresses that FDEs don't do formalities. He says:

"At Palantir, we discovered early on that going to people from traditional government sales was useless. They'd say: 'I can take you to dinner and introduce you to clients, but I won't touch the product.'"

FDEs aren't ordinary engineers either.

Engineers draw architecture diagrams in the office; FDEs embed at the customer's site and solve unprecedented problems on the spot.

Bob McGrew used a scenario to illustrate:

"Customer problems are usually brand-new, never-seen-before challenges. The value of an FDE lies in this: where others see the impossible, they make it possible."

And FDEs must turn a demo into a deliverable product in a short time. It's fine if the prototype is rough, but the result has to work.

Not code that goes ten years without a bug, but code that can go live within three months and genuinely solve the customer's problem.

✅ What does an FDE actually do?

McGrew gave an example:

When he was building intelligence systems at Palantir, customers were initially unwilling to share their real needs. As intelligence agencies, their information was classified.

So they brought a basic demo, and the customer said it had nothing to do with their work. But then came the next sentence:

"The customer said: 'If you could change this part and switch to a different view, we might be able to use it.'"

So the FDE noted the feedback on the spot, made the changes overnight, and brought it back.

This is the complete on-site iteration process: the traditional product approach has sales relaying customer feedback while engineers write code at headquarters; FDEs put engineers directly next to the customer, listening and changing things immediately.

So what's the difference between an FDE and a product manager? McGrew put it bluntly:

A product manager stands beside the user to understand the workflow; an FDE directly changes the workflow.

And good FDEs keep going deeper. After solving the first problem, they look for new entry points, sometimes uncovering more value than originally anticipated.

This is a process of fully penetrating the customer's business.

This section isn't about "defining the FDE," but answering a more concrete question:

"When building AI products, who is the key role that can actually win over customers?"

Section 2 | Why are AI companies all fighting over FDEs?

The core problem the FDE model solves is the "last mile" between technology and business in complex enterprise scenarios.

McGrew captured this model with a vivid metaphor:

FDEs lay the gravel road first, and the product team later paves it into a highway.

This division of labor allowed Palantir to resolve the tension between personalized needs and scalable replication.

✅ It's not that the company is special—it's that the scenario has changed

Palantir took this path because its customers were extremely complex:

  • the National Security Agency, counterterrorism command, law enforcement systems...
  • Every organization is different, every workflow is different, and there's simply no standard product that can be used out of the box.

Bob McGrew believes AI agents are now in exactly the same position Palantir was in back then.

"You think you're building a general-purpose agent tool, but every company's workflows, tools, and data permissions are all different. No one can buy it and go live immediately."

This is completely different from traditional SaaS. SaaS products rely on "workflow standardization":

  • Every company runs payroll in roughly the same way, and the steps for producing financial reports are similar too.
  • So SaaS companies built a unified template—set up your account, and everyone can use it.

But AI agents aren't like that.

They're an entirely new species, without SaaS's mature processes to replicate. Nobody knows what an AI agent should look like.

In this situation, simply waiting for customers to use it on their own rarely produces real results.

✅ It's not a service transformation—it's that the product's path to deployment has changed

When many people hear "FDE," they assume it's a services role—"companion-style operations" or "custom development."

McGrew directly rejected this notion:

"The FDE isn't about making the product fit the customer; it's about using the customer to distill truly general-purpose needs."

For example:

An AI company builds risk-control systems for banks. The first bank wants credit card fraud detection, the second wants money laundering identification, the third wants to prevent internal violations... every customer's needs are different.

If you custom-develop every time, the work will never end.

But the FDE realized these needs are all essentially "identifying anomalous behavior patterns," so the product team abstracted a general anomaly-detection framework—one system solves all the problems.

This is the key value of the FDE model: it completely changes the product's path to deployment.

✅ Why do today's AI agent companies need FDEs even more?

Over 100 YC startups are now hiring FDEs—three years ago, hardly anyone had heard of the role.

Why the sudden explosion?

Because AI companies have discovered that the key to deploying agents is integrating into real business workflows. But where are those workflows?

Not everyone can explain the process clearly—many customers can't even articulate why they do things the way they do. You have to be on-site, watching and following along, to find the real entry point.

That's why the teams behind OpenAI, Anthropic, and Claude increasingly emphasize: an AI agent isn't just about capability, it's about the outcome of action.

The technology is ready, but the world's pace of adoption hasn't caught up.

What enterprises need isn't a stronger model or higher accuracy, but someone who can plug into their processes and deliver results.

That person is the FDE.

Section 3 | How FDEs Make AI Projects Profitable

If the FDE's first role is "making AI land in the real world," then their second role is "helping enterprises land big deals."

You can think of the FDE as the "owner of outcomes"—what they sell isn't features, but results.

In the SaaS model, companies sell "licenses," "modules," and "seats." But in the world of AI agents, customers will only pay for results.

✅ The logic of making money has changed: from "selling software" to "delivering results"

How does traditional SaaS make money? Through scale.

You polish one feature and replicate it across a hundred companies. The contracts aren't big, but they're replicable.

AI agents are different:

  • Every customer's problem is unique
  • The process is complex, workflows keep changing, and the data is a mess
  • If you try to replicate quickly, chances are you can't

McGrew's answer is:

"Don't chase rapid replication—go deep into solving the problem. What you're selling isn't the product itself, but a proven solution."

When an FDE is at a customer's site, it's not to install a product, but to take on a specific problem and deliver a result.

This explains why the business logic of the FDE model is so distinctive: the initial deployment may lose money, but over time, as the results delivered become more valuable, costs actually go down.

This process unfolds in three stages:

  • At the start, the FDE goes on-site and spends a lot of time solving the first problem—costs are high;
  • Then, the customer starts relying on the AI tools, feedback becomes more precise, and needs become more focused;
  • Finally, the FDE no longer needs to change much—the product can now adapt to more customer scenarios, and profits start to come in.

✅ A real case: how to go from "pilot" to "big deal"?

McGrew mentioned several real examples of AI agent startups, such as Castle and Happy Robot:

Castle is a voice AI company that helps several major US banks handle mortgage calls

  • At first they could only handle a few scenarios, but the results they delivered were highly accurate and customers were satisfied
  • Later, customers proactively increased their contract value and handed over their entire workflow

Another is Happy Robot, which also builds voice agents, mainly serving logistics companies like DHL:

  • Their contracts with customers aren't priced by features, but by "number of successful calls"
  • The more customers use it, the more they see the labor costs saved, and the more willing they are to increase their budget

Both examples illustrate one point:

The FDE is not a cost center, but the starting point for driving revenue.

✅ Only with customer trust can you pull in the contracts

Many people mistakenly believe the biggest hurdle in AI sales is insufficient features, but the real hurdle is: customer trust.

McGrew says:

"Enterprise customers have often been through many failed projects—they may not even believe you can succeed. So you have to deliver the first result first, win their trust, and only then can you discuss what comes next."

That's why an FDE must be someone who can deliver on-site, someone who can get the entire process running end to end within two or three months and produce tangible results. Only with that result will customers truly trust you—and only then is the next big contract possible.

As long as the results you deliver keep growing in value, the customer's budget will follow you willingly.

Section 4 | Who Can Be an FDE, and How Should the Product Team Work with Them?

At its core, the FDE is the first person to make AI land in the real world.

But not everyone can do this job.

Bob McGrew put it very clearly in the conversation: when hiring FDEs, you're not looking for "smart people," but for "people who can carry the load."

✅ What kind of person can be an FDE?

Within Palantir, McGrew divided FDEs into two types of roles:

  • The Echo team: embedded at customer sites, understanding needs and finding scenarios for deployment
  • The Delta team: quickly building working product prototypes, deploying them on-site, and delivering results

The Echo team ideally has industry backgrounds—retired military officers, medical experts—but more importantly, they need to be "rebels": people who know the industry's processes well but recognize the old ways aren't good enough, and who come to tear down old workflows and build new paths.

The Delta team comes from engineering backgrounds, but they're different from ordinary engineers. Don't look for people who pursue perfect architecture—look for people who can write code quickly, accept rewriting versions, and cope with changing workflows. The priority is delivering first; the code may be ugly, but it has to work.

Both types of people are essentially "project founders": able to blaze trails in the face of the unknown, hold up under pressure, and converse with customers while maintaining a sense of direction.

✅ How do the product team and FDEs work together?

McGrew was very candid:

"We've interviewed many product managers from large traditional tech companies. They're all excellent, but they're not used to abstracting general solutions from the needs of multiple customers."

For example:

One customer says they want to look up "people," the next says they want to look up "ships," and another says they want to look up "accounts."

So how should the product be designed?

McGrew's answer is: don't customize for each customer—abstract instead:

"People, ships, and accounts are different, but they share common traits: they all have basic information, and they all form connections with other things. Design this shared underlying logic well, and one system can handle all types of data."

This is the origin of Palantir's famous "ontology" design. It wasn't the FDEs who proposed the abstraction—it was the product team who distilled it from the FDEs' hard-won lessons.

But in practice, there's also friction between the product team and FDEs. The product team often feels the tools they've built are "great to use," only to find the FDEs on-site don't want to use them.

What's the solution?

"Send the product team on-site to use the tools alongside the FDEs and improve them together."

This is the core of the FDE model: deliver results first, then abstract them into general capabilities.

The product team's role isn't to work behind closed doors, but to scale solutions that have been validated in the field.

Conclusion | FDE: Not a Job Title, but a Path

The FDE is a product of reality's demands. If you want results, someone has to take the first step.

As McGrew said:

Model capabilities have advanced incredibly fast, but enterprise deployment has lagged far behind.

It's not that the tools aren't strong enough, or that the models fall a bit short—it's that enterprises have no one who can put them into practice. What you're missing is the person who can integrate AI into real-world workflows and deliver the first tangible result.

What the FDE does is turn "uncertain opportunities" into "repeatable processes." It's the starting point of results, the front line of growth.

If an enterprise wants to use AI well, don't start by asking whether the model is good enough—start by asking:

"Do we have someone like that?"

Someone who can pave the way at the customer's site. Someone always has to walk first.
