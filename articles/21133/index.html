This is a 50-item checklist for advancing the hiring, development, and utilization of AI talent. It is organized into 7 stages, covering the definition of utilization goals, identifying the required talent profile, choosing between hiring, development, and outsourcing, and the confirmation items for implementation and adoption—where many companies stall.

Published date Last updated

"We don't have enough AI talent," "We want to start hiring, but we don't know what to decide first." While AI utilization has become a management-level issue, there is still no established playbook for how to secure talent.

This article organizes everything—from designing the goals of AI utilization, identifying the required talent profile, securing talent through hiring, development, or outsourcing, to "implementation and adoption," where many companies stall—into a checklist of 7 stages and 50 items in total. Please use it as a decision-making tool to check how far your company has progressed and where the gaps are.

First, let us explain why the latter half, "Implementation and Adoption," is given the largest number of items. According to the Ministry of Internal Affairs and Communications' "2026 Information and Communications White Paper" (published July 2026), companies in Japan using generative AI for some business tasks reached 86.4% (survey targeted companies with 10 or more employees that have started digital transformation; Japan n=477). However, in the same survey, 27.0% of companies answered that "there are no organizational initiatives for business transformation through generative AI." The figures for the US and Germany are 1.4% and 4.9%, respectively*1. The tools are in place, but the work itself has not changed. Whether this gap can be closed is the main theme of the latter half of the checklist.

Note that details on the definition and types of AI talent and the required skills are covered in a separate article, "What Is AI Talent? Types, Skills, Development Points, and a New Option That Handles Implementation and Adoption in the Field." This article is a practical checklist for confirming "what your company should decide and where to start," building on that content.

① Goals of AI Utilization and Target Tasks

Before starting to secure AI talent, the starting point is clarifying "why AI talent is needed." If you proceed with hiring or development while the purpose remains vague, you will not be able to make full use of the talent you secure.

A common failure is proceeding with securing talent first, without defining the purpose or target tasks, thinking "things will work out if we have someone knowledgeable about AI." The hired or assigned person is left in a state of "not knowing what to solve," and time passes without them being able to demonstrate their abilities.

Narrow the target tasks down to one. Running multiple tasks simultaneously makes it likely that all of them will stall at the trial implementation stage. Experience reaching implementation, even on a small scale, becomes the foundation for expanding to the next task.

How to measure results also needs to be decided before starting. Will you measure by reduced work hours, lead time, or usage rate? If you start without deciding this, you will end up at stage ⑦ (measuring results) in a state of "we tried it, but we don't know if it produced results."

Do not keep the goals and KPIs confined to the hiring or AI promotion team alone; obtain management's agreement and share them with key internal members. If consensus-building is insufficient, you will later be asked to explain "why we use AI for this task" each time, slowing down the pace of promotion.

② Identifying the Required AI Talent Profile

Although they are all called "AI talent," their roles are not the same. If you mistake which role your company needs, both hiring and development will miss the mark.

AI Talent Roles Fall into 3 Categories

When AI talent is categorized by role, it can be organized into the following 3 types.

RoleWhat the person doesMain job titles
People who drive things within the organizationAnalyze field operations, redesign them with AI, and handle everything from implementation to adoption and trainingAI promotion officer, AI Operations Manager, AI Enablement Lead
People who build (applied)Build business systems and AI agents based on existing AI modelsAI Engineer, FDE (Forward Deployed Engineer)
Builders (Foundational)Research and develop the models themselves and create new methodsMachine learning engineers, research scientists

When people hear "AI talent," the third type is probably what comes to mind. However, for most companies, the first need is the first type. Research and development can draw on external resources, but the role of designing AI use tailored to your own operations and rooting it in the workplace is one you cannot simply outsource and wait for results.

The same trend is visible in job postings. In a previous article, "Why Is Demand for FDEs Surging? An In-Depth Analysis of Job Postings from 73 Japanese Companies," when we checked domestic job postings on companies' own recruitment pages in September 2026, 7 of the 73 companies posting FDE positions were hiring to introduce AI into their own or their group's operations. In sales and marketing, some companies are also creating "GTM engineers" who design and implement systems ("What Is a GTM Engineer? Job Description, Differences from Sales Planning, and the Reality at 4 Domestic Companies Hiring").

This imbalance also shows up in the data. According to "DX Trends 2026," published by IPA (Information-technology Promotion Agency) in July 2026, the roles Japanese companies most often reported as "in short supply" were personnel who can plan and drive data management (71.9%), employees who know the workplace and can drive AI adoption (69.6%), and employees who can analyze data with AI and apply it to the business (66.5%). Meanwhile, only 39.1% of companies reported a shortage of AI researchers developing cutting-edge algorithms—along with management with AI understanding (39.0%), the lowest among the seven categories—and 56.7% answered that they "do not need them"*2.

Note that the "Digital Skills Standards" formulated by METI and IPA do not include a category called "AI talent"*3. On the other hand, Japan's Artificial Intelligence Basic Plan, approved by the Cabinet in July 2026, organizes the people responsible for AI use and development into five talent profiles. At the core of efforts to rethink how work is done with AI in mind is the "AI implementation talent." The plan describes this talent as "those who provide AI solutions tailored to the workplace, rather than generic AI solutions," and requires "understanding both industry/administrative operations and AI"*4.

What is required: five capabilities for understanding and reassembling business operations

The emphasis varies by role, but for roles focused on introducing AI into operations and delivering results, the foundational areas are shared. What distinguishes this from the traditional IT talent profile is that the ability to understand and reassemble business operations comes before specialized programming or statistics skills.

Skills/KnowledgeDescription
Practical AI tool skillsUse generative AI effectively in practice and build systems by combining business automation tools. Keep up with technology trends that change monthly
Business process designAnalyze workflows to identify bottlenecks and redesign procedures with AI in mind. Demonstrate return on investment in numbers
Project managementMobilize stakeholders across multiple departments and carry projects through to implementation. Oversee operations after rollout
Education and adoptionExplain things in terms non-engineers can understand and create a state where the workplace can use AI on its own
Legal and ethical literacyJudge what data may be handled and how, based on laws such as the Personal Information Protection Act and the Copyright Act

This does not mean expertise in machine learning and data science is no longer needed. It remains necessary when developing models in-house. However, the challenge most companies face first is "which operations to restructure with AI and how," and without people who can handle that, adoption will not progress even if you assemble a team of technical specialists.

Separate what to develop in-house from what to bring in from outside

Of the five capabilities, business process design and education/adoption favor people who already know your company's operations. How many hours each step takes, who approves what, and why the procedure is what it is—outsiders cannot acquire this knowledge right away. Existing business improvement staff or experienced mid-level employees whom departments rely on are natural candidates.

On the other hand, practical AI tool skills and implementation progress slowly through self-study. Because the available tools change monthly, the burden of keeping up is high, and if no one in-house can teach it, the time spent on trial and error becomes a direct cost.

In other words, rather than treating the required capabilities as one bundle, you should first decide the allocation: which capabilities to develop in-house and which to bring in from outside. Doing everything yourself runs short on time, while outsourcing everything leaves no know-how behind. This allocation directly determines the acquisition method chosen in step ③ below.

③ Choosing the acquisition method

Three options—hiring, internal development, and outsourcing—and their respective limits

Methods for securing AI talent can be broadly organized into three: "hire," "develop in-house," and "outsource." Each has advantages, but it is rare for any single one to be sufficient, and common barriers remain for most companies.

Acquisition methodAdvantagesLimits and common remaining issues
HiringCan bring in ready-to-work talent from outsideCompetition for talent is fierce, and building compensation and onboarding systems takes time. Retention after joining is also an issue
Internal developmentCan develop talent well-versed in your own operationsTakes time before results appear. A teaching structure and know-how on the instructor side are also essential
Outsourcing (training/consulting plus contracted work)Can quickly incorporate external expertiseOften stops at advice or training and does not reach on-site implementation. Some cases see operations stall after delivery

A fourth option: if the product is already decided, leave it to the vendor

If the product to introduce has already been decided, one option is to leave the implementation to that vendor. An increasing number of vendors now offer FDEs (Forward Deployed Engineers), a role that brings the company's own product into the customer's workplace and handles everything from identifying issues to implementation and embedding it into operations. Because the people who know the product best enter the field, this is the fastest route to getting the product up and running.

However, there are things you can delegate to the vendor and work that remains outside that scope.

Easy to delegate to the vendorLikely to fall outside the vendor's scope
Adapting the product to frontline operationsDeciding which product to choose in the first place
Connecting existing systems to the productRedesigning business processes that span multiple tools
Starting operations in the adopting department and initial adoptionRolling out company-wide across departments
Frontline training on how to use the productHandover until in-house staff can run things on their own

Work outside the vendor's scope is hard for a party selling the product to take on, yet if it is entirely outsourced, no know-how remains in-house. If you commission the work without deciding who owns this part, progress stalls beyond the first business process you introduced. The structure of the FDE role and the details of what can be delegated are explained in the separate article "What Is an FDE (Forward Deployed Engineer)? Salary, Skills, Career, and How Companies Can Secure Them".

Four things to decide before bringing in external help

Whether it is a vendor's FDE or another arrangement, what you should decide before bringing external help into the field is the same.

  • Scope of access to the field and data: Without being able to observe operations or touch the data, defining the issues cannot even begin
  • How much decision-making authority to delegate: The value of this role lies in being able to make judgments and act on the spot. If everything must be taken back for internal approvals, speed suffers
  • How to measure results: Align the metrics defined in ① with the external party before work begins
  • The goal for handover: Define, in terms of both operations and deadlines, the state in which your own team can ultimately run things themselves

If you bring someone in while these four points remain vague, even a highly capable person will not deliver results. Conversely, if these are settled, the line between what to outsource and what to keep in-house also falls naturally into place.

Note that these approaches are not an either-or choice. In practice, it is realistic to combine them: hiring or external help for immediate capability, and in-house development for medium- to long-term organizational strength. Sections ④ and ⑤ cover the practicalities of hiring and development respectively, and ⑥ addresses the "execution gap" that remains no matter which approach you choose.

④ Securing talent through hiring

Hiring lets you bring in people who can contribute immediately, but competition for talent is fierce, and preparation that accounts for retention after joining is essential. If you start recruiting with vague job requirements, you will either attract no applicants or hire people who do not match your expectations.

What to look for in selection is not a match with the technology stack they can handle. It is whether they have experience observing business operations, identifying bottlenecks, and rearranging processes to deliver results. Building your criteria around the areas your company lacks among the five areas outlined in ② will make your judgments more consistent.

Even after a successful hire, the work is not done. The more specialized the talent, the harder it is for them to perform when "what they are entrusted with and what results they are evaluated on" is unclear. You need to prepare the entire flow, from defining requirements to post-hire onboarding and evaluation design.

21HR★★★Have you concretely articulated the profile of the talent you seek as job requirements?
22HR★★Do you understand the market rates for AI talent (compensation levels and the state of competition for talent)?
23HR / Field★★★Have you prepared criteria for assessing "a track record of rearranging business processes to deliver results" during selection?
24Field manager★★Have you designed post-hire onboarding and support through ramp-up?
25HR★★Have you put in place evaluation and career design for retention, along with a reception framework with other departments?

⑤ Securing talent through in-house development

In-house development lets you cultivate people who know your own business well, but it takes time to see results. A common pitfall is treating employees as "developed" simply because they completed external training or e-learning. Training that imparts knowledge and hands-on experience using AI in actual work are different things; without opportunities to accumulate the latter, what was learned will not stick.

The way to develop people is not classroom instruction but assigning them to real projects. Pick one target business process and see it through to implementation. For example, if preparing quotations and getting internal approval takes one to two hours every day, redesign that entire flow with AI in mind and bring it to a state where the field can actually use it. People who have been through this cycle once become able to choose the next process themselves. Conversely, no matter how much training they complete, they will not acquire the judgment needed for implementation.

National subsidies now draw the same line. From August 3, 2026, the Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare's Human Resources Development Subsidy Program (Business Expansion Reskilling Support Course) excludes from subsidy eligibility training that teaches generic prompt-writing techniques without applying them to specific business processes. Training directly tied to business operations, such as courses for sales staff creating product proposals with generative AI, remains eligible*5. When choosing training, too, you need to decide in advance which business process it will be applied to.

As noted in ②, the areas best suited to internal development are business process design and education/adoption. Focusing development targets on these areas while bringing in AI tool expertise and implementation from outside, then transferring the entire approach in-house, is faster overall.

Letting time pass without a development plan is itself a factor in the talent shortage. In a survey published by IPA in June 2025, 19.4% of companies said they lacked AI talent yet were taking no action to secure it *6.

⑥ Implementation and adoption [Most important]

The "execution gap" remains no matter which acquisition method you choose

Many companies make it as far as a pilot implementation (PoC). Where they stall is what comes after. PoC results never spread across the company. Even with an AI promotion office or dedicated staff in place, execution on the ground doesn't move. This state—which remains whether you choose hiring, internal development, or outsourcing—is the "execution gap."

It's not only Japanese companies that are stalling. In a survey published by S&P Global Market Intelligence in 2025 (North America and Europe, over 1,000 respondents), 42% of companies had abandoned most of their AI initiatives, a sharp rise from 17% the previous year. On average, 46% of PoCs are abandoned before reaching production *7.

There is a common structure behind this gap. Hiring, development, and outsourcing all focus primarily on "securing people." However, the execution process—how the secured talent actually works in the field and delivers results—will not be filled unless it is designed separately from acquisition. Even if you secure excellent AI talent, if that person just works on ideas in isolation, the field will not change.

What needs filling is not a job vacancy, but the process between concept and adoption

In other words, "AI talent" has been discussed in the context of acquisition—hiring, training, and dispatch. But what is truly lacking is someone who can get into the field, work hands-on, and carry things through to implementation and adoption. What needs filling is not a job vacancy, but the execution process between concept and adoption.

Evaluation of this process is determined not by whether deliverables are complete, but by whether the work has changed. Even if a system runs, if the field doesn't use it, the results are zero. This work therefore includes dialogue with field resistance of the "the new way is a hassle" kind. The fact that this process cannot be isolated into purely technical tasks is also why it is hard to fill through outsourcing. The procedure for advancing this process in five steps, from taking inventory of work processes to measuring results, is organized in a separate article, "How to Drive AI Adoption | 5 Steps to Turn Individual AI Use into Organizational Results."

The option of professional AI talent embedding within the company

One option that is spreading as a way to fill this gap is for professional AI talent to embed within the company and, together with internal staff, achieve company-wide AI use and adoption. It's neither waiting on outside experts nor waiting for in-house development to bear fruit. They are welcomed as if a member of the company, advancing everything from concept to implementation and adoption together.

The mode of engagement depends on how many capable people are already in-house. If no one can act yet, the professional takes the lead: "full-scale promotion." If there are candidates, form a team and hand over the promotion framework: "collaboration." If people can already act but lack only knowledge, focus on teaching: "instruction."

The entry mode is also selectable: a "right-hand mode" supporting the judgment of executives and the AI lead, a "project mode" handling implementation of projects with defined tasks, or a "department mode" embedded directly under each department's manager to support field-level promotion. In any form, the point of embedding in-house and working together remains the same.

There are three things to check when using outside help. Before spending months on hiring: can someone who can execute join and start immediately? Will the approach and know-how remain in-house? And is it clear in advance what kind of talent will join, with what scope of engagement, and at what cost? These three points are also included in the items below.

⑦ Effect Measurement and Company-Wide Rollout

Once implementation and adoption are complete, the next stage is to visualize the results and spread successful patterns to other departments. The review at this point is the turning point that transforms AI utilization from "an initiative in one department" into "the norm across the entire company."

Use the metrics you defined in step ① as they are. If the metrics change across stages, you lose the ability to compare against the pre-initiative baseline. If you neglect effect measurement, you will be left with only the initiative itself, unable to judge whether it produced results.

In particular, if you cannot show return on investment (ROI) in reports to management, you will not get approval for investment in the next stage, and your hard-won implementation will end as a one-off. Carrying out quantitative effect measurement together with building consensus for the next rollout based on it is what leads to company-wide adoption.

Summary

Securing AI talent becomes a shortcut to results when designed to include not just "hiring" and "training," but also "who will carry implementation and adoption through to completion." Start by checking your company's current state against the 22 items with priority ★★★.

In many companies, it is step ⑥ that remains unfinished to the end. After organizing your objectives, talent profiles, and securing methods in steps ①–⑤, focus on checking the items in step ⑥. Once you decide who will fill the execution gap, both the requirements for people brought in from outside and the roles to keep in-house will naturally fall into place.

First, pick one business process and one challenge in your company where AI should be implemented, and put someone who can execute it there. Don't leave AI promotion to outsiders and wait—bring people inside and move forward together. I hope this checklist serves as the blueprint for that design.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q. Where should I start when using this checklist?

First, go through the 22 items with priority ★★★ out of the 50 total. Then, calculate the percentage of checked items per chapter; the chapter with the lowest percentage is your company's bottleneck. The stage where most companies stall is step ⑥, "Implementation and Adoption."

Q. What should I start with when writing the requirements for AI talent?

Start from the target business process, not the job title. First narrow down to a single target business process, then break down the required capabilities into five areas (practical AI tool skills / business process design / project management / education and adoption / legal regulations and ethics), and decide which areas to develop in-house and which to bring in from outside. Writing in this order determines both the job requirements and the scope of external utilization at the same time.

Q. Should AI talent be secured through hiring or training?

Neither alone will fill the gap. Business process design and education/adoption favor people who know your company's operations, so in-house training suits them, while practical AI tool skills and implementation progress faster when brought in from outside. Rather than an either/or choice, it is more realistic to treat it as a matter of allocation.

Q. Can this checklist be used even if no one in the company is knowledgeable about AI?

Yes. In fact, the first achievement will be identifying "items where the decision-maker has not been determined" by looking at the owner column. This is because what tends to stall progress is not the lack of knowledgeable people, but the fact that no one has been assigned to own the execution process.
