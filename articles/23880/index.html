What Is Shadow AI: How FDEs Turn Risk into Use Cases

Original title: 影子 AI 是什么：FDE 如何把风险变成用例

FDE Times · 2026-11-17 · 5 min read

Summary

This guide argues that customer shadow AI is a requirements list, not an incident. Citing surveys from TELUS Digital, PagerDuty, and IBM, it shows widespread personal AI use with sensitive data, explains why bans fail, and walks FDEs through a no-blame interview method: inventory each paste as a use case with data type and frequency, prioritize by risk, migrate high-risk daily tasks to approved tools first, and co-write policy based on real findings. It includes interview scripts, a tracking table, common trust-breaking mistakes, and resume framing advice.

Key points

  • 68% of employees use personal AI accounts; 57% entered sensitive data.
  • Bans can't recover pasted data or remove the underlying work need.
  • Each paste reveals task, data type, frequency, and acceptable output quality.
  • Interviews must promise no name-recording to keep answers honest.
  • High-risk, high-frequency tasks should define the first deployment scope.

A blame-free interview can turn data leak risks into the first use case list for your deployment.

Key takeaways

  • Before you even arrive, the customer's employees have almost certainly already pasted data into public chatbots
  • Bans can't recall data that's already been sent, nor can they eliminate the work demands that drove employees to do it
  • Every paste is a use case with a data type and frequency; inventory them one by one, then replace them with approved tools

Imagine it's your first week at an insurance company. You're interviewing the claims team to design an internal assistant, and the team lead casually mentions: "We already do report summarization—we just paste it into ChatGPT."

The room goes quiet for a few seconds, and someone glances at the IT colleague. How you react in those seconds determines whether the requirements research can continue. Show any panic or blame, and no one will tell you the truth again.

This scenario is common. A TELUS Digital survey found that 68% of employees use personal AI accounts at work, and 57% of them admit to entering sensitive information.

PagerDuty's 2026 shadow AI survey found that 34% of office workers have entered customer data or information into public AI tools, and 31% have entered financial information or confidential documents and strategy.

A Forward Deployed Engineer (FDE) bringing AI into an enterprise almost always arrives after these pastes have already happened.

Bans can't recall what's already been pasted

The most common reaction is to ban it. In 2023, a Samsung engineer uploaded sensitive internal source code to ChatGPT, causing an accidental leak, and the company subsequently issued a memo banning generative AI tools. The company's reasoning: data sent to chatbots is stored on the provider's servers.

The concern is legitimate, but a ban only stops future pastes. It can't recall data that's already been sent, and it won't make the stack of reports awaiting summarization disappear every morning. Without a replacement tool, the demand remains, and employees will likely just switch to more covert workarounds.

There's usually no existing policy to fall back on either. IBM's 2025 Cost of a Data Breach report found that 63% of organizations that experienced data breaches had no AI governance policy to manage AI or stop employees from using shadow AI. You're likely the first person to raise this question systematically.

When you need to convince the project sponsor, there's also a cost argument. The same report found that high shadow AI usage increases the global average cost of a data breach by $670,000. Bringing AI usage into the open is more valuable than banning it and driving it underground.

Every paste is a use case

The core idea is simple: shadow AI tells you both where the risks are and what users actually need.

Every time an employee pastes data into a chatbot, they're giving you four pieces of information that are hard to extract in any workshop: which task is most time-consuming, what data it involves, how often it happens, and what output quality they already consider good enough.

Seen this way, your role changes. You're not there to catch people—you're there to inventory what users are already doing and migrate it to a controlled environment.

First, run the numbers

Suppose the claims team has 40 people. Treat TELUS's percentages as a rough estimate, not a measurement: 68% of 40 people is about 27 people using personal AI accounts. Of those, 57% have entered sensitive information—about 15 people.

This number isn't for the report; it's to help you ask the right questions. Once you assume that as many as 15 people may have pasted sensitive data, you'll stop asking "Is anyone using it?" and start asking "What are they using it for, and what kind of data is involved?"

Interview script

Before the interviews, agree on boundaries with the project sponsor and the customer's security contact: you only collect tasks and data types, never names.

If you encounter strictly regulated data such as medical records or account numbers, report it only to the one pre-agreed person. It's precisely this boundary that makes your "no blame" promise credible.

Open with a sentence that lowers defenses: "I'm not here to check up on anyone. I want to know which work is heavy enough that people went and found their own tools, so the new tool can take over exactly those tasks."

Then ask five questions in order. "What do you most often ask AI to do?" "What do you paste in: reports, emails, or spreadsheets?" "Roughly how many times a week?" "What would the output need to look like for you to actually use it?" "If you couldn't use that tool starting tomorrow, which work would slow down?"

The last question is the most valuable. It reveals which tasks are truly worth prioritizing, and it shows the sponsor what a ban would slow down. Record the answers in a list like this:

From the list to approved tools

Once the list is built, rank it by risk and frequency. The row that's high-risk and done daily is the scope of your first deployment. The rationale is practical: replacing it eliminates the biggest risk and immediately gives you users who engage every day.

Data that's already been sent should be assessed and decided by the customer's security team. Hand the list over to them; don't judge the consequences yourself, and don't promise anyone the data is safe.

When building the replacement tool, design access controls from the start—don't leave it until the end of the project. According to IBM, 97% of organizations that experienced AI-related security incidents said they lacked adequate AI access controls. That figure covers AI incidents in general, but it shows where vulnerabilities typically lie.

The final step is to write a short policy with the customer: which data types may go into which tools. A policy based on a real list is far more grounded than one drafted from thin air.

Mistakes that cost you trust

The most common mistake is recording names. Once a single name appears in a report to leadership, every subsequent interview will yield only safe answers. The second mistake is tossing the whole issue to the security team as out of scope—when it's precisely this list that defines the real scope.

The third mistake is taking the easy route and proposing a ban without offering a replacement tool. The fourth is the easiest to commit, because you cause it yourself: pasting customer logs or data samples into your personal AI account to debug faster. In that moment, you become a row in your own list.

How to write this skill on your resume

If the FDE role you're targeting mentions AI governance, security reviews, or collaborating with customer security teams, put this skill near the top. Write it as a result on your resume—for example, the size of the team across which you inventoried unauthorized AI usage, and which use cases you migrated to controlled tools.

In behavioral interviews, it's also a weighty story: a sensitive situation, a no-blame promise you kept, and a deployment that landed as a result. The interviewer will see that you can both do the engineering and protect the customer's trust.

The few seconds of silence after "we already just paste it into ChatGPT" are the moment you get the most honest information in your entire requirements research. Don't let it slip away in a frown.
