Chapter 5 · Three Hundred Pages of Requirements Documents, and Still Not Requirements

Original title: 第 5 章 · 三百页需求文档，为什么仍然不是需求

FDE 的来路 · 6 min read

Summary

This chapter argues that thick requirement documents still miss real requirements: skilled operators can't articulate their tacit knowledge, requirements get distorted as they pass from boss to IT to vendor, and documents rarely state what outcome counts as solving the problem. The author, drawing on Palantir's description of forward deployed engineers, recommends observing users for a full day before building, questioning whether a process deserves automation at all, and using prototypes with messy real data to expose unknowns rather than win approval. Requirements are ultimately a set of choices co-built by client and engineer.

Key points

  • Tacit knowledge of experienced operators never makes it into documents
  • Requirements change shape through each layer of translation
  • Observing real work reveals processes that should be deleted, not automated
  • Prototypes should use messy data to surface problems, not just demos
  • Requirements are a set of trade-offs, co-built with the client

Requirements documents record settled rules, yet they easily miss on-site exceptions, tacit knowledge, and organizational disagreements. This chapter explains why real requirements must be continuously discovered at the actual workplace.

The most common sentence in requirements research is:

"We currently do this manually—just automate it following this process."

It sounds perfectly clear.

But sit down next to the operator, and things look completely different.

He first opens the system to check orders, then digs through the shared drive for last month's spreadsheet, and when he sees a certain type of customer name, he searches an old notification in the chat history. When an amount doesn't match, he doesn't immediately flag an error—he relies on experience to guess which category of historical problem it might be. If he truly can't figure it out, he asks the colleague next door who has been doing this for eight years.

You ask, why is this step handled this way?

He says, it's always been done this way.

You ask again, when can't it be done this way?

He thinks for a moment and says, well, it depends.

The entry point of a project is often hidden in those three words: "it depends."

Being Able to Do It Doesn't Mean Being Able to Explain It

Much work only becomes fast after it becomes second nature.

The cost of proficiency is that the operator no longer consciously notices what he is judging. When he sees a field, a customer name, or a remark, the classification has already happened in his head. Ask him to articulate the complete rules on the spot, and he'll find it surprisingly hard.

This isn't the user being uncooperative, nor is it poor logic on the part of business staff.

A person who handles dozens of exceptions a day turns much of that knowledge into muscle memory. System documentation only records the normal flow; the exceptions live in people's memories. When a newcomer can't learn it, the veteran walks them through it a few times; by the time the newcomer becomes a veteran, the rules still haven't been written down.

In requirements interviews held in conference rooms, what you most easily get is "how things should be done."

What you can only see on-site is "how things are actually done."

The two differ, and it isn't necessarily because anyone is lying. The formal process represents how the organization wants work to happen; the on-site actions represent how people get things done when time, data, and permissions are all imperfect.

If an FDE only listens to the former, they will build a system that demos smoothly and stalls the moment it goes live.

Requirements Change Shape Several Times in Transmission

The boss says, we want to improve collections efficiency.

The business lead translates that into accounts receivable alerts.

The IT department translates it further into a dashboard and message notifications.

The requirements the vendor receives become a set of fields, pages, and interfaces.

No single layer of translation contains an obvious error, yet the final deliverable may end up far from what the boss originally cared about.

The boss wants the money back sooner; the business lead is thinking about which accounts are overdue; IT is thinking about how to display it; the vendor is responsible for launching on time. As for who follows up after the alert, why the customer isn't paying, and how disputes are handled—none of that naturally lands on anyone.

A requirements document can be written thick, because pages, fields, permissions, and processes can all be expanded. But the hardest page to write is often missing:

What outcome must occur for this problem to count as solved?

Without that page, the more complete the document, the more likely the team efficiently delivers something with no clear direction.

Watch a Full Day First, Then Talk About What to Build

There is a very plain practice in the FDE method: first work alongside real users.

Don't rush to ask what features they want; first watch how a day goes. Which tasks take the most time, which steps require constantly switching between systems, where people have to wait for someone else's reply, which anomalies require calling in the veteran, which rules sound simple when spoken but produce endless exceptions the moment they touch real data.

Palantir's description of Forward Deployed Engineers emphasizes that they embed in customer environments, solve customer problems using the existing platform, and also handle production incidents, deploy fixes, and coordinate with customers and product teams. The "forward" here isn't a geographic location—it means being close enough to where the work actually happens.

Close enough to see the parts that aren't in the requirements document.

This isn't entirely different from traditional business analysis. Excellent implementation consultants, process consultants, and systems analysts have long been doing exactly this. FDE didn't invent on-site observation; it simply makes this old work impossible to skip in the AI era.

The reason is simple: in the past, a missed exception might be remedied manually by an operator; now, if an AI agent handles it automatically, missed exceptions get executed at scale.

Don't Automate a Bad Process Faster

On-site observation has another easily overlooked function: judging whether the work is worth automating at all.

The customer says they copy data from System A to System B every day, and the team's most direct reaction is to build a bot. But keep asking, and you may find that no one uses the fields in System B at all—they're just a requirement left over from some audit five years ago.

Automating the copying would certainly save time.

The better answer might be to delete it.

Processes inside enterprises accumulate like old code. The policy changed, but the form wasn't removed; the system was replaced, but the approval step was kept; some incident once added a check, and three years later no one remembers what it guards against.

If an FDE replicates the user's existing actions step by step, it's easy to turn historical baggage into a faster, more expensive new system.

So on-site observation isn't just about watching "how people do it"—you also have to keep asking:

  • What risk is this step guarding against?
  • If it weren't done, what would happen?
  • Who uses its output?
  • Is there an earlier point where the problem could be eliminated?
  • Is this a stable rule, or just one person's temporary habit?

Some answers belong in the system; others should first drive the business to change its process.

Prototypes Aren't for Getting the Customer to Nod

Requirements are hard to articulate in one pass, which is why prototypes have value.

But there are two ways to use a prototype.

One is to prove the technology can do it. Prepare a batch of clean data, produce beautiful results, and the customer says "that's exactly it." This kind of prototype is good for showing direction, but it easily sends the project into the proof-of-concept graveyard: everyone approves, and no one knows how the next step enters real work.

The other kind of prototype forces problems into the open.

Use dirty data close to reality, and let users operate it with their own hands; put the normal flow and the most troublesome exceptions in together; agree in advance on a business metric and a referee. After the prototype is done, the team doesn't just know whether the model runs—they know where human intervention is needed, which permissions can't be granted, and which errors are unacceptable.

Palantir's publicly available AIP workflows also explicitly remind users: the examples use hypothetical data, and to deploy this as a real business process, you must swap in your organization's own data. This seemingly ordinary sentence precisely marks the line between demo and production.

The value of a prototype isn't to make the customer nod faster—it's to make both sides discover sooner that they hadn't thought things through.

Requirements Aren't a Sentence; They're a Set of Choices

At this point, look back at that sentence "just automate the existing process," and you'll see it decided nothing.

Which steps stay unchanged, and which should be deleted? The normal cases run automatically—who handles the exceptions? How do you trade off speed, accuracy, and risk? Does the system make suggestions, or can it execute directly? If the business rules change, who updates them?

Every answer shapes what the product looks like—and also determines on whose shoulders the responsibility falls.

The value of an FDE isn't being better than others at "digging out requirements." That phrase makes it sound as if requirements were buried there all along, just waiting for a clever person to excavate them.

Reality looks more like co-construction. The customer brings business experience, the engineer brings technical boundaries, and through on-site work, data, and prototypes, the two sides together decide which problems are worth solving—and what counts as solved.

This also means not every requirement a customer voices should be accepted.

But once you start rejecting requirements, another contradiction arises: the vendor makes money on projects and person-days, and the customer is willing to pay for customization. Everyone knows too much customization will drag down the product, but the contract in front of them is real money.

In the next chapter, we'll do the math on this.
