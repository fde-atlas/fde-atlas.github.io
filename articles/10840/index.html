Senior Palantir FDE in a Deep Interview with OpenAI's Head of FDEs: How Real Forward Deployed Engineers Drive Enterprise AI Adoption

Original title: Palantir 资深 FDE 深度采访 OpenAI FDE 负责人：真正的 FDE 如何推进企业落地 AI

53AI · 2026-09-08 · 2 min read

Summary

This article recaps a roughly 40-minute interview in which a senior Palantir FDE speaks with Colin Jarvis, who leads OpenAI's FDE team, about deploying AI into enterprises. The core argument: FDEs do not just build demos; they embed with customers, solve painful 0-to-1 problems, build trust, and then abstract reusable parts into products. Two cases are described. With Morgan Stanley, GPT-4 was applied to wealth management research; about 98% of advisors adopted the system and research usage roughly tripled, though the prototype took 6-8 weeks while earning trust took about 4 months. At a European semiconductor company, AI moved from bug investigation to a full workflow of finding, fixing, testing, and creating PRs. Jarvis warns the biggest FDE mistake is generalizing too early—solve the specific problem first, then productize.

Key points

  • FDEs embed on-site to solve real customer problems, not just build demos.
  • Morgan Stanley case: ~98% advisor adoption, research usage up ~3x.
  • Prototypes took 6-8 weeks, but building trust took about 4 months.
  • Semiconductor case: AI handled bugs end-to-end, from detection to PRs.
  • Biggest FDE mistake is generalizing too early; solve first, productize later.

For AI to truly enter the enterprise, it's not about building a demo. The real difficulty is: stepping into the customer's actual business, solving real problems, making employees confident and willing to use it, and ultimately turning it into a replicable product. This is what the FDE (Forward Deployed Engineer) does.

This share is a 40-minute in-depth interview well worth watching: a senior Palantir FDE in conversation with Colin Jarvis, head of OpenAI's FDE team, discussing how OpenAI truly deploys AI into enterprises, plus Colin's sharing of three successful enterprise cases from the FDE team he leads. Packed with insights.

The video has been prepared with both Chinese and English subtitles for easy viewing of the original interview.

Morgan Stanley: 6 weeks for the technology, but 4 months for trust

OpenAI partnered with Morgan Stanley to apply GPT-4 to wealth management research.

The technology itself wasn't the biggest problem; the real question was:

"Would a financial advisor really dare to show an AI-generated answer to a client?"

The FDEs worked directly with the client's engineers, financial experts, and advisors, built test sets, had real users continuously validate and give feedback, and kept iterating on the system.

The result: roughly 98% of wealth advisors adopted the system, and usage of research reports increased about 3x.

An interesting detail:

The prototype might take just 6-8 weeks to build, but getting the enterprise to truly trust it took another 4 months.

European semiconductor company: FDE solves the enterprise's 0-to-1 problem and designs workflows

Semiconductor engineers spend a huge amount of time on bug triage, verification, debugging, and maintenance.

At first, the FDEs had AI help investigate bugs; later, they gradually let AI:

Identify the issue → attempt a fix → run tests → verify results → create a PR.

The goal wasn't "getting engineers to use AI," but rather:

By the time engineers start work, many routine bugs have already been handled by AI, leaving only the most complex problems for humans.

This is no longer a simple AI tool—it's redesigning the entire engineering workflow.

03 FDE: Turning customer problems into products

FDEs are not a consulting firm, nor an outsourcing team.

A great FDE team first goes on-site with the customer and solves a very specific, very painful 0→1 problem.

Then they keep looking for the reusable parts within it.

Real customer problem → solution → abstracted general capability → product

So Colin mentioned the most common mistake FDEs make:

Generalizing too early.

Don't first build something that looks very general and then go looking for customer problems.

Instead, first genuinely solve the customer's problem.

Doing what doesn't scale.

You could even sum up the FDE with a vivid phrase:

Eat pain and excrete product.

Take in the customer's most painful problems, and turn them into a product.
