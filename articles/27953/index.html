Glean Founding FDE: what the role tests, how to apply

FDE Times · 2026-10-25 · 6 min read

Summary

The article examines Glean's Founding Forward Deployed Engineer posting and argues it defines a genuinely product-oriented FDE role. The core job is 0-to-1 product creation for strategic customers, and what the engineer builds ships in Glean's product. Citing Thomas Otter, it contrasts this with FDE roles billed to customers, which serve projects rather than products. Requirements include 4+ years of technical experience, building production software from scratch under ambiguous requirements, and skills in agent development, evals, and deployment at scale. The role sits in a pod with a Forward Deployed PM, partners with customer C-suites, and requires 25–50% travel. The author also advises structuring CVs around business problems, ambiguity, self-built solutions, and reuse.

Key points

  • Glean's Founding FDE role centers on 0-to-1 product creation, not deploying existing software.
  • Otter's test: FDEs billed to customers serve the project, not the product; Glean chose the opposite model.
  • Requirements: 4+ years experience and building production software from scratch in ambiguous environments.
  • AI skills tested are agent development, evals, and scaled deployment, not just API calls.
  • The role involves a pod with a Forward Deployed PM, C-suite access, and 25–50% travel.

What they build for strategic customers ships in Glean's product, which is why strong implementation experience alone will not clear the hiring bar.

In brief

  • Glean's job post states plainly that the core job of a Founding FDE is 0-to-1 product creation, and that what the engineer builds will be shipped by Glean in its product.
  • Thomas Otter argues that FDEs billed to customers serve the project rather than the product. Glean has chosen the opposite model.
  • To apply, you need evidence that you have built production software yourself while requirements were still vague; being good at deployment is not enough.

“Your primary focus will be 0-to-1 product creation.” The sentence comes from Glean’s posting for a Founding Forward Deployed Engineer, and it puts the weight on creating products rather than rolling out ones that already exist.

A later line in the post is blunter still: what you build will be shipped by Glean in its product. That makes the person in this seat more than an extension of the product team. They sit at the source of the roadmap.

This matters because the FDE title is being used very loosely. Thomas Otter, writing about the profession, warns that many FDE roles are really implementation or pre-sales jobs with a new label.

Glean’s post shows what a “real” FDE role tests for, and why experience installing an existing product, however well done, will not get you past the screen.

Why strong deployment work falls short

In a deployment role the product already exists. The question is how to make it run in the customer’s environment. Glean’s post assigns a different task: find the biggest unsolved business problems at its most strategic customers. At that point there is no product to install.

Hence the core requirement in the post: you have built production software from scratch in an ambiguous environment. The word that matters is “ambiguous.”

An implementer works from a specification that already exists. Someone doing 0-to-1 work has to write that specification, often after a few conversations in which the customer has not yet worked out what it needs.

Otter describes exactly this kind of work. In his view, FDEs suit jobs where the scope is uncertain, where you are building something nobody has built before.

Otter’s test: project or product?

Otter also offers a quick test: if FDEs are billed to the customer, they work for the project, not the product. The reasoning is easy to follow. When the customer pays for an engineer’s time, both sides are motivated to deliver what the contract says, and nobody has a reason to turn the solution into something reusable.

Glean has chosen the opposite path. The line “what you build will be shipped by Glean in its product” is a commitment about the operating model as much as a recruiting pitch. Underdog’s 2026 career guide makes the same point: FDEs turn chaos on the customer side into product insight the company can reuse.

Put Glean’s post next to Otter’s warning and you get a table for checking any FDE listing:

Aspect Deployment role labeled FDE Glean-style Founding FDE
Starting point An existing product that needs to be installed and running A business problem nobody has solved
Output A completed project for one customer Software that then goes into the shared product
Who pays for the time Usually the customer, per project The company, because the results feed the roadmap
Experience to prove Configuration, integration, support Building production software from scratch with vague requirements
Who you work with Support and sales teams A Forward Deployed PM, C-suite leaders

The left-hand column is not lesser work. It is a different job, one that needs a different CV and leads to a different career path.

A week at the customer

Say the strategic customer is an insurance company whose legal team spends hours each week hunting for clauses in contracts. A deployment engineer would ask which storage systems to connect and how to set permissions. Those are the right questions, but they start from the product.

A Founding FDE asks about the business first: which decision is being held up, what the delay costs the company, who signs off. The real problem may turn out to be comparing new contracts against a standard template, with search a secondary concern.

The engineer then builds a small agent, writes an eval set from the legal team’s real cases, and moves it to production only once the eval scores are reliable enough.

The last step is what separates the two roles: rewriting the solution so a second and third customer can use it too. In Glean’s model, that is the moment something built for one customer becomes a feature of the whole product.

A pod, a seat beside the C-suite, and a lot of flights

Glean’s post, listed on General Catalyst’s jobs board, says the role sits in a pod with a Forward Deployed PM and acts as the technical partner to the customer’s C-suite.

That structure says a lot. Having a PM alongside means the engineer does not carry all of the product-shaping alone, but they still have to argue as an equal about what should be built.

The post also asks you to work with the autonomy and ownership of a founder. In practice, nobody hands you tickets. You choose the problem, and you own the consequences if you choose wrong.

There is a concrete personal cost too: the role requires travel 25–50% of the time. If your family situation cannot accommodate that, know it before you enter the interview process rather than weighing it once an offer arrives.

The AI requirements are not decoration

The floor in the post is 4+ years in a technical role with a record of shipping software to production. But it also names a further area of experience: agent development, evaluation frameworks and deployment at scale.

These three belong together for a reason. Anyone can build a demo of an agent in a week at a customer. What separates genuine 0-to-1 builders from demo builders is evals: do you know when an agent is good enough for production, and can you prove it to the customer’s leadership?

So if you are moving over from backend or data engineering, learning to call a model’s API is only the start. Learn to write eval sets from real data and to monitor agents as traffic grows. That is what postings of this kind are testing.

How to prove 0-to-1 on paper

Most engineering CVs read as a list of technologies and systems worked on. For a role like Founding FDE at Glean, that format hides exactly what the recruiter needs to see.

They are not asking whether you know Kubernetes. They are asking whether you have ever started from an unsolved problem and finished with software that real people use.

Write each important project in four beats: the business problem, the initial ambiguity, what you built yourself, and where it was reused afterward. Most people skip the last beat, yet it is the one that answers Otter’s question: were you working for the project or for the product?

When reading job posts, run the test in reverse. If a post talks only about configuration, onboarding and demo support, it is a deployment role, whatever the title says.

If it talks about building from scratch, about the roadmap, about working with customer leadership, you are looking at a product role, and you need your 0-to-1 story ready.

The word “Founding” in the title sounds like an honorific. Read Glean’s post closely and it turns out to be an interview question: have you ever created something that did not exist, when nobody could tell you what it should look like?
