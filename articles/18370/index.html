FDE: Hire In-House or Use External Talent? Decide Based on the Volume and Continuity of Development Demand

Original title: FDEは採用か外部活用か｜開発需要の量と継続性で決める

株式会社Sai · 2026-08-26 · 14 min read

Summary

This article by Japan's Sai argues that choosing between hiring a forward deployed engineer (FDE) and using external support should hinge on the volume and continuity of development demand, not candidate quality. Companies with constant full-time demand should hire; those with fluctuating demand benefit from external engagement. Job postings from Palantir and OpenAI (Tokyo) require both implementation skills and customer-facing experience, meaning Japanese firms compete with global AI companies for the same talent pool. Labor data shows IT engineer job openings-to-applicants ratios above the all-occupation average, and METI's 2019 estimate projects a 450,000-person IT talent gap by 2030, so the hiring market won't loosen soon. External contracts fall into three types—quasi-delegation (time-based), contracting (deliverable-based), and advisory—but only time-based arrangements suit on-site FDE work. The recommended path: start externally with one business task, transfer skills internally, then hire once demand stabilizes.

Key points

  • The hire-vs-outsource decision depends on demand volume and continuity, not ability.
  • Palantir and OpenAI FDE postings demand both coding skill and customer-facing experience.
  • Japan's IT talent shortage (450,000 projected by 2030) means the hiring market won't ease soon.
  • Only time-based quasi-delegation contracts support on-site, exploratory FDE work.
  • A third path: start externally, transfer skills, then hire once demand stabilizes.

  • Palantir's FDE role includes direct interaction with customer stakeholders, and OpenAI lists five or more years of practical experience including customer-facing work; if Japanese companies recruit the same profile, they will compete for the same talent pool as overseas AI companies.

The question of hiring an FDE versus using external resources is a decision about whether to employ, as staff, engineers who combine implementation skills with on-site negotiation ability, or to procure them through external accompaniment contracted by workload.

After being convinced by the FDE way of working, things stall at the stage of deciding who will fill that role. Articles explaining the job title do not cover how to handle development demand while waiting for applications that may not come. This article narrows the decision criteria down to two—volume and continuity of your company's development demand—and shows the sequence of filling the gap until hiring is completed with external resources, leading into a successful hire.

Whether to hire or use external resources is determined by the volume and continuity of development demand, not by ability

In a word, of the five axes in the table, speed and agility favor external resources, while accumulation and control favor in-house hiring. That is why the earlier the business phase, the more rational external resources become, and the more stable the development demand, the more rational hiring becomes.

  • Time to start: Hiring passes through four stages in order: recruiting, selection, onboarding, and ramp-up. External engagement starts as soon as the contract is signed and does not wait for a recruiting period
  • Nature of cost: Hiring is a fixed cost—salary, recruiting expenses, and training costs. External engagement is a variable cost only for the period needed, and the decision to stop can be made at the contract level
  • How knowledge remains: With hiring, business understanding accumulates in internal people. With external engagement, it leaks outside unless you take care, but it can be retained if skill transfer is built in
  • Flexibility: With hiring, since they are employees, you can flexibly change the tasks you assign. With external engagement, the contract scope is the boundary, but increases and decreases in workload can be adjusted at contract renewal
  • Risk: With hiring, mismatches and turnover are painful. With external engagement, the main risks are variation in partner capability and hollowing out from over-delegation

The idea that "hiring one excellent person will solve it" often misses because hiring success is not determined by that person's ability alone. In companies where full-time work never runs out, excellent people stay longer; in companies where demand fluctuates month to month, the same person becomes idle and drifts away. The starting point of the decision is not candidate quality but taking inventory of your company's development demand.

Overseas AI companies' FDE job postings seek experienced people with both implementation skills and customer negotiation ability

The best material for verifying the reality of the hiring side is what requirements the companies that created this role set. The job posting from US Palantir, which pioneered the FDE role, describes it as a role that works alongside customers to quickly understand problems and design and build solutions. The duties include direct interaction with customer stakeholders from technical teams to executives, and requirements include more than one year of practical experience after graduating from university and up to 25% travel to customer sites (Palantir official job posting, Forward Deployed Software Engineer, New York position, verified September 15, 2026).

OpenAI's FDE job posting for its Tokyo base is even more specific. Required experience includes five or more years of engineering or technical implementation experience including customer-facing work, the ability to write and evaluate production-quality code in both frontend and backend, and experience building or implementing systems using LLMs (large language models). The position is based in Tokyo with a hybrid schedule of three days in the office per week, travel is mainly domestic, and fluency in both Japanese and English is mandatory (OpenAI official job posting, Forward Deployed Engineer - Tokyo, verified September 15, 2026).

Two things can be read from this. First, neither company looks only at implementation skills. Palantir includes direct interaction with customer stakeholders in the duties, and OpenAI lists practical experience including customer-facing work among required experience. Second is the fact that recruiting the same profile in Tokyo means competing for the same talent pool as overseas AI companies. Job postings disappear when recruitment ends, so we noted the date. Verifying the four skills required of an FDE in interviews also requires discernment on the hiring side, but before that, you need to know who you will be competing with for this profile.

Job openings ratios and future projections show the IT engineer hiring market will not loosen anytime soon

To verify hiring difficulty with numbers rather than impressions, use the occupation-by-occupation data from the General Job Placement Situation published monthly by the Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare. In the July 2026 figures, the overall effective job openings-to-applicants ratio (seasonally adjusted) was 1.18 (Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare, "General Job Placement Situation (July 2026)", published August 28, 2026). Looking at the reference statistical tables (by occupation, regular employment, including part-time, raw figures) in the same release, the all-occupation total was 1.05, information processing and communications engineers were 1.34, and the new job openings ratio was 2.98. For regular employment excluding part-time, information processing and communications engineers were at 1.50 against an all-occupation total of 1.16 (reference statistical tables PDF from the same release, reference tables 6 and 7-2).

These figures are the ratio of jobs to job seekers via Hello Work and do not include hiring through private job boards or recruiting agencies. Even so, it is clear that the ratio for IT engineers exceeds the all-occupation average. However, since the figures are not extreme, it is reasonable to conclude that the essence of hiring difficulty lies not in the sheer number of engineers in general but in the scarcity of the profile described in the previous section.

The long-term outlook is shown by METI's estimates. The "Survey on IT Talent Supply and Demand," conducted jointly by METI, the Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare, and MEXT, estimated the IT talent supply-demand gap in 2030 at 450,000 people under a median scenario assuming IT demand growth of about 2.7% per year and labor productivity growth of 0.7% per year. The same document projects that if labor productivity growth of 3.54% per year is achieved, demand and supply will be in balance as of 2030 (METI, "Survey on IT Talent Supply and Demand (Summary)", April 2019).

It should be noted that this is a 2019 estimate, but what matters for the decision is not the number of shortages itself but the latter condition. The premise for shortages to be resolved is rising productivity, not the job market naturally loosening. The more generative AI expands the scope one engineer can cover, the higher the requirements tend to become for the one person you want to hire. How to handle development demand while waiting for a hire is not a matter of waiting for the market to loosen—it is a decision your company must make itself.

In-house hiring suits companies whose full-time development demand never stops

If multiple of the following conditions apply, it is worth considering hiring as the core of your approach.

  • Development demand is constant: There is a continuous stream of operations and systems you want to improve, and the workload never falls below full-time
  • Software is core to the business: The systems you build are themselves the source of competitive advantage, and you do not want that knowledge to leave the company
  • You value long-term accumulation: You want one person to accumulate understanding of both the operations and the systems, and to leverage it over a span of years
  • You have a support structure in place: You can provide the discretion and evaluation mechanisms needed, and create an environment where the engineer does not work in isolation

Even for companies that meet these conditions, three difficulties specific to hiring remain. The first is assessment. Skills on a resume are a different matter from being able to see a project through on the ground, which is why the job posting in the previous section describes customer-facing work in its duties and required experience. The second is dependence on a single person. The first hire becomes the only FDE, so the function disappears the moment they leave. The third is time. Development demand does not wait during the four stages of recruiting, selection, onboarding, and ramp-up. How to fill this gap leads to the next topic, external resourcing, and the third path.

External resourcing suits companies with fluctuating demand that want to start immediately with one operation

  • You want to start immediately: Without waiting for a recruiting period, work on the ground can begin as soon as the contract is signed
  • Demand fluctuates: You can adjust workload through the contract—heavier during launch phases, lighter once things stabilize
  • You want to trial one operation first: You can start small to verify whether it is a problem where FDE-style support is effective, then expand
  • You do not want to increase fixed costs: Instead of fixed costs like personnel expenses, you can treat it as a variable cost for only the period needed

There are two points of caution. If the scope of the contract is poorly designed, costs can balloon with "this and that." And if you leave everything to the external party, no knowledge will remain in-house. The latter can be prevented by building skill transfer into the accompaniment process. The fact that an FDE's work includes the final stage of handover to the in-house team is explained in the six phases of an FDE's work.

External resourcing contracts come in three forms—quasi-mandate (junji inin), contracting, and advisory—and quasi-mandate is what meets the expectation of working on the ground

Even when lumped together as "external resourcing," contract forms fall into three types.

  • Quasi-mandate (capacity-based): A form of accompaniment with a set weekly or monthly workload. FDE-style support centers on this form, allowing flexible changes to the content as problems evolve
  • Contracting (deliverable-based): A form where what is to be built is fixed in advance and ordered. It suits projects with settled specifications, but is poorly suited to exploratory work
  • Advisory / spot consultations: A form of involvement a few times a month as a sounding board for technical decisions. It does not add implementation capacity, but can reinforce decisions on hiring and technology selection

The only form that can realistically meet the FDE-like expectation of "embedding in the field and identifying problems from within" is the quasi-mandate type. An FDE seeing things through to results is a matter of how they work in the field; what the contract specifies is workload and the scope of duties. It is not a contract promising completed deliverables. If the desired contract form and the expected way of working diverge, discrepancies will surface after work begins. The reasons the contracting framework does not fit exploratory work are explained from the structure of the contract in five patterns of new business outsourcing failure. The distinction between quasi-mandate and SES (client-site dispatch) is summarized in the SES section of how FDEs differ from conventional engineers. Please consult legal counsel or specialists regarding contract form selection and clause details.

To look concretely inside a quasi-mandate arrangement, we use SAI Corporation's FDE accompaniment development as an example. The contract is a monthly fee system, with workload set according to the scale of the problem. Indicative workload figures are listed on the service page. The initial phase, from identifying problems to minimal implementation, proceeds mainly through on-site visits, switching to a primarily remote approach once operations become established. The engineers who enter the field have 3 to 5 years of experience, and work can begin as early as the week after the initial consultation. Start timing varies depending on team availability and your company's preparation. When comparing external resourcing options, it becomes easier to judge if you ask each company about three things at the same level of granularity: workload, the ratio of on-site visits to remote work, and the experience level of the engineers assigned.

The third path—starting externally and connecting to hiring—is less likely to fail when demand is still unclear

For this question, which appears to be a binary choice, there is an approach that solves it through sequencing.

  • First, start with one operation using external FDE-style support (to confirm effectiveness and fit)
  • Through the accompaniment process, transfer skills to in-house staff and build the foundation for insourcing
  • Once development demand is stable and visible, switch to hiring or promoting internal talent

The advantage of this sequence is not only that it reduces the risk of hiring failure while keeping knowledge in-house. Having run one operation with external support makes the requirements in your job posting concrete: "someone who can take over this part of our operations." You enter hiring able to translate the ideal candidate profiles in overseas AI companies' job postings into your own company's terms. The option of building capability through internal development rather than hiring is covered in steps for developing AI talent. External resourcing is not a substitute for hiring—it also functions as a stage before hiring.

Frequently asked questions about FDE hiring and external resourcing

Q. Is there an option of working with a side-job or freelance FDE?

Yes. However, since an FDE's work centers on embedding in the field and building ongoing relationships, value is hard to realize if the workload becomes too fragmented. First determine whether the scope of work can function with one day per week of involvement, and set the workload using the quasi-mandate form described in the contract forms section. For those looking for FDE work as a side job, How to become an FDE? Required skills and career paths will be more useful than this article.

Q. If we hire, what should we look for in interviews?

Rather than an impressive career history, look for two things: experience completing a project alone from start to finish, and experience engaging directly with customers and the field. As seen in the job posting section, overseas AI companies' postings also place these two in their duties and required experience. In interviews, ask candidates to actually show what they have built, and ask concretely how they overcame points where they got stuck. Someone who can describe what they built and the process in their own words has aptitude, even if their title is not FDE.

Q. If we outsource, won't the knowledge end up outside the company?

That is what happens if you leave everything to them. To prevent it, decide two things at the contract stage: designate one internal owner to attend the accompaniment sessions, and include a step in the stabilization phase to hand operations over in-house. Placing "how knowledge is retained" as the weakness of external utilization in the five-axis comparison means it drains away without deliberate effort, not that it cannot be retained.

Q. Can we proceed with hiring and external utilization at the same time?

Yes, you can. In fact, the third path takes exactly that form. Once you can see the actual state of development demand through external accompaniment, the requirements for hiring and the work to assign after onboarding become concrete. When a hire is decided, reduce the external engagement and overlap only for a handover period; this keeps the period of doubled fixed and variable costs short.

SAI's perspective: why we, an external provider, support you all the way to hiring

The first thing we look at in a "hire or outsource" consultation is whether development demand will continue uninterrupted over the next year. If that is unclear, we believe it is still too early to decide on hiring.

SAI Inc. is, so to speak, a company on the external utilization side, providing FDE accompaniment development. Even so, we welcome clients ultimately building their own FDE function in-house. During accompaniment we transfer skills and leave behind material for decisions on internalization and hiring—because our approach is to design support all the way to its exit. When introductions of talent are needed, we handle them through licensed partner companies, since SAI itself does not hold a paid employment placement license (a business that introduces talent to companies for a fee, which requires government approval). The foundation remains accompaniment by SAI's own engineers, and introductions are positioned as one of the exits from that support.
