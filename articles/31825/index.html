Contracts and Pricing: Setting Boundaries for Both Delivery and Exploration

Original title: 合同与价格：为确定交付，也为探索留边界

pafa.github.io · 2023-06-20 · 7 min read

Summary

This chapter argues that FDE contracts must separate what is already known from what still needs exploration. Fixed-price deals covering uncertain outcomes invite disputes, so pilots should have defined deliverables like reproducible evals and documented limitations. Pricing models—time-based, fixed-scope, subscription plus implementation, outcome-based—each shift risk differently; Sierra's outcome-based pricing for AI agents acknowledges some interactions fit per-session billing. Accenture reported roughly 60% of FY2025 work was fixed-price, with outcome-based arrangements still a small share. Payment milestones should verify deliverables, not activity, and change handling, IP ownership, and exit terms must be explicit upfront.

Key points

  • Exploration and committed delivery should be contracted as separate phases with clear evidence.
  • Each pricing model shifts uncertainty differently; outcome-based pay requires measurable results and supplier influence.
  • Sierra's December 2024 pricing note endorses hybrid per-session billing for some AI agent interactions.
  • Accenture: about 60% of FY2025 work was fixed-price; outcome-based deals remain a small share.
  • Contracts should specify payment verification, change rules, IP licensing, and exit deliverables.

A problem not yet fully understood can hardly be captured in a single quote. The client wants to control budget; the vendor needs to bear the cost of exploration. If both sides merely hide the uncertainty behind a phrase like "complete as required," every act of learning after signing can turn into a dispute.

An FDE contract needs to accommodate two things at once: what is already known to be deliverable, and what work is still needed to clarify the unknown. The former cannot be endlessly delayed in the name of exploration; the latter cannot be packaged as a settled production commitment.

First agree on which segment of work the money buys

Continue with the maintenance company's teaching pilot. The client wants to reduce the time supervisors spend searching for materials, and may later open up automatic dispatch. At this stage, whether repair reports are complete, whether suggestions actually help supervisors, and whether actions can be safely rolled back all still need verification.

If they immediately sign a fixed-price contract covering "company-wide intelligent maintenance," the two sides would not even share a common deliverable. A more defensible arrangement is to first approve a trial of material organization and manual confirmation for one equipment type in one area; the exploration phase must produce an approved data sample description, reproducible evals, known limitations, the scope of use, and options for the next phase. Automatic dispatch, as an unproven option, does not enter the current launch commitment.

This way, even if the outcome is to hold off on automatic actions, the exploration may still have completed the agreed work. Conversely, delivering only a demo without clarifying the materials and failure conditions cannot count as full performance just because the team "did some exploring." This is this book's teaching arrangement, not any company's actual contract text.

Separating phases does not mean guaranteeing in advance that the next phase will be purchased. The client should be able to continue, shrink, or end the work based on evidence; the vendor also needs to know how this segment of work will be paid, and cannot rely on an unsigned follow-on contract to recover the entire investment. Joint research that requires upfront costs can be handled by each side bearing its own share, by separate fees, or through other commercially acceptable arrangements—the key is to state it explicitly.

Pricing should correspond to who bears which uncertainty

This book does not offer a quoting formula that fits every company. But we can compare how several options change behavior.

Time-based billing makes effort easy to see, but clients may worry that the slower the problem is solved, the bigger the bill. Fixed-scope pricing brings budget certainty, but requires both sides to manage scope changes carefully. Software subscription plus implementation fees separates ongoing product capability from one-time integration, but requires explaining which support is included and which is billed separately. Paying for outcomes looks closest to value, provided the outcomes are measurable and the vendor has sufficient influence over the key variables.

If the project's goal is to reduce a certain business loss, and pricing, client policies, market conditions, and internal execution can all change that loss, settling purely on outcomes invites prolonged disputes. The issue is not whether this model is inherently good or bad, but whether the measurement scope, ability to influence, and risk allocation match.

In its December 2024 pricing announcement, Sierra listed customer service issue resolution and cancellation retention as outcomes that can be agreed upon; the same article also acknowledged that interactions like greetings and routing may be better suited to a hybrid model billed per conversation. 1 This is more explanatory than a blanket "from now on we charge by results": some tasks have endpoints that can be clearly measured, while others are only responsible for handing work to the next step.

Put into a teaching contract, the two sides would need to jointly judge at least one bill: if the bot says "resolved" and the customer follows up the same day, is that one case or two? If AI collects materials first and a human handles the rest, how is each paid? Does a successful retention count only after the refund window closes? These agreements determine who bears the cost of incomplete cases, repeated contacts, and later reversals. It is also necessary to distinguish acceptance criteria from pricing methods: a fixed-price project can still stipulate payment only upon meeting standards; when settling by the number of confirmed outcomes, the unit price per outcome can be fixed—what varies is the total cost.

In its Q1 FY2026 earnings presentation, Accenture happened to separate the two: the company said that in FY2025 about 60% of its work was fixed-price, while commercial arrangements based on actually realized outcomes still accounted for only a small share. 2 So seeing a service provider increase fixed-price contracts does not mean it has moved to charging at scale for business outcomes. The two sides may merely have reallocated the gains and risks of overruns, rework, and tool-driven efficiency; the client's business results need not become the unit of settlement.

A more pragmatic approach is to first break the uncertainty apart. The exploration phase gets a limited learning budget, with scope expanded only after clear evidence is reached; features that are already stable get more definite delivery and maintenance agreements. This prevents an uncomfortable situation: the vendor commits to outcomes it cannot control, while the client assumes all subsequent changes were already included in the price.

Payment milestones should recognize delivery, not just activity

The UK DDaT Playbook's pricing guidance links certainty to payment arrangements: fixed price can be used when scope is fairly certain, and variable approaches considered when uncertainty is higher; it also cautions that payment milestones for agile projects should not be set merely by the number of development rounds completed. 3 This is a procurement design principle, not proof that any particular contract is sound.

Returning to the maintenance pilot, "the team has worked for several weeks" and "supervisors can find verifiable evidence within the agreed scope" are not the same kind of delivery. The former can confirm effort; the latter requires samples, working procedures, and exception-handling records. Even with time-based billing, these phase artifacts can be required; with fixed-price billing, an acceptance owner and a dispute-resolution method are still needed.

The operating period should also clarify what is being billed. Call volume, number of users, completed items, human support, and emergency response are different units of cost or value. It is unacceptable to show only a cheap per-call price during the sale and then treat necessary human support as an unexpected add-on after signing. Only by agreeing on covered service hours, response scope, and how usage changes are handled can budget and operations connect.

Payment timing also changes both sides' cash-flow pressure. If acceptance materials sit unconfirmed and final payments are delayed, the total contract amount may be unchanged, but the vendor's ability to keep delivering is affected. For how amount, cost, and timing of receipt are separated, see the standalone teaching ledger in "Project Economics: Getting Both Sides' Numbers Straight"; that 800,000-yuan figure is not a real quote for this chapter's maintenance pilot.

How a change enters the contract, rather than entering overtime

When a maintenance supervisor proposes adding an equipment category, first confirm which inputs, permissions, tests, workload, and operational responsibilities it changes. The FDE provides the technical impact, the client's business owner explains the value, and those with authority to adjust commitments then choose to accept, shrink, or defer. The original agreement does not automatically expand just because the new requirement was discussed.

If the obstacle is data the client has not yet provided, the time risk cannot simply be dumped on the vendor; if a capability the vendor promised is not yet usable, it cannot be renamed as an additional client request. Stating the provider, the evidence of completion, and the handling when unmet for each precondition helps both sides see what actually happened.

Not every anomaly requires changing the contract. Defect fixes within scope, support already included, and normal engineering adjustments should be handled under the original arrangement. Only matters that genuinely change functional scope, risk allocation, scale of use, or service period call for a corresponding commercial decision. Otherwise small issues drag into commercial negotiations while big ones get buried in instant messages.

Before co-development, clarify how the results will be used

The client's raw materials, adaptation code written for the client, the vendor's public platform, and third-party components cannot be merged into a single sentence like "all results belong to the client" or "all belong to the vendor." Ownership, usage licenses, maintenance responsibilities, and the permissions needed for migration need not all be held by the same party.

For example, the client may only need the right to use and maintain the adaptation code, without buying the platform's source code; the vendor may be willing to maintain public features but cannot grant third-party rights it does not itself own. Specific terms should be confirmed by each side's commercial and rights personnel, object by object. The field engineer can explain technical dependencies, but cannot conjure a missing license with a phrase like "we can move it out later."

This also bears on whether co-developed results can serve other clients. Reusable engineering methods must be separated from the client's protected materials; whether use is permitted and how client-specific information is removed should follow actual authorization—one cannot presume external distribution is allowed just because something was written as a generic component.

What must be delivered at the end should also be estimated at the start: available data and state, necessary documentation, handover support, and arrangements for retaining and revoking access. A detailed rights inventory and migration rehearsal are left to Boundaries, Risks, and Exit. The groundwork this chapter must do is to ensure these responsibilities have owners and funding, rather than discovering at the end of the relationship that the two sides understood things differently.

An agreement suited to co-building should give both parties the same understanding of what comes next: committed work cannot be quietly scaled back, and unproven futures are not sold in advance. Price sets the resource boundary, stage evidence decides whether to continue, and change rules give learning somewhere to land.
