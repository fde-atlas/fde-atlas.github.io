Discovery workshop: how to run one as an FDE

FDE Times · 2026-10-22 · 7 min read

Summary

This article argues that discovery workshops decide most deployment outcomes, and walks through a complete example with a fictional logistics company's invoice complaints process. It covers pre-workshop preparation, mapping a process from both endpoints, scoring candidate tasks on value and effort, and validating assumptions afterward. The goal is a shared problem statement and success metric, not a wish list, with only one task selected to start.

Key points

  • Fix the scope, start and end points, and one guiding question before the workshop.
  • Keep attendees under ten and include the people who actually do the work.
  • Score tasks on frequency, repetition, reach and effort, then pick exactly one.
  • Ask about data constraints before making any promises in the room.
  • Everything from the workshop is a hypothesis; rank and test assumptions with real client data.

Nine o’clock in the morning at a client’s office. There are twelve people in the room. The head of operations opens: “We want AI for the entire complaints department.” The head of IT sits at the end of the table, arms folded. The three staff who answer complaints every day have not yet been asked a single question. You have ninety minutes.

Scenes like this are routine for a forward deployed engineer, and how you run that session decides most of what happens next. Scaler’s FDE playbook puts it bluntly: discovery is where most deployments are won or lost.

The goal is a problem statement and a definition of success that both sides agree on, not a wish list.

What follows is a worked example from start to finish, built around a made-up logistics company and its process for handling invoice complaints.

Before the meeting: one question, two markers, fewer than ten people

Koji’s guide to running discovery workshops advises writing down, before anything else, the single question the session must answer. For the logistics company, that question should not be “How can AI help customer service?” A better one: “In the invoice complaints process, which step should be automated first, and how will we measure success?”

Triaster, a company that specializes in process mapping, recommends fixing the scope before the meeting and then opening with the start and end points of that scope. In this example, the process starts when a complaint email lands in the shared inbox. It ends when a credit note is issued, or when the complaint is rejected with a reason.

The attendee list matters just as much. Triaster treats subject-matter experts, the people who actually do the work, as mandatory. It keeps the group under ten, wants someone who understands how the process connects to other departments, and needs the backing of the process owner.

Scaler makes the same point: do not talk only to leadership; sit with the people who will actually use the system. So with those twelve people, the first job is to cut the list.

Keep the process owner, the three complaints handlers, the person in accounting who issues credit notes, the head of IT and the warehouse manager. The head of operations may only need to join for the last fifteen minutes, when priorities are settled.

Map from both ends toward the middle

Once the two markers are on the board, ask a handler to walk through a real complaint they dealt with last week.

Each step goes on a sticky note: receive the email, categorize it, look up the invoice in the ERP, check it against proof of delivery, request approval, issue the credit note.

Under each note, record four things: who does it, on which system, with what data, and where the case tends to wait.

Some questions will have no answer from anyone in the room, such as which system holds the proof of delivery. Do not stop to dig until you find out. PostHog’s FDE handbook advises using the client’s real work to surface real problems, rather than trying to model everything up front.

Mark the note with a “?”, assign someone to check, and move on.

Ten sensible tasks, pick one

By the end of the mapping, the board usually holds a dozen ideas that all sound reasonable. PostHog describes the core judgment of an FDE as lying right here: knowing which of ten sensible tasks to do first, and which not to do at all.

OpenAI Academy’s prioritization matrix asks two questions of each workflow. If it works, how much value does it deliver? How much effort and complexity will it take to implement?

To estimate value, it points to three strong signals: frequency, repetition and reach. Here is an illustrative scoring for the logistics example, on a 1-5 scale:

Candidate workflow Frequency Repetition Reach Effort Decision
Categorizing complaint emails 5 4 3 2 Do first
Matching invoices to proof of delivery 4 5 3 4 Do later, once the data is clear
Monthly complaints report for the CFO 1 4 1 2 Don’t do
Predicting which customers will complain 2 2 2 5 Don’t do

The table is no formula. Its value lies in forcing the room to say out loud why one task was chosen over another. The CFO report is easy to build, but it runs once a month and has a single reader. Complaint prediction sounds the most exciting, yet it takes the most effort and its value is unclear.

At that very moment someone will ask: “While we’re at it, could we do the prediction piece too?” PostHog’s advice is to push back on every “should we also…”. Write the idea in a “later” column on the board, so the person who raised it can see it has not been thrown away. But do not let it slip into today’s scope.

The questions you cannot skip

The first is for the head of IT, whose arms are still folded. Scaler stresses that if the client’s data is not allowed to leave the internal network, you must know before you design anything. Ask directly: can complaint emails and ERP data be sent to an external model, and who signs off on that?

The second is one PostHog includes in its scoping checklist: does the problem described point to a deeper pattern? Suppose that, while walking through cases, a handler mentions that most complaints come from the same warehouse.

The root cause might be faulty proof-of-delivery scanning at that warehouse, with an email-reading agent merely cleaning up the consequences. Because the warehouse manager is in the room, you can ask on the spot whether that hypothesis is worth checking.

The third goes back to the goal Scaler sets: what does success look like? Do not leave the room without a sentence both sides nod to, such as “the time from receiving an email to the complaint landing in the right queue falls from X to Y,” with X measured on real data over the following week.

After the meeting: everything on the board is still a hypothesis

Koji is clear that a workshop does not produce evidence. The sticky notes, the scores, even the line “most complaints come from one warehouse” are all hypotheses.

Koji also advises ranking assumptions by impact and uncertainty. In the logistics example, “complaint emails are clear enough to categorize automatically” has high impact, because the first task rests entirely on it, and nobody has measured it. “Most complaints come from one warehouse” is also high impact and also uncertain.

So the first job after the meeting is to test those two assumptions: take a sample of real complaints, say a week of emails, run the categorization step on it and count complaints by warehouse. That is the spirit PostHog recommends, using real work to surface problems.

Send a summary within a day, covering the central question, the process map, the scoring table, the “later” column, the answer on data and the definition of success.

A document like that is also something you can take into an interview once the client’s name is removed. On a CV, the line “led a discovery workshop, narrowed scope from ten ideas to one and measured results against an agreed metric” says more than “strong communication skills.”

The mistakes that turn a workshop into a wasted meeting

One mistake is mapping the whole company because no start and end points were fixed, so ninety minutes pass without reaching a single step worth automating.

Another is making promises in the room before asking about data constraints. Such a promise can collapse after one email from the security team. The last is treating whatever the room agreed on as verified fact and building on it straight away.

A good workshop does not end in applause. It ends when you have one task to start on Monday morning, one number to measure, and a list of things you have tactfully told “not yet.”
