How to Write RAID Logs and Decision Logs: A Practical Guide for FDEs

Original title: RAID 日志与决策日志怎么写：FDE 实操指南

FDE Times · 2026-10-09 · 7 min read

Summary

A hands-on guide for FDEs on building RAID logs and decision logs in client projects, so that when an AI agent causes an incident the team can answer who approved what. Using a hypothetical insurance-company RAG chatbot as a running example, it walks through a six-step process with Markdown templates, citing OWASP LLM Top 10 2025 and NIST AI RMF as justifications. Key themes: separate owner and approver columns, write assumptions before risks, and record only high-impact decisions with named client approvers.

Key points

  • RAID logs track risks, assumptions, issues, and dependencies; owner and approver columns must stay separate.
  • Write assumptions first, each with a verification method and deadline, then derive risks from unverified ones.
  • Use OWASP LLM Top 10 2025 as a checklist, rewriting each item as a concrete scenario on the client's system.
  • Record major decisions as ADR files with named participants; the approver must be a specific client-side person.
  • Keep logs alive by reviewing them in weekly meetings; avoid copying OWASP verbatim or hiding logs on personal machines.

Key takeaways

  • A RAID log tracks four types of entries: risks, assumptions, issues, and dependencies. On AI projects, write assumptions first, then risks.
  • Use the OWASP LLM Top 10 2025 as a checklist for mapping AI risks, but rewrite every item as a concrete scenario on the customer's system.
  • An ADR-format decision log records only major decisions, with participants named. The approver must be a specific person on the customer's side.

OWASP added "Excessive Agency" to its 2025 LLM risk list, numbered LLM06.

On customer projects, this risk rarely surfaces in code review. It usually appears in meetings: someone nods along to letting an agent write to the customer's system, but no one records that nod.

That's why a Forward Deployed Engineer (FDE) needs two documents that sound boring but can save the project at critical moments: a RAID log and a decision log. When an incident happens, they answer three questions: what was anticipated in advance, who accepted the risk, and why.

Both documents can live directly in the project repo, provided you add one column, kept separate from the owner: the approver, i.e., the person with the authority to accept the risk.

What to do and what to prepare

The final deliverable is a

raid-log.md

file and a

decisions/

directory, with one file per decision. Markdown is a sensible choice: it can be reviewed via pull requests, has change history, and customers can view it without installing any software.

If the customer insists on their own project management tool, just keep the set of columns below.

The running example throughout is a hypothetical project: building an internal RAG chatbot for an insurance company's customer service department, plus a proposal to let an agent automatically send email replies. All roles and dates in the example are fictional.

You need three things: a repo that both the team and the customer contact can read, a list of people on the customer side with decision-making authority, and half an hour sitting down with the tech lead.

Step 1: Set up the four-part framework, with two columns that must not be merged

According to Process Street's definition, a RAID log is a central repository for tracking risks, assumptions, issues, and dependencies throughout the project lifecycle.

Simply put: a risk is an event that could delay the project or prevent it from meeting its goals; an assumption is something treated as fact but not yet verified; a dependency is work that can't proceed until other work or external factors are complete; and an issue is something that has already happened.

Keeping all four parts in one file with a shared set of columns makes later filtering and cross-referencing easy.

# RAID 日志 — 客服聊天机器人（示例）

| ID | 类型 | 描述 | 影响 | 负责人 | 审批人 | 状态 | 更新日期 | 相关决策 |
|----|------|------|------|--------|--------|------|----------|----------|

The "Type" column takes only one of four values: R, A, I, or D. The owner is the person who must act on the entry; the approver is the person with the authority to accept the risk or close the entry. The two columns are deliberately separate: the FDE is often the owner but rarely has the authority to accept risk on the customer's behalf.

The approver column also aligns with the NIST AI RMF. According to that framework's Playbook, GOVERN 2 requires establishing accountability structures so that the appropriate teams and individuals are empowered and responsible for AI risk management. GOVERN 2.1 requires roles and responsibilities to be documented.

NIST released the framework on January 26, 2023, and adoption is entirely voluntary. Even so, when the customer asks why the log needs this column, citing GOVERN is far more persuasive than "the team felt it was needed."

Check: Ask yourself: if the FDE quit tomorrow, would someone reading the log know who is responsible for each row.

Step 2: Write assumptions first, then risks

Imagine the project kickoff meeting, where the customer asserts that their business documents are in good shape and can be indexed directly. That's not yet a fact—it's an assumption. In a RAG project, this is the first assumption worth verifying, because the chatbot's answer quality depends on the documents it reads.

Writing assumptions before risks may seem backwards, but many risks grow precisely out of assumptions no one has verified. Writing the assumptions down first makes clear what you're standing on.

| A-01 | A | 业务文档足够干净、足够新，可作为 RAG 数据源 | 若不成立：聊天机器人按过时流程作答 | FDE | 客服负责人 | 未验证 — 截止 2026-10-16 | 2026-10-09 | — |

Every assumption needs a verification method and a deadline. For example, sample 20 documents and mark outdated sections together with the customer service lead. If verification shows the assumption doesn't hold, don't delete the row—instead create a new R or I row that references back to A-01.

Check: Every A row must either say "unverified" with a deadline, or "verified" with a date. Never just "OK."

Step 3: Use OWASP as a checklist for mapping AI risks

When you sit down with the tech lead to list AI risks, the hardest part is facing a blank page: everyone knows the model will make mistakes, but no one can say where. That's when you can open the OWASP LLM Top 10 2025 list as a prompt. For the example project, three items are worth considering: LLM01 Prompt Injection, LLM02 Sensitive Information Disclosure, and LLM06 Excessive Agency.

| R-01 | R | 权限不足的员工向聊天机器人提问，拿到了投保人的个人信息（OWASP LLM02:2025） | 数据泄露；项目可能被叫停 | FDE | 客户安全代表 | 未关闭 | 2026-10-09 | DEC-002 |
| R-02 | R | Agent 向真实客户发送了内容错误的邮件（OWASP LLM06:2025） | 客户投诉，声誉受损 | FDE | 客服总监 | 未关闭 | 2026-10-09 | DEC-001 |
| R-03 | R | 源文档或提问中的内容扭曲了模型行为（OWASP LLM01:2025） | 回答错误，限制被绕过 | FDE | 客户技术负责人 | 未关闭 | 2026-10-09 | — |

Write every risk as a concrete scenario on the customer's system—don't copy OWASP's item names verbatim. A row that just says "Prompt Injection" tells no one what to do; a row that spells out who did what and what the consequence was will get claimed.

Check: The approver for every AI risk must be someone on the customer's side, because only they have the authority to accept risks involving their own data and customers.

Step 4: Dependencies and issues, the two sections most often left empty

On-site with a customer, what stalls you first is usually not the model but the account that never gets provisioned. Access to the customer's systems is a classic dependency. Use the

DEP-

prefix to avoid confusion with decision numbers.

| DEP-01 | D | 需要客服文档库的读取权限以建立索引 | 没有权限就无法开始评测 | FDE | 客户 IT 负责人 | 等待中 | 2026-10-09 | — |

Keep the line between R and I simple: things that might happen go in R; things that have already happened move to I. If DEP-01 drags on for two weeks, open a new I row referencing DEP-01 rather than just changing the date.

Step 5: One ADR-format file per major decision

Engineers already know a suitable format. An Architecture Decision Record (ADR) documents one architectural decision and its rationale. According to adr.github.io, the collection of all ADRs in a project is that project's decision log.

The decision files in the example use the QĐ prefix, from the Vietnamese quyết định ("decision").

# DEC-001：Agent 只起草邮件，从不发送

日期：2026-10-09（示例）
状态：已批准
参与人：FDE、技术负责人、客服总监、安全代表
审批人：客服总监
相关风险：R-02

## 背景
有人提议让 Agent 自动回复客户邮件。

## 决策
Agent 只生成草稿；由客服人员点击发送。

## 被否决的方案
对“简单问题”类邮件自动发送。

## 影响
响应变慢；有了评测数据后重新评估。

QĐ-002 follows the same template, for example: the chatbot only retrieves documents the asker is authorized to view, and policyholders' personal profiles are not indexed. The approver is the customer's security representative, and the related risk is R-01.

Not everything needs a file. Fellow recommends keeping a detailed decision log only for high-impact decisions, such as hiring, budget, or major matters, rather than recording every small discussion. On AI projects, you can set the bar like this: any decision that changes what data the model can see or what actions the agent can perform must have a file.

Recording participants' names isn't a formality. Fellow notes that the list comes in handy when someone later denies having taken part in the discussion.

Check: Every risk with status "open" must point to a decision or mitigation plan. The approver for every decision must be a specific person, not "the whole team."

Step 6: Keep the logs up to date

Process Street recommends updating RAID logs regularly but doesn't specify a frequency. The most durable approach is to attach it to an existing rhythm, such as the last ten minutes of a weekly client meeting, to review open items and assumptions approaching their deadlines. A log that is never mentioned in meetings becomes a dead artifact within a month.

Mistakes that render the log useless

The first mistake is filling both the owner and approver columns with the FDE's name. This effectively takes on risks that belong to the client. The second mistake is copying the entire OWASP checklist into the log; it looks comprehensive, but nobody knows what action to take.

Two other mistakes go in opposite directions. Some teams record every conversation as a decision, and eventually the real decisions get buried in the notes. Others keep the log on a personal computer where the client never sees it. When an incident happens, no one can claim the client accepted a risk they never read.

Where this skill fits in FDE work

On-site with a client, set up the RAID log in the first week and walk through it with the client's project sponsor. Reading out assumptions one by one in front of someone with decision-making authority surfaces misunderstandings between the two sides early. By the time the client proposes giving the agent more permissions, you already have a place to record who agreed and why.

When reading job descriptions, if terms like stakeholder management, risk, or governance appear alongside deployment, prepare a sample log for the interview. Don't just write "risk management" on your resume.

Be specific, for example: built a RAID log and decision log for a RAG deployment, mapping each LLM risk to an approver on the client side. For the interview, prepare a story about a decision you logged and what outcome it produced.

Models get replaced and prompts get rewritten. When someone asks why the agent was allowed to do that, what really matters is that one line: "Approver: Head of Customer Support."
