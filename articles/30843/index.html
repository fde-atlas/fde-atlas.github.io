In short: companies have spent heavily on AI, but most of it never reaches daily work. MIT's NANDA research found only about 5% of enterprise generative-AI pilots achieved rapid revenue acceleration, and Gartner expects over 40% of agentic AI projects to be cancelled by the end of 2027. The models are not the main problem. Fitting them into a real company's data, systems, rules and habits is. The person hired to do that is the Forward Deployed Engineer (FDE), and demand is rising fast: OpenAI launched a $4 billion company built around FDEs in May 2026, and FDE postings on Indeed India were nearly 19 times higher in June 2026 than a year earlier, from a small base. If you can build software and work with a client, this is the gap worth training for.

Most AI projects inside companies follow the same story. The demo goes beautifully. A model answers questions about the company's policies, drafts emails, summarises a contract. Leadership is impressed, a pilot is approved, a vendor is signed. Six months later the tool is used by eleven people, nobody trusts its answers, and the budget review asks what happened.

Nothing was wrong with the model. What was missing was the work between the model and the business: understanding how this company actually runs, connecting the AI to its real data and systems, deciding what it must never do, proving it works, and staying until people use it. That work now has a job title, and companies are hiring for it. I teach this role, so I have a stake in it. That is exactly why everything below is a published number with a link, and why there is a section on who this is not for.

1. The problem nobody budgeted for: the deployment gap

In August 2025, MIT's NANDA initiative published The GenAI Divide: State of AI in Business 2025. Based on 150 interviews with leaders, a survey of 350 employees and an analysis of 300 public AI deployments, it found that about 5% of AI pilot programmes achieve rapid revenue acceleration, while the vast majority stall with little or no measurable impact on profit and loss (Fortune).

The interesting part is the reason. The lead author, Aditya Challapally, told Fortune the core issue is "not the quality of the AI models" but a "learning gap" for tools and organisations. Generic tools work well for individuals and stall inside companies, because they don't learn from or adapt to the company's workflows. The report points to flawed enterprise integration, not regulation or model performance.

Gartner sees the same pattern with AI agents. In June 2025 it predicted that over 40% of agentic AI projects will be cancelled by the end of 2027, because of escalating costs, unclear business value or inadequate risk controls. Its analyst Anushree Verma said most projects are early experiments that can blind organisations "to the real cost and complexity of deploying AI agents at scale, stalling projects from moving into production" (Gartner).

Salesforce put it in one phrase when it explained why it hires FDEs: "Without FDEs, we risk having thousands of customers stuck in pilot purgatory — signed up, but not successfully deployed" (Salesforce). Pilot purgatory is where most corporate AI lives today. Getting it out is a job.

2. The fix turned out to be a person

The Forward Deployed Engineer comes from Palantir, which needed engineers who would sit with a single customer and build what that customer needed. Palantir describes its product engineers as building "one capability, many customers", and its forward deployed engineers as delivering "one customer, many capabilities" (Palantir). For years it was a niche role. Generative AI made it mainstream, because every company buying AI now has the problem Palantir's customers had.

The clearest signal came on 11 May 2026. OpenAI launched the OpenAI Deployment Company with more than $4 billion of initial investment, built to "embed engineers specialized in frontier AI deployment, known as Forward Deployed Engineers, or FDEs, into organizations". It agreed to buy Tomoro, an applied AI consulting firm, to bring about 150 experienced FDEs and deployment specialists on day one (OpenAI). OpenAI's own explanation is the deployment gap in one line: "building powerful AI models is only part of the work."

OpenAI also describes what the work looks like. An engagement starts with "a focused diagnostic of where AI can create the most value", then a few priority workflows chosen with the customer, and then FDEs "work inside the organization to design, build, test, and deploy production systems, connecting OpenAI models to the customer's data, tools, controls, and business processes". Its FDE job description says the person will "own discovery, technical scoping, system design, build, and production rollout" (OpenAI careers).

Put simply, an FDE is a software engineer who can also run the client meeting. Half the job is engineering: APIs, data, retrieval, agents, cloud, testing. The other half is the part most engineers are never taught: finding the real problem, agreeing what good looks like, explaining trade-offs to people who are not technical, and staying until the system is used.

4. Why this is the right move now

Plenty of job titles get hyped and fade. Four reasons this one looks different:

  • 1It sits on the bottleneck, not the model. Better models do not remove the deployment work; they let companies attempt bigger, riskier workflows, which need more of it. OpenAI says as much: "as models become more capable, businesses can apply AI to larger, more important parts of how they operate."
  • 2It rewards skills many Indian engineers already have. Client calls, messy integrations, production support and working inside someone else's systems are daily life in IT services. On our own India jobs board, the most common requirement is client-facing work: 65 of 137 roles on 17 Oct 2026, ahead of AI agents (55) and AWS (39).
  • 3The title is still early. Most of the growth in the tables above happened within the last year, so few people yet have a deployed AI project and client delivery experience to show. Early in a role's life, proof of work counts for more than years on the CV.
  • 4It is hard to automate away. The job is judgement inside one particular company: which problem matters, what the AI must never do, when to stop. In my view that is the part of software work least exposed to AI coding tools.

On pay, be careful with headlines. TeamLease Digital told Moneycontrol that entry-level FDEs earn between ₹18 lakh and ₹25 lakh a year and mid-level professionals ₹30 to 45 lakh (Moneycontrol). Those are one staffing firm's figures for people who already hold the title. They are a sign of how scarce the skill is, not a promise about what any course, including ours, leads to.

5. Who this is not for

A longer weighing of pay, day-to-day reality and the downsides is in Is Forward Deployed Engineer a good career?. The short version:

  • Anyone who wants to avoid code. An FDE ships production software every week. If you dislike programming, look at analyst or product roles instead.
  • Anyone who wants to avoid people. You will run meetings, take criticism of your work from non-engineers, and explain delays. If that sounds draining, a pure engineering role will suit you better.
  • Anyone expecting an instant senior title. Most postings want experience. On our board the median stated minimum is 4 years, and ANSR puts the median GCC hire at six years. Freshers can get in, mostly through related titles such as Applied AI Engineer, but it takes real projects to show.
  • Anyone buying a certificate. There is no official, industry-wide FDE certification. What gets you shortlisted is a deployed project you can demo and explain.
