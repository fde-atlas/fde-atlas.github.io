Case Review: Building Customer Success from 0 to 1 with the V1 Approach

Original title: 案例复盘：客户成功职能从 0 到 1 的 V1 搭建法

FDE中国 · 2026-09-04 · 6 min read

Summary

This piece reviews a First Round Review interview with Stephanie Berner, Atlassian's SVP of Customer Success, who has built customer success (CS) functions from scratch at LinkedIn, Box, and Medallia. Her V1 playbook: hire the first CSM based on whether customers need industry knowledge or technical help, have CS report to the CEO, template support tickets and onboarding first, and start with only two metrics—customer health and renewal rate. Concrete benchmarks include one CSM per 5-10 strategic accounts (contracts over $500k) and 20-25 enterprise accounts. The editors caution that the model assumes subscription SaaS; in China's project-based toB market, renewal rate should be reframed as repurchase or upsell, and early hires risk becoming single points of tribal knowledge.

Key points

  • First hire depends on whether customers need industry expertise or technical integration help.
  • CSM ratios: 1 per 5-10 strategic accounts, 20-25 enterprise, 40-50 mid-market.
  • Start with two metrics only: a purchase-deployed-usage health equation and renewal rate.
  • Onboarding V1 is a shared spreadsheet plus a templated ten-step process.
  • Compensation should be base plus bonus tied to KPIs, not pure renewal quotas.

I. Background

The case comes from First Round Review's interview with Stephanie Berner, Senior Vice President of Customer Success at Atlassian. She previously led the customer success team for LinkedIn's Sales Solutions business, served as Senior Director of Customer Success Management at Box, and gained early-stage startup experience at Medallia, repeatedly building customer success (CS) functions from scratch. Her observation: in the early days, CS is almost always managed by the founder or a co-founder; when the customer base grows and the founder spends more than half their time "resolving customer experience issues," it's time to formalize CS operations—this is exercising a different muscle: building an organization.

II. The Problem

The first batch of customers is often served by the founder personally firefighting: the first one or two hires act like firefighters, extinguishing customer issues one after another as they emerge. Once the customer count rises, this model immediately breaks down—there's no systematic channel for collecting customer feedback, the multi-step onboarding process isn't documented, and the company's understanding of "what it takes for customers to realize value" remains weak; meanwhile, the industry has no precise timetable for "when to build a dedicated CS team," so founders need to catch up simultaneously on hiring profiles, interviews and compensation, reporting lines, team size, starting systems, and metrics. If any layer is missing, CS cannot serve as a growth lever or a cross-functional connector.

III. The Solution

Berner offers a V1 playbook you can follow directly (per the interview). The profile of the first hire depends on "what problem you want to solve for customers": if going from purchase to implementation requires deep understanding of industry knowledge like retail workflows or accounting functions, hire for industry expertise; if reaching value involves complex integrations, data migrations, and other backend operations customers shouldn't do themselves, hire for technical skills. Organizationally, CS should report directly to the CEO; for systems, template two things first—support tickets and the customer onboarding process; for metrics, start with just two: customer health and renewal rate.

IV. Implementation

The first hire. Berner prefers to hire an individual contributor (IC) first; the ideal candidate is someone who has managed people or aspires to manage. If a major hiring push is clearly coming within a few months, you can hire a manager first to take over IC recruiting, at the cost of a longer lag in tactical output. Four repeatedly validated traits: a sense of speed—fast-moving, with strong curiosity about both technology and customers; communication skills as the feedback loop to product and engineering; an action orientation—solving problems when found, with concrete examples to back it up; and natural customer empathy—but don't over-weight it, as candidates must be able to balance customer interests with business interests.

Interviews and compensation. The interview loop should include at least one founder, a product or engineering leader, and someone from the sales/marketing side. Must-ask questions include "Tell me about a time you spotted a problem and solved it" (action orientation), "The most impactful feedback you received in your last performance review" (self-awareness), "What would you change and keep if you took over the team" (systems-level thinking), and "Talk about your experience receiving great and terrible customer service" (empathy). Use base salary plus bonus rather than pure commission, with bonuses tied to specific KPIs like renewal rate, expansion, or adoption. She warns of the risk of pure renewal quotas—a team incentivized only on renewals has no one who cares whether customers actually realize value, and customers soon start churning.

Organization and scale. Reporting to the CEO takes priority, letting the executive closest to the customer experience see customer dynamics directly. Reporting to the CRO only makes sense "when renewals and growth are equally weighted and most revenue comes from existing customers." She doesn't recommend reporting to the CPO—CS is a go-to-market motion, and aligning with revenue teams is what creates the flywheel. CSM ratio reference values: strategic accounts (contracts typically over $500K) at 1 CSM per roughly 5-10 customers, enterprise at 20-25, mid-market at 40-50, SMB at 80-120, then adjust for technical complexity and number of use cases—you have to start somewhere and be willing to adjust constantly.

Systems, metrics, and churn analysis. Template two things first: support tickets (systematically collect and categorize customer feedback) and customer onboarding (V1 uses a shared spreadsheet plus a templated ten-step process, which must include a kickoff meeting and requirements gathering). Metrics start with two: customer health, measured by the three-part equation of "seats purchased, seats deployed, seats actually used"; and renewal rate, measuring value delivery, with ownership divided between CS and sales based on "whether detailed negotiation is involved." Leave NPS, CSAT, and the like for later—in the early days they're easy to game and yield no action items. When churn happens, record first, then analyze; ask churned customers three questions (why they chose a competitor, how they decided, what alternatives they considered). You'll typically get six to eight reasons; classify them as controllable or uncontrollable and focus on the controllable ones.

V. Results

This is a methodology interview; the original article doesn't disclose quantitative before-and-after comparisons from any company applying the method, so results are presented qualitatively (per the interview): the V1 playbook breaks "building a CS function" into an executable sequence of actions—first-hire profiles and must-ask questions, base-plus-bonus incentive design, a CEO reporting line, CSM ratio reference values, two starting systems, two starting metrics, and the three churn questions. Its effectiveness hinges on whether customer health and renewal rate can drive the team's daily actions. She has used and refined this method repeatedly across her multiple 0-to-1 CS experiences.

VI. Constraints

The method's boundaries of applicability: CSM ratios are starting reference values, not standard answers—they fluctuate with annual contract value, business stage, number of countries and languages, and the level of support customers need; the incentive structure depends on the binary question of "whether CS is accountable for closing renewals"—copying someone else's bonus design will drive the wrong behaviors; and there's no universal formula for choosing between an industry expert and a technical powerhouse as the first hire—it depends on the product and what customers do with it.

VII. Reusable Lessons

Relevance to FDE delivery: FDEs are responsible for getting systems delivered and live, while CS is responsible for customers continuously deriving value from them; the two share the same "customer-facing feedback loop."

  • Use the three-part equation to track customer health. Calculate a usage rate for each customer from three numbers—purchased, deployed, used—and after delivery, proactively open conversations with low-usage customers instead of waiting for complaints.
  • Template the onboarding process from day one. A kickoff meeting plus requirements gathering is the minimal skeleton; without a checklist mindset, the customer experience inevitably becomes inconsistent at scale—this maps to the FDE's handover and operations transfer stages.
  • Use the three churn questions as a retrospective tool. "Why they chose a competitor, how they decided, what alternatives they considered" applies equally to FDE project retrospectives and follow-ups on lost deals—classify causes as controllable or uncontrollable before acting.
  • Transfer the interview questions directly. The three questions on action orientation, receiving feedback, and systems-level thinking work just as well for FDE team interviews—field roles especially need people who can take feedback.

China context differences: the ratios and incentives in the article assume subscription-based SaaS, while domestic toB businesses often rely on project-based acceptance and annual service fee renewals, so "renewal rate" needs to be reframed as repurchase, expansion, or contract renewal before applying it.
