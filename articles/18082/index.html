How to conduct customer needs research as an FDE

Original title: FDE 客户需求调研怎么做

FDE Times · 2026-10-08 · 4 min read

Summary

Drawing on Rippling FDE Kevin B.'s experience, an Educative course, The Mom Test, and PostHog's FDE handbook, this article explains how FDEs should run customer discovery. Customer complaints are symptoms, not requirements; the real goal may lie in the underlying workflow. Users' workarounds are the clearest map of pain points, best observed onsite. Good questions target past concrete events rather than opinions about the future. The customer owns the problem definition, while the FDE owns the solution and may need to correct their assumptions. It also advises practicing discovery on ordinary tickets before landing the title.

Key points

  • Complaints are symptoms; a slow dashboard may really be a conversion-rate problem.
  • User workarounds, like manual Excel exports, are the clearest signal of pain points.
  • Ask about past specific events, not polite promises about the future.
  • Customers define the problem; FDEs own the solution and may push back.
  • Solve the real problem, not the one easiest to write as a ticket.

A Rippling FDE treats customer complaints as symptoms; good requirements discovery means tracing the issue back to a workflow problem before writing any code.

Key Takeaways

  • Customer complaints are almost always symptoms; behind dashboard latency, the real goal may be conversion rates
  • When the official process breaks down, the workarounds users invent for themselves are the clearest signal of where the pain points are
  • Good requirements discovery asks about specific past events, not opinions about the future; sometimes it also means correcting the customer's assumptions rather than doing exactly what they ask

A customer complains that the dashboard loads slowly. A good engineer's first instinct is to open the profiler, check the queries, and add a caching layer. But if what the customer really wants is higher conversion rates, the latency is just a symptom. An FDE could spend the entire project cycle shaving milliseconds without touching any of the customer's actual goals.

Kevin B., a Forward Deployed Engineer (FDE) at Rippling, sums up this lesson in one sentence: the problem is rarely the problem. In his view, what customers tell you is only the symptom.

If you want to transition into an FDE role, this skill is exactly what separates you from engineers who just work tickets. Educative's FDE course describes requirements discovery as a structured technical investigation conducted in the form of a conversation. The purpose of the discovery meeting is to understand the problem, not to show the FDE how they plan to solve it.

Three ways customers obscure the real problem

Educative points out that customers rarely state their real constraints in the first conversation. What they say out loud is either a symptom, a solution they've already chosen, or a vague goal. All three are reasonable starting points, but none of them is a requirements specification.

Back to the dashboard example. "It loads slowly" is a symptom; "we need a caching layer" is a pre-selected solution; "we want to grow revenue this quarter" is a vague goal. All three sentences could come from the same person in the same meeting. Whichever one you treat as the requirement, you'll end up building whatever is easiest to write up as a ticket, not what is actually correct.

So Educative argues that the FDE's job is to start from these descriptions and work backward to the underlying workflow problem. Keep asking: who opens this dashboard? At what time? What do they do after reading the numbers?

The answer might be: the sales team only glances at the dashboard each morning to decide who to call first; the real slowness isn't in the query, but in lead data arriving half a day late.

Workarounds are a map of the pain points

One clue is more valuable than any complaint: what users do for themselves when the official process fails. Educative calls workarounds the clearest signal of where the real business pain lies.

The Excel file manually exported every morning, the Slack channel dedicated to asking for numbers, the colleague assigned to "keep an eye on" the dashboard—each one is a ready-made map, waiting for you to read it.

To read it, the direction of your questions has to change. In The Mom Test by Rob Fitzpatrick, introduced in Yevgeniy Brikman's book review, the first rule is: talk about their life, not your idea. The next rule is: ask about specific things in the past, not general opinions about the future.

The difference sounds small, but it determines the quality of your evidence. "Would you use the dashboard more if it were faster?" only earns you a polite promise. "The last time the dashboard was slow, what did you do next?" earns you behavior that actually happened—and very likely the workaround you're looking for.

Palantir once required FDEs to work three to four days a week at the customer's office. Nabeel Qureshi, who wrote about his experience there, believes it was precisely being on-site that gave FDEs a detailed understanding of business processes in complex industries, which they then used to design software that solved the right problems.

Workarounds rarely surface in Zoom meetings; they appear when you're sitting next to the person with the Excel file open.

The problem belongs to the customer, the solution belongs to you

FDEs also face a daily tension: who decides what. The Mom Test offers a pact: you don't get to tell customers what their problem is; in exchange, they don't get to tell you what to do.

Meanwhile, PostHog's FDE handbook says that sometimes the right move is to correct the customer's understanding rather than doing exactly what they ask.

These two principles don't conflict; they divide the territory. The pain point belongs to the customer: you don't argue about whether the dashboard is actually slow. The solution belongs to you: when the customer insists on "adding a cache," you have both the right and the responsibility to put the evidence on the table and show that caching won't make the lead data arrive any earlier.

PostHog adds one more point: solve the real problem, not the one that's easiest to write up as a ticket. The cache is the easiest problem to write up as a ticket. Redesigning the lead data pipeline so the sales team has the numbers before 8 a.m.—that's the real problem. It's hard to write up as a ticket, but it's the reason the customer will remember you.

Start practicing before you get the title

You don't need the FDE title to start practicing. The next ticket a PM or internal customer gives you is a mini requirements discovery: before writing code, ask when this last happened, how they handled it then, and who ultimately uses the output.

Write a problem statement in your own words and send it to the requester for confirmation; if they correct you, you've found the gap between the symptom and the problem.

When reading job descriptions, watch for words like discovery, scoping, customer workflow, and onsite. When you see them, have a story ready: one about how you proactively went looking for the problem instead of waiting for someone to hand it to you.

On your resume, the most persuasive framing isn't "optimized latency by 40%," but "the original request was X; after investigating, I built Y, because Z."

That sentence shows the interviewer that you can tell the difference between a symptom and the underlying cause.

The next ticket you receive may still say "the dashboard is slow." Your task is to find out who is opening Excel to compensate for it.
