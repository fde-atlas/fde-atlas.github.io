How to negotiate an FDE offer: what moves and what to say

FDE Interview Prep · 2026-09-25 · 13 min read

Summary

This guide explains how to negotiate an FDE job offer, arguing for a fixed sequence: confirm the level first, ask which package components are flexible, make one specific ask backed by evidence or a real competing offer, and get everything in writing. Base salary is only one lever; level, equity, sign-on bonus and start date are others. The author cites candidate reports from Blind about Anthropic, Palantir and Google showing negotiation room varies widely by company, with no published data on counter-offer success rates. It includes scripts for common recruiter lines, a checklist of what a written offer should name, guidance on using competing offers honestly, and tactics for handling short deadlines.

Key points

  • Negotiate in order: settle the level before discussing numbers.
  • Base pay is one lever; equity, sign-on and start date also move.
  • Make one specific ask with a reason the recruiter can repeat.
  • Get every answer and the deadline in writing.
  • Candidate reports show negotiation room varies widely by company.

The offer arrived on a Thursday afternoon, the recruiter sounded delighted, and the email ended with “please let us know by Monday”. You want the job, you suspect the number could be better, and you have no idea which parts of it are allowed to move. Here is the short answer: negotiate an offer in a fixed order. Confirm the level first, ask which parts of the package are flexible, make one specific ask backed by evidence of your scope or a real competing offer, and get every answer and the deadline in writing. Base pay is only one of the levers; the level, the equity, the sign-on bonus and the date are the others. This post is about that conversation; for the posted bands and filings behind the numbers, read the FDE salary guide.

What candidates report about FDE negotiations

First, what to do when you hear each line; then the reports behind them.

What you hear Your next move
“Offers aren’t negotiated here” Ask how the level was set, then ask about the deadline, the start date and the sign-on
“This is the top of the band” Ask what the next level would have needed
“Let me see what I can do” Give one specific ask, with a reason, in writing
“We need an answer by Monday” Ask what drives the date, and name the date you need

Settle the level before the number

The level decides which band you negotiate inside, and it can be set before the offer exists. Glean’s Founding Forward Deployed Engineer posting says its range covers several levels and that your level is assessed during the interview process. Source 8Founding Forward Deployed EngineerPublisherGlean (Greenhouse job board)Source typecompany job posting The pay conversation can start even earlier: the application form for Palantir’s US Government FDSE posting in Washington, D.C., asks whether you are comfortable with the compensation range advertised on the posting. Source 9Apply: Forward Deployed Software Engineer - US Government (Washington, D.C.) - Palantir on LeverPublisherPalantir Technologies (Lever)Source typecompany job posting

So your negotiation starts at the recruiter screen. When the recruiter asks about level or expectations, find out what they are hiring for before you name anything:

“Which level is this role scoped at, and what would a candidate need to show in the loop to come in at the one above?”

Then spend the loop giving them that evidence. In our method, level evidence is scope: how many customers you owned, what broke on your watch and how you fixed it, what you decided without being asked. Practice saying that evidence out loud with the free question What level do you think you are, and why?.

If the offer comes in a level below what you interviewed for, that is its own conversation, with its own script. Down-leveled on your FDE offer? covers what candidates report and how to respond, and the question the offer came in one level below has a model answer you can practice out loud.

The first call after the offer: what to say

The recruiter may read the offer to you before you see it. Your only job on that call is to stay warm, get the details, and not decide. Here is our sequence.

First, thank them and ask for it in writing.

“Thank you, I’m excited about this team. Before I respond, could you send the full breakdown in writing so I can look at it properly?”

Next, ask what each component is. Palantir’s New York FDSE posting says total compensation may also include RSUs, a sign-on bonus and other potential incentives on top of the salary range. Source 10Forward Deployed Software Engineer (New York, NY)PublisherPalantir (Lever)Source typecompany job posting Ask about each line even if the recruiter only read out one.

“Is the base the whole cash figure, or is there a bonus target on top? What form is the equity in, and what’s the vesting schedule? Is there a sign-on?”

Then ask where the room is, before you ask for anything. This gets the recruiter to tell you which lever to pull.

“Which parts of this have some flexibility, and which are fixed by the level?”

Finally, make one ask, with a reason, on the next call. Not on this one. Go away, compare the written offer with the posted band (the FDE salary guide collects the posted ranges and filings) and your alternatives, then come back with a single request tied to evidence:

“Given the scope we discussed, owning the rollout for two customers end to end, I’d like to ask for the base at the upper end of the band. If base is fixed at this level, could we look at the sign-on instead?”

One ask, one reason, one fallback. A list of five demands gives the recruiter nothing to carry to the hiring manager.

What the written offer should name

  • The level and the exact title
  • Base salary, and any bonus target and how it is paid
  • The equity instrument, the vesting schedule and the cliff
  • Any sign-on bonus, and when you would have to repay it
  • Start date, location and the travel the role expects
  • The deadline, and whether it runs from the written or the verbal offer

Palantir’s US Government FDSE application form, for example, asks whether you support the travel percentage the posting advertises. A worked example: Priya’s offer, start to finish

The details are invented. Priya gets a verbal offer on a Thursday, with a Monday deadline. On the call she thanks the recruiter, asks for the offer in writing, and asks which parts have flexibility. The recruiter says base is fixed at the level and the sign-on has some room. She does not ask for anything yet.

That evening she checks her own numbers: stock at her current job vests in March, after her planned start. Then she sends this:

“Thanks for walking me through the offer today. As discussed, I’d like to ask for a sign-on bonus to cover the unvested stock I’d leave behind in March. Could we also confirm the deadline runs from the written offer? I’m keen to give you a clean answer by [date].”

The recruiter comes back on Friday with a sign-on added and the deadline moved to run from the written offer. It worked for three reasons: one ask, a reason the recruiter could repeat to the hiring manager, and everything in writing.

Competing offers: how to use one you have

A competing offer is the strongest leverage you can hold, and only if it is real. Say what it is plainly: the company, the level, and the components that differ.

“I have a written offer from [Company] at [level], with a larger equity grant and a sign-on bonus. You’re my first choice, and I’d like to make this work. Is there room on the equity or the sign-on to close the gap?”

Only say “first choice” if it is true; if they match, you should be ready to sign. Three things make this script work. It says you prefer them, so the recruiter has a reason to push internally. It names the components that differ, so the ask is specific. And it asks a question, not an ultimatum.

What to avoid:

  • Inventing or inflating an offer. A bluff can be checked, and a called bluff can cost you the offer.
  • Comparing unlike things. A grant at a private company, a front-loaded vest and a role with heavy travel are not the same as the same headline number elsewhere. Put both offers on one basis first; Comparing FDE offers has the math.
  • Using an offer you would never take. It puts you in a match you do not want.

If you hold no other offer, do not pretend. Say where your other processes stand, in stages, if they ask, and negotiate on the level and your evidence instead. The question Where else are you interviewing? covers how to answer that without overstating anything.

Equity and sign-on

Look here when the base is fixed by level.

Sign-on varies by offer, so ask. One poster on Blind reported, in May 2026, a Google FDE offer at L5 that included a sign-on bonus. Source 11Google (FDE) vs NVIDIA (AI Infra) – Offer comparisonPublisherBlind (teamblind.com)Source typecandidate report on Blind Another poster on Blind reported, in September 2026, a Databricks Staff FDE offer with no sign-on at all. Source 12Databricks staff FDE offerPublisherBlind (teamblind.com)Source typecandidate report on Blind The strongest reason to ask for one is money you lose by leaving: an unvested grant, a bonus that pays out after your planned start date.

“I’d be leaving unvested stock at my current employer that vests in March. Could a sign-on bonus cover that, or could we set my start date after it vests?”

A later start date is another lever: if it moves your start past a vest date, it keeps money a sign-on would otherwise have to replace. Ask for whichever one the recruiter says is easier to change. Ask whether the sign-on has a repayment clause if you leave within a set period, and get that clause in writing.

Equity needs definitions before it needs negotiation. Before you ask for more units, ask what they are: the instrument, the vesting schedule, the cliff, and whether there are refresh grants. FDE equity explained sorts RSUs from options and lists the terms to get in writing. If the recruiter tells you the equity figure is final, the question the recruiter says the equity number is final has the follow-up questions that still get you information without testing “final” with a bluff.

Exploding deadlines

A short deadline is a negotiating position too. Not every employer uses them. Anthropic’s careers FAQ tells candidates to take their time deciding on an offer and says it is happy to give them space to finish any other interview processes. Source 13CareersPublisherAnthropicSource typecompany hiring page So asking for time to finish your other processes is a request at least one employer invites in writing; make it plainly.

Use this order:

  • Ask what drives the date. “Could you tell me what’s behind the Monday date? Is it another candidate, or a team start?” The answer tells you whether it can move.
  • Pin when the clock started. “Does the deadline run from the written offer? I only received the details this morning.”
  • Ask for a specific date, with a reason. “My final round elsewhere is next Wednesday. Could we set the deadline for the Friday after, so I can give you a clean yes?” A named date with a reason gets a better hearing than “a bit more time”.
  • If the date holds, decide on what you have. Compare the real offer with a possible one, and know which you would take.

Never let a deadline pass in silence, and never accept to buy time with the intention of backing out. The question a one-week offer deadline has a model answer for the version of this that comes up before the offer.

When the answer is no

Sometimes the answer is no on everything. Whether that is a tactic, you cannot know, and you do not need to. Ask once, politely, about each component, and accept what you hear.

“Understood, thank you for checking. Just so I’m clear: is that final across the whole package, including the sign-on and the start date, or only on the base?”

If it is final across the board, you have three good options, and none of them is arguing:

  • Accept, and ask for a review. “Could we put a compensation or level review at the first performance cycle in writing, with the criteria?”
  • Accept as it is, if the role is worth it to you on its own.
  • Decline with thanks. Recruiters move, and a clean decline keeps the door open.

Mistakes that cost you the offer or the money

  • Naming your number before you know theirs. In the recruiter screen, ask for the range first. Our free model answer to What are your compensation expectations? gives the sequence, word for word.
  • Asking for “more” with no reason. Every ask needs a reason the recruiter can repeat: scope, a real offer, or money you lose by leaving.
  • Making the recruiter the opponent. They carry your case to the people who decide. Give them a clean, specific ask to carry.
  • Accepting verbally, then reopening. Once you say yes, the negotiation is over. Say “I’d like to see it in writing first” instead.
  • Negotiating only by email when a call would help. Use email for the record and a call for the ask, then confirm the call in writing.

Practice the call before you get it

The words above only help if they come out calmly on a call you did not schedule. Say each script out loud until it sounds like you, and write your one ask and its reason on a card before the offer call. If your offer call is this week, rehearse tonight. Questions people ask

Can you negotiate a forward deployed engineer offer?

You can always ask; what moves depends on the company and the component. Palantir’s New York FDSE posting, for example, says total compensation may also include RSUs and a sign-on bonus beyond the salary range, so base is not the only lever. Ask which parts are flexible, starting with the level, before you ask for more.Source 10Forward Deployed Software Engineer (New York, NY)PublisherPalantir (Lever)Source typecompany job posting

How long should I get to decide on an FDE offer?

Ask, and get the date in writing. Anthropic’s careers FAQ tells candidates to take their time deciding on an offer and says it is happy to give them space to finish any other interview processes. If another employer sets a short deadline, ask what drives it and for enough time to finish the processes you are in.Source 13CareersPublisherAnthropicSource typecompany hiring page

Should I tell the recruiter about a competing FDE offer?

Only if it is real, and then say what it is: the company, the level and the components. An invented or inflated offer costs you your credibility if it is checked. If you hold none, negotiate on the level and on evidence of your scope instead.
