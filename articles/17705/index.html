The difference between an FDE and a Solutions Architect

Original title: FDE 和解决方案架构师的区别

FDE Times · 2026-10-08 · 5 min read

Summary

This guide distinguishes the forward deployed engineer (FDE) from the solutions architect (SA): FDEs write, ship, and maintain production code in customer environments, while SAs deliver architecture documents and proofs of concept during pre-sales. Drawing on job descriptions from OpenAI, Palantir, Anthropic, and others, the author advises candidates to identify the real role by examining verbs in job postings, asking who fixes post-launch failures, and rewriting resumes to show shipped, maintained systems rather than artifacts.

Key points

  • FDEs own production code; SAs produce documents and PoCs.
  • SAs work pre-sales; FDEs join after the deal, through launch and operations.
  • Verbs like 'own' and 'ship' versus 'guide' reveal the role's nature.
  • Ask in interviews who fixes your code after go-live.
  • Palantir FDSE roles involve 25–50% travel; clarify vague postings yourself.

Job postings often mix "solutions" and "customer-facing" together. Before accepting a role, ask who is responsible for production code, so you don't end up just drawing architecture diagrams.

Key Takeaways

  • FDEs own production code at the customer; Solutions Architects deliver architecture documents, often stopping at the proof of concept.
  • FDEs come in after the deal is signed, going deep into launch and operations; Solutions Architects serve as technical advisors during the pre-sales phase.
  • When reading job descriptions and writing your resume, look for evidence of commits to production, not architecture diagrams.

Imagine you're at the first kickoff meeting with a major client. On the table sits a thick architecture design: clear diagrams, every arrow pointing in the right direction. The question that silences the room isn't "is this design correct?" but "who opens the first Pull Request (PR) in the client's code repository?"

That question is exactly the dividing line between Forward Deployed Engineers (FDEs) and Solutions Architects (SAs). Netguru puts it in one sentence: FDEs are responsible for code running in production, while Solutions Architects produce architecture deliverables. To leave no doubt, it adds: SAs produce artifacts, not commits.

For developers considering a move to FDE, this is the first thing to clarify. Many job postings mix "solutions," "architecture," and "customer-facing" together—you might think you're going to deliver code, only to end up with a drawing job. The reverse happens too.

Designs don't run; commits do

Start with what gets delivered. Aced's comparison table puts the two side by side: an FDE delivers a working production system for a specific customer; an SA delivers an implementation plan. On the code front, the FDE continuously delivers and maintains; while according to the same table, the SA usually stops at the proof of concept (PoC).

The timing of involvement differs too. Aced places the FDE after the deal is signed, going deep into launch and operations. Anthropic, meanwhile, describes its Solutions Architect role as a pre-sales position: serving as a technical advisor to enterprise customers, influencing architecture choices, and helping customers integrate Claude—but not owning the customer's production code.

These two descriptions aren't contradictory; they're complementary. The SA stands before the contract is signed, answering "should we do this, and how"; the FDE stands after the contract is signed, answering "is it running, and why did it just go down?"

Companies draw the line with verbs

If you're still unsure, read the employer's job description. OpenAI's FDE role in San Francisco requires candidates to own the technical delivery of multiple deployment projects, from the first prototype all the way to a stable production environment. The same description also includes writing and reviewing production code in Python, JavaScript, or a similar stack, on both the frontend and backend.

Palantir's FDSE role describes engineers embedded directly in customer teams, who build applications, LLM workflows, and production-grade solutions, designed around each customer's actual situation. It also states the cost: expect 25–50% travel depending on team and location.

Capicua puts it more briefly: writing production code directly on the customer's infrastructure and data.

Notice these verbs: own, write, review, build, ship. These FDE job descriptions are heavily weighted toward hands-on work with code, in contrast to the guide in Anthropic's SA posting. When quickly browsing job descriptions on your phone, this is the fastest way to tell the two roles apart.

The same problem, two approaches

Let's look at the actual difference with a hypothetical case. An insurance company wants to use an LLM to classify and route claims. Both an SA and an FDE are brought in.

The SA sits down with the client's CTO, asking about business volume, service level agreements (SLAs), and where data is allowed to flow. The output is a document: data ingestion pipeline, classification steps, routing steps, model selection, fallback plan. Perhaps accompanied by a proof-of-concept notebook, run on a few hundred samples to prove the idea works.

The document is approved, the contract is signed, and the SA moves on to the next client.

The FDE takes that document and clones the client's code repository. He discovers: the real claims table has a status column the document never mentioned; the internal authentication system blocks newly created service accounts; the nightly data pipeline starts running before the data sync completes. None of these three things appear anywhere in the design.

The FDE fixes the data migration, writes an adapter for the authentication system, adjusts the scheduling, opens a PR, waits for the client's platform team to review it, then watches the monitoring dashboards throughout the first week after launch.

Now imagine the client sends the same message on a Friday afternoon: "The schema just changed; it must be running by Monday." The SA's response is entirely reasonable: the document needs updating, and the estimates need re-evaluating. The FDE's response is a PR link. Both are doing their jobs—it's just that the jobs themselves are different.

Figure out which side you're being hired for

Step one: read the job description and pick out the verbs. If they're mostly guide, advise, design, recommend, it's an SA role even if the title says "engineer." If they're own, write, ship, maintain, debug, it's an FDE role even if the title says "solutions."

Step two: ask one specific question in the interview: "After launch, if code I wrote breaks, who fixes it?" If the answer is "the client's team" or "the product team," you're talking about a role that produces artifacts. If the answer is "you," it's a role that produces commits.

Step three: clarify timing and location. Do you join before or after the contract is signed? Does the code run on the client's infrastructure, or in your company's demo sandbox? How much time will you spend on-site with the client? Palantir answers the last question in its job description; where the description is vague, you'll have to ask yourself.

Step four: write your resume with the same logic. An experience like "designed the architecture for system X" tells an FDE hiring manager that you produce artifacts. An experience like "delivered system X to production at client Y and maintained it across three release iterations" shows you produce commits and take responsibility for them. Hiring managers are looking for the latter.

Common Pitfalls

The biggest pitfall is assuming an FDE is just "an SA who can code." Not so. Many SAs have excellent coding skills. The difference isn't in skills, but in whose name is on the commit that actually runs, and who is accountable when it breaks. A proof of concept running on a laptop, however well done, is still an artifact.

The second pitfall is mistaking pre-sales demos for deployments. Demos exist to prove capability and close deals; deployments must withstand the client's dirty data, unfamiliar authentication systems, and batch scheduling. If all your "customer-facing" experience is demos, you don't yet have evidence you can handle an FDE role.

The third pitfall is avoiding operational responsibility once you become an FDE. Aced places the FDE in a position of "going deep into launch and operations" for a reason. If you disappear after delivery, you're doing SA work while carrying FDE-level risk.

This Week's Exercise

Open your resume and annotate each work experience: which ones describe artifacts, and which describe commits that actually ran. Pick one artifact-type experience and rewrite it as something you delivered and maintained—or admit that you don't yet have such evidence.

Design tells you how a system should run. The FDE makes it actually run.
