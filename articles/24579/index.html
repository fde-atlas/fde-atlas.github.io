How to Set Up a Vulnerability Reporting Channel: Streamlining Your Intake Process with security.txt and IPA Submissions

Original title: 脆弱性報告窓口の作り方は？security.txtとIPA届出で整える受付フロー

FDE総研 · 2026-11-02 · 10 min read

Summary

This Japanese guide explains how organizations can set up an official vulnerability reporting channel. It covers publishing a security.txt file under RFC 9116 (Contact and Expires are mandatory), establishing a VDP before considering bug bounties, and following a five-step handling process aligned with ISO/IEC 30111: acknowledgment, triage, remediation, disclosure coordination, and post-review. It also explains Japan's IPA reporting system, which routes software product reports through JPCERT/CC and publishes via JVN, and offers staffing models by company size. A final section addresses FDEs who find vulnerabilities in customer environments, advising them to identify ownership first and use formal reporting channels rather than making unauthorized fixes.

Key points

  • security.txt requires Contact and Expires fields, HTTPS delivery, and regular expiry checks.
  • Build a VDP before launching a bug bounty, or report volume will overwhelm capacity.
  • Five-step process: acknowledgment, triage, fix, disclosure coordination, post-incident review.
  • IPA routes software reports via JPCERT/CC with JVN publication; website reports stay unnamed.
  • FDEs should classify vulnerability ownership and report through formal channels, not fix unilaterally.

What is a vulnerability reporting channel, and why is it necessary?

A vulnerability reporting channel is an official route that allows external discoverers to report vulnerabilities safely and without confusion. It prevents reports from being ignored or exposed on social media.

What happens if there is no reporting channel?

Without a channel, reports get lost in inquiry forms or emails addressed to sales. It can also push discoverers to go public with their findings.

Even well-intentioned discoverers, if they cannot find contact information, have no choice but to send reports to a general-purpose email address or a social media DM. The recipient lacks the security expertise to make a judgment, and after weeks without a reply, the discoverer concludes they were ignored. Such misunderstandings occur in many organizations regardless of size. Even looking at publicly disclosed data breach incidents, it is not uncommon for external reports to take considerable time to reach the appropriate internal department.

How does a VDP differ from a bug bounty?

A VDP (Vulnerability Disclosure Policy) is a declaration of intake rules. A bug bounty is a program that adds monetary rewards on top of that; what should be established first is the VDP.

ItemVDP (Vulnerability Disclosure Policy)Bug Bounty
PurposeProvides a route and rules for accepting reportsEncourages research through monetary rewards
CostMainly staff time for operationsReward payments and platform operating costs
PrerequisitesA structure capable of intake and initial responseA stable VDP operation
Suited toAll organizationsOrganizations that can secure triage staff
Main riskLate replies erode trustA surge in reports overwhelms processing capacity

If you launch a bug bounty without a VDP in place, your capacity cannot keep up with the number of reports. The realistic order is to first clarify your "contact point" and "commitments" with a VDP and security.txt.

How do you publish contact information with security.txt?

security.txt is a standard that places a text file at

/.well-known/security.txt

to indicate contact information. RFC 9116 requires two fields: Contact and Expires.

What are the required and recommended fields?

Contact and Expires are required. For practical operation, adding Policy, Preferred-Languages, and Canonical reduces misunderstandings at intake.

The main fields defined in RFC 9116 (published by the IETF in April 2022) are as follows.

FieldRequired/OptionalRoleOperational notes
ContactRequired (one or more)Email address (mailto:) or URL for reportsSpecify a shared intake address, not an individual's
ExpiresRequired (exactly one)Expiration date of the fileRFC recommends setting it within one year
EncryptionOptionalLocation of the public key used for encryptionKey updates are easy to forget
PolicyOptionalLink to the VDP documentClearly state the scope and safe harbor (a commitment not to pursue good-faith reporters legally)
Preferred-LanguagesOptionalLanguages supportedExample: ja, en
CanonicalOptionalCanonical URL of this fileCombined with a signature, it helps detect tampering
AcknowledgmentsOptionalAcknowledgments pageList reporters only with their consent

Here is an example (the domain is for illustration purposes).

Contact: mailto:security@example.com
Contact: https://example.com/security/report
Expires: 2027-10-31T23:59:59+09:00
Encryption: https://example.com/.well-known/pgp-key.txt
Preferred-Languages: ja, en
Canonical: https://example.com/.well-known/security.txt
Policy: https://example.com/security/policy

What are common mistakes when deploying it?

The three most common failures are an expired Expires field, serving the file without HTTPS, and a Contact address that no one monitors. After deployment, set a schedule for periodic checks.

RFC 9116 requires that the file be served over HTTPS and returned at

text/plain

OpenPGP digital signatures are also recommended. In the view of the FDE Soken editorial team, the harder part is not placing the file itself, but deciding who checks the Contact address daily. Once you set Expires to one year ahead, register an update task well before the deadline to prevent expiration.

What is the flow from intake to disclosure?

From intake to disclosure, proceed through five stages: receipt confirmation, triage, remediation, disclosure coordination, and post-incident review. This follows the same flow as the ISO/IEC 30111 handling process.

What are the standard five steps?

  • Receipt confirmation: Upon receiving a report, first reply confirming receipt. State the target deadline in your VDP.
  • Triage: Verify reproducibility, evaluate severity using CVSS (FIRST released v4.0 in November 2023), and confirm the scope of impact.
  • Remediation and mitigation: Prepare a permanent fix as well as interim workarounds for the interim period.
  • Publication coordination: Agree with the reporter and the coordinating body on the publication date, credit attribution, and whether a CVE ID will be assigned.
  • Post-incident review: Record the classification of the cause, why it was not detected, and measures to prevent recurrence.

ISO/IEC 29147 is the international standard covering external interactions (disclosure), while ISO/IEC 30111 covers internal handling processes. Referencing the two as a pair makes it easier to check for gaps and omissions.

How is the deadline for publication decided?

In principle, the publication deadline is set by agreement with the reporter. As a guideline, many organizations reference the well-known 90-day disclosure policy used in the industry.

A well-known example is Google Project Zero, which uses 90 days from the report as the basic disclosure deadline. However, this is a research team's policy, not a legal standard. The period varies depending on how difficult the fix is and whether the vulnerability is already being exploited. If you want to extend the deadline, explaining the reason and expected timeline to the reporter and keeping a record of the process makes it less likely to cause trouble later.

How should you use your own contact point versus reporting to IPA?

Your own contact point is a channel for directly receiving reports about your company's products or websites. Reporting to IPA is a public coordination channel available when coordination between the parties is difficult.

How does the IPA reporting system work?

Under IPA's "Information Security Early Warning Partnership," the coordination routes differ between software products and websites. For products, JPCERT/CC handles coordination with developers.

CategoryReceptionCoordinatorPublication
Software productsIPAJPCERT/CC coordinates with the product developerPublished on JVN (Japan Vulnerability Notes)
WebsitesIPAIPA notifies the site operatorIn principle, individual site names are not published

This system operates on the basis of the METI ordinance "Handling Regulations for Vulnerability-Related Information on Software Products, etc." The specific procedures are described in the "Information Security Early Warning Partnership Guidelines" published by IPA and JPCERT/CC. IPA publishes the number of reports quarterly, so please check the latest figures on the organization's official website.

Even if a company has its own contact point, will it still be contacted by IPA?

Even if you have your own contact point, if the discoverer reports to IPA, you will be contacted by IPA or JPCERT/CC. You need to be prepared to receive reports through both channels.

Discoverers are free to choose either channel. To avoid being in a "no one is assigned" state when contacted by a coordinating body, consider registering in JPCERT/CC's product developer list, and consolidating the contact point for coordinating bodies' communications to the same address as your own contact point reduces confusion. If you provide products in the EU, the reporting obligations under the EU Cyber Resilience Act (CRA), which began applying on September 11, 2026 (reporting vulnerabilities confirmed to be exploited to authorities), also apply, so you need to confirm this with your legal department.

How should the structure change depending on company size?

The differences by company size come down to "who takes on the roles" and "how many hours from receipt to initial judgment." Even small companies can build a minimal structure.

What are examples of structures by size?

Small companies can start sufficiently with a small team of people holding multiple roles. Mid-sized and larger companies should make a PSIRT (Product Security Incident Response Team) or CSIRT (Computer Security Incident Response Team) the primary body for receiving reports.

Size guidelineReceiving bodyMinimum requirementsExternal coordination
Small (a few to a few dozen developers)Two people: CTO and development lead sharing the rolessecurity.txt, shared mailbox, simplified VDPAbility to receive contact from IPA and JPCERT/CC
Mid-sizedDesignated staff from the IT or development departmentVDP, triage criteria, ticket managementCommunication network with legal and PR
Large enterprises and product vendorsPSIRT/CSIRTProcedures aligned with ISO/IEC 30111, CVE assignment structureJVN, overseas coordinating bodies, regulatory authorities in each country

Since staffing guidelines vary by industry, use the table above as an example for organizing "who takes on what."

What are the priorities when starting small?

The initial priorities are three things: securing a contact address, a template for acknowledgment notifications, and an escalation path. Bug bounties and dedicated tools can wait.

From a practical standpoint, the most effective thing at launch is summarizing "who returns what, by when" in a single table. Even before introducing tools, simply deciding on the wording of acknowledgment notifications and "who to call if severity is high" greatly reduces hesitation in the initial response.

What should an FDE do when receiving a vulnerability report in a customer environment?

When an FDE encounters a vulnerability report or discovery in a customer environment, the top priority is to connect through formal channels to both the customer's contact point and your own company's contact point before attempting to fix it yourself.

How do you determine whose vulnerability it is?

First, determine whether the affected system is the customer's system, your own product, or a third party's OSS. The reporting destination differs for each.

Location of the vulnerabilityFirst reporting destinationWhat the FDE should doWhat to avoid
A system developed in-house by the customerThe customer's security contact pointShare the facts and reproduction steps in writingMaking fixes unilaterally outside the contract
Your own product or a component your company providedYour company's PSIRT/contact pointFile a ticket with your own contact point and align on the policy for explaining to the customerOnly verbally informing the customer and leaving it at that
Third-party OSS or vendor productsThe developer's contact point or IPAObtain agreement from both the customer and your company before reportingSending it externally while still including the customer's environment information

An FDE (Forward Deployed Engineer) is the role of "an engineer who embeds in the customer's site and handles implementation," popularized by Palantir. Because they work close to customers' production data and internal networks, it is a role with many opportunities to notice vulnerabilities. On the other hand, since they also handle the customer's confidential information, they need to agree in advance on what and how much can be included in external reports.

What on-site judgments are easy to get wrong?

The judgment that is easy to get wrong is "I can fix it right away, so I'll fix it." Rather than deciding whether to fix it, it is safer to prioritize keeping records and following the reporting channel.

According to the FDE Soken editorial team's analysis, the three judgments FDEs tend to struggle with on-site are as follows.

  • Making small fixes unilaterally: Even if the fix itself is correct, if the change record or approval is missing, you will be unable to explain it during audits or later root cause investigations.
  • Handle it with a report in Slack: Chat messages scroll away and disappear. Issues need to be recorded somewhere trackable later, such as a ticket system.
  • Discussing disclosure timing in the field: Disclosure timing should be coordinated by the designated point of contact, and it is safer for FDEs not to make commitments on their own.

In terms of an FDE career, in addition to being "technically able to fix things," being able to "route issues through the proper channels" is another evaluation criterion that builds customer trust. What distinguishes FDEs from SWEs and IT consultants is their role in bridging the point of contact between the customer's operations and the company's own product. Companies considering adopting FDEs should agree at the contract stage on "where to report vulnerabilities found in the field and what information may be included in reports," so that field staff do not have to hesitate.
