How Loop gets into automotive R&D: FDEs on-site, turning AI into an autonomous closed loop

Original title: Loop 怎么跑进汽车研发：FDE 驻场，把 AI 做成自主闭环

53AI · 2026-07-28 · 11 min read

Summary

This article explains how the Combo Agent platform brings AI into automotive R&D through two ideas: the Forward Deployed Engineer (FDE) and the autonomous loop. Instead of selling a tool, FDEs sit inside the customer's office, learn their Polarion workflows, state machines and field definitions, and configure an agent that works autonomously. Using CronJobs and event triggers, the agent analyzes new requirements, derives test cases, judges bench/HIL results, writes results back to Polarion, creates defects and links traceability automatically. A demo on the DrivePilot project shows a defect (DP-544) written entirely by code. Engineers keep decision authority, data stays on-premise, and judgment rules live in a configurable knowledge base rather than hard-coded skills.

Key points

  • FDE, a Palantir-originated role, delivers working software on-site and owns outcomes.
  • Configured agents run continuously via CronJobs and event triggers, not chat-style Q&A.
  • discover-schema reads each customer's custom Polarion structure into a reviewable workflow.md.
  • Automotive signal parsing plus a knowledge base (e.g., UDS/ISO 14229 rules) enables automated pass/fail judgment.
  • Failed tests auto-create defects with traceability links; engineers only review disputed items.

Full text

The old way: you open a chat window, paste in a requirement, and wait for the answer. The new way: at 2 a.m., nobody opens a chat window—a new requirements document gets dropped into Polarion by a colleague; by the time you arrive at your desk in the morning, the requirements analysis, a conflict list, and a draft Test Run are already sitting there. That's because there's an agent we "configured on-site" that has been watching your project all along. This article is about the two words that make AI in automotive R&D actually land: FDE (Forward Deployed Engineer) and Loop (autonomous closed loop)—and how it runs on the customer's own Polarion.

About the platform: What is Combo Agent

The "it" that appears throughout this article refers to Combo Agent™—our enterprise-grade AI agent platform. Its core concept is "pluggable skills": professional capabilities such as parsing, modeling, diagram generation, compliance analysis, ALM read/write, and test result write-back are packaged into standardized, on-demand skill units attached to the platform's application layer. You describe what you want in natural language, and the platform automatically orchestrates the relevant skills in a sandbox, runs the entire workflow, and delivers the results.

What it solves is straightforward—using AI agents to empower your existing enterprise workflows and cut costs while boosting efficiency, not replacing your existing toolchain (DOORS / Polarion / Jama / Jira / CANoe / dSPACE stay as they are). In the previous article we covered "from design documents to ASPICE compliance diagrams"; this one takes a different angle on how to make it land: instead of handing you a tool, we "send the engineer over."

The first word: FDE—not selling tools, but "sending engineers on-site"

The role of FDE (Forward Deployed Engineer) was first introduced by Palantir. Palantir's products (Foundry / Gotham) are powerful but general-purpose—no customer could turn such a platform into a solution for their own problems "out of the box." So instead of sending a "team of consultants," Palantir sent senior engineers on-site: they sat in the customer's offices, attended the customer's morning meetings, learned the customer's business, and then delivered software that could actually run in production, staying until the system went live and was genuinely in use.

Here's the key distinction—an FDE is not "a consultant who gives advice," nor "a pre-sales person who builds a demo":

传统顾问 / 售前 FDE
DeliverablePPTs, recommendations, temporary demosSoftware running in the customer's production environment
RelationshipHands over and leaves once the contract is signedStays until the system is live and in use
ResponsibilityProvides solutionsAccountable for results—if something breaks at 2 a.m., they're the one who fixes it

We've brought this model into automotive R&D. For us, being an FDE means every engineer must achieve two kinds of "deep understanding":

Deeply understanding the customer's workflow

—not a vague "understanding of requirements," but mapping out the customer's R&D process section by section: which state machine requirements follow in Polarion, how test cases are derived, how bench / HIL testing is executed, which fields results are written back to, and how traceability links are connected. Combined with our consulting engineers' understanding of ASPICE / functional safety processes, we know exactly how this chain should run.

Deeply understanding the boundaries of AI

—equally important is knowing where AI should not go: which tasks are mechanical alignment, cross-tool transfers, and draft generation that can safely be handed to an Agent; and which are judgment, decisions, and sign-offs that must stay with humans. Draw the boundary wrong, and people either won't dare use it or will misuse it.

The value of an FDE lies precisely at the intersection of "understanding the process" and "understanding AI's boundaries": configuring the Agent into a colleague who understands your project—rather than handing you a generic tool and leaving you to figure it out yourself.

In one sentence: we don't sell a license and walk away; instead, we sit in the customer's office and configure the customer's R&D processes, the fields and state machines in Polarion, and the decision rules of each domain into the Agent, one by one. The deliverable is not "a generic tool" but "a colleague who understands your project and can start working on its own."

The second word: Loop—the configured Agent starts working on its own

A configured Agent is no longer "one question, one answer." It stays resident, watches for events, and starts working on its own. The mechanism is simple:

CronJob: when the time comes → a message is pushed into the session → the Agent decides what to do based on its prompt. EventTrigger: when an event arrives → a message is pushed into the session → the Agent decides what to do based on its prompt.

The trigger system does not predefine actions like "review / notify / report"—the Agent is the logic layer, your prompt is the configuration, and the trigger system only handles delivery. You say "analyze when new requirements arrive," and it analyzes when it receives the event; you say "judge and write back when results arrive," and it judges and writes back when it receives the results.

Triggers: event-driven is the engine of the closed loop

What allows the closed loop to "start work on its own" is a set of event-triggering infrastructure running in parallel with scheduled tasks. Event sources can be:

Polarion changes—new work items, status transitions, Test Run updates;

a monitored upload directory / cloud drive—starts scanning whenever a new requirements document appears;

SCM—PR / push (code side, already in production).

When an event arrives, the trigger system matches rules by "source + filter conditions," formats it into a message, and delivers it to the target session; the rest is left to the Agent.

Configuring it takes just one sentence:

You: From now on, whenever a new requirements document comes in for DrivePilot, automatically run requirements analysis and log conflicts as issue tickets; no need to call me every time. Agent: ✅ Event trigger "auto-analysis for new requirements" created. From now on, when DrivePilot receives a workitem.created (requirements-type) event, I will handle it automatically in this session.

A few engineering "foolproofing" details ensure it won't run wild in real projects:

MechanismPurpose
main / isolatedmain reuses the current session and keeps context; isolated is a temporary session, deleted after use
debounceIf the same requirement is edited 5 times in a short period, it triggers only once—no spam
conflict_policyWhen a session is busy, events are queued (or dropped), avoiding conflicts
distributed lockMultiple workers don't process the same event twice

Writing into the customer's Polarion: read/write + custom fields + flexible definitions

The key to landing the closed loop is being able to write into the customer's own Polarion—and every customer's Polarion is different: work item types differ, fields are custom, and so are the state machines and link rules. Our approach is to treat Polarion as a "customizable tree":

Project → document space / LiveDoc → work item types (including custom types) → fields / enum values / workflow state machine / link rules; test management (Test Run fields / Step columns) is another branch.

This tree has no hardcoded support list. A single command, discover-schema --deep, automatically pulls out the entire real structure: which types exist, what fields each type has (including custom ones), the valid options for enum fields, the state machine's "action → target state → prerequisite fields," link relationship rules, and so on.

The discovered structure is written back into a workflow.md that the customer can review:

If a spec exists, follow it

—if the customer has written types/fields/enums/links/status transitions in workflow.md, the Agent follows them strictly and takes no liberties;

If not written, discover it

—blank sections are auto-filled, marked "(AI auto-discovered · pending confirmation)," for the customer to review;

Anything the customer has already written by hand is never modified or overwritten.

When writing fields, the tools have you covered on the pitfalls: enum fields are filled with valid option ids (automatically wrapped into the type Polarion expects), and status transitions automatically include prerequisite fields (e.g., fill in resolution before transitioning)—otherwise Polarion will reject them. Naming differences across customers for the same concept (DOORS calls it Safety, Polarion calls it safety, ZenTao calls it customSafety) are handled with a per-customer mapping yaml that translates once between "native name ↔ canonical name"; business code only recognizes canonical names.

Look at a real one: defect DP-528 was written into DrivePilot by the closed loop itself—fields like type, severity, status, priority, and version are all complete; the description contains the Test Run, Test Case, the 8 test steps, and the verdict: "HIL: torque 14.2Nm exceeds 10Nm limit".

Signals and rules: two layers of automotive-specific skill

Beyond requirements and testing, automotive work has a layer others don't have. It's really two things stacked together—one is extracting the data, the other is knowing "whether the extracted values actually pass."

First: signal analysis—restoring runtime data from buses and calibration into physical quantities that can be judged.

SkillWhat it does
automotive-data-parseBLF + DBC → signal timelines; A2L + HEX → calibration physical values (streaming parsing for large files)
canoe-test-automationDBC → signal-level test cases → CAPL / Test Module (including UDS diagnostic scripts), using stable IDs for bidirectional requirement↔case traceability
basetest-analyzerReads ECU-TEST / CANoe reports + parsed results, makes domain-by-domain verdicts according to KB rules, produces non-conformances and writes them back to Polarion

Second: the knowledge base (KB)—extracting the signals is only the first half; "whether this value passes" depends on the KB. What the KB accumulates is this customer's parsing of two kinds of things, turned into executable judgment rules:

Requirements / expected-value parsing

—normalizing the expected values, thresholds, and tolerances in the customer's test requirements into a precisely queryable library (E2E / DTC / CCDB, communication matrices, etc.); during judgment, values are drilled down along "symptom → test case → requirement."

Regulation / protocol parsing

—hard constraints from industry standards and diagnostic protocols written as rules. Take UDS diagnostics (ISO 14229) as an example; these rules are very specific:

Positive response format

: e.g., the positive response of 0x22 ReadDataByIdentifier must be 0x62 + echoed original DID + data record, with length and framing (ISO-TP) compliant;

Timing

: the service must respond within P2_server; if longer is needed, 0x78 (responsePending) must be used as a legal delay—exceeding P2* is judged non-conformant;

Negative response codes (NRC)

: Return the right codes when you should refuse—0x13 for invalid length, 0x31 for out of range, 0x33 for security access denied; and don't wrongly reject what should pass.

The most critical design principle: rules are never hard-coded into skills. Expected values, thresholds, and UDS / regulatory judgments are all KB entries, configured per customer / vehicle model / regulation version and overridable at any time; adding a test domain = adding a file (network testing is already live; UDS / diagnostics and DTC / OCC will be extended per the requirements list), with the main framework untouched.

Closing the write-back loop: how HIL results become verdicts + defects + traceability in Polarion

Once the verdict is out, how does it "land" in Polarion? This step is the true closing of the loop, with all rules in the business layer, overridable per customer configuration.

Step 1: Normalize verdict terms. Raw verdicts from test benches / analysis tools come in all shapes and forms, so first map them to Polarion's standard result enums:

Raw verdictPolarion result
SUCCESS / PASS / OKpassed
FAILED / FAILUREfailed
INCONCLUSIVE / ERRORblocked
SKIPPED / NOT TESTEDnot_tested
The mapping table is customer-overridable; when encountering an unrecognized verdict term, it's better to raise an error than to write blindly—writing a wrong result into Polarion is more dangerous than raising an error.

Step 2: Close the loop in order (each test case is processed independently; one failure doesn't interrupt the whole batch):

  • Write the test case result (set_test_result) and step-level results;
  • Verdict first, evidence second—evidence attachments are uploaded only after the results are written (Polarion doesn't accept attachments for unexecuted records);
  • Failed items automatically create defects, carrying the verdict details (failed signals, thresholds, measured values, etc.); if the customer has customized fields in Polarion such as DTC / signal / OCC, they can be written directly by field;
  • Connect traceability links to the defect: defect ↔ test case, defect ↔ requirement (link types translated per the customer's naming).

Boundaries: it doesn't replace engineers

No matter how automated the loop is, the judgment always stays in the engineer's hands. The Agent does mechanical alignment and drafts; the engineer makes decisions and signs off.

Frequently asked questions

Q: Our Polarion fields are quite special—we've customized a bunch of them. Will it work? A: Yes. That's exactly the design starting point—discover-schema reads out your actual structure (including custom types/fields/enums/state machines), writes it into workflow.md for your review, and then performs direct read/write access, without requiring you to change any fields.

Q: Will event triggers run wild, run repeatedly, or flood engineers with notifications? A: There's debounce (multiple changes to the same item trigger only once), conflict_policy (queues when a session is busy), and distributed locks (multiple workers don't duplicate work)—it's controllable.

Q: We don't want full automation; we want human confirmation at key points. A: That's possible. Leave items with "controversial judgments / confidence below threshold" for human confirmation, and automate the rest. The threshold can be set lower early on (letting the Agent suggest more) and raised later as it matures (less interruption).

Q: Can it work across multiple ALM tools? A: Yes. There are adapters for DOORS / Polarion / Jama / Jira / Feishu Projects, and customers are not required to switch tools.

Q: What about data security? Does data leave the domain? A: Private deployment, runs on intranets, tenant-level physical isolation, and no cross-customer benchmark training.
