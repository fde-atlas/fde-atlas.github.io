How to Write an FDE Weekly Report: A Weekly Report Template for Client Projects

Original title: FDE 周报怎么写：客户项目周报模板

FDE Times · 2026-10-08 · 6 min read

Summary

This guide argues that FDE projects often stall on undecided customer decisions rather than code, and that a one-page weekly report is the cheapest fix. Using a hypothetical email-classification agent project for a logistics company, it walks through a six-section Markdown template: agree on format with stakeholders upfront, keep the structure fixed, report outcomes instead of activities, back status colors with evidence, surface risks early with mitigation owners, and consolidate customer decisions into a table listing decision-maker, options, deadline, and cost of delay. Reports should go out before the weekly meeting so discussion focuses on decisions. The article closes with advice on framing this skill on resumes and in interviews.

Key points

  • Projects stall on pending decisions more than on slow coding.
  • A fixed one-page template answers outcomes, risks, and needed decisions weekly.
  • List outcomes, not activities; each line should be verifiable by the customer.
  • Status colors need evidence; marking everything green erodes trust.
  • Send the report before the meeting and state the cost of delayed decisions.

Key Takeaways

  • Report outcomes, not activities, and keep the same structure every week so the client knows where to look.
  • Every status color must be backed by evidence. Marking everything green while the team knows there are problems will destroy trust.
  • Put everything the client needs to decide in one section, specifying the decision-maker, the options, and the deadline, and send the report before the weekly meeting.

According to The Bricks' guide on report templates, many projects fall behind not because teams are slow, but because decisions drag on. Forward Deployed Engineers (FDEs) working on-site with clients know this situation well: the model works, the data pipeline is done, yet a whole week passes with no one deciding whether the AI agent can send emails to real customers on its own.

To unblock this kind of bottleneck, a weekly report is the lowest-cost tool.

A good weekly report answers only three questions: what was accomplished this week, which risks are rising, and what the client needs to decide and by when. This article builds a one-page template around these three questions, fills it out completely with a hypothetical case, and finally explains how to write this skill into your resume.

What to Prepare Before You Start

This article uses a hypothetical scenario throughout: you are a Forward Deployed Engineer (FDE) deploying an AI agent for the customer service department of a logistics company, responsible for classifying emails and drafting replies. By the end of this article, you will have a reusable Markdown weekly report template, plus a completed version for this project.

You will need: a list of milestones agreed with the client, your notes from this week's work, and a list of people on the client side who have decision authority over each decision. If it is still unclear who decides what, make that the first risk recorded in your weekly report.

Step 1: Ask the Client What They Want Before Writing

Simply Stakeholders, a website focused on stakeholder management, recommends agreeing on three things at the start of a project: what information stakeholders want, how often they want it, and in what format or through what channel. At the logistics company in the example, the operations director may only read email, while the IT lead prefers Slack or Jira.

This question takes only five minutes at the kickoff meeting, yet it prevents your carefully written report from going unopened. Write the answers at the top of the template so the rest of the team knows them too.

Check: You have noted the recipients' names, the delivery channel, and a fixed weekly send day.

Step 2: Build the Structure, Then Keep It Fixed

The structure has six sections, kept in the same format every week so the client always knows where to look. Below is a template you can use directly. This is the condensed version; adjust it to fit your project:

# 周报 – [项目名称] – 第 [周数] 周，[发送日期]
整体状态：绿 / 黄 / 红 — 原因：[一句话，附证据]

## 1. 本周成果
## 2. 下周计划
## 3. 风险与问题
| 风险 | 趋势 | 影响 | 缓解措施 | 负责人 |
## 4. 需要您做的决策
| 决策 | 决策人 | 可选方案 | 截止时间 | 若延误 |
## 5. 客户团队待办
| 事项 | 负责人 | 截止时间 |
## 6. 里程碑
| 里程碑 | 计划日期 | 状态 |

Usually one short page is enough. If you have written onto a second page, you are probably restating work done rather than reporting outcomes.

Check: The blank template fits entirely within one laptop screen.

Step 3: Write Outcomes, Not Activities

The rule for Section 1 is simple: list outcomes, not activities. If you are used to standups in the "here's what I did yesterday" style, this is where mistakes are most likely.

Compare two ways of writing about the same week of the email AI agent project:

Activities

  • Held 3 meetings with the operations team
  • Built a data pipeline to read emails from the shared inbox
  • Tried several classification prompts

Outcomes

  • Agreed with the operations team on the list of email types the AI agent must handle
  • The AI agent reads real emails from the shared inbox in the test environment
  • The AI agent classified the sample emails provided by the client, and the results have been sent to the customer service lead for review

The right column answers the question the client actually cares about: "What do I have now that I didn't have before?" A useful self-check: does every line contain something the client can see, run, or sign off on?

Check: No line in Section 1 starts with "met," "researched," or "explored."

Step 4: Set Colors Based on Evidence, Not Feelings

Status colors must be based on evidence: whether a milestone has slipped, how severe the current blocker is, and whether risk is rising or falling. You can set the rules yourself. Green when all milestones are on track; yellow when a milestone is at risk of slipping but mitigation measures are in place.

Red when a milestone will definitely slip, or when there is a blocker the team cannot resolve on its own. Under these rules, the email AI agent project is yellow this week: the real-user trial milestone is at risk because the team still cannot access the shipment tracking system, but a workaround is in place.

The Bricks warns that marking everything green while the team knows there are problems erodes trust and renders the report useless. On the contrary, stakeholders value honestly reported yellows and reds, because those give them a chance to step in before things get worse.

Check: The "reason" line next to each status color mentions a specific milestone, blocker, or risk.

Step 5: Raise Risks While They Are Still Small

Clients generally accept problems reported early, with clear mitigation measures, far better than last-minute surprises. So give the risk section a "trend" column, letting the client see a risk growing before it becomes an incident.

In the example, the main risk line could read: "Still no access to the shipment tracking system. Trend: rising, waiting for two weeks. Impact: the AI agent cannot answer order status questions for now. Mitigation: temporarily using daily data export files. Owner: client IT lead."

Do not pad this section to look busy, and do not leave it empty to look smooth. Three real risks beat ten vague ones.

Check: Every risk has a mitigation measure and a designated owner.

Step 6: Write the Decisions Section So the Client Can Decide on the Spot

Since so many projects stall on unresolved decisions, this section is what keeps the project moving. Everything the client needs to do should be gathered in one place, each item with an owner and a deadline. Decision requests in particular should state: what is being decided, who decides, the options, the deadline, and the cost of delay.

Applied to the email AI agent project:

| 决策 | 决策人 | 可选方案 | 截止时间 | 若延误 |
| Agent 应直接发送回复，还是只起草？ | 客服负责人 | A：只起草，由员工审核后发送。B：简单类型的邮件自动发送 | 周三 | 下周无法启动真实用户试用 |

Note the last column. Without it, decisions easily get pushed to the next week; with it, readers can see at a glance what hesitation costs the project. You can include the option recommended by the FDE team, but the choice remains with the client.

Check: The client can answer every line with just an "A" or a "B."

Step 7: Send Before the Meeting, Not During It

Edworking recommends publishing the report before the weekly meeting so stakeholders can read it in advance and come ready to decide. If the meeting is Monday afternoon, send it Friday afternoon or Monday morning. That way meeting time goes to Section 4 instead of reading Section 1 aloud word by word.

Check: After the meeting, update the "deadline" column or mark decisions as completed, then use the same document as the starting point for next week's report.

Mistakes That Render a Report Useless

The first mistake to avoid is changing the structure every week on the grounds that "a lot happened this week." The client has to hunt for information from scratch, and decisions get buried mid-paragraph. Equally harmful is scattering decision requests throughout the progress section, phrased as "it would be great if you could confirm."

And one more: keeping the status green for another week in the hope of fixing things in time. That is precisely the last-minute surprise the client wants least.

Putting This Skill on Your Resume and in Interviews

Simply Stakeholders holds that regular reporting keeps the right people informed, and systematic stakeholder management helps identify and mitigate risks. When reading FDE job descriptions, look for requirements about working with stakeholders or communicating with clients—that is exactly where this skill proves its value.

Don't write "strong communication skills" on your resume. Write a one-line description with an action and a result, for example: "Wrote a one-page weekly report for the client, flagging risks and open decisions, which unblocked a data access issue."

In interviews, be ready to describe a time when you proactively changed a status to yellow, and what happened afterward.

The code you write on-site at a client only creates value once someone agrees to let it run. The Friday report is where you win that agreement, so write it so the client can respond within a minute.
