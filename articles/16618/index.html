Your part in a cross-functional launch

FDE Interview Prep · 2026 · 2 min read

Summary

This is a practice interview prompt for FDE candidates on describing your role in a cross-functional product launch. Citing Ramp's engineering blog, it frames forward deployed engineering as spanning the sales funnel through implementation, rollout, and long-tail support, sitting between sales, engineering, and support. The article prescribes a five-step answer: summarize the launch in one sentence, describe your part through concrete artifacts (promise lists for sales, support runbooks, rollback plans), present one conflict with both sides' reasons, review launch day and what broke, and name what only you were holding. It warns against two traps: answering as a project-manager tour of meetings, and framing a conflict with a villain. Sample follow-up questions and common failure modes are included.

Key points

  • Five-step structure: launch summary, your artifacts, one conflict, launch day, what only you held.
  • Cite Ramp's blog: FDEs span sales, implementation, rollout, and support.
  • Prove ownership with artifacts like promise lists, runbooks, and rollback plans.
  • Avoid the project-manager tour and conflicts told with a villain.
  • Includes follow-up questions and common ways answers go wrong.

A practice prompt we wrote. No company or candidate report names it, so it carries no company tag.

How to answer

Ramp’s engineering blog says its team works with customers from when they are prospects in the sales funnel, through implementation and rollout, to long-tail support. Source 1Forward Deployed EngineeringPublisherRamp Builders (engineering blog)Source typecompany blog That work sits between the people who sell, build and support a product. Most of your answer belongs to your part, not the launch.

Build it in five steps:

  • The launch in one sentence. What shipped, to which customers, by what date, and what each function had to have ready.
  • Your part, as artifacts. Name what you made or decided, not the meetings you ran: the you agreed with engineering (what had to be true to ship, and what would make you hold the date), the list of what sales could and could not promise, the support runbook, the rollback plan. Name who owned the rest.
  • One conflict, both sides. A specific disagreement, such as sales promising a date or feature engineering hadn’t committed to, or support refusing to take tickets without training. Give each side’s reason, what you proposed, and who decided.
  • Launch day and after. What broke and who fixed it. Whether the first tickets went to support and were closed there, or came straight to you; if they came to you, what you changed so the next ones didn’t. Then one result you measured.
  • What only you were holding. The decision or context that would have slipped if you’d been out the week before launch, and whether it was written down. End with one thing you’d change, tied to what broke in step 4.

Quote the artifact: “I wrote the page sales used on calls: what the integration does on day one, what it doesn’t, and what to say when asked.”

The first trap is the project-manager tour: status meetings and a tracker, with nothing that was yours. The second is a conflict with a villain. If sales pushed, they had a quota and a customer asking; say so, then say what you did.

Follow-ups

What the interviewer may ask next, once your first answer is on the table.

  • What did sales want to promise that you would not let them, and how did they take it?
  • What was the first support ticket after launch, and who handled it?
  • If you had been out the week before launch, what would have slipped?

Where answers go wrong

  • Describes running status meetings and a shared tracker, work anyone could claim, and never names an artifact or decision that was theirs.
  • Picks a launch with no real conflict, or tells the conflict as sales or support getting in the way, with no account of their reasons.

Answer this in two minutes

Write the answer you would say out loud. The clock starts with your first word.

Illustrative answer about a fictional project

The launch was a two-way sync between our field-service scheduling product and the accounting system most of our customers used, released to every existing customer on one date in March with a sales push behind it. By that date engineering had to have the sync stable, sales had to know what they could promise, and support had to handle sync tickets without sending each one to engineering.
