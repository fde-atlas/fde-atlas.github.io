What Is Generative AI Implementation Effect Measurement? Explaining KPI Design and ROI Calculation Procedures from an FDE Perspective

Original title: 生成AI導入の効果測定とは？KPI設計とROI計算の手順をFDE視点で解説

FDE総研 · 2026-09-04 · 11 min read

Summary

This Japanese article explains, from a forward deployed engineer (FDE) perspective, how to measure the impact of generative AI adoption. It argues measurement should start with mapping specific workflows and recording pre-deployment baselines (time, volume, rework rate, subjective load), not with picking KPIs. KPIs are structured in three layers—time, quality, and money—plus usage (leading) and safety (guardrail) metrics. ROI calculation must include hidden costs like training, operations, and model-update revalidation, and use conservative conversion rates agreed with finance. A worked example shows roughly 27% ROI under stated assumptions. When results disappoint, the cause is usually poor task selection or process design, not model performance. The article ties this to FDE work, citing Palantir's bootcamp-style approach of getting production workflows running quickly.

Key points

  • Start measurement by mapping workflows and recording pre-deployment baselines.
  • Design KPIs in three layers: time, quality, and money, plus usage and safety metrics.
  • Include hidden costs (training, operations, revalidation) and conservative conversion rates in ROI.
  • A sample calculation shows about 27% ROI with clearly stated assumptions.
  • Poor results usually stem from task selection or process design, not model capability.

Where should you start when measuring the impact of generative AI adoption?

In practice, the fastest route is not choosing metrics but starting with an inventory of target tasks and recording pre-adoption baseline values.

What often happens in the field is a reversal of order: the tool is rolled out first, and only then comes the question "So, what were the results?"—leaving teams stuck. Generative AI does leave usage logs, but "how much time this task used to take" can never be measured after adoption. Impact measurement design should safely begin almost simultaneously with the adoption plan.

Define "which task and which process" before measuring

The unit of impact measurement is the process, not the department. Pick one recurring task and narrow the measurement scope to it.

At the granularity of "improving sales department productivity," no one can explain what actually improved. Narrow the measurement target down to processes with clear start and end points, such as "drafting proposals," "first-response handling of inquiry emails," or "summarizing and distributing meeting minutes." Once the process is set, the measurement unit (per item / per hour) follows automatically.

As a practical rule of thumb, choosing an initial target that is "frequent and recoverable even if it fails" tends to speed up adoption. Conversely, starting with high-risk tasks like contract review tends to increase verification workload and offset the benefits.

Always keep a baseline (pre-adoption current values)

Recording pre-adoption time requirements and quality for about two weeks makes post-adoption comparison dramatically easier.

You don't need to record many items. At minimum, the following four provide a foundation for comparison.

ItemDescriptionExample measurement method
① Time requiredWorking time per itemStopwatch / self-reporting
② Items processedMonthly number of items processedWork logs
③ Rework rateRate of redo occurrencesReview records
④ Subjective workloadPerceived workload of the person in charge (5-point scale)Simple survey

If stopwatch measurement is difficult, self-reporting by the person in charge is fine. What matters is continuing to measure the same way after adoption. If you change the measurement method midway, you can no longer tell whether the numbers moved due to "improvement" or "measurement method." It's unglamorous, but it's the part that matters most later.

Decide in advance when to stop measuring

Documenting evaluation timing and exit criteria before adoption reduces initiatives that linger with vague results.

Document in advance the evaluation timing (e.g., a short-term evaluation right after adoption and a mid-term evaluation a few months later) and the minimum thresholds to be met there. Deciding when to judge beforehand lets the field try things with confidence precisely because exit criteria exist. PoCs with postponed decisions piling up internally dulls the very next investment decision.

How should you design KPIs for impact measurement?

The basics are to build KPIs in three layers—"time," "quality," and "money"—and break them down to a granularity the field can input weekly.

The reason for splitting into three layers is that any single metric will inevitably distort somewhere. Track only time and quality drops; track only quality and verification workload balloons. In practice, "usage" as a leading indicator and "safety" to prevent deterioration are added to these three layers for management.

LayerExample metricsMeasurement methodUpdate frequency
Time (efficiency)Time required per item, monthly items processedWork logs / self-reportingWeekly
QualityRework rate, number of corrections, customer-reported issuesReview recordsWeekly
MoneyMonetary value of reduced hours, outsourcing cost savings, sales contributionAccounting data + conversion formulaMonthly
Usage (leading)Weekly active rate, uses per userTool usage logsWeekly
Safety (guardrails)Detected confidential information inputs, reports of inappropriate outputsAudit logs / report formsMonthly

Separate leading and lagging indicators

Separating leading indicators like usage rate from lagging indicators like hours and sales makes countermeasures concrete.

Sales and hours reduction take months to start moving. What to watch in the meantime are leading indicators such as weekly active rate and uses per user. If leading indicators aren't growing, discussing only lagging indicators yields no concrete actions.

Decide the assumptions for converting saved hours into money

Agree in advance among stakeholders on the hourly rate basis and what percentage of saved hours counts as monetary value.

Saved hours don't translate directly into profit. Only when the freed time is redirected to other value-added work does it gain monetary meaning. In practice, applying a conservative conversion rate such as "counting only a portion of saved hours as benefits" and clearly noting its rationale shortens discussions with the finance department. Presenting big numbers while leaving this vague loses trust all at once later.

Set quality and safety guardrail metrics

Without risk metrics for errors and information leaks in place simultaneously, you get distorted operations where only efficiency grows.

Guardrail metrics are not for "making things better" but for "confirming things haven't gotten worse." Is the rework rate higher than before adoption? Is confidential information input being detected? Just confirming monthly that these two remain flat significantly improves the quality of explanations to management.

How do you calculate the ROI of generative AI?

ROI is calculated as (reduction benefits + added benefits − total cost) ÷ total cost, and must always be presented with the target period and assumptions attached.

The formula itself is simple. The hard part is building consensus on what to put in the numerator and denominator.

Visible costs alone aren't enough

Unless you include not only license fees but also personnel costs for validation, training, and operations/maintenance, ROI will diverge from reality.

Cost categoryExamplesLikelihood of being overlooked
Direct costsAPI usage fees, SaaS licenses, additional storageLow
Build costsRequirements gathering, prompt design, development of internal system integrationsMedium
Training and adoption costsTraining, documentation, internal help desk supportHigh
Operations and maintenance costsMonitoring output quality, revalidation on model updates, log auditsHigh
Risk mitigation costsEstablishing information management rules, legal and security reviewsHigh

The last three are the easiest to miss. In particular, "revalidation when the model is updated" is often left out of first-year estimates, and it gradually takes its toll from the following fiscal year onward.

Estimate benefits conservatively, in phases

Presenting benefits not as maximum values but as both a conservative lower bound and an expected value makes it easier to gain stakeholder agreement.

Below is a sample calculation to illustrate the structure of the math (not actual results from a real company; all figures are hypothetical).

ItemAssumptionAnnualized
Target taskFirst-response handling of inquiries / 400 per month (assumed)—
Time saved20 minutes to 12 minutes per inquiry (8 minutes saved, assumed)640 hours (calculated: 400 inquiries × 8 minutes × 12 months)
Conversion rate40% of saved time counted as benefit (assumed)256 hours (calculated: 640 hours × 40%)
Hourly rateAssumed at 4,000 yenApprox. 1.02 million yen (calculated: 256 hours × 4,000 yen)
Total costLicense + build + operationsApprox. 800,000 yen
ROI(102 − 80) ÷ 80Approx. 27%

The advantage of this structure is that you can see at a glance which assumptions change the conclusion. If you can also present sensitivity analysis—such as "dropping the conversion rate from 40% to 20% makes ROI negative"—the discussion moves away from subjective arguments.

Also show the payback period

Along with ROI (as a percentage), stating how many months it takes to recoup the investment gives management the materials they need to decide.

For projects with high initial build costs, single-year ROI may be negative yet the investment can be recovered in subsequent years. By presenting the first year separately from later years and clearly labeling the project as "initial-investment type" or "quick-win type," it becomes easier to put it on the table for comparison.

What to review when benefits don't materialize

Most causes of missing benefits lie not in model performance but in the selection of target tasks and operational design.

Start by isolating the likely problem area based on symptoms.

SymptomLikely culpritFirst countermeasure
Not being used at allTouchpoints with the workflow, user pathwaysEmbed it into existing tools
Used once, then abandonedOutput quality, mismatched expectationsNarrow the use case and share good examples
Used but workload doesn't shrinkProcess design, review proceduresEliminate duplicate checks in downstream steps
Benefits vary wildly by individualKnowledge locked in individualsStandardize top users' prompts
Quality metrics worsenedRisk suitability of the target taskNarrow the scope of application, reinstate manual review

Suspect a mismatch in the target task

Tasks with weak benefits are often low-frequency, or involve judgment that is person-dependent and can't be defined by a standard answer.

Streamlining a task that occurs only two or three times a month yields very little annual time savings. Since the magnitude of the benefit is determined by "time saved per instance × number of instances," tasks with small instance counts may need to be excluded from measurement altogether.

Break down usage rate to find the bottleneck

When you break usage rate down into "aware, tried, use weekly," you can see at which stage users are getting stuck.

If users stall at "aware," it's an awareness and pathway problem; if they stall at "tried," it's a first-experience quality problem. These two require completely different countermeasures. Looking only at a single number—company-wide average usage rate—hides this difference.

When it's used but produces no benefits, change the process

If people are redoing all of the generative AI's output from scratch, you need to change the design of the process itself, not the tool.

What's often seen in the field is a state where, in addition to the generative AI's draft, the conventional review remains in place, so a step has simply been added. To realize benefits, you need to explicitly document "which checks may be skipped." This is not a technical matter but a matter of designing authority and responsibility. My impression is that whether you can make this decision is the biggest fork in the road in practice.

How does benefit measurement connect to FDE practice?

An FDE is a role responsible for defining benefit metrics at the customer's site and embedding the tracking of those metrics into daily operations.

An FDE (Forward Deployed Engineer) works on-site with customers to identify problems, build working software, and make it stick in the field. Benefit measurement is not a "reporting task" outside that job—it is a core design target.

Benefit measurement is a primary FDE deliverable

An FDE's job extends beyond building something that works to sharing evidence of the benefits with the customer.

FDEs who work on-site with customers are in a position to directly observe business workflows. In other words, they are one of the few roles that can capture a baseline. Standing on-site on day one with a stopwatch, measuring existing task times—it's unglamorous, but whether you do this changes how persuasive you'll be at contract renewal six months later.

An FDE's remit extends to not simply accepting the KPIs written in the requirements document, but verifying "can this metric really be measured weekly in the field" and rebuilding it if needed.

What can be learned from Palantir's approach

Palantir advocates an approach of getting into production workflows quickly, confirming value, and then scaling.

Initiatives like Palantir's publicly available AIP Bootcamp aim to work with real data and run production-equivalent workflows within a short period of just a few days. Rather than iterating through a long-term PoC, put something small into production and quickly confirm whether it delivers value. This order is also rational from a benefit-measurement standpoint, because you can't measure the benefits of something that isn't running.

The same approach can be applied on the ground at Japanese companies as well. For services like VACAN that handle facility congestion in real time, changes in wait times and visitor stays before and after an initiative are recorded as data. Starting from areas where existing operational data is already being collected greatly reduces the initial cost of measuring effectiveness.

Track record that FDE candidates can start building today

A record of completing an initiative through to effectiveness measurement for a single task at your own company becomes a strong first-hand experience you can talk about in FDE selection processes.

The number of people with experience "having used generative AI" has grown. What makes the difference is whether you can select a target task, take a baseline, design KPIs, and speak to results with numbers a few months later. Even if the effect turns out to be negative, if you can explain the reason in terms of process design, that is still a highly valuable accomplishment.

What is required of an FDE is the ability to connect both technical skill and business understanding. Effectiveness measurement sits exactly at that intersection, and you can start practicing with even a single small internal task.
