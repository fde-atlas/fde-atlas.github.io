FDE Career Path Map: From Your First On-Site Delivery to Delivery Lead

Original title: FDE 职业路径地图：从第一份驻场交付到交付负责人

FDE中国 · 2026-09-07 · 11 min read

Summary

This FDEChina article replaces the vague years-of-experience narrative for FDE careers with a locatable path map. Its core framework divides growth into four stages by "delegable delivery radius": entry (owning a module), independent delivery (owning a client), multi-project (reuse and leverage), and team lead (owning the organization), each with capability priorities, milestone outcomes, and common pitfalls. Stage is defined by who covers your failures, whether your work is reused, and how close you are to client decisions—not by title. The article also frames lateral exits (returning to product, moving to pre-sales, founding a startup) as legitimate paths the role enables, and adds China-specific context: high on-site ratios, scattered job titles, and fewer chances for standardized artifacts, requiring deliberate compensation.

Key points

  • Four stages defined by delegable delivery radius: module, client, multi-project, organization.
  • Stage is set by who covers failures, reuse of your work, and proximity to client decisions—not title.
  • People stuck at a stage usually lack basic habits like documentation and reporting, not technical skill.
  • Lateral exits to product, pre-sales, or startups are framed as legitimate outcomes, not escapes.
  • China-specific: high on-site ratio, varied titles, fewer standardized artifacts—compensate proactively.

If you ask ten FDEs (Forward Deployed Engineers) how to get promoted, you'll most likely get ten different answers. That's not surprising: FDE is a hybrid role where engineering ability, customer management, and scenario judgment are all mixed together, and traditional engineering career ladders don't map onto it directly. But once you filter out the noise, this path actually has a fairly clear structure—by "radius of delivery you can be entrusted with," it divides into the entry stage, the independent delivery stage, the multi-project stage, and the team-lead stage, each with its own capability priorities and signature achievements, plus lateral exits into product, pre-sales, and entrepreneurship. This article draws the full map and gives you a method for judging which stage you're at. If you're still unsure what this role looks like day to day, we suggest reading A Day in the Life of an FDE first, then coming back to the path.

Why FDEs Need Their Own Growth Map

First, let's clarify the axes of this map. The traditional engineering ladder is measured mainly by technical depth and code output: how complex a system you can write, how hard a problem you can solve. The FDE ladder is measured differently: it is "how large a scope you can take end-to-end responsibility for the customer's final outcome." This sentence has two keywords. Outcome means you're assessed on success or failure as perceived by the customer, not on how many development tasks you completed. Radius means growth is essentially the scope you can be entrusted with, expanding from a single module to a project, then to multiple projects and a team.

Viewed through these axes, many common confusions get answers. For example, "why am I still seen as a junior after three years of writing integration code"—because your radius is still stuck at the module level, and when something goes wrong on the customer side, you're still not the first person they turn to. Another example: "why can some people go on-site independently after two years"—it's not that they write the most code, but that they built up those "non-coding abilities" like scope management and expectation management early on. The site's FDE Capability Model breaks these capabilities into specific dimensions; this article covers how they're sequenced over time.

Entry Stage (0-2 Years): From Being Able to Work to Being Entrusted with a Module

The typical form of the entry stage is: you join a delivery team and participate in concrete projects under the guidance of a senior FDE. Daily work falls roughly into a few categories: writing customer-side integration code, handling issues reported by the customer, preparing demo environments, maintaining eval cases, and turning verbal information from customer meetings into written records. The core task at this stage is not to produce a spectacular solution, but to build two foundations: solid engineering fundamentals, and the habit of translating vague problems into engineering tasks.

Engineering fundamentals are easily underestimated. On-site at customers, the gap between junior FDEs often shows up in the plainest places: given a bug report, can you produce a reproduction path within an hour; when fixing an issue, can you state the root cause, the impact scope, and the fix clearly in one go; given a vague task, can you proactively ask about boundaries before starting. None of these habits is sophisticated, but they determine whether the team dares to hand you more.

As for signature achievements, there are usually two signals that the entry stage is ending. First, you have independently owned the end-to-end delivery of at least one functional module: from requirements clarification and solution design to development, integration, and customer acceptance, led by you throughout, with the senior FDE only reviewing. Second, the customer's point of contact starts bypassing your team lead to raise requirements and report issues directly to you—this shows that in the customer's eyes you have become "a deliverer they can talk to," not just "a member of the team."

The two most common pitfalls at this stage: First, treating yourself as a pure developer—sitting on-site but waiting for requirements to be assigned, never proactively observing how customers use the system, ending up with zero growth in understanding of customer scenarios after two years. Second, failing to build documentation habits: what the customer said, what you promised, how problems were fixed—all scattered across chat logs and your head. Without accumulating this information, you don't even have material for retrospectives, let alone a portfolio—the FDE Portfolio Guide discusses how process records are precisely a deliverer's greatest asset.

Independent Delivery Stage: One Person Responsible for an Entire Customer

The independent delivery stage is the watershed from "executor" to "operator." The typical form is you on-site alone, or leading a complete project as the technical owner. Your output is no longer defined by a task list but by customer-side results: whether the project launches on time, whether the system is stable, whether the customer is satisfied, whether they renew and expand. The capabilities to build at this stage are mainly not technical but three kinds of management.

The first is scope management. Customer demands naturally diverge while your resources are naturally limited; the daily work of an independent deliverer is converging between the two: what goes into this phase, what goes into the next, and what gets a clear no. The difficulty is not drawing the line itself but explaining the reasoning so the customer accepts it—which requires understanding the customer's business priorities, not just technical feasibility. The second is expectation management, whose core is reporting discipline: progress, risks, and support needed—three threads delivered to the customer and internally on a fixed rhythm, with bad news shared early. A widely observed pattern in the industry is that the leading cause of failed delivery projects is not technical failure but risks being concealed until the last moment. The third is crisis handling: when a major issue occurs on-site, can you organize the fix, manage the customer's emotions, and sync information back internally—all three in parallel without chaos.

As for signature achievements, the hardest evidence at this stage is: you have gone through at least one full project lifecycle—from pre-sales support or project initiation, to launch and acceptance, to stable operations or handoff; see The FDE Project Lifecycle for the stage breakdown. Next is renewal or expansion: the customer voting with renewals and new requirements is more honest than any performance score. Then, assets: you should have accumulated your own issue library, eval sets, and document templates—they are the raw capital for your next stage.

To judge whether you've completed this stage, use two plain questions: when something big goes wrong on-site, do you still default to expecting headquarters to send someone as a fallback; and does someone at the customer forward your weekly project report directly to their superior. If both answers are "yes," you're at the upper edge of this stage.

Multi-Project Stage: From Deliverer to Operator of Delivery Assets

The typical form of the multi-project stage is: you handle two or three projects simultaneously, or mentor one or two newcomers. On the surface it's just more work, but in substance the nature of the work changes—your attention shifts from "investing in one customer" to being "a resource contested by multiple projects," and you're forced to answer a new question: which things are worth doing yourself, and which must be delegated to others or turned into process.

The capability priorities at this stage are reuse and leverage. You'll quickly notice that project requirements are highly similar: all need integration acceptance, all need eval sets, all need weekly reports and issue lists. An ordinary deliverer repeats the labor one by one; a qualified senior deliverer abstracts the commonalities: a reusable integration solution, an eval methodology template, an onboarding guide for newcomers. The standard for judging leverage is straightforward—has your accumulated asset been used by a second project, and does someone maintain it when you're not around. What only you use isn't an asset; it's a backup.

For milestone outcomes, this stage typically maps to three signals. First, you have experience taking a newcomer from onboarding to independently owning a module, and this mentoring approach has been reused more than once. Second, you can make priority trade-offs across multiple projects and articulate the reasoning clearly to both clients and internal teams—the hardest part of the multi-project stage is not scheduling, but making every client whose priority got lowered still feel respected. Third, when you take a week off, none of the projects collapse; this is the best test of whether your leverage actually exists.

A common mistake is turning multi-project work into multi-threaded firefighting: switching between several clients every day, rushing wherever the fire breaks out. It looks exhausting, but in reality you accumulate no reusable assets and develop no one who can share the load. After six months you'll find you've become a single point of failure for every project—a drain on you personally and a hidden risk for the organization. Another mistake is focusing on leverage while neglecting clients: no matter how beautifully designed your reusable assets are, if delivery quality on current projects slips, the loss of trust will immediately wipe it out.

Team Lead Stage: What a Delivery Lead Actually Manages

At the team lead stage, what you're evaluated on becomes the organization. This role typically manages three things at once, and none can be missing. The first is people's growth: what kind of people to hire, which projects to assign to each person, when to step in when someone is stuck, and when to delegate when someone is ready. A delivery team's output is highly dependent on people, and the lead's core job is to turn "people who can deliver independently" into a stable supply for the team, rather than remaining the biggest producer yourself.

The second is the health of the project portfolio. A single project is judged on delivery; a portfolio is judged on structure: which projects are draining the team, which are accumulating assets, which types of clients are worth investing people in, and which types of requirements should be steered toward standardized products. You need to start viewing delivery over a longer horizon—some projects don't make money in the current period, but the industry and scenario understanding they bring is ammunition for the team's next deal; some projects look great on paper while dragging the team into a custom development assembly line. There's no formula for these judgments; they rely on the density of projects you've delivered yourself.

The third is organization-level client relationships. At the independent delivery stage you cultivate trust with your point of contact; at the team lead stage you need to cultivate multi-layered relationships within the client's organization: business stakeholders, technical decision-makers, procurement, and compliance. The client leadership's trust in you determines whether your team can land bigger engagements. For how to staff and structure this role, there's a dedicated article on FDE team design on this site that can be read alongside this one.

The most common failure mode at this stage is path dependence: the lead personally jumps in to fight fires, the team gets used to "the boss will always bail us out," and as a result a second or third person capable of independent delivery never emerges. Another class of problem is homogeneous hiring—only recruiting people who resemble your own profile, leaving the team collectively missing an entire capability dimension.

Lateral Moves: Back to Product, Into Pre-Sales, or to Entrepreneurship

This map is a web, not a ladder. The FDE capability structure naturally leads to three lateral exits, all legitimate paths enabled by the role itself, not escapes from it.

The first is returning to product or engineering headquarters. The FDE is one of the most client-proximate technical roles in a company—you know how requirements mutate in real environments and how much hidden cost it takes to land a feature. Returning to headquarters to do product or core engineering with this judgment gives you a layer of "landing intuition" that people who never left headquarters lack. The right timing is usually at or beyond the independent delivery stage, when you find you enjoy building big products more than serving individual clients.

The second is moving into pre-sales or a Solutions Engineer role. The two roles overlap heavily in capabilities; for a detailed boundary analysis see The Difference Between FDE and Solutions Engineer. The main difference is that the focus shifts from delivery to winning deals, and the work rhythm shifts from project cycles to sales cycles. Deep understanding of client scenarios is the FDE's biggest chip when moving into pre-sales—you can describe the pitfalls of a solution in real environments, which is more persuasive than beautiful slides.

The third is starting a company or joining an early-stage one. Early-stage companies in toB and AI application directions need exactly the hybrid profile of "someone who can sell the thing and deliver it," and the FDE is almost tailor-made for that position. But be clear about the cost: early-stage company delivery often has no team or methodology to fall back on, and your independent delivery capability will be tested at amplified intensity. The reverse transition paths—entering FDE from engineering, pre-sales, consulting, or implementation—follow the same logic; there's a dedicated article on this site, see Four Paths for Engineers Transitioning to FDE.

The Real Differences Between Domestic and Overseas Paths

Being an FDE in China versus abroad means noticeably different growth soil, and understanding these differences helps you calibrate expectations. Based on general industry observation, FDE roles at leading overseas tech companies are recruited in a more standardized way, career narratives center on impact and judgment, project forms are relatively diverse, and hybrid remote-onsite arrangements are common; in promotion narratives, "drove product improvements" carries considerable weight.

The domestic situation has several characteristics. First, the roles are more concentrated in LLM companies, toB software vendors, and vanguard teams on large digital transformation projects, and the title isn't necessarily FDE—Solutions Engineers, AI Application Engineers, and delivery specialists may all be doing FDE work; reading the actual responsibilities in the JD is more reliable than reading the title. Second, the proportion of onsite work is higher, especially with large clients in finance and manufacturing, where you'll enter the client's rhythms, processes, and compliance constraints earlier. Third, evaluation is closer to project outcomes and client satisfaction, with renewals and repeat purchases generally carrying significant weight in performance reviews.

These differences have real effects on growth. The advantage of the domestic path: you encounter hard constraints like on-premise deployment and data-stays-in-domain earlier, and your client management skills get high-intensity training; the cost is fewer opportunities for standardized accumulation—many projects end as custom work, and reusable artifacts require stronger initiative to emerge. The overseas path is usually more systematic in methodology and productization feedback. If you're an FDE in China, it's advisable to consciously hedge your weaknesses: structure the artifacts from every project so they can be reused in the next one—this is also an important training method for multi-project stage capabilities.

How to Tell Which Stage You're At

Finally, here's a self-assessment method. Your stage isn't defined by your title, but by the answers to three questions.

  • Who covers for your failures? If someone cleans up after you when things go wrong, you're at the entry stage; if you cover for yourself, you're at the independent delivery stage; if you have mechanisms and people to cover for you, you're at the multi-project stage or beyond.
  • Is your work being reused by others? If only you use it, you're in the first two stages; if your templates, methods, and components are being used and maintained by others, you've entered the stage of managing assets.
  • How close are you to client decision-making? If you only talk at the execution level, you're at the entry stage; if you jointly manage scope and expectations with your point of contact, you're at the independent delivery stage; if you talk directly with multiple layers of the client's organization, you're at the team lead stage.

Against these three questions, most people's stage is immediately clear. If you've been stuck at the entry stage for a long time, what you need to build first isn't technical skill but basic habits like documentation, reporting, and proactive observation; if you're stuck at the leap from independent delivery to multi-project, focus on checking your reusable assets and your track record of developing people. For capability details, calibrate item by item against the FDE Capability Model.

Summary and Next Steps

To compress this map into one sentence: FDE growth is the process of the delivery radius you can be entrusted with expanding from one module, to one client, to multiple projects and a team—each stage has verifiable milestone outcomes rather than automatic promotion by tenure. At the same time, it's a web, not a ladder—returning to product, moving into pre-sales, and entrepreneurship are all legitimate paths this role has enabled.

Suggested next steps: if you're currently employed, use the three questions above to locate yourself, then address your weakest item against the capability model; if you're job hunting, put interview prep on your agenda, starting with Complete FDE Interview Preparation; if you want to systematically browse career content, enter through the FDE Knowledge Guide and the Career Channel. The value of a path map isn't in bookmarking it—it's in finding your current position right now and identifying the next step to take.
