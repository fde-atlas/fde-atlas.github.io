How to Measure Forward Deployed Engineering

Reveneau · 2026-09-08 · 11 min read

Summary

Reveneau argues that forward deployed engineering should not be measured with professional-services metrics like utilisation and billable hours, since these reward endless engagements—the model's main failure mode. The article proposes four core metrics: time from contract signature to production, share of engagements reaching production, revenue per embedded engineer over time, and capabilities returned to the product. It also recommends tracking handover completion (verified a quarter later) and shared-versus-custom code share. The litmus test: if a metric would look better on an engagement that never ends, it is the wrong metric. Citing MIT NANDA's finding that 95% of generative AI pilots show no P&L impact, it frames a strong production rate as a key sales asset, and invokes DORA research to argue speed and stability rise together through eval-driven verification.

Key points

  • Four core metrics: signature-to-production time, production rate, revenue per engineer, capabilities returned to product.
  • Utilisation and billable hours reward keeping engineers on accounts and should never be targets.
  • Track failure causes separately: complex environments point to discovery; stalled approvals point to sales.
  • Handover completion must be checked a quarter later, not just on the scheduled date.
  • DORA research shows speed and stability rise together via verification, not caution.

Key takeaways

  • Four metrics: signature-to-production time, share of engagements reaching production, revenue per embedded engineer over time, and capabilities returned to the product.
  • Do not measure utilisation. It rewards keeping an engineer on an account, which is the failure mode of the whole model.
  • Signature-to-production should fall across successive deployments. That is the number a customer feels.
  • DORA's long-running research finds the strongest teams release more often and cause fewer failures, so speed and stability rise together here too.

Most measurement of embedded engineering functions is borrowed from professional services, which is why it drives the wrong behaviour. Utilisation and billable hours were built to run a business that sells hours, and importing them unchanged into a function that is supposed to build a platform imports the wrong incentive along with the spreadsheet. Here is what to track instead.

The four that work

Time from contract signature to production. Not from the start of work. From signature, because the gap between contract signature and somebody actually starting is real and it is yours to fix.

This is the number a customer experiences, and it should fall across successive deployments as the platform takes in what earlier ones learned. A flat signature-to-production time over eight deployments means nothing is being reused. It is also the number a16z's Marc Andrusko suggests investors ask about directly: how many engineer-months from contract signature to production.

Share of engagements that reach production. Count started engagements and count the ones running in production for real users. This is the honest version of a success rate, and it should be embarrassing at first.

Compare it against the context: MIT NANDA reported in July 2025 that 95 percent of generative AI pilots produced no measurable profit-and-loss impact, from 52 executive interviews, 153 leader surveys and 300 public deployments. If your rate is well above that, it is your strongest sales asset and you should be able to prove it.

Track this rate by cause of failure, not just as a single percentage, because the two most common causes call for different fixes. An engagement that stalls because the customer's environment turned out to be more complex than scoped points at the discovery phase needing more time before commitments are made. An engagement that stalls because internal approval never came points at a sales or contracting problem that has nothing to do with the engineering. Lumping both into one number tells you that something is wrong without telling you which part of the organization needs to change.

Revenue per embedded engineer, over time. The business-model measure. It should rise, because the platform takes in custom work and each engineer handles more accounts. Its own page covers the detail, and the summary is that flat revenue per engineer with growing custom work means you are running a services business.

Capabilities returned to the product. Count them, per period, with a name attached. This is the output of the productization loop and the only direct measure of whether the margin you spent bought anything lasting.

The three that look useful and are not

Utilisation. The standard professional services metric and the most damaging one here. It rewards keeping engineers on accounts, which is the endless engagement. A function at 95 percent utilisation is a function with no time to generalise, which means no reusable work is building up. If you track it at all, track it as an upper limit, not a target.

Billable hours. The same underlying problem, and it also pays the vendor to be slow. How to price covers what that does to behaviour.

Customer satisfaction alone. A customer whose engineers never learned the system and who now depends on you is frequently delighted. Satisfaction is worth knowing and it does not distinguish a successful deployment from a comfortable dependency. Pair it with whether the customer's own engineer has released a change unaided.

Lines of code shipped, or story points closed. These measure activity, not the two things that matter: whether the work reaches production and whether it generalises. An engineer who writes a large amount of custom code that never gets reused looks productive on this metric and is quietly building the dependency problem this whole model exists to avoid. If a function tracks a metric like this at all, it should be a secondary check on individual output, never a target reported upward as a measure of the function's health.

Two more worth tracking quietly

Share of shared versus custom code per deployment. Should trend toward shared. If the custom share grows with each account, custom work at customer sites is growing faster than the platform.

Handover completion rate. The share of engagements where the customer's team took the system over on the agreed date and still runs it a quarter later. This is the metric that detects the dependency failure mode, and almost nobody tracks it.

Measure it properly, because a shallow version of this metric hides the exact problem it is meant to catch. "The handover happened" is not the same claim as "the customer's team is running the system unaided a quarter later." A handover can happen on schedule, on paper, with a training session and a document, and the customer's team can still be calling the vendor every week months later because nobody actually transferred the operational knowledge. The quarter-later check is what separates a real handover from a scheduled event with the same name. Handover and exit covers what the test for a real handover looks like.

What good measurement discipline looks like over a full year

A function that has this right produces a short, consistent report every quarter rather than a different set of numbers each time somebody asks how things are going. The four core metrics appear in the same format every quarter, tracked as a line on a chart rather than restated as a fresh snapshot, so a reader can see the trend without needing last quarter's report open next to this one.

The discipline that makes this possible is deciding the metrics and their definitions before the first deployment, not after the first awkward conversation about whether the function is working. A definition changed midway through the year to make a number look better is the single fastest way to lose the credibility that makes any of this reporting worth doing. If a definition genuinely needs to change, say so explicitly in the report the quarter it changes, and show both the old and new numbers side by side for at least one quarter so the trend line is not silently broken.

Do not trade speed for stability

Worth stating because the instinct in an embedded engagement is to slow down for safety when working inside somebody else's production environment.

Google's DORA research, which has studied software delivery for over a decade, finds that the strongest teams release more often and cause fewer failures at the same time. Speed and stability rise together rather than trading off. The mechanism is the verification, not caution.

In this context that means the way to move quickly inside a customer's environment is better checks rather than fewer changes. Generated code is not secure by default, so if a model is writing it, the checks have to be derived from the specification and graded independently of the model that produced the code. That is eval-driven development, and it is what makes a fast embedded engagement safe rather than reckless.

What to report, and to whom

To the board or leadership: signature-to-production time, share reaching production, revenue per engineer, capabilities returned. Four numbers, tracked as a trend rather than a snapshot.

To the customer: what was released this week, progress against the production date, and an honest, current list of what has been discovered that changes the plan. Not utilisation, not hours.

To yourself, weekly: the time split per engineer against the roughly 20 percent customer-facing and 80 percent building that First Round Review describes. It is the earliest warning that an engagement is going wrong, and it moves before any of the other outcome metrics on this page do.

The one-line test

If your metrics would look better on an engagement that never ended, they are simply the wrong metrics to be using at all.

Every measure on the recommended list gets worse when an engagement goes on too long: signature-to-production rises, revenue per engineer stops rising, handover completion falls. Every measure on the discouraged list gets better. That is the whole reason to choose between them deliberately.

Run the test against your own current reporting before adopting anything on this page. If the metrics your function reports today would all look fine, or even flattering, on a customer who has been dependent on the same two embedded engineers for years with no handover in sight, that reporting is measuring the wrong things, whatever it happens to say this quarter.

Best for

  • Choosing the metrics for a new embedded engineering function
  • Replacing professional services metrics inherited from a services organization
  • Reporting the function's value to leadership

Avoid if

  • You need the pricing model rather than the measurement set

Check before you decide

  • Confirm utilisation is not a target, and ideally not tracked at all
  • Measure from signature to production rather than from the start of work
  • Track handover completion a quarter after the handover date
  • Apply the test: would these metrics look better on an engagement that never ended

Common questions

What should you measure in a forward deployed engineering function?

Four things. Time from contract signature to production, the share of engagements that reach production, revenue per embedded engineer tracked over time, and the number of capabilities returned to the product with names attached. Track all four as trends rather than snapshots.

Why should you not measure utilisation?

Because it rewards keeping engineers on accounts, which is the endless engagement, the main way this model fails. A function at 95 percent utilisation has no time to generalise, so no reusable work builds up. If you track it at all, treat it as an upper limit rather than a target.

Why measure from signature rather than the start of work?

Because the gap between a contract being signed and somebody actually starting is real, it is yours to fix, and measuring from the start of work hides it. Signature-to-production is also the number a16z suggests investors ask about directly, and it is what the customer experiences.

Is customer satisfaction a good measure?

On its own, no. A customer whose engineers never learned the system and who now depends on you is frequently delighted. Satisfaction does not distinguish a successful deployment from a comfortable dependency, so pair it with whether the customer's own engineer has released a change unaided.

Should you slow down to be safe in a customer's production environment?

The evidence says no. Google's DORA research finds the strongest teams release more often and cause fewer failures at the same time, so speed and stability rise together. The mechanism is verification rather than caution, which means better checks rather than fewer changes.

What is the simplest test of whether your metrics are right?

Ask whether they would look better on an engagement that never ended. Signature-to-production, revenue per engineer and handover completion all get worse when an engagement goes on too long. Utilisation and billable hours get better. That difference is the whole reason to choose deliberately.

How to price forward deployed engineering

There are four ways to price embedded engineering: time and materials, fixed scope, outcome, and bundled into a licence. Each produces different behaviour, and the behaviour matters more than the rate. Billable hours pay the vendor to be slow and to keep the engagement going. Fixed scope pays them to argue about scope. Outcome pricing aligns both sides and is hard to write. When AWS announced its own forward deployed engineering organization it said the work would be structured around shared goals and business results rather than billable hours, which is a standard to ask of every vendor.

Outcome-based pricing for embedded engineering

An outcome you cannot measure on the day of signature is a hope with a payment schedule attached. A workable outcome passes three tests: it is observable rather than inferred, somebody named takes the measurement, and neither party can change the definition of success alone. The most reliable outcome for an embedded engagement is the least exciting one, which is a defined system running in production for real users by a named date. Business-metric outcomes sound better and fail more often, because neither side fully controls them.

What forward deployed engineering costs

Two compensation figures for this role are traceable to a source. Levels.fyi reports Palantir forward deployed engineer total compensation in the United States with a median of $278,000 and a range from $185,000 to $631,000, read on 8 September 2026. Across 1,000 job postings analysed by Revealera and published in November 2025, the median advertised salary was $173,816. Everything else published is unsourced. Add benefits, travel and unbilled time to one of those two to get your input cost, then compare it against the cost of the deployment not happening.

Revenue per forward deployed engineer

Revenue per embedded engineer, tracked over time, is the single number that tells you which business you are in. It has to rise. When the platform takes in what engineers learned at customer sites, each engineer handles more accounts and the number rises. If it stays flat while custom work accumulates, you are running a services business that has not admitted it yet. The benchmark for what a rising curve looks like is the previous generation: ServiceNow's gross margin at IPO was 63.2 percent and Workday's 54.1 percent, reaching 79 and 75 percent by 2024.

The contract for an embedded engagement

Before an outside engineer touches your repository, six things belong in writing: IP assignment on creation, scoped repository access rather than organization-wide, production access as a named exception with an audit trail, a production date, a handover date with a defined artefact, and subcontractor binding. The one people get wrong most often is the first: an NDA covers secrecy and does not assign ownership of what the vendor creates. Those are two separate clauses and you need both.
