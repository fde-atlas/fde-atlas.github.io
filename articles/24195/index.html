Comparing FDE Implementation Case Studies | Structure, Timeline, Results, and the 4 Stages to In-House Development

Original title: FDE導入事例を比較｜体制・期間・成果と内製化までの4段階

FDE総研 · 2026-11-06 · 10 min read

Summary

This article from FDE Soken (run by VACAN Technologies) proposes a framework for comparing FDE adoption case studies. Instead of sorting cases by industry, it recommends three axes: where data resides, distance to decision-makers, and outcome metrics. It breaks adoption into four stages—field research, prototyping, production rollout, and internalization—identifying the prototype-to-production transition as the biggest hurdle since security and permission requirements surge there. It also maps how company size, in-house engineers, and English capability change the approach, contrasts FDE with SIer outsourcing, IT consulting, and internal builds, lists four conditions making FDE unsuitable, and suggests estimating cost by scope of external reliance rather than published figures.

Key points

  • Compare FDE cases by data location, decision-maker distance, and metrics—not industry.
  • Adoption runs through four stages: field research, prototyping, production, internalization.
  • The prototype-to-production shift is the riskiest point; involve IT/security teams early.
  • FDE fits vague requirements; fixed-spec projects suit SIer outsourcing better.
  • Cost should be estimated by which stages are outsourced, not by public case figures.

FDE (Forward Deployed Engineer) is a role made widely known by Palantir as the core of its customer deployment model. FDEs embed themselves in the customer's workplace and take end-to-end responsibility from requirements definition through implementation to operational adoption. In this article, FDE Soken (operated by VACAN Technologies, Inc.) presents the comparison criteria it uses when reading FDE implementation case studies. It also organizes, for both decision-makers and engineers aspiring to become FDEs, what happens at each of the four stages of "field investigation → prototyping → production rollout → in-house development" and where failures are most likely to occur.

How Should FDE Implementation Case Studies Be Compared by Industry and Challenge?

FDE Soken recommends comparing FDE implementation case studies not by "industry name" but along three axes: "where the data resides, distance from decision-makers, and success metrics." The way FDSEs work, as introduced in Palantir's official blog, is also premised on "staying close to field-level challenges" regardless of industry.

Case study articles are often organized by industry, such as "FDE implementation in manufacturing" or "AI adoption in finance," but whether they apply to your company cannot be judged from the industry name alone. Even within manufacturing, a company whose data is scattered across factory PLCs and one whose data is consolidated in core systems require completely different work from the FDE in the first month.

The Three Comparison Axes: "Data, Decision-Makers, and Success Metrics"

When comparing FDE implementation case studies, reorganizing them along the following three axes rather than by industry makes the differences from your own company concrete.

Comparison axisWhat to look atQuestion to ask when reading a case study
Where data residesConsolidated in core systems / scattered across field equipment, paper, and ExcelDid the company in the case study start with data integration, or could it prototype with existing data?
Distance from decision-makersField leads hold the budget / headquarters approval is requiredWho decided to move to production, and in how many days, after seeing the prototype results?
Success metricsField KPIs such as labor savings, inventory, and utilization rates / abstract goals like "DX promotion"Were the metrics for measuring success defined before implementation?

According to FDE Soken, case studies that match your company on two of the three axes are worth referencing. For case studies that match on only one axis, it is safer not to imitate the approach directly, even if the industry is the same.

By Challenge Type, "Where FDEs Are Effective" Falls into Three Categories

FDEs are most effective for challenges whose requirements can only be finalized in the field. By challenge type, the FDE's role falls into the following three categories.

  • Business visualization type: Gathering scattered data to inform field-level decisions (e.g., understanding inventory, equipment, and staffing status)
  • Decision support type: Adding predictions and rules to visualized data to reduce decision-making effort
  • Business automation type: Delegating the decisions themselves to systems or AI agents, with humans handling exceptions

From VACAN's practical experience, projects that start directly at stage 3 tend to stall because field-level exception handling exceeds expectations. Reading case studies with the assumption that stages proceed in the order 1 → 2 → 3 makes it easier to correctly understand "why this company achieved results." Specific Palantir Foundry implementation case studies are organized in Foundry Implementation Case Study Comparison, and its deployment in Japan in Palantir Case Studies in Japan.

What Happens at Each Stage of FDE Implementation: "Field Investigation → Prototyping → Production Rollout → In-House Development"?

FDE Soken divides FDE implementation into four stages and recommends deciding in advance the "conditions for moving to the next stage" at each stage. Palantir's Foundry documentation also describes the process from data integration through application building and operations as a staged flow.

The purpose of dividing into stages is not so much to set timeframes as to quickly identify "why progress has stalled." By establishing a decision point at the end of each stage, you can avoid situations where "who is actually going to use this?" becomes an issue just before production rollout.

Deliverables and Conditions for Progressing at Each of the 4 Stages

StageFDE's main workDeliverablesConditions for progressing
① Field investigationBusiness interviews, confirming where data resides, identifying stakeholdersBusiness flow diagrams, data inventory, prioritized challengesHypotheses to validate through prototyping are narrowed down to 1–2
② PrototypingQuickly build screens or analyses that run on real data and have the field use themWorking prototype, record of field feedbackThe field lead decides they "want to keep using it," and success metrics are agreed upon
③ Production rolloutPermissions, auditing, incident response, automation of data updatesProduction environment, operational procedures, monitoring mechanismsThe field uses it in daily operations, and rules for exception handling are defined
④ In-house developmentHandover to internal engineers and business owners, trainingHandover materials, internal ownership structureThe internal team can perform maintenance and add features on its own

Timelines vary greatly depending on the organization and the challenge, so FDE Soken does not provide a uniform guideline. Instead, we recommend recording internally how long it takes to meet the "conditions for progressing" at each stage. This makes it immediately clear which condition is blocking progress when things fall behind schedule.

The Transition from Prototyping to Production Rollout Is the Biggest Hurdle

Of the four stages, projects most often stall at the transition from ② prototyping to ③ production rollout. Prototypes are evaluated simply by showing "something that works," but production rollout brings a sudden influx of requirements that were unnecessary at the prototype stage, such as the IT department's security standards, permission design, and incident communication protocols.

From a VACAN practical standpoint, we believe that involving even one member of the information systems department in reviews from the prototyping stage onward reduces rework when moving to production. How to conduct field research is covered in detail in Field Interview Practices, and handover is covered in Designing System Handover.

Does the FDE implementation approach change depending on company size, in-house engineering structure, and English proficiency?

FDE Soken organizes the idea that the "starting point" and "endpoint of in-housing" of FDE implementation change based on three conditions: company size, presence of in-house engineers, and English proficiency. The IPA's DX White Paper 2023 also cites the shortage of DX-driving talent at Japanese companies, in both quantity and quality, as a challenge.

Differences in approach by condition

PrerequisitesStage that is easy to start fromRealistic endpoint of in-housingPoints to note
Large enterprise with in-house engineers① Company-led from field researchIn-house team handles modifications and even new developmentCoordination between departments takes time, and prototyping decisions tend to be delayed
Large enterprise without in-house engineers② Prototyping with external FDEsBusiness staff handle configuration changes and simple analysisProlonged external dependency inflates costs
Mid-sized and small businesses② Prototyping with the issue narrowed down to oneIn-house handling of operations and minor modificationsReliance on a single person means operations stop when they transfer or leave
Using overseas products or English-speaking vendors① Field research in Japanese, technical coordination in EnglishEstablish an in-house point of contact that can coordinate with vendors in EnglishEnglish proficiency is required for confirming specifications and reporting bugs

English proficiency matters for "technical coordination with vendors"

Many products that adopt the FDE-style implementation model, starting with Palantir, are developed by English-speaking companies. Field research and communication with business staff can proceed in Japanese, but when verifying product constraints or isolating bugs, situations arise where you need to read technical documents in English and communicate with overseas development teams.

From an individual engineer's perspective, since Japanese companies' FDE implementation projects have few people who "can do technical coordination in English," that alone broadens your range of roles. For how to improve your English, see English Skills Needed for FDE; for how to enter the field by years of experience, see How to Pursue FDE Without Prior Experience.

How does FDE implementation differ from SIer outsourcing, IT consulting, and in-housing with in-house SWEs/PdMs, and which companies is it unsuitable for?

In FDE Soken's comparison framework, the differences between FDE and SIer outsourcing, IT consulting, and in-house development boil down to two points: "where requirements are determined" and "the scope of accountability for results." Palantir's careers page also introduces FDSE as a role that solves problems at customer sites, distinguishing it from typical software engineers.

Four conditions of companies unsuited to FDE implementation

If the following conditions apply, a different approach than FDE implementation may be a better fit.

  • Requirements are already finalized: For projects like renewing core systems with fixed specifications, SIer outsourcing makes costs easier to predict
  • Permission to enter the field is not granted: In environments like factories or call centers where outsiders cannot observe the work, FDE's strengths are hard to demonstrate
  • Success metrics cannot be defined: With a vague purpose like "we just want to use AI," prototypes cannot be evaluated
  • No intention to in-house: If the assumption is to keep outsourcing, a maintenance-contract model fits better than the FDE style

From a VACAN practical standpoint, we believe that implementation in condition 3, "success metrics cannot be defined," is the hardest to recover from later. For individual engineers as well, suitability can be judged on the same criteria as for companies. For details, see Who Is Suited to FDE.

What are the common signs of failure in FDE implementation case studies, and what materials help judge cost levels?

FDE Soken organizes the idea that signs of FDE implementation failure emerge from two things: "skipping stages" and "retrofitting success metrics." For costs, we recommend estimating based on "how much of the four stages to outsource" rather than on figures from published case studies. The IPA's DX White Paper 2023 also points out that many companies lack mechanisms for measuring results.

Signs of failure you want to notice early

StageSign of failureExample countermeasure
① Field researchInterviews are only with managers, with no contact with actual workersSecure time for shadowing and observing workers
② PrototypingThe demo gets high marks, but the field does not use it in daily workAsk why it is not used and re-narrow the issue to one
③ Production rolloutSecurity and permission requirements surface just before productionInvolve the information systems department in reviews from the prototyping stage
④ In-housingThe contract end approaches without a handover recipient being decidedDesignate the handover recipient at the start of production rollout

Details of failure patterns are summarized in FDE Implementation Failure Patterns.

Estimate costs as "outsourced scope × duration × success metrics"

The cost of FDE implementation is the sum of product licenses, external FDE labor costs, in-house staff hours, and data preparation costs. Published case studies often do not disclose amounts, and even referencing other companies' figures from different industries or sizes is not very reliable.

When judging costs, gathering the following materials makes internal approval easier to obtain.

  • Within the four stages, the demarcation between stages outsourced to external FDEs and stages handled in-house
  • The "conditions to proceed to the next step" at each stage, and the withdrawal line for when conditions are not met
  • Pre-implementation baseline figures for performance metrics (work hours, inventory, utilization rates, etc.)
  • The number of people and roles required in-house after in-house development

The pricing philosophy of Palantir products is explained in Palantir's pricing structure, the approximate costs for FDE talent in FDE implementation costs, and how to measure results in ROI and KPI design.
