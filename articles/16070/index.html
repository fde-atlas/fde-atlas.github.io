The FDE Workflow in the AI Era: Amplifying On-Site Delivery with AI Tools

Original title: AI 时代 FDE 的工作流：用 AI 工具放大驻场交付

FDE中国 · 2026-09-04 · 9 min read

Summary

This practical article from FDEChina maps AI tools onto the daily workflow of forward deployed engineers, arguing that constraints come before efficiency gains. Three gates—network isolation, data compliance, and device approval—determine which tools are usable on-site, so the tool list must be confirmed with the customer before deployment. High-value use cases include turning meeting notes into structured requirements, generating eval candidate cases, drafting deployment docs, and writing retrospective first drafts, all under the rule that AI produces drafts while humans make judgments. The author marks clear boundaries: formal commitments, sensitive data, production code, and interpersonal judgment must never be outsourced to AI. Tool selection follows three criteria: verifiable, exitable, and auditable.

Key points

  • Confirm network, compliance, and device constraints before designing any AI workflow.
  • High-yield tasks: meeting notes, eval candidates, doc drafts, retrospectives.
  • Core rule: AI drafts, humans judge, with explicit confirmation points per task.
  • Never outsource commitments, sensitive data, production code, or people judgment.
  • Pick tools by three standards: verifiable, exitable, auditable.

The spread of AI coding and AI office tools has given many FDEs two extreme feelings: one is "these tools are so powerful, they'll do half my job," the other is "customers won't let me use this or that on site, so what does it have to do with me?" Both feelings are off. The reality is: AI tools can significantly amplify an FDE's output, but only if you know, under on-site constraints, which tools are available, which steps are suitable to hand to AI, and which steps you must keep for yourself. This article goes through the FDE's actual work steps one by one: first the constraints, then the efficiency gains, and finally the boundaries where you cannot rely on AI. For the FDE's daily rhythm itself, see A Day in the Life of an FDE; this article discusses which steps within that same rhythm can leverage AI.

First, the constraints: three gates to using AI tools at customer sites

The first gate is network isolation. What is commonly observed in domestic toB projects: customers in finance, government, and large-scale manufacturing keep their production environments on intranets or dedicated networks, physically or logically isolated from the public internet, and at your on-site workstation you may be unable to reach any external AI service. This means "just open an online tool" doesn't hold in many customer scenarios, and tool selection must be confirmed before you arrive on site. The second gate is data compliance: customer data, business code, and internal documents are often bound by NDAs and may not be fed into any external model service. This doesn't depend on your habits but on the contract and the customer's regulatory environment; one violation could cost you the entire customer relationship. The third gate is devices and processes: some customers require you to work on designated devices and route software installations through approvals, so introducing a tool is itself a process that needs customer sign-off.

These three gates determine the correct order for FDEs to adopt AI tools: first confirm with the customer the list of usable tools—which services may be accessed, which data may be fed into which systems, which approval process must be followed—and only then design your workflow. Common industry observation is that there are usually three viable routes: customer's privately deployed model services (an increasing number of large customers build these in-house), specific external tools approved by the customer, and small tools that run entirely locally. Before the list is confirmed, the default attitude should be conservative: better to go without than to violate first. Only once the constraints are clear do all subsequent efficiency discussions make sense.

AI assistance for requirements clarification and documentation

FDEs spend far more time on "written work" than outsiders imagine: meeting minutes, requirements organization, solution documents, weekly reports, delivery documents. These steps happen to be where current AI tools are most mature, and they offer the highest return on investment as an entry point. The first scenario is turning meeting records into structured requirements: after an on-site interview or requirements meeting, hand the transcribed text (with the customer's permission) to AI to organize into three categories—"requirement items, questions to clarify, implicit assumptions"—and you add your judgment on top. The human confirmation point here is very clear: AI organizes "what the customer said," but the judgment of "what the customer actually wants" must be made by you—treating paraphrase as requirements is a cardinal sin for an FDE.

The second scenario is document drafting. For documents with clear structure—deployment docs, API descriptions, solution first drafts—have AI generate a draft based on the skeleton you provide, then verify facts paragraph by paragraph before sending anything out. Focus your verification on version numbers, configuration items, environment details, and similar content—"exactly what AI is best at fabricating with a straight face"—and let none of it pass on gut feeling. The third scenario is diff comparison: after a customer environment upgrade, have AI compare old and new API docs and configuration differences to find call points that may be affected. For this kind of mechanical work, AI's accuracy and speed far exceed manual effort, and the results are easy to verify.

The overarching principle on this track is "AI produces drafts, humans produce judgment." Judgment includes: which requirements are worth taking on, which document should go to whom, which sentence is ambiguous in the customer's context. An FDE who abandons this principle will, at some customer meeting, suffer a credibility loss from an AI hallucination—and an FDE's career asset is precisely credibility. One easily overlooked detail: transcription and organization naturally produce many copies of customers' original statements; where these copies are stored and how long they are kept must also fall within the scope of compliance confirmation, rather than being assumed to be mere "working drafts."

Using AI to accelerate eval set generation

Eval sets are the foundation of FDE delivery quality (for metric design, see FDE Delivery Quality Metrics), but building them is notoriously time-consuming: they must cover real scenarios, annotate expected results, and be continuously maintained. AI has two mature acceleration points here. First, generating candidate cases from historical material: hand historical tickets, customer feedback, and failure logs (after desensitization) to AI and have it summarize problem patterns and generate candidate task descriptions with expected outputs. This turns "thinking up cases from scratch" into "reviewing candidate cases," an order-of-magnitude efficiency gain. Second, variant expansion: generate variants of existing cases with the same structure, different data, and altered boundaries to quickly broaden coverage, especially boundary conditions—human patience usually only suffices for the happy path, but AI never tires.

But the principles on this track are stricter than in other scenarios: AI-generated cases must pass human sampling checks to confirm representativeness, and expected results must be annotated or reviewed by humans. The reason is that the eval set is the baseline for all quality judgments; if the baseline itself is contaminated by AI hallucinations, every subsequent "success rate" becomes untrustworthy—an error amplified across the entire delivery process. Common industry observation is that the viable division of labor is AI for candidates and variants, humans for screening and annotation; an eval set built with human confirmation skipped entirely should not be put into use. For a systematic discussion of eval methodology, see Agent Topics.

AI assistance for knowledge consolidation

Knowledge consolidation is the step FDE teams most need to do yet most often neglect, and the reason is not that it's unimportant but that it's costly: having anyone sit down to write a retrospective in the gaps between projects means fighting human nature and the schedule. AI's value is precisely to bring the cost of consolidation down to an executable magnitude. Three concrete practices: first, extracting decisions from chats and meeting records—hand a week's communication records (within compliance limits) to AI and have it list "what was decided this week, what changed, what remains unresolved," which you verify and archive; second, automating drafts of deployment and delivery documents—at project close, feed configurations, scripts, and change logs to AI to generate document drafts, and you add context and pitfall notes; third, retrospective first drafts—have AI write a first version based on project materials using a fixed template, and your job is to correct conclusions and distill reusable lessons, not to start from a blank page.

One line must be held here: AI extracts "what happened," while the most valuable parts of a retrospective are "why" and "what to do next time," and these two layers must be written by humans. Archiving AI-generated retrospectives as-is means adding a batch of documents to the team's knowledge base that look complete but contain no judgment—worse than having no documents at all, because they will mislead those who come later.

When you cannot rely on AI

Boundary awareness is the core competency for using AI in this role. The following scenarios should be clearly off-limits or used only in a limited way. First, formal commitments and acceptance conclusions to the customer: schedule commitments, acceptance judgments, and performance guarantees—these are your professional judgment and credibility on the line; AI output can at most serve as a reference for your judgment. Second, any scenario involving sending sensitive data out: even if the customer permits a certain tool, anything involving core business data or user privacy must still be handled according to the contract's terms; when in a gray area, choose not to input it. Third, code going into the customer's production environment: AI-generated integration code and scripts must go through the same review and testing as human-written code before going live—the damage caused by one faulty automation script at a customer site far exceeds the time saved. Fourth, on-site interpersonal judgment: who in the customer's organization supports this project, which words should not be said in which setting, how to pace your reporting—these are the situational instincts an FDE lives on, and no tool can do this for you. Letting AI understand people on your behalf means giving up the most irreplaceable part of this role. There is no shortcut to this judgment; it can only be accumulated on-site—which is exactly why, as AI becomes more widespread, FDEs with field experience become scarcer, not less.

A useful self-check question is: if this decision goes wrong, who does the customer turn to? The answer is always you, not the tool. For any judgment you are accountable for, do not outsource the responsibility to AI—a tool can amplify your judgment, but it cannot replace your accountability.

Three criteria for choosing tools: verifiable, exitable, auditable

Once the constraints list is confirmed, how do you pick specific tools? Filter them with three criteria. The first is verifiability: can the tool's output be checked by you at low cost? AI-generated documents can be fact-checked paragraph by paragraph, but with a black-box automated decision tool, you cannot even see where it went wrong—prioritize handing to AI the steps where output is transparent and verification is cheap. The second is exitability: if the tool becomes unavailable tomorrow, can your workflow still function? Any choice that makes the project unable to continue without a certain tool is a risk source at the customer site, especially when the tool is an external service whose availability is not in your hands. The right approach is to treat the tool as an accelerator, not to bind your process to it.

The third is auditability: if the customer asks "how was this result produced," can you explain it clearly? In China's compliance-sensitive toB context, auditability is not a bonus but an entry ticket—whether it's AI-generated code going into the customer's system or AI-organized data going into deliverable documents, you must be able to answer for the source and processing. These three criteria also have a side benefit: they simultaneously serve as the talking points for explaining to the customer "how we use AI responsibly." Large customers increasingly care about how vendors use AI, and teams that can proactively articulate boundaries and processes are actually more likely to get permission to use data.

Weaving tools into a day: a reference workflow

Finally, fit the steps above back into your daily rhythm. After the morning customer sync: hand meeting notes to AI to organize into to-dos and requirement items, spend ten minutes verifying and archiving, replacing the previous half hour of manual organizing. The afternoon deep-work zone: pair with AI during development—generating boilerplate code, explaining unfamiliar codebases, drafting test cases; during evals, use AI to generate candidate cases from this week's failed samples, and you filter them into the library. Fragmented time: have AI compare environment differences and draft document sections. Friday consolidation time: use AI to generate a first draft of the weekly report and a retrospective draft, and spend the saved time on the parts that truly require judgment—analyzing this week's metrics and distilling reusable lessons.

The hallmark of this workflow is not "using AI for everything," but placing AI in high-repetition, verifiable steps like "drafting, comparing, expanding, extracting," while concentrating human time on the irreplaceable steps of clarification, judgment, and communication. After running this for a while, it's worth doing a self-assessment against the FDE competency model: after AI, is your time allocation closer to the high-value capabilities in the model? If so, you're using the tools right; if you find yourself squeezed by AI into only coordination work, you need to rethink your capability structure rather than blame the tools.

Summary and next steps

To recap: the prerequisite for using AI at customer sites is drawing clear constraint boundaries first—the three gates of intranet, compliance, and devices determine the tool list; high-return scenarios concentrate in written work and eval set building, with the principle of "AI drafts, humans judge"; knowledge consolidation becomes truly viable as costs drop, but the "why" and "what to do next time" must be written by humans; formal commitments, sensitive data, production code, and interpersonal judgment are the four boundaries that cannot be outsourced to AI; finally, weave the tools into your daily rhythm so they amplify rather than replace your judgment.

Suggested next steps: first, list the tools and data boundaries allowed in your current project and confirm them once with the customer's point of contact; then pick one consolidation step (such as drafting the weekly report or retrospective) and trial it for a week to feel the change in cost.
