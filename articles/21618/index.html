What Is a GTM Engineer? Differences from FDE (Forward Deployed Engineer), Salary Benchmarks, and the Right Choices for Working Engineers | Career Support Media for FDEs, Engineers, and IT Freelancers

Summary

This Japanese article by Shohei Tokumitsu, CEO of DeFactory, is aimed at executives deciding whether their company needs a GTM Engineer or an FDE (Forward Deployed Engineer). The core distinction is the main battlefield: GTM Engineers work inside the company, automating sales, marketing, and customer success processes so the same mechanism scales across all deals, while FDEs embed at a single customer site to solve individual business problems with AI and systems. The article offers decision criteria, sourcing strategies by growth stage (freelance contracts, agency support, then full-time hires), and screening questions for non-technical evaluators. Citing its BizDev Tech platform, it reports 6 FDE listings at 1.1–1.5 million yen monthly versus zero GTM Engineer listings, and estimates salary ranges of 10–25 million yen for FDEs and 8–15 million yen for GTM Engineers in Japan.

Key points

  • GTM Engineers automate internal go-to-market processes; FDEs optimize at individual customer sites.
  • LayerX's GTM Engineer job posting sparked attention in Japan, but public openings remain scarce.
  • BizDev Tech data: 6 FDE listings at ¥1.1–1.5M/month; zero GTM Engineer listings.
  • Estimated salaries: FDEs ¥10–25M, GTM Engineers ¥8–15M annually in Japan.
  • Recommended sourcing evolves with stage: freelance, agency, then full-time hires.

You may have been seeing the job titles "GTM Engineer" and "FDE (Forward Deployed Engineer)" more often in the hiring market and on social media. When LayerX published a hiring position for a "Go To Market Engineer (GTM Engineer)" and its CEO posted about it on X, attention surged in Japan almost overnight.

However, these two terms are very similar and their definitions tend to waver, so many executives and business owners find themselves unable to decide "which one does our company need?" or "should we hire them as full-time employees or secure them through outsourcing?"

In this article, without going too deep into technical details, we will explain the "differences," "how to determine what fits your company," and "concrete options for securing talent" needed for executive decision-making.

Author of this article: Shohei Tokumitsu (徳満翔平)

Let me start with the conclusion. The biggest difference between GTM Engineers and FDEs lies in their main battlefield: whether to "build mechanisms that can be rolled out across multiple clients and sales deals" or to "embed deeply in a single site and optimize for that specific case."

  • You want to automate your company's sales, marketing, and customer success processes and roll out the same mechanism across all deals and clients → GTM Engineer
  • You want to embed into a specific client company's site (e.g., a client of your development or SaaS implementation support services) and solve their individual business challenges with AI and systems → FDE

With this decision framework in mind, let's take a closer look at both roles in Chapters 2 and 3.

The job title GTM Engineer spread after overseas companies providing AI-powered sales intelligence tools proposed it as "the sales designer of the AI era." Beyond the LayerX case, engineer job-change platforms have begun treating "GTM Engineer" as an independent job category, and this movement in the hiring market is gradually expanding.

FDE, on the other hand, is known as a role established by Palantir Technologies, working on-site at client companies to solve AI and data utilization challenges. In Japan as well, job openings and projects are concretely emerging in the areas of AI-driven development and SaaS implementation support (we will present actual data in Chapters 3 and 5).

Both share the commonality of "using technology to build business growth processes with AI," but their actual work content and the benefits of introducing them into your company differ greatly. Correctly distinguishing between these two, which are easily confused due to their similar names, is the first step in preventing mismatches in hiring and outsourcing.

A GTM Engineer (Go-to-Market Engineer) is a specialist who leverages AI, data analysis, and automation tools to build and optimize processes in the GTM (Go-to-Market) domain—sales, marketing, and customer success—from a technical standpoint.

The key point is that GTM Engineers work "inside your own company (or the company they support)." Rather than embedding deeply with a single client, they build mechanisms that have cross-functional impact on the entire company's revenue process.

The work that can be delegated to a GTM Engineer can be organized into the following three main areas.

  • Building CRM and data infrastructure: Customizing CRMs such as HubSpot and Salesforce, and integrating data via API connections with external tools
  • Implementing AI workflows: Automating lead research using generative AI, and auto-generating personalized proposals and email copy
  • Automating processes: Developing automation pipelines connecting sales, marketing, and CS departments using workflow automation tools such as n8n, Make, and Zapier

If you want to learn more about the GTM Engineer's role, required skills, and career path, What is a GTM Engineer? A Complete Guide from Salary and Skills to Career Strategy and Going Freelance explains this in detail, including from an individual career perspective.

An FDE (Forward Deployed Engineer) is an engineer who embeds into a client company's business operations and works alongside them all the way to actually solving individual business challenges using AI and data platforms.

The biggest reason FDEs differ from traditional outsourced development engineers or SIers is that they don't stop at "deliver and done"—they take responsibility until the client has actually embedded the product or AI into their operations. If your business model involves supporting clients with AI implementation or SaaS adoption, FDEs are the core talent of that business.

If you want to learn more about the FDE role, typical salary, and differences from SES/IT consulting, What is an FDE provides a comprehensive explanation.

In contrast to GTM Engineers, FDE work centers on "embedding deeply into one company, one site."

  • Interviewing and visualizing the client's business flows and data structures
  • Designing and implementing PoCs for client-specific AI use cases
  • API integration with existing systems and building data pipelines
  • Training on-site staff and supporting adoption
  • Continuously proposing improvements for post-implementation operational issues

If your company faces challenges in its own sales, marketing, or CS functions—such as "doing the same tasks manually every time" or "spending too much time on lead responses and proposal creation"—the solution should be a mechanism that works across all customers and all deals, not just for a single specific client. In this case, a GTM engineer is the right fit.

On the other hand, if your company faces challenges such as "customers who adopt our product or service fail to make it stick and end up churning" or "each customer's workflows are too different for standard implementation support to handle," then an FDE, who dives deep into individual clients to solve their problems, is the right fit.

The priority of which role to secure also changes depending on your company's situation.

  • If you have zero in-house engineers: The first step is to articulate whether your company's challenge is "cross-functional automation" or "client-specific implementation support." Since neither type of talent will deliver results if hired without a deep understanding of the business, you need at least one person in-house who can explain your business processes.
  • If you have engineers but they are fully occupied: In many cases, it is unrealistic to ask existing engineers to additionally serve as a bridge to sales, marketing, and CS teams or to be stationed at client sites. A realistic division of roles is to let existing engineers focus on development while securing GTM engineers or FDEs externally.

According to DeFactory's view, a salary range of roughly 10 to 25 million yen is forming for full-time FDEs in Japan. Meanwhile, we estimate GTM engineers in Japan will settle at around 8 to 15 million yen, but hiring cases like LayerX have only just begun to appear, public job postings are still scarce, and the market rate remains fluid.

When we checked the actual project data listed on BizDev Tech, our own freelance engineer job-matching platform, there were 6 projects classified as FDE at the time of writing, with rates ranging from 1.1 to 1.5 million yen per month. As an industry, they tend to be concentrated in sectors with complex business processes requiring client-specific handling, such as AI-driven development support at major SIers, on-site PM roles at construction SaaS companies, and contract development for social infrastructure and construction.

Meanwhile, when we searched for GTM engineer projects under the same conditions, the result was 0 listings. This reflects the fact that the job title "GTM engineer" itself has only just emerged in Japan's hiring market, and projects for freelancers and contractors have not yet developed.

However, this does not mean there is no way to find them. For roles with few public job postings, proactive searching is more effective than waiting for public information. BizDev Tech receives around 40 referrals and applications from engineers daily, giving access to talent not visible in public job postings. While full-time public postings for people with GTM engineer-like skill sets (experience with API integrations, CRM building, and automation tools) are scarce, there is ample room to discover and approach them individually as contract or spot projects.

In addition, BizDev Tech also has registered talent with FDE-like qualities—people with experience as PdMs, EMs, or tech leads who combine technical understanding with a strong sense of ownership over the business and organization. There is a visible tendency for people who can organize problems from the perspectives of customers, management, and the organization—not just technical skill alone—and drive progress while bringing others along, to be highly valued in this field.

Whether you choose a GTM engineer or an FDE, the realistic way to secure talent changes depending on your company's growth stage.

At the seed to early Series A stage, in most cases the workload has not yet stabilized enough to justify hiring a dedicated GTM engineer or FDE as a full-time employee. At this stage, the realistic approach is to secure freelance talent on a contract basis for just the period needed and have them focus on getting things up and running. The benefit is that you can start with small-scale validation while keeping recruitment effort and fixed-cost risk low.

From late Series A through Series B, the workload grows and a single freelancer may no longer be able to handle it all. One option at this point is "combining with an agency." This means flexibly securing multiple people (or teams) as needed through staffing firms or agents.

Compared to contracting directly with individuals, going through an agency adds referral fees to the rate, but in exchange you gain flexibility: "a replacement can be arranged quickly if one person leaves" and "the number of people working can be adjusted up or down depending on workload." During expansion phases when workload is hard to predict, this flexibility increasingly becomes more valuable than the risk of fixed costs.

After product-market fit (PMF), once the business growth process has stabilized, the work of GTM engineers and FDEs becomes a recurring, ongoing need. At this stage, in many cases hiring full-time employees and accumulating knowledge in-house offers better long-term cost performance than repeatedly paying for external talent each time. "Has the same work been occurring continuously for more than 3 months?" is one useful benchmark for considering a full-time hire.

Even if you cannot evaluate the technical details, asking questions like the following can help you gauge, to some extent, the true capabilities of candidates or contractors.

  • "What business problems have you solved in the past, and with what kind of mechanisms?" (Can they explain starting from a concrete business problem?)
  • "Is that mechanism still actually being used in the field after implementation?" (Or did it end once it was built?)
  • "How much did you understand about our industry and workflows beforehand before making your proposal?" (Do they prioritize hearing the client out?)
  • "Do you have a system in place so operations won't stop even if the person in charge changes midway?" (Do they have measures against over-reliance on individuals?)
  • Warning sign: When a vendor only touts their track record, saying "We have extensive experience implementing XX," without asking any questions about your business processes. The better the GTM engineer or FDE, the more likely they are to open the first meeting with questions like "Could you first walk me through your sales process?" or "Which tasks currently take the most time?" Conversely, if they deliver a polished proposal without ever asking about your operations, you should suspect it may be a template-based proposal.
  • Reassuring sign: Whether the quote or proposal includes a phased approach, such as "Start small with XX, verify the results, and then expand." If they propose a large-scale system all at once from the start, the rework required if it diverges from actual field conditions will be substantial.
  • Sign to check before signing a contract: Be cautious if they cannot give a clear answer to the question "If the person in charge changes midway, how will the handover be handled?" Someone who can answer concretely about maintaining operational documentation and handover arrangements is evidence that they understand the risk of knowledge becoming person-dependent in advance.

Yes, it is possible. When a company needs to automate its sales, marketing, and customer success functions across the board while also supporting implementation for a specific major client, some companies secure both types of talent in stages. The realistic approach is to first clarify which issue is higher priority for your company (a cross-functional issue or a client-specific issue) and secure talent according to that priority.

It is difficult for non-engineers to evaluate technical validity with 100% accuracy, but perspectives like those introduced in Chapter 7—such as "Can they explain things in terms of business problems?" and "Is the solution actually being used in the field?"—are fully assessable even by non-technical people. Rather than raw technical skill, whether they can understand and explain problems in business terms is the key criterion for judgment.

Yes, it is possible. In fact, as introduced in Chapter 6, the typical path is to start with contract-based work and consider converting to a full-time hire once the workload stabilizes. There is no need to insist on full-time hiring from the outset.

GTM engineers and FDEs are both new technical roles that have emerged against the backdrop of the spread of AI adoption, but they differ significantly in their main battleground and evaluation criteria: GTM engineers work to automate their own company's revenue processes across the board, while FDEs dive deep into customers' operations to solve individual problems.

The shortcut to preventing mismatches in hiring and outsourcing is to first determine whether your company's problem calls for "automation that can be scaled horizontally" or "client-specific implementation support," and then use a mix of full-time hires, contract-based workers, and agencies according to your growth stage.
