How to Implement Generative AI: 5 Steps to Avoid Getting Stuck at PoC and a Practical Guide to In-House Development

Original title: 生成AI導入の進め方は？PoC止まりを避ける5ステップと内製化までの実務解説

FDE総研 · 2026-07-30 · 11 min read

Summary

This FDE Soken article explains why generative AI initiatives often stall at the PoC stage and proposes a five-step, FDE-centered approach. It argues that generative AI's capabilities cannot be fully specified upfront, so requirements-first, batch development structurally fails. PoC stagnation stems from three disconnects—problem, implementation, and adoption—caused by distance between builders and the field. The recommended cycle is Discovery, Prototype, Iterate, Embed, Enable, with most projects stalling after Prototype. Internal handover proceeds through three phases: substitution, collaboration, and self-running.

Key points

  • Generative AI requirements can't be fixed upfront; value and limits only emerge through real use.
  • PoC failure comes from three disconnects: problem, implementation, and adoption, blocking field learning from returning to development.
  • Five steps: Discovery, Prototype, Iterate, Embed, Enable; most efforts stall after Prototype when teams disband.
  • FDE-style adoption starts from ambiguous field problems and ends at actual usage, unlike delivery-focused vendor projects.
  • Internalization follows substitution, collaboration, self-running phases, transferring judgment criteria, not just deliverables.

What is the right approach to successfully adopting generative AI?

The right way to adopt generative AI is not to build something large and deliver it, but to build small, put it into the field, and refine it based on reactions. Generative AI is a technology whose value and limitations only become apparent once you start using it. Rather than finalizing requirements and developing all at once, the implementer embeds themselves in the field and consistently handles everything from problem discovery through adoption—this approach structurally avoids getting stuck at PoC.

The hands-on, field-embedded role for this is the FDE (Forward Deployed Engineer). This role personally handles everything from workflow design to implementation and testing at the customer's site. It was established by Palantir, and in recent years it has become a core role sought by generative AI companies such as OpenAI and Anthropic (Palantir official blog).

Can generative AI requirements be fully specified upfront?

Generative AI is a technology whose value and limitations only become visible through hands-on use, so requirements cannot be fully specified before starting. In traditional system development, you could extract business requirements, translate them into specifications, and judge success by whether the system worked as specified. With generative AI, that premise breaks down.

What it can and cannot do remains uncertain until you try it with actual business data. Even if you decide on paper to "automate this task," using it in the field may reveal issues with input variability and exception patterns, and it may settle into usage patterns different from what was expected. That is why you need an approach that delivers a working product to the field quickly—not a specification document—and feeds the reactions you get there back into the design.

Why does PoC stall?

When a demo works and gets management approval but is never used in the field, the cause is the physical and cognitive distance between the builders and the field. It does not stall because of insufficient technical skill. The less directly the builders observe the field's actual work, the more likely you get something that runs but is not usable.

Generative AI initiatives often succeed at the proof-of-concept stage but stumble when it comes to embedding into daily operations. The cause is proceeding with development relying only on organized requirements, even though the problems to solve lie within the field's ambiguous work. By the time requirements are written down, much of what the field truly struggles with—the exceptions that cause hesitation, each person's personal habits, the manual handoffs between systems—has been stripped away.

What is FDE (Forward Deployed Engineer)-style adoption?

FDE-style adoption is an approach where engineers capable of implementation enter the customer's field and consistently handle everything from problem discovery through implementation and adoption. Its distinguishing feature is building while putting it to use in the field and polishing it, rather than handing over requirements and having someone else build.

Even Anthropic's FDE job postings list, alongside strong implementation skills, "high agency to navigate ambiguity" and the ability to converse with customers to dig into problems (Anthropic official job posting). The ability to cycle between "implementation and dialogue" within one person (or one team) becomes the driving force for field adoption. Because clues gained through dialogue can be turned into implementation on the spot, no meaning is lost through a game of telephone.

What causes PoC to stall?

The cause is that the three elements—problem, implementation, and adoption—are fragmented, and learning from the field never flows back into development. The more a technology like generative AI assumes "refine while using," the more this fragmentation hinders adoption. Conversely, if you keep repeating validation without changing the structure, you will keep stalling in the same place.

What are the three disconnects?

The three disconnects are the disconnect from problems, the disconnect from implementation, and the disconnect from adoption—all arising from the distance between builders and the field. Behind every stalled PoC, one of these is always lurking.

  • Disconnect from problems: The field's real problems lie in ambiguous work before it becomes requirements, yet development starts based only on organized requirements.
  • Disconnect from implementation: Planning, development, and field operations are siloed, and intent fades through a game of telephone.
  • Disconnect from adoption: Delivery becomes the goal, and no one owns "through to actual use."

These three are not independent problems; they chain together. A disconnect from problems produces something off-target, a disconnect from implementation delays course correction, and a disconnect from adoption leaves it unattended with no one fixing it.

Why don't one-shot development and "refine while using" fit together?

The traditional approach of finalizing requirements and building all at once fundamentally clashes with generative AI, which assumes iterating while in use. When siloed roles and one-shot development are the premise, learning gained in the field cannot be reflected quickly.

The result is "build once and done"—the improvement loop never turns, and the tool falls out of use. Generative AI is a technology where you only learn what it can and cannot do by touching it. The richest learning comes not right before delivery, but after the field starts using it with real data. The peak of learning coincides with the dissolution of the development team—this is the structural weakness of the one-shot development model.

What are the signs of a PoC about to stall?

Flip the three disconnects around and the warning signs appear. Having only organized requirements at hand, and having no implementer who has entered the field and observed the work—these two points are the gateway to a stalled PoC.

Another indicator is whether success is defined only as "it works." If the goal is delivery, the project proceeds with no one owning "through to actual use." If, before starting, you cannot answer "who will keep using this, when, and for which tasks," the disconnect from adoption has already occurred.

What are the 5 steps to success?

The formula for success is a cycle of building small, applying it in the field, and refining based on feedback, run continuously across five stages. Each step in the table below is not an independent process but a continuous flow where each learning builds on the previous one.

StepContentGoal
1. Discovery 現場に入り課題を掘る 解くべき課題を現場と合意する
2. Prototype 最小で作って当てる 早い実物で価値と限界を確かめる
3. Iterate 反応で直す 実データで使われる形に近づける
4. Embed 業務に組み込む 既存フロー・データに定着させる
5. Enable 内製へ引き渡す 社内が自ら改善できる状態にする

First half: How to dig into issues and build minimally?

The initial Discovery, Prototype, and Iterate stages are about finding the right answer, and a fast, tangible artifact is the best requirements definition. Enter the field, agree on the problems to solve, deliver a minimal working version without waiting for a perfect design, and refine it based on feedback.

Having people actually touch it reveals operational constraints that were invisible on paper and the approaches that truly work. What matters here is not raising the polish of the Prototype but increasing the number of times it is applied. This iteration (Iterate) simultaneously improves accuracy and buy-in from the field. Field buy-in pays off in the later adoption phase, because tools that reflect their own feedback are more likely to be used.

Second half: How to integrate into operations and hand over to in-house?

The later Embed and Enable stages are about consolidating results, designing both the operational owners and the improvement loop. Rather than ending as a one-off tool, integrate it into existing workflows and data, and finally hand it over so in-house staff can improve it themselves.

Whether or not this is designed in advance is the turning point between ending as "temporarily working" and becoming "continuously used." Embedding it, including operational owners, exception handling, and the improvement loop, is the final key to moving beyond a stalled PoC. In Embed, decide where in the existing workflow it will plug in and who will handle exceptions and how. In Enable, hand over to the organization, including the reasoning behind decisions.

Where do the 5 steps tend to stall?

Most initiatives progress through Prototype and stall at the iteration and embedding stages after Iterate. This is because once a working demo exists, it gets reported as a result and the team is disbanded.

This structure is a direct manifestation of the "disconnection from adoption" mentioned earlier. Prototype is a visible deliverable and thus easily evaluated, while Iterate, Embed, and Enable are unglamorous and take time to show results in numbers. That is precisely why agreeing upfront on all five steps as a single flow is a practical safeguard against stopping midway.

How does this differ from traditional vendor implementations?

The biggest difference is the starting point. The traditional approach starts from organized requirements, while the FDE approach starts from the ambiguous issues in the field themselves. This difference in starting point changes everything: the team structure, the process, and the goal.

How do the starting point and goal differ?

The traditional approach sets system delivery as the goal, while the FDE approach sets a state where the tool is used in the field and produces results as the goal. Even when both use the word "implementation," the outcomes differ greatly depending on where the endpoint is set.

PerspectiveTraditional vendor implementationFDE-style implementation
Starting pointOrganized requirementsAmbiguous issues in the field
Team structurePlanning, development, and field separated by division of laborThe implementer enters the field and handles everything consistently
ProcessFix requirements, then develop all at onceBuild small, apply it, and refine based on feedback
GoalSystem deliveryA state where it is used in the field and produces results
Beyond thatContinued vendor dependencyPlanned through to in-house capability building

If the goal is delivery, whether it gets used falls within the customer's responsibility. If the goal is "a state where it is used," exception handling and operational ownership become the builder's concern. This difference in where the line is drawn determines whether a stalled PoC can be avoided.

Which should you choose?

The traditional approach suits areas where requirements are fixed and specifications won't change; the FDE approach suits areas where operations are ambiguous and the tool is refined through use. The FDE approach is not superior for every project.

In areas where specifications are clear and what to build is decided from the start, fixing requirements and building all at once is faster and costs are predictable. On the other hand, applying generative AI usually starts from exploring where in operations it will be effective. If the problems to solve are themselves undetermined, entering the field and applying something quickly is a shortcut rather than thickening the requirements definition. The deciding factor is not the novelty of the technology but whether the problems are settled.

How does this lead to in-house capability building?

In-house capability building is achieved by an external FDE producing results in the field while transferring the same process to the organization. The goal is not to lock in vendor dependency but to create a state where the customer can run things on their own.

What are the three stages of "substitution → collaboration → self-sufficiency"?

The transition proceeds through three stages—substitution, collaboration, and self-sufficiency—gradually handing the external FDE's scope of responsibility over to the organization. The key is not to hand over all at once but to shift the center of gravity while producing results.

StagePrimary ownerExternal FDE's roleIn-house role
SubstitutionExternal FDEHandles everything through implementation and adoption, making results and methods visibleProvide field issues and operational knowledge
CollaborationExternal FDE + in-houseTransfer decision criteria and tools to the organizationParticipate in design and implementation working side by side
Self-sufficiencyIn-houseSupport only at key pointsDrive improvements as the primary owner

First, the external FDE handles everything through implementation and adoption, making results and methods visible; next, in-house members work side by side to transfer decision criteria and tools; finally, in-house takes over as the primary owner while the external side supports only at key points. What is handed over is not just the deliverables. Passing on the decision criteria—why that design was chosen—is the condition for self-sufficiency.

What changes when in-house capability building is the premise?

With in-house capability building as the premise, generative AI investment changes from a disposable PoC into an asset: implementation capability that stays within the organization. Even with the same spending, what remains is different.

If self-sufficiency is designed as the endpoint from the start, even the substitution stage is built with "how to hand it over" in mind. Documentation granularity, tool selection, articulating decision criteria—these tend to be postponed unless handover is the premise.
