Updated

Choose the smallest valuable change you can evaluate.

How it works

After discovery you usually have too many problems, not too few. Scoping is choosing the smallest change that delivers real value and can be tested in a short window. It matters more in field work than in product work because the customer's patience is finite and their trust is earned by the first thing you ship, not the best thing you plan.

Ramp lists "always be scoping" among its FDE principles. Their write-up describes a customer blocked by a feature gap estimated at about three engineering days, which the team resolved with a workaround found on a single call with the customer. The same post admits a tension: ad-hoc customizations add complexity, so the team generally prefers general features, and choosing between a quick fix and a reusable feature comes down to judgment. Scoping is how you make that choice on purpose instead of by default.

A useful method is to rank candidates on three axes. Value is how much of the measured problem goes away. Feasibility is whether you have the data, access, and skills today. Uncertainty is how much you would learn by trying. A project with high value and high uncertainty is often the right first bet if you can test it cheaply. A project with high value and low feasibility belongs on a list for later, with the blocker named.

Then write the contract for the pilot. Acceptance criteria say what must be true for the pilot to count as working, in terms a user can check. A baseline says where the number is today. A stop condition says what result would make you quit or change course. The stop condition is the part teams skip, and it is the one that protects you from a pilot that runs for months because nobody wants to call it.

Sierra's interview format rewards the same skill: candidates are judged partly on how they fit an idea to a time limit and cut what does not matter. Experienced FDEs scope by asking what they could show a real user in two weeks that would change a decision. If nothing would change, the scope is wrong, however clean it looks.

Worked example

Hypothetical: continuing the insurance case, you have three candidates. A coverage lookup panel in the claims screen scores high on value and high on feasibility, because the policy data is already in one database. Auto-approving small claims scores high on value but low on feasibility, because it needs a compliance review. A claims summary scores low on value, because discovery showed nobody needed it.

You propose a two-week pilot of the lookup panel with 5 adjusters. Baseline: about 10 minutes per invoice match. Acceptance: median time falls to 4 minutes or less on at least 50 matched invoices, with zero coverage errors found in a supervisor spot check. Dependencies: read access to the policy database and one hour of IT time to embed the panel.

Stop condition: if by day 7 the median is still above 8 minutes, you stop building and go back to watching adjusters work. Auto-approval goes on the later list, blocked on compliance.
