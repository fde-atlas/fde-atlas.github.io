FDE Change Management: The ADKAR Model and Train-the-Trainer

Original title: FDE 变革管理：ADKAR 模型与培训培训师

FDE Times · 2026-10-08 · 6 min read

Summary

This guide argues that a successful deployment is not the finish line for FDEs: if users quietly revert to old workflows like Excel, the project has failed regardless of system health. Using an invoice-extraction agent for a 12-person finance team as a running example, it walks through Prosci's ADKAR model (Awareness, Desire, Knowledge, Ability, Reinforcement), noting classroom training only reaches Knowledge. It also recommends train-the-trainer programs and customer-facing docs to prevent knowledge loss, citing Tryolabs, Planisware, and a Tungsten Automation job posting, and ends with a one-page checklist and four common mistakes.

Key points

  • Deployment success means users keep using the system after 30 days.
  • ADKAR has five stages; training alone only covers Knowledge.
  • Train with real, messy data, not clean demo examples.
  • Train-the-trainer and docs prevent knowledge loss when staff leave.
  • FDE resumes should show onboarding and retention results.

Even if the system runs flawlessly, the project still counts as a failure if users quietly return to their old Excel spreadsheets two weeks after launch.

Key Takeaways

  • A system being deployable doesn't mean most of the work is done: user onboarding, change management, and integration into real workflows are still ahead.
  • Classroom training only teaches users how the system works. Users must practice on real tasks, and new habits must be continuously reinforced after launch, or they will revert to old ways.
  • Train-the-trainer programs keep knowledge within the client organization, even when client employees turn over.

Imagine this scenario: the demo is done, the client has signed acceptance, and the dashboard is all green. Two weeks later you open the logs and find the system running fine—the only problem is that almost no one is using it.

For a Forward Deployed Engineer (FDE), this is one of the most frustrating kinds of failure. No incidents, no bugs to fix, no complaints in the ticket queue. Users simply went back to their old way of working. Six months later, when the contract comes up for renewal, the client will ask: what did this system actually deliver?

Project management software vendor Planisware points out explicitly that clients often assume most of the work is done once the system can be deployed, when in fact it is not.

Tryolabs calls the remaining work the FDE's "last mile": user onboarding, change management, and workflow integration. This work is not separate from engineering—it determines whether the system you built can create value.

Why Is One Training Session Usually Not Enough?

Most engineers understand "user training" as a slide deck before launch, a demo, a few Q&A answers, and a mass-emailed user guide. This approach misses a key point: knowing how to use something and actually being able to use it are two different things.

Prosci's ADKAR model breaks the change process each person goes through into five outcomes, one for each letter: Awareness, Desire, Knowledge, Ability, and Reinforcement. Knowledge is knowing how to change; Ability is actually putting the new skills and behaviors into practice.

A single class can get you to Knowledge at best. An accountant can nod along through an entire demo, yet still be lost when the first crooked scanned invoice shows up.

Both ends of the model are often skipped too. At the starting point, Prosci reminds us: sending employees a notice doesn't mean they have Awareness—that is, an understanding of why the change is needed.

At the endpoint, Reinforcement means making the new way of working stick so users don't slide back into old habits. According to Prosci, without this step, users may revert to old practices and the project may fail to deliver its intended benefits.

A Concrete Case: Deploying an Invoice-Recognition Agent for a Finance Team

Suppose you deploy an agent that extracts invoice data for a 12-person finance team at a distribution company. The agent reads PDF files and fills in fields in the ERP system; low-confidence fields are pushed into a queue for human review. Technically, everything works.

Here is what you should do at each stage.

Awareness. Don't start by sending an email saying "starting Monday, the team will switch to the new tool." First sit down with the finance manager and ask her: "How many evenings does the team spend manually entering invoices at month-end?" Her answer is the reason for change, and that reason must come from within the team, not from the vendor.

Desire. Understanding the reason doesn't mean wanting to change. An accountant who has manually entered invoices for years may worry the new tool will expose past errors or make them redundant. Invite two people from the team to help design how the review queue is organized, so the system contains something of their own contribution.

Knowledge. Train with the company's own real invoices, not demo data. Deliberately include tricky cases—blurry photos, two-page invoices, invoices with obscured tax IDs—because these are exactly what users will encounter in their first week on the job.

Ability. In the first week after launch, everyone processes that day's invoices in the new system while you sit beside them. Every time someone is about to open Excel, write it down. Each such moment signals a spot where the system doesn't yet fit the actual way of working.

Tryolabs describes the goal of this stage precisely: eliminating the friction between "the system works" and "how people actually work."

Reinforcement. After 30 days, review the logs: who is still processing through the system, who has stopped, whether the review queue is backing up. Together with the finance manager, fold the system into the official month-end close process, making the new practice the default rather than just an option.

The Next Trainer Must Come from the Client Side

The five stages above don't cover one risk, which calls for a separate, parallel track: you will eventually leave, and the person you trained most thoroughly may also leave the company.

A job posting for an FDE at Tungsten Automation lists targeted train-the-trainer courses and technical documentation written specifically for each client among the responsibilities. The stated rationale: reducing knowledge loss caused by client employee turnover.

In the finance case, this means selecting two people, not one, to teach their colleagues, while you listen and fill in gaps.

Documentation must also be written for the right audience. Instead of a README for developers, write a one-pager on "what to do when an invoice is pushed to the review queue," with screenshots from their own system.

Tryolabs describes the ultimate goal as: a stronger client organization that fully owns what has been built. If no one knows how to run the system after you leave, the handover isn't complete.

Four Common Mistakes

The first mistake is treating launch as the finish line, spending the entire project cycle writing code without setting aside a week to accompany users. The second is training with clean demo data—users feel confident in class, then are caught off guard by real work.

The third mistake is assuming a notice is enough for users to understand why the change is needed. An email from management only tells people a change is coming; users must be able to articulate the reason in their own words. The fourth is relying on a single "power user." When that person leaves, all the knowledge leaves with them.

For engineers looking to transition into an FDE role, this is also a resume booster. Don't just write "built an invoice data extraction pipeline"—also include how many people you helped onboard, how many were still using the system after 30 days, and whom you trained to continue teaching colleagues.

When reading job descriptions, watch for phrases like "user onboarding", "change management", or "train-the-trainer". Tryolabs includes user enablement and change management in the FDE's responsibilities, while Tungsten Automation's job posting lists training the trainers directly in the job duties. So, prepare a specific story for each of these phrases.

Planisware treats training as part of change management, which has a major impact on user adoption and performance. Code gets the system running, but whether users keep using it after you leave depends on this work.
