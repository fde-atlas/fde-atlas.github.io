The Rapidly Growing Role of "FDE" — In an Era Where AI Writes Code, Where Does an Engineer's Expertise Go?

Original title: 急拡大する「FDE」という職種 ── AI がコードを書く時代に、エンジニアの専門性はどこへ移るのか

Last year, I built an AI document search system for a manufacturing client. The PoC went well. Accuracy was good, and the demo even drew applause. Six months later, that system still isn't running in production.

The reason wasn't technical. It turned out that HR evaluation documents were mixed in with the search targets, and no one inside the company could decide who was allowed to see what. The existing authentication infrastructure was an old Active Directory, and the permission groups didn't reflect reality. And above all, no one had ever defined "which business processes would benefit, and by how much, from this search."

Technically, everything was possible. And yet it didn't move.

What I was missing at that moment is what's now called an FDE (Forward Deployed Engineer) overseas. Job postings have grown more than tenfold in a year, and frontier AI companies are fighting over them.

In this article, I'll organize what an FDE is, why this role is growing now that AI can write code, and what "the higher level of expertise demanded in the AI era" actually means, based on public data and examples from Japanese companies.

What is an FDE?

A Forward Deployed Engineer is an engineer who embeds in the customer's environment and solves the customer's problems using their own company's product. The name comes from the military term "forward deployment," meaning an engineer stationed at the front line (the customer's environment) rather than in the rear (their own office).

The role originated at Palantir in the early 2010s. The fact that their first customer was the CIA was decisive. Intelligence agency analysts cannot explain their workflows to external vendors. They can't hand over their data. And since threats change daily, requirements can't be fixed.

What do you do with customers for whom requirements definition is structurally impossible? Palantir's answer was to put engineers inside the customer. Stop asking, observe, and build on the spot.

How it differs from similar but distinct roles

I think the first thing a Japanese engineer would think is "How is that different from being an on-site contractor?" That's what I thought at first, too.

The decisive difference is whether you have a product. Once you grasp this, everything else falls into place.

In a survey analyzing 1,000 job postings, 0% of FDE postings mentioned quotas. The evaluation axis is fundamentally different from sales engineering. Also, while solutions engineers typically leave after a successful PoC, FDEs stay on for weeks to months after production launch.

Individual engagements feed back into the product

The most important thing about the FDE model is the feedback loop in which problems discovered in the field reach all customers as product features.

This is the dividing line from contract development. In contract development, what you build stays with the customer and ends there. With FDEs, problems found at one company get incorporated into the product and reused by the next hundred. It's a structure where the more individual engagements you do, the more the company's assets accumulate.

Why is it growing so rapidly right now?

Before looking at the numbers, one caveat. This topic has generated a flood of articles from job boards and recruiting agencies, and the numbers differ completely depending on the source. "800% growth," "1,000% growth," "10x," "4x" are all mixed together, and salaries range from "a median of 17 million yen" to "40-80 million yen." Since the populations, measurement periods, and definitions all vary, it's better not to take any single number at face value.

With that said, here are surveys that disclose their methodology.

In a survey analyzing data from Revealera, which provides job posting data for financial institutions, FDE job postings grew 1,165% year over year (January-October 2025 vs. the same period in 2024). The same survey found the median of published salary ranges to be about $174,000, with 70% of postings including equity.

According to TechCrunch reporting, as of January 2026, 5-10% of companies were planning to hire FDEs, but by the end of June that had jumped to 70%. There are an estimated 17,000 FDEs in the US.

The reason salary ranges differ by an order of magnitude between sources is that they're measuring different things. Compare the median disclosed base salary in job postings with the total compensation (including stock) of senior-level staff at OpenAI or Anthropic, and naturally there's a gap of more than 3x. If you're considering a career move, the only way is to look at specific positions at specific companies, not ranges.

The "deployment gap"

Behind this growth lies the reality that AI projects never reach production.

In MIT's "GenAI Divide" report published in 2025 (based on interviews with 150 executives and 300 public case studies), it was stated that 95% of generative AI pilots had not produced clear returns. This "95%" tends to take on a life of its own, but MIT's definition is quite strict: "sustained productivity gains and P&L impact confirmed by both users and executives." It does not simply mean "over 90% of AI projects fail technically."

The causes of failure repeatedly cited are not model performance but things like the following.

  • Can't connect to the customer's legacy SQL database
  • OIDC / SAML authentication methods are unusual and can't be integrated
  • Can't meet data residency requirements
  • Started with an ambiguous definition of success
  • Never integrated into business processes and ends up unused

My project at the beginning fits this list perfectly. The current picture is that this is called the "integration wall," and people who can get over it command high compensation.

FDEs are needed because AI can now write code

I think this is the most interesting part.

Intuitively, it seems like the opposite. If AI can write code, engineers should become unnecessary. In fact, some claim that "software engineers will no longer be needed by 2027."

But what actually happened in the field was a shift of the bottleneck.

Ryuta Sakamoto of Loglass (co-founder and executive officer) said the following in a dialogue on LevaTech LAB.

From 2024 to 2025, many engineering organizations realized this. AI coding was supposed to speed up development, but the true bottleneck lies outside of coding and technology.

Even if the cost of writing code drops to one-tenth, things like "not knowing what to build," "what was built doesn't take hold in the business," and "no one is designated to approve it" don't get faster at all. Rather, as implementation got faster, the other stages became relatively more visible as bottlenecks.

Sakamoto counters the "engineers are unnecessary" argument as follows: "Writing code will no longer be the center of value, but the two poles of problem identification and organizational implementation will become engineers' important roles."

I think the expression "two poles" is accurate. The middle (implementation) became lighter, so value shifted to the front (what to build) and the back (how to make it stick). FDEs are precisely the role that handles both poles.

What surprised me was that the demand for AI-specific skills was lower than expected. RAG is at 12%, and mentions of specific vendors are under 10%. Conversely, very ordinary full-stack skills like Python, TypeScript, and cloud rank at the top.

In other words, what's being sought is not an "AI specialist" but "an ordinarily strong implementer who can also use AI." This is an easily misunderstood point. It's not a role that requires a machine learning degree.

Years of experience

Mid-level (3-5 years) at 60%, senior (6-8 years) at 20%, Staff and above at 8%. The mid-level tier is the main battleground. It's not "impossible unless you're a 10-year veteran."

Company size and customer industries

58% of the job postings come from companies with 11-200 employees, i.e., startups around Series B to D. Customer industries are finance at 24%, government and defense at 18%, healthcare at 17%, insurance at 17%, and energy at 13%—concentrated in regulated industries.

This makes sense to me. The more heavily regulated the industry, the less data can leave the company, the more unique the certifications, and the heavier the approval processes. In other words, the walls to integration are high. Where the walls are high is precisely where the value of people who can climb over them lies.

Required non-technical skills

Communication with customers at 47%, problem-solving at 42%, collaboration at 38%. Nearly half of the job postings explicitly require communication skills.

Previous roles

Software engineers (backend/full-stack) at 45%, solution engineers at 22%, data engineers/scientists at 15%. Transitioning from ordinary engineers is the most common path.

What about in Japan?

The situation is reaching a point where it can no longer be dismissed with "that's a story about overseas."

LayerX has an FDE group within its AI Workforce division. It has been studying Palantir since its founding in 2018, and since its blockchain business days it has adopted a methodology of going deep into customer sites. Initially it called the role "LLM Engineer," but the name didn't catch on, and from July 2025 it began officially recruiting for FDE positions.

Hidei Shirai of the company (FDE Group EM) positions FDEs as follows.

FDE is closer to a business model than a role

And he draws the distinction from SES by the presence of a product. The problems FDEs solve "accumulate as company assets," whereas with SES, "it's over once you leave the client company."

Loglass had also been paying attention to Palantir since its founding in 2019. The company reportedly recognized the need for FDEs in the process of implementing "Loglass AI Agents." Sakamoto explains the uniqueness of FDEs as follows.

Carrying two goals that are inherently in trade-off: individual optimization for customers and the scalability of one's own product

In addition, organizations moving in the same direction are launching in Japan under different names, such as SmartHR's "Enterprise Success Unit" and AI Shift's FDE positions.

LayerX's FDE + DS structure

A useful implementation example is that LayerX pairs FDEs with DSs (Deployment Strategists). According to Shirai, the ability to discern the essence of a problem is higher on the DS side, while the ability to solve technical problems is higher on the FDE side.

The design doesn't demand everything from one person; it divides the roles and makes them complement each other. I think this is more realistic than searching for a superman who "can define problems, implement, and communicate perfectly."

An antithesis to Japanese business practices

I want to organize the difference between Japanese SIers/SES and FDEs one level deeper. On the surface, they look the same as "engineers working at client sites," but the structures are complete opposites.

The structure of contracting/SES is that the client decides the requirements, we sell man-hours, and the results remain with the client. Engineers are executors of instructions, and what gets evaluated is hours worked. It presupposes that the client can define the requirements.

The structure of FDE starts from the premise that the client hasn't even been able to articulate the requirements. Shirai says the following.

There are almost no cases where the client can articulate "the problem to be solved" from the start

That's why FDEs go in to define the problem itself. And the results of solving it flow back into their own product. What gets evaluated is the client's business outcomes.

In other words, FDE functions as a structural antithesis to the "customer orders, vendor delivers" business flow of Japan's IT industry. Even though both involve "going to the customer," the meaning is completely opposite.

What is "higher expertise" in the AI era?

I believe this is where the real topic begins. More than the job title of FDE itself, what matters to most people is the substance of the expertise behind it.

First, let me clear up a misconception. The expertise discussed here is not "knowing harder technologies." It is not being able to explain the internal structure of a Transformer, nor memorizing all Kubernetes resources. Those are the areas where LLMs excel most, and their relative value has actually declined.

The expertise that emerges from field data and case studies can be organized into the following five areas.

1. The ability to define problems

Customers cannot articulate the "problem to be solved." They can only describe symptoms. Behind "search is slow" may lie "we're actually searching for the wrong things," and behind that, "the task itself is unnecessary."

My failure at the beginning was exactly this. I took "we want document search" at face value as a requirement. What was actually needed was for staff to be able to reference past cases, with supporting evidence, for three specific types of application forms. Had I understood that, the solution would have been built differently, not as full-text search.

This ability is hard to replace with AI because the necessary information has not been verbalized. You can only access information that emerges from observing the field, identifying key people, and building up trust.

2. The ability to make decisions within constraints

FDEs operate within the constraints of their own company's platform. They cannot build anything. Judgment is required: "The customer says this, but it doesn't align with the product's philosophy, so I'll propose a different solution."

Sakamoto defines this ability as follows.

The ability to move forward within the constraints of one's own platform, taking into account not only technical and logical correctness but also human complexity

I think the phrase "not only technical and logical correctness" is everything. In practice, correct designs failing to get adopted is an everyday occurrence. The ability to keep moving forward without stalling in such situations is a different thing from the ability to calculate correctness.

And above all, the core of this expertise is deciding what not to build. In a world where implementation has become cheap, the value of "being able to build" has declined, while the value of "deciding not to build" has risen.

3. The ability to design evaluation

This is the statement from Sakamoto that resonated with me the most.

AI agents operate probabilistically. Organizational decision-making operates on accountability.

How do you integrate a probabilistic system into a deterministic structure of accountability? Under whose authority, in which meeting forum, and at what level of granularity do you use the answers produced by an agent with 95% accuracy?

This is not a technical problem but a design problem, and moreover, you cannot design it without understanding the technology. That is why it remains an engineer's job.

Concretely, this involves designing offline evaluation sets, linking them to business KPIs, deciding where to insert human review, and planning fallbacks for failures. It's unglamorous work, but systems that don't handle it never make it to production.

4. The ability to implement into organizations

Building a system and embedding it into an organization are different jobs. Whose authority needs to change, how existing meeting structures should be altered, how to explain things to those who oppose.

I believe the current situation where 95% of pilots stall is the result of separating this out as "not an engineer's job." Conversely, engineers who can step into this space have high scarcity value.

5. The ability to distill specific solutions into general ones

This is an ability specific to FDEs. Looking at what was built for one particular customer, you judge: "Is this common to other customers too?" "Should this become a product feature, or remain a configuration just for this customer?"

Over-generalize and you end up with generic features that serve no one; over-customize and unmaintainable branches multiply. Drawing this line is impossible without understanding both the customer's business and the product.

What these five have in common is that all of them are work that involves "making judgments with incomplete information." LLMs are good at producing answers from given information, but they cannot go out and find information that doesn't exist. The center of gravity of expertise has shifted in that direction.

How to think about it as a career

Who it suits and who it doesn't

Let me organize what can be said from the data and case studies.

People who seem well-suited are those who don't mind working at the boundary between technology and business. Those who can take an interest in customers' business operations. Those who can act when specifications are undefined. And those who want to see how the code they write is actually used, and by whom.

People who are likely a poor fit are those who want to dig deep into technology itself. Those who want to deliver the best possible implementation given clear specifications. Those who prefer conversing with computers over coordinating with people.

This is not at all a matter of superiority or inferiority. Deep technical expertise has not become unnecessary; I think it's more accurate to say that the options have branched. Work building compilers and database engines will still be needed, and that expertise is scarce in its own right.

If you're transitioning from your current role

Looking at the breakdown of my previous role (45% SWE, 22% solution engineer), an ordinary backend/full-stack engineer is on the shortest path. What's additionally needed is not a machine learning degree, but things like the following.

  • Hands-on experience building applications with LLMs / agents (35% of job postings mention AI agents)
  • Experience talking directly with customers (even a different department within your own company counts fully)
  • Implementation experience around authentication, permissions, and data governance (this is the substance of the integration barrier)
  • Domain knowledge in regulated industries (76% of job postings are in finance, healthcare, insurance, and the public sector)

As a realistic thing you can start doing tomorrow, I think it's being able to state in numbers, for the systems you're currently involved with, "whose work did this improve, and by how much." If you can't say it, FDE-like work begins with going out to investigate that. After my failure at the beginning, I made a habit of doing this every time.

Also consider the risks

Since this is a hyped-up role, I should also calmly point out some things to keep in mind.

Some believe this role will disappear within 5-10 years. TechCrunch's coverage also quotes a remark that "in two years everything will be automated, and agents may be automating agents." In fact, Palantir offers an agent called "AI FDE" that operates Foundry via natural language. The fact that a job title has become a product name is quite telling.

There is a risk of burnout. Job postings explicitly mention "debugging production at 2 a.m." The structure of being at the mercy of client companies is no different from SES. Whether a company has its own product is a matter of the company's structure; it doesn't automatically improve an individual's working conditions.

Title inflation is also happening. Names have splintered into "Forward Deployed AI Engineer," "Applied AI Engineer," "Deployment Solutions Engineer," and some postings are essentially no different from sales engineers. Checking whether there are sales quotas and whether you have commit access to the product's code is, I think, the practical way to tell them apart.

Summary

  • An FDE is an engineer who goes into the customer's environment and solves problems using their own company's product. Palantir created the role to deal with "customers for whom requirements cannot be defined."
  • The decisive difference from on-site client work is whether you have a product. Individual solutions feed back into the product and accumulate as company assets.
  • Job postings are up 1,165% year over year (Revealera survey). However, figures vary widely depending on the source, so don't trust any single number.
  • The reason for growth is the "integration wall." MIT's research found that 95% of generative AI pilots have not produced clear revenue contributions.
  • Because AI has made implementation cheap, the bottleneck has shifted to either end. Value is concentrating on the "two extremes": problem definition and organizational implementation.
  • What's needed is not an AI specialist but a solidly strong implementer who can also use AI. Python 66%, RAG 12%.
  • In Japan too, organized adoption has begun at companies like LayerX, Loglass, and SmartHR.
  • Expertise in the AI era is the ability to make decisions with incomplete information. Five skills: problem definition, judgment under constraints, evaluation design, organizational implementation, and generalization.

Back to the project from the beginning. It wasn't that my technical skills were lacking back then. What was lacking was an approach that identified, within the first week, "who in the company can decide who is allowed to see these search results."

AI helps with writing code. But the work of figuring out "who to ask" will remain with us for a while yet. I think that's probably what it means for the center of gravity of expertise to be shifting.
