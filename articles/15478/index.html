Teaching a customer’s engineers your system

FDE Interview Prep · 2026 · 2 min read

Summary

A practice FDE interview prompt about teaching a customer's engineers to run your system without you. AWS states its model aims for customer self-sufficiency when deployments end, and Ramp engineering director Leo Mehr notes candidates with genuine teaching experience handle customers well. The article outlines a five-part answer structure and warns that delivering a runbook proves you taught, not that the customer learned.

Key points

  • AWS invests $1 billion in embedding FDEs, with customer self-sufficiency as the stated goal.
  • Ramp's Leo Mehr says candidates who enjoyed instructing or TA work handle customers well.
  • A strong answer covers who inherited the system, defined ops tasks, teaching method, and a pre-set pass line.
  • The trap: equating delivery with handover—a runbook and walkthrough don't prove learning.
  • Likely follow-ups probe test failures, post-handover contact, and unready team members.

A practice prompt we wrote. No company or candidate report names it, so it carries no company tag.

How to answer

The second half of the question separates answers. AWS names self-sufficiency as the goal: it says its model is designed so customers are self-sufficient when a deployment ends. Source 1AWS invests $1 billion to embed AI forward deployed engineers with customersPublisherAbout AmazonSource typecompany blog Leo Mehr, a director of engineering at Ramp, wrote on Ramp’s engineering blog, in August 2025, that among FDE candidates, people who have been instructors or teaching assistants, and actually enjoyed it, have the ability and interest to handle customers well. Source 2Forward Deployed Engineering (Leo Mehr, Director, Engineering)PublisherRamp Builders (engineering blog)Source typecompany blog Teaching experience helps, but this question asks for different evidence: proof they ran it without you.

  • What you built and who inherited it. Their team, what they already knew and what was new to them: a language, a model, an on-call rotation.
  • What “run it” meant. The jobs they had to do without you, chosen before you taught: deploy a change, answer the alerts, rerun a failed batch, rotate a credential, re-run the evaluation before changing a threshold.
  • How you taught. They drove while you watched, and your involvement shrank on a schedule. Every question they asked became a runbook edit, so the document was written from their confusion, not your knowledge.
  • The test, with a pass line set in advance. A drill or real incident they handled while you stayed hands-off. What broke, what they fixed, what they missed and what you changed because of the miss.
  • The transfer, then what happened after you left. The alerts moved to their pager and your access was removed. Then a later incident or change they handled on their own, and what, if anything, they still asked you.

Step four answers “how did you know?”. Name what would have counted as failing it. The trap is equating delivery with handover: a runbook written and a walkthrough held prove you taught, not that they learned.

Forward deployed engineerA software engineer who builds and ships production systems inside a customer’s problem and environment, accountable to that customer’s outcome.

Follow-ups

What the interviewer may ask next, once your first answer is on the table.

  • What did they get wrong in the test, and what did you change because of it?
  • When did they last contact you after the handover, and about what?
  • Who on their side was not ready, and what did you do about it?

Where answers go wrong

  • Offers the runbook and the training session as the proof, so “how did you know they could?” goes unanswered.
  • Tells a story in which the customer still calls them for every incident, which shows the system was never handed over.

Answer this in two minutes

Write the answer you would say out loud. The clock starts with your first word.
