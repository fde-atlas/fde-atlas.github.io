The First Step in AI Utilization Is "Connecting Information" | A PM's Practice of Creating Time to Focus on Customers Through Systems | A Career Support Media Specializing in FDEs, Engineers, and IT Freelancers

Original title: AI活用の1丁目1番地は「情報をつなぐこと」｜お客さまに向き合う時間を、仕組みで生み出すPMの実践 | FDE/エンジニア/ITフリーランス特化のキャリア支援メディア

Summary

This interview with a project manager (referred to as D) working on financial and insurance system development explains his approach to AI adoption. His central claim: the first step is securely connecting AI to approved business information—without that, tools like Cursor or NotebookLM stay underused. He automated recurring work so freed time goes to customer-facing thinking. Concrete examples: AI drafts meeting minutes from transcripts and auto-posts them to a Wiki after roughly five minutes of human review; UAT findings are auto-registered as GitHub Issues with response proposals and likely code locations; daily management-sheet checks that took about an hour per PM were streamlined, an estimated 4,800 hours saved annually across a 20-person team. He built a shared hub called Master Brain and plans cross-project WBS visibility to spot overloaded engineers. He also prototyped a browser-based fix with Cursor on-site and previously proposed a system rebuild targeting halving an annual 140 million yen maintenance cost.

Key points

  • Connecting AI to approved business information is the essential first step.
  • AI-generated minutes are auto-posted to Wiki after ~5 minutes of review.
  • UAT findings become GitHub Issues with response proposals and code leads.
  • Estimated 4,800 hours saved yearly across a 20-PM organization.
  • He built a shared hub (Master Brain) and plans cross-project WBS visibility.

"What makes me happy at work is when something I came up with brings joy to someone else. I want to increase the time I can spend on that."

Mr. D, a project manager (PM) involved in system development for financial institutions, explains why he uses AI.

Creating meeting minutes, compiling issues, checking progress.

All of these are essential tasks for moving a project forward.

However, when you're swamped with searching for, gathering, and transcribing information, you don't have enough time to think deeply about customers' challenges or act quickly on team problems.

What Mr. D wants to change is how that time is used.

Leave repetitive tasks to systems, and use the time gained to improve the quality of proposals and decisions.

His starting point was safely connecting AI with the information needed for his work.

In the finance and insurance sector, where strict security considerations are required, how did he create an environment where AI could be used and incorporate it into his daily work?

We asked him about the background of his initiatives and where things stand now.

From programmer and systems engineer (SE) to PM.

Has experience in system development in the finance and insurance sector, as well as on the user side.

Has also worked on system rebuilding and cloud migration, and is working on building systems that reduce workload and improving work through AI.

Wanting more time to think, in order to delight customers

What kind of work are you doing now?

I work as a PM for system development, mainly in the finance and insurance sector.

My job is to stand between the customer and the development team and organize issues and approaches.

While working on multiple projects, I started thinking about how I could address more issues while maintaining the quality of my work.

Opening documents for each project, checking the status, and compiling reports.

The more work I take on, the more time this takes.

That's why I want to leave repetitive tasks to systems and secure time for PMs to think and make decisions.

You say you "hate tedious tasks." What's behind that statement?

It's a waste to spend a lot of time every time on things that could be shortened by organizing the steps.

For example, tasks like gathering fixed information or copying the same content to another place.

These are necessary tasks, but I believe that if we change the system, they could be done with much less burden.

Instead, I want to think about what customers are really struggling with and how to solve it.

I want to spend time refining how I explain things, comparing multiple options, and finding issues that haven't yet been noticed.

What makes me happy at work is when I can bring joy to someone. When something I thought hard about is useful to that person. That's where I find fulfillment in my work.

But if daily tasks use up all my time, I can't think things through sufficiently.

The quality of proposals and the ability to act proactively also suffer.

That's exactly why I want to create time for thinking through systems.

Is that way of thinking related to your career so far?

I started as a programmer, then worked as an SE doing design, requirements definition, and coordination with customers.

There was also a period when I was seconded to a user department and worked as a member of an insurance company.

When you're on the user side, you can see difficulties that weren't visible from the system-building side.

I also heard voices saying, "I've always wanted to do this, but it hasn't been realized."

As I proposed plans to solve those issues and moved them forward as projects, I came to be entrusted with the PM role.

Because of that background, for me, listening to customers' difficulties and thinking about how to change things is the origin of a PM's work.

Even if you start using it, it's hard to make it stick without connecting to information

How did AI usage spread?

I myself have been using Cursor.

I've also seen firsthand that when an environment is set up where people can use tools, adoption spreads rapidly.

Once people start using them, each person begins experimenting with "how could I use this for my own work?"

I find it interesting to see those movements emerge.

However, I thought that whether it becomes part of daily work depends greatly on whether it's connected to the necessary information.

When you actually tried using it, what challenges did you feel?

I felt that if you can't access project information, it's difficult to entrust daily work to it.

It can be used for polishing text and generating ideas.

But what I want to know is things like "what happened on this project yesterday" and "where is work currently stalled."

For that, it needs to be able to reference meeting minutes, issues, and past history.

If a person has to search for documents and hand them to the AI every time, the effort of gathering information remains.

When preparation before use takes effort, the busier you are, the more likely you are to return to your usual methods.

Even with tools available, it's hard to reach the point of continuing to use them in everyday work.

For tasks involving Google Drive documents, there were also situations where NotebookLM was more convenient with my usage at the time.

From this experience, I realized that to make AI stick in daily work, it's essential to make it able to reference the necessary information and handle the work at hand.

What barriers did you face in connecting to information?

Since it involves handling business information, we need to confirm which tools can be connected and the scope of information allowed.

Even if I want to try things myself, there are parts that can't proceed on individual judgment alone.

Especially in fields like finance and insurance, where careful handling of information is required, organizing usage rules and connection methods is essential.

I felt that to enable the field to use it without hesitation, the environment needs to be set up, including that preparation.

Once "ready for work use" is in place, individual ingenuity begins

What exactly does "ready to use for work" mean?

It means being able to access the necessary documents and issues through approved methods so you can proceed with the work in front of you.

You also need to make sure people know the setup and steps required to start using it.

When you ask it to "summarize what was decided in yesterday's meeting" or "tell me which issues have stalled," it returns answers based on the necessary information. Once that works, it becomes easier to imagine using it in everyday work.

If people don't know how far they're allowed to go with it, or if preparing to hand over documents takes a long time, that first step becomes heavy.

I believe reducing that effort and hesitation is essential.

What changes occur once it becomes usable?

Once it proves useful for one task, people start trying it out thinking, "Maybe I can use this for other work too."

Some people create meeting minutes, others organize issues, and others create manuals.

Each person starts innovating based on their own pain points.

I myself have tried many things with Cursor to see how it could change my daily work.

Seeing such experimentation increase around me made me realize how significant it is to build a usable environment.

Connecting to the necessary information and seeing that it helps with your own work—that's where users' innovation begins.

What do you prioritize in expanding adoption?

Concretely setting things up so that each person can actually start using it within their work.

Where is the necessary information, how do you connect to it, and which task should you try first?

Once those are clear, it becomes easier to get started.

If people have an experience that's useful in their work, they develop their own reasons to try it.

I want to build that foundation first, so that users can keep refining their approach.

When I say "the first step of AI adoption is connecting information," I mean creating a state where people can access the necessary information through approved methods.

On top of that, refine it until it's usable in daily work.

I want to think of all of that as the job of those driving AI adoption.

Reduce time spent gathering information and create time for making decisions

How much time did checking information take before?

As a PM, I check tasks, progress, issues, and bug status every day.

Previously, just opening each management sheet one by one and cross-referencing the information took about an hour per person per day.

This is only a rough estimate, but in an organization with 20 PMs, that's 20 hours a day, or roughly 4,800 hours over 240 business days a year.

I wanted to think about how to handle problems when they arise, but the checking beforehand was eating up my time.

While opening and checking each management sheet one by one, the time available for dealing with problems kept shrinking.

So I made the management sheets accessible from a program, allowing AI to compile and review task, progress, issue, and bug status all at once.

By shortening the time spent gathering information, I now use that time to address problems instead.

I haven't yet measured exactly how much time was saved.

I can also now do the initial investigation of bugs myself.

Previously, I would start by asking the engineers.

Now, I check the bug details, which environment it appears in, and whether it's been fixed before taking action.

What specific tasks do you use AI for?

The first thing I tackled was meeting minutes.

Every meeting requires preparing records and sharing them with the client.

That task used to take a significant amount of time each time.

Depending on the company or meeting, creating minutes can take one to two hours.

I wanted to secure more time to turn meeting decisions into next actions.

The flow consists of the following three steps.

First, based on the meeting transcript, the AI creates minutes in a predefined format.

Next, I visually review the content and, if there are errors or corrections, I instruct the AI to fix them.

If there are no problems, I give the instruction "OK to post," and it automatically posts to the Wiki of the issue management tool we use with the client.

Accuracy has improved, so corrections are not that many, and review takes about five minutes.

Initially, you need to compile the format and creation instructions as a Skill (a set of procedures for delegating work to AI).

After that, though, you can run projects using this flow.

People can focus on the meeting itself, and afterward, they can immediately move on to decisions made and required actions.

The effect of eliminating the burden of writing and polishing from scratch is significant.

I hear you also use AI for bug management.

Yes.

Taking UAT (user acceptance testing) as an example, I use it at two stages: "when issues are raised" and "when verifying that fixes have been applied."

First, I have Cursor read the issues raised in a spreadsheet and automatically register all of them as GitHub Issues.

Then, I have it investigate and present a draft response policy for each issue, along with the parts of the program likely requiring fixes.

Based on those results, a human makes judgments such as "matches the spec, no fix needed," "fix required," or "requirements need confirmation."

When the PM side uses AI to write into the Issue information narrowing down where problems might be, engineers can more easily start their investigation and fixes.

In addition to the issue details, the starting point for investigation can be shared, which has the benefit of speeding up responses.

Next is verifying fixes after engineers have addressed them.

I have the list of issues in the spreadsheet cross-checked against the response details on GitHub to confirm nothing has been missed.

In addition, I have them investigate the deployment time and deployment details to the target environment, and confirm that the necessary fixes are reflected in the program.

Then, for the items that are deemed fine, I personally do a visual check in the actual environment at the end, and if there are no problems, I ask the customer to confirm.

If done entirely by hand, just searching for and cross-checking the information takes a considerable amount of time.

By leaving the investigation and organization to AI, I can focus on deciding the response policy and verifying the actual deployment results.

In both cases, the idea is to leave information gathering, investigation, organization, and transcription to AI, while people spend their time on judgment and verifying results.

How would you like to change the way you grasp the overall status of a project?

What I'm aiming for is a state where everything that happened the previous day arrives in a consolidated form every morning.

Looking at that, we respond where there are signs of delays or problems.

I want to reduce the time spent gathering information for each project and use that time for necessary decisions and support.

This part is still in the process of being built out.

I'd like to connect what we've achieved with meeting minutes and issue management to grasping the overall project status.

Turning one person's ingenuity into something the whole team can use

What challenges arose after it became usable by individuals?

Once everyone starts using AI, useful tools and usage methods emerge in all sorts of places.

There has also been an increase in people sharing things like "I made this."

However, as the number grows, it becomes hard to know who made what, and for what purpose.

Even if something useful exists, it won't be utilized unless it reaches the people who need it.

How do you share individual ingenuity?

We created a mechanism called the "Master Brain" that consolidates tools and usage methods in one place.

It's the shared entry point where "you'll understand if you look here first."

Whether it's creating agents or making manuals with AI, I want each person's ingenuity to be usable by others.

Is there anything you're planning to tackle next?

I'm thinking about making WBS information viewable across projects.

A WBS is a tool that breaks down a project's work into fine details and organizes it.

When managed per project, it becomes difficult to grasp the total workload across the board.

Creating one individually has become easier than before, since AI can help with that too.

What I want to make visible next is the overall workload.

Engineers involved in multiple projects may be carrying a heavy burden from overlapping work, yet that situation can be hard for others to see.

I want to notice busy members early and be able to help them. That's why I want to gather this information.

When the team loses its slack, it also affects how we serve our customers.

If we can grasp imbalances in workload, I believe we can rethink our approach before it's too late.

How are you planning to make this happen?

I'm considering creating an entry point in each project's folder that shows where the WBS is located.

The management location can be either an issue management tool or a file on Google Drive.

First, I want people to be able to reach each project's WBS just by looking there.

On top of that, I aim to reach a state where we can look at each person's overlapping work and workload, and connect that to necessary support.

Shaping things on the spot moves problem-solving conversations forward

Are there examples where you used AI to give concrete form to a customer's problem?

I was once consulted about whether the application procedure could be made simpler.

For users, the problem was that the required confirmations during the application process were cumbersome, causing them to drop out at that point.

So, using Cursor, I built a prototype that runs in a browser, enabling confirmation with simpler operations.

Once you have something you can actually touch, you can concretely verify how it would be operated and where the remaining friction lies.

Since it was something that ran on HTML, building it itself was simple.

Even so, I was able to tell the customer concretely, "This looks like it will work."

To adopt it in the actual service, we would need to examine whether the necessary requirements can be met.

What mattered most was that, at that preliminary stage, we were able to turn the solution into something testable.

How do you view a way of working where you find problems in the field and give them form on the spot?

When you enter the customer's workplace, you can see concretely where they are struggling.

Based on what I hear there, I feel my value lies in being able to shape things into "wouldn't this make a difference?"

Like FDEs (Forward Deployed Engineers), who work on-site with customers and are involved from problem discovery through implementation, I empathize with that way of working in that respect.

When you can try things with AI, even things that were hard to convey with words alone can be discussed while touching them together.

It's great that we can think about solutions more concretely with the customer.

Does that attitude also connect to your earlier proposal for rebuilding the system?

Yes.

The system I previously managed had business rules that changed every fiscal year, requiring modifications and testing each time.

Handling roughly the same things every year cost about 140 million yen annually.

It was work sustained by the experience and effort of the people in charge, but since the same burden was incurred every year, I wondered whether the structure itself could be rethought.

What prompted me to consider rebuilding was when I was seconded to the user's department and was told, "We've been asking for 10 years, but every person in charge has refused."

When I heard they had been struggling for a long time, I thought, if that's the case, I want to do something about it.

I also told the colleagues I was developing with, "I don't want to repeat the same work next year."

So, with the goal of cutting annual costs in half, I proposed rebuilding the system and moving it to the cloud.

Make the data easier to use and reduce the burden of development and operations.

Beyond that, I wanted to achieve what our customers actually wanted to do.

To avoid having to rebuild everything from scratch at the start of each fiscal year, I standardized the way we handle these tasks.

I also built a platform that allows automated testing and moved it to the cloud.

As a result, the system also became easier to apply AI to later on.

I don't have the post-release cost figures on hand, so I can't say for certain that we cut costs in half.

However, this rebuild did establish the mechanisms for reducing the yearly burden of modifications and testing.

Our current use of AI follows the same approach.

I want to reduce recurring burdens so we can tackle challenges we couldn't reach before.

Getting What You Build Used in Daily Work

What do you find difficult as you move these initiatives forward?

Getting people to use what I've built in their everyday work.

Everyone is enthusiastic about building useful things.

But if it's not clear where something is located or in what situations it's useful, other people can't use it.

I feel that to make individual ingenuity benefit the whole team, you have to think as far as making things easy to find and explaining how to use them.

If there's a shared location, will things naturally get used?

Even if you provide a common place, individually managed items can remain, and the people who need them may not come to look.

In daily work, you need to think through to the reason for going there.

So I want to gather the WBS information I mentioned earlier and create a reason to visit it as part of daily work.

I'd like people to be able to reach others' ingenuity while checking the information they need.

What stage are your efforts to change how work is done at?

We have reduced manual work in some areas, such as preparing meeting minutes and compiling UAT feedback.

On the other hand, there's still room for improvement in grasping the overall situation and sharing information.

We also haven't yet reached the goal of increasing the number of projects we handle.

Right now, we're in the middle of the road, steadily accumulating these improvements.

If increasing the number of projects we handle means our service to customers gets thinner, we'd drift away from what we're aiming for.

How can we be involved in more projects while securing time to think and engage?

That's what I want to work out.

Do you also use AI for things you're not good at?

I want to.

For example, I don't have much confidence in putting materials together in an easy-to-understand way.

Even if you have ideas, if they don't get across, they won't help the other person make decisions.

I'd have AI help with organizing and expressing things, turning my thinking into a form that's easy for others to understand.

I'd like to expand that kind of usage too.

Growing the System and Increasing Time to Face Customers

How do you think the PM role will change going forward?

I think work like gathering information, organizing it, and reporting will become even more automated.

I'd like to make my own work run automatically as much as possible.

Right now, I'm putting in place what's needed for that, one piece at a time.

Beyond that, what kind of work do you want to spend your time on?

Thinking about what customers truly need, and choosing the better path together from among several options.

When the team is struggling, I want to notice quickly and be of help.

That kind of work can't simply be cut just because time is short.

I believe whether you can think things through sufficiently affects the results.

That's why I want to use the time created by systems for that.

Do you have anything to say to companies that want to advance their use of AI?

First, I think it's good to be specific about the tasks you want to reduce and the work you want to spend that time on instead.

Then, build an environment where AI can safely connect to the information it needs.

And make sure the setup for getting started is clear, along with which tasks it's useful for.

When people can feel it's useful in their own work, they start thinking about the next way to use it.

If that ingenuity reaches others, the whole team can accumulate improvements.

As for me, I want to keep doing work where I find challenges on the customer's site and think about how to change them.

When something I thought up makes someone happy. Increasing the time for that is what using AI means to me.

Precisely because these are necessary tasks, there's room to reconsider the manual work each time.

Mr. D's initiatives are built on that line-drawing.

"Connecting information" is the starting point for that.

Being able to reference the necessary information and move the work at hand forward.

When such an environment is in place, each person starts devising ways to solve their own problems.

On the other hand, even with the environment in place, there's one more step before it keeps being used in the field.

Create a reason to use it, make it easy to find, and let people start without hesitation.

This is a part that tends to be put off if you try to advance it only within the company.
