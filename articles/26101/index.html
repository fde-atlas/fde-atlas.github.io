Why Doesn't Data Quality Improvement Move Forward?

The biggest reason data quality improvement stalls is that poor quality is left unaddressed as a shared cost that belongs to no one. Unlike system failures, no alarms go off, and the field absorbs it through manual work, so problems remain invisible and are preserved for years. Gartner estimates that poor data quality costs organizations an average of $12.9 million per year, but this loss never arrives as an invoice. What arrives are only individual issues like "the totals don't match" or "we contacted the customer twice."

As an FDE (Forward Deployed Engineer) working in the field, this pattern appears in similar forms across industries. Before building dashboards, first go look at the actual state of missing values and notation inconsistencies in the source data—initiatives that skip this step almost invariably stall with "the numbers can't be trusted."

Who Suffers First from Poor Data Quality?

The first to suffer from degraded data quality is the field, and it manifests as invisible overtime spent on verification and rework. What reaches management is only the conclusion that "AI accuracy isn't materializing," but behind that, staff are matching records in Excel every morning and sales reps are calling customers to re-confirm addresses.

The more experienced the person, the faster this absorption work goes—and the faster it goes, the less it gets reported as a problem. As a result, ad-hoc corrections become embedded in business processes, and quality problems surface all at once the moment that person transfers. I believe a realistic starting point for improvement is not technical measurement, but counting "who is currently spending how many minutes fixing which data."

Why Does "Fix It Later" Keep Piling Up?

"Fix it later" piles up because quality improvement tends to become work with neither a deadline nor an owner. New features have requirements and release dates, but remediation of existing data has no due date.

Moreover, the benefits of a fix appear not in the department that made it, but in downstream departments that use the data. The department bearing extra input effort and the department receiving the benefit don't align—this asymmetry creates a wall that goodwill alone cannot overcome. That's why improvement must be designed not as a plea to "try harder when entering data," but as changes to the system itself: reducing input options, narrowing required fields, and auto-correcting downstream.

What Should You Measure to Improve Data Quality?

What you should measure are the six dimensions—accuracy, completeness, consistency, timeliness, uniqueness, and validity—plus the threshold values at which business breaks. Quality is a relative value against a use case, not an absolute one, so measurement without deciding "at what percentage can the business run" ends as just staring at numbers.

What Are the Six Dimensions for Measuring Data Quality?

The six dimensions are a standard classification organized by DAMA (Data Management Association), applicable to data in any industry. Since each is measured differently, mapping your own data to this framework once for an inventory will reveal which dimensions are missing.

DimensionQuestionTypical Measurement MethodSymptoms When Broken
CompletenessAre required values present?NULL rate and empty-string rate per fieldSilently excluded from aggregation targets
AccuracyDoes it match reality?Sampling and reconciliation with primary sources or on-site dataDecisions themselves are wrong
ConsistencyIs it consistent across systems?Count of value differences for the same keyDifferent departments produce different numbers
TimelinessIs it fresh enough?Distribution of elapsed time since last updateDecisions made on past states
UniquenessIs it free of duplicates?Duplicate rate of matching keysCustomer and record counts are inflated
ValidityDoes it conform to defined formats and ranges?Violation rate of types, digits, code values, and rangesFails with errors during integration

In practice, measuring accuracy is the most costly. Because it requires comparison against reality, estimate the error rate through sampling rather than checking all records, and measure the other five dimensions mechanically across all records—this division of labor is realistic.

Which Metrics Should You Start With?

The first metrics to tackle are completeness and timeliness, narrowed to columns that directly change business decisions. Trying to raise quality uniformly across all fields causes effort to diverge, and you lose sight of what actually worked.

The narrowing criterion is simple: ask the staff, "Does the business stop if this column is empty?" Sorting columns into three tiers—columns that stop the business, columns that cause hesitation, and columns that are merely reference—usually reduces the target to around 10-20 columns. Then, for each column, agree with the business side on pass thresholds such as "NULL rate of 5% or less" or "update delay within 24 hours." The key point is to base target values not on industry benchmarks, but on the threshold at which your own business breaks down.

What Is the Fastest Sequence for Data Quality Improvement?

The fastest approach is to run one cycle of the five stages—visualization, agreement, upstream fixes, automated detection, and operational handover—on a narrowed scope. Rather than targeting all company-wide data, running all five stages through on one business process and one table lets you reuse the pattern for subsequent rollouts.

What Is the Overview of the Five Stages?

The five stages follow the flow of measure, decide, fix, monitor, and hand over; skipping any stage will always force you back. The most common failure is starting with fixes without visualization first, leaving you unable to explain "how much things improved."

StageWhat to DoKey DeliverableGuideline Duration
1. VisualizationMeasure the six dimensions of the target data and establish current valuesQuality report1-2 weeks
2. AgreementSet pass thresholds and responsible departments per columnQuality standards document1-2 weeks
3. Upstream fixesFix input screens, master data, and integration specsFix patches, operational changes3-8 weeks
4. Automated detectionImplement threshold monitoring and notificationsDetection rules, notification flows1-3 weeks
5. Handover to operationsTransfer ownership to internal staffOperations manual, handover2-4 weeks

Since the duration varies greatly depending on data size and the number of departments involved, these are only rough guidelines for a single small-scale cycle. What matters is not the length of each stage, but whether all five stages are included in the design.

What counts as "done" at each stage?

Completion is judged not by the existence of deliverables, but by whether the person responsible for the next stage can proceed without hesitation. Merely creating a report or writing standards does not move things forward.

  • Completion criteria for visualization: Current values for each target column are output as numbers, sorted from worst to best.
  • Completion criteria for agreement: Pass thresholds and responsible department names are filled in for each column, and the business side has approved them.
  • Completion criteria for upstream fixes: Metrics meet the pass thresholds in new data ingested after the fixes.
  • Completion criteria for automated detection: Intentionally invalid data has been fed in, and receipt of notifications has been confirmed.
  • Completion criteria for handover to operations: Internal staff can independently change and reconfigure thresholds.

The fourth stage, "intentionally breaking things to test," tends to be skipped, but if you skip it, you won't notice the monitoring isn't working until six months later.

Where do things tend to stall?

The most common sticking point is the third stage, upstream fixes, which involves changes to other departments' operations and therefore takes time to reach agreement. Even if technically it's just a matter of making fields required or unifying code values, operational changes on the ground cause coordination costs to skyrocket.

To get past this, the numbers created in stages one and two are effective. If you can concretely show "how many inquiries and how many hours of rework this column's missing data causes per month," the discussion shifts from likes and dislikes to cost-effectiveness. Conversely, proposing new input rules without numbers will be received purely as an increased burden on the field. Show upfront what the department bearing the temporary burden will get in return—this is a job of design and negotiation, not technology.

How do you build an operational structure to maintain data quality?

The conditions for maintenance are assigning a single owner to each dataset and establishing a mechanism that detects breakage before people do. Improvement ends as a one-time project, but quality inevitably degrades if left unattended. Organizational changes, system upgrades, the addition of new business partners—triggers for degradation are countless in daily operations.

Who should be the quality owner?

The owner should be the business department that uses the data most, with the IT department handling the infrastructure side. If quality responsibility is shifted to the IT department, only format checks will increase, without anyone able to judge whether the values are actually correct.

RoleAssigned toScope of responsibility
Data ownerManager of the business department using the dataSetting pass thresholds, business decisions when deviations occur
Data stewardOperational staff in the business departmentDaily value checks, first-line handling of exceptions
Platform teamIT / data platform departmentImplementing detection, maintaining pipelines
Improvement promotionFDE / data teamDesigning upstream fixes, building consensus across departments

These four roles do not need to be full-time dedicated positions; concurrent assignments are fine. What matters is that each dataset has exactly one defined owner. A structure of "everyone protecting quality together" effectively becomes a structure where no one protects it.

How do you detect breakage early?

For detection, simply monitoring three things daily against thresholds—record counts, NULL rates, and freshness—will catch most incidents. Before introducing advanced anomaly detection algorithms, covering these three points should offer better cost-effectiveness.

  • Record counts: Notify when day-over-day or same-weekday-week-over-week comparisons fall outside a set range (detecting stopped integrations or duplicate imports).
  • NULL rate: Notify when the pass threshold is exceeded (detecting input spec changes or side effects of screen modifications).
  • Freshness: Notify when elapsed time since the last update exceeds a threshold (detecting job failures or upstream delays).

What makes operations effective is how notifications are sent. If everything is sent by email, it will inevitably be ignored, so direct notifications to a business team channel rather than to individual staff, and write "who should do what today" rather than "what happened." In my experience, this one extra step determines whether monitoring stays alive or dies.

How far is FDE involved in improving data quality?

FDEs consistently handle everything from visualizing quality to building consensus on upstream fixes, implementing detection, and handing over to in-house teams. This is because if the person writing SQL and the person coordinating with the business are separate, things will inevitably stall at stage three. Palantir's concept of Ontology also overlaps with the idea of redefining quality as a business requirement, in that it treats data in connection with its business meaning.

What scope does FDE handle in the field?

An FDE's role is to fill the "no one's responsibility" gaps at the boundary between data and business. It is neither just building data infrastructure nor just business consulting.

Specifically, they go into the field, observe manual corrections, translate them into metrics, implement fix proposals, and explain them to the relevant departments—running this loop alone (or with a small team). Context that would be lost through a game of telephone if roles were divided is preserved by being able to go back and forth. The FDE role itself is outlined in What is FDE.

That said, it is not correct for the FDE to take on everything. Pipeline operations and infrastructure maintenance must be shared with the IT department from the start, or there will be nothing to hand over when the handover time comes.

How do you hand over to in-house teams?

In the handover, rather than the rules themselves, pass on the reasoning behind why each threshold was set. If you hand over only numbers, no one will be able to update them when the business changes, and monitoring will become a formality.

What should be documented at handover is three things: (1) the business boundary that justified each threshold, (2) first-response procedures and contacts when a deviation occurs, and (3) when to review thresholds (at system upgrades, quarterly, etc.). With these in place, internal staff can grow the standards themselves. The phased approach from implementation to in-housing is covered in detail in How to Implement Generative AI.

What should you read next?

For giving meaning to data, "Ontology" is the match; for measuring results, "ROI and KPI" is the match. The approach of designing quality in connection with business meaning is summarized in What is Palantir Ontology, and the method of demonstrating improvement effects with numbers is summarized in How to measure FDE ROI and KPI.

For the five stages in this article, rather than perfecting any single one, the value lies in running through the full cycle on a small target. Start by selecting the single most-used table in your own department and measuring it against the six perspectives—this may seem like a detour, but it is actually the shortest path.
