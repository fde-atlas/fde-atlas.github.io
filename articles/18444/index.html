What is in-housing? A roadmap for breaking free from outsourcing dependency

Original title: 内製化とは？外注依存から抜け出すロードマップ

株式会社Sai · 2026-07-05 · 9 min read

Summary

This article by Japan's Sai explains in-house development (naiseika): taking back system development, operation and improvement from external vendors. Its real value, the author argues, is speed and organizational learning, not cost savings. A three-stage roadmap—co-creation, handover, self-sufficiency—avoids the pitfalls of going fully in-house at once. Citing IPA and Ministry of Internal Affairs surveys, it notes 85.1% of Japanese firms lack DX talent and many struggle to find effective AI uses. Generative AI lowers the entry barrier for non-engineers. The piece closes with three criteria for choosing an in-housing partner and promotes Sai's FDE-based service.

Key points

  • In-housing aims at speed and learning, not cutting outsourcing costs.
  • Progress through co-creation, handover, then self-sufficiency stages.
  • Generative AI lets business staff build tools without coding experience.
  • IPA survey: 85.1% of Japanese firms report DX talent shortages.
  • Pick partners who promise skill transfer, work on-site, and plan their exit.

"We leave everything system-related to the vendor. Even a small fix requires a quote and weeks of lead time." — This structure, common in many companies, becomes a serious handicap in the fast-changing AI era. That is why in-housing is attracting attention.

However, in-housing does not mean "stop outsourcing and build everything ourselves." This article explains the true meaning of in-housing and a realistic way to proceed.

What Is In-Housing?

In-housing means making it possible to handle the development, operation, and improvement of systems previously entrusted to external vendors with your own team members.

Importantly, the purpose of in-housing is not "reducing outsourcing costs." Its essential value lies in speed and learning.

  • A "we want to change this" from the field takes shape within the same week, without quotes or purchase orders
  • The build-test-fix cycle runs in-house, and business know-how accumulates in the system
  • You can independently "try new technologies like AI on a small scale first"

One caveat: these effects tend to go unmeasured. In a survey by IPA of companies in Japan, the US, and Germany, fewer than 30% of Japanese companies said they had "set" metrics for tracking DX outcomes, versus over 80% in the US and Germany. Among Japanese companies working on DX, 26.2% answered "don't know" regarding outcomes (IPA "DX Trends 2025" press release, published June 26, 2025 / survey conducted February 10 – March 28, 2025). The same thing happens with in-housing. If you only watch outsourcing costs go up and down, the real benefits—speed and learning—never show up in the record.

Pros and Cons of In-Housing

Pros

  • Improvement speed increases by orders of magnitude: fixes that take weeks when outsourced take same-day to a few days in-house
  • No more requirements-definition waste: the people who know the business build it, eliminating rework from the "telephone game"
  • Resilience to change: in areas like generative AI where the landscape shifts monthly, organizations that can experiment themselves are overwhelmingly advantaged
  • Costs shift from variable to fixed: the more you iterate, the lower the per-item cost

Cons and Cautions

  • Talent recruitment and training are required: hiring immediately productive engineers is difficult and time-consuming
  • Risk of knowledge silos: a key person's departure can turn the system into a black box
  • Ensuring quality and security: some areas are dangerous without expert review

The first point is also a challenge for Japanese companies as a whole. In the same IPA survey, 85.1% of Japanese companies said they lack personnel to drive DX (IPA Discussion Paper 2025-02, "Developing Digital Talent in the AI Era", published October 9, 2025). In the US and Germany, the combined share answering "neither shortage nor surplus" or "slight surplus" was roughly 70% and 50% respectively—Japan is the most serious of the three countries (IPA "DX Trends 2025" press release, June 26, 2025). It is safer to assume that plans premised on filling headcount through hiring will easily fall apart in this market.

In other words, "jumping straight to full in-housing" is an approach that hits the drawbacks head-on. You need to proceed in stages.

A Three-Stage Roadmap for Breaking Free from Outsourcing Dependence

Before getting into the process, it helps to understand where outsourcing stalls, making it easier to plan. The structure of outsourcing itself is organized in 5 Patterns of New Business Outsourcing Failures.

Stage 1: Co-Creation (Building Together)

This is the stage where external engineers and your own members build together on the same team. The key point: rather than "placing orders" externally, external professionals accompany internal members as they do the hands-on work. Here, design thinking, tool usage, and how to get past stumbling blocks begin to transfer in-house.

The emergence of generative AI has dramatically lowered the barrier at this stage. We are in an era where even business staff without programming experience can build business tools with AI assistance. In fact, in a case study of a BCP support company, starting with AI education enabled the staff themselves to reach the point of building business automation.

That said, being able to use a tool is different from the business actually changing. According to the Ministry of Internal Affairs and Communications' 2025 (Reiwa 7) Information and Communications White Paper, 55.2% of Japanese companies use generative AI for some business tasks. The white paper notes this figure is an estimate based on respondents aware of their company's AI usage policy. The most commonly cited concern about adoption was "not knowing effective ways to use it" (Ministry of Internal Affairs and Communications, "2025 Information and Communications White Paper," Current State of AI Use in Companies, published July 2025 / FY2024 survey). Deciding what to use it for requires breaking down your own business processes. This is why external professionals enter the field during the co-creation stage.

Stage 2: Handover (Transferring Ownership)

This is the stage where development ownership moves in-house and external parties shift to review and advisory roles. Day-to-day improvements are completed in-house, with experts checking only critical decisions on security and design. At this stage, you also put in place documentation to prevent "knowledge silos" and knowledge sharing among multiple people.

Stage 3: Self-Sufficiency (Running It Yourselves)

This is the state where the in-house team alone can "build, test, and fix" for new business challenges. External partners are limited to catching up on new technologies and spot support for major builds. At this stage, hiring FDE-type talent in-house also becomes an option; for that decision, How to Choose Between Hiring an FDE and Using External Resources is a useful reference.

Three Checkpoints for Choosing an In-Housing Partner

Companies offering in-housing support have increased, but their actual practices vary widely. We recommend checking the following three points.

  • Promise "transfer," not "delivery" — If the deliverable is just a system, that's simply outsourced development. Check whether transferring skills and operational capability is included in the goal.
  • Get into the field — Support that consists only of documents and training leaves a gap with real work. The deciding factor is whether engineers work hands-on with actual operations and actual data alongside your team.
  • Is graduation designed in? — The structure of support businesses is that the longer support lasts, the more they earn. A partner you can trust is one that can show upfront "when and under what conditions support will be reduced."

Frequently asked questions about in-house capability building

Q. Do I need to hire engineers to start building in-house capability?

Not in the first stage of co-creation. You can start with a format where your own business staff work hands-on while external engineers support them alongside. 85.1% of Japanese companies lack personnel to drive DX (IPA Discussion Paper 2025-02 "Developing Digital Talent in the AI Era", published October 9, 2025). If hiring is the starting point, getting started itself tends to be postponed. Considering hiring can wait until the third stage of self-sufficiency is in sight.

Q. Will outsourcing costs go down if we build in-house capability?

Not necessarily. The work you previously ordered for each improvement moves in-house, but the effort of training and operations becomes a new in-house burden. The benefits of in-housing show up in speed and learning, so it fits reality better to evaluate not by outsourcing costs but by metrics like "days from starting work to reflecting changes" and "number of improvements fixed entirely in-house."

Q. With generative AI, can we build in-house capability without external support?

Having a tool in hand and changing how work is done are different matters. The most common concern among Japanese companies about adopting generative AI was "not knowing how to use it effectively" (Ministry of Internal Affairs and Communications "2025 White Paper on Information and Communications: Current State of AI Use in Companies", published July 2025 / FY2024 survey). Deciding what to use it for requires breaking down your own operations, and this process moves faster with an outside perspective. Merely contracting for a tool is not in-housing—it's the stage before getting started.

Q. If we push in-housing, won't knowledge just become person-dependent instead?

It can, depending on how you proceed. That's exactly why the second stage of transfer builds in documentation and knowledge sharing among multiple people. A state where only the person who built it can fix it has the same structure as one where only the vendor can fix it. Make "readable by whoever takes over" part of the completion criteria for the transfer.

SAI's perspective: In-housing starts with "building together," not "teaching"

Our FDE Accompanied Development at SAI Inc. is a service that puts this "co-creation → transfer → self-sufficiency" into practice. Engineers embed themselves in the customer's workplace, transfer knowledge to internal members while implementing, and the goal is designed as a state where your team can run everything on their own (self-sufficiency).

The process starts with observing on-site operations, places something working in the field within a few weeks, and transfers skills while building together with your members. We support on-site presence, visits, and remote work alike, and decide upfront on the plan for preparing documentation and operational structures while gradually tapering support.

Think of it as the FDE (Forward Deployed Engineer) model, which originated at Palantir, adapted into a form that mid-sized and small Japanese companies can use.
