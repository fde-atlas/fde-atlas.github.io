Summary

This long Japanese guide by Shohei Tokumitsu, CEO of DeFactory, explains SES (System Engineering Service) contracts in Japan's IT industry. Legally based on quasi-mandate (jun'inin) contracts under Article 656 of the Civil Code, SES pays engineers for their skills and hours worked rather than deliverables, distinguishing it from contractor contracts (which carry warranty liability) and worker dispatch (where the client holds command authority). The article compares four contract types, lists pros and cons for companies and engineers, and details the risk of disguised contracting when clients give direct orders. Using data from its job platform BizDev Tech (about 1,790 quasi-mandate listings), it reports an average monthly rate of 7.64 million... correction: 764,000 yen (median 720,000 yen), with consulting roles highest at a 1.15 million yen median. It concludes SES experience can be a stepping stone to higher-paying freelance direct contracts.

Key points

  • SES contracts are quasi-mandate contracts paying for hours and skills, not deliverables.
  • Key differences from contracting and dispatch lie in deliverable liability and command authority.
  • Clients giving direct work orders risks illegal disguised contracting; requests must go through the SES firm.
  • BizDev Tech data: average monthly rate 764,000 yen, median 720,000 yen across 1,511 listings.
  • Only about 31% of listings are fully on-site; hybrid work is most common at roughly 49%.

SES stands for "System Engineering Service." It refers to a work format in which IT engineers are stationed at the client's site and provide technical services such as system development, infrastructure construction, and operation and maintenance.

The most distinctive feature of SES contracts is that compensation is based not on "deliverables" but on "the provision of technical skills and working hours." In other words, even if a project is not completed, payment is made as long as the contracted hours and work are performed.

The SES market continues to expand amid the shortage of IT talent. According to the Survey on IT Talent Supply and Demand published by METI, if IT demand grows at a high rate, a shortage of up to approximately 790,000 IT professionals is projected by 2030 (approximately 450,000 even under the medium scenario). As a mechanism to fill this supply-demand gap, the importance of SES contracts continues to grow.

The legal basis of SES contracts is the "mandate contract (quasi-mandate contract)" stipulated in Articles 643 to 656 of the Civil Code. Article 656 defines a quasi-mandate contract as "entrusting affairs other than juristic acts." SES is positioned as a type of this quasi-mandate contract.

There are two types of quasi-mandate contracts.

  • Performance-ratio type: Compensation is generated according to hours worked and volume of work (this is the common form for SES contracts)
  • Completed-deliverable type: Compensation is generated on the condition that a specific deliverable is completed

Since SES contracts are in principle performance-ratio type quasi-mandate contracts, no liability for defects (warranty liability) arises with respect to the completion of deliverables. This is the fundamental difference from subcontract (ukeoi) contracts.

* Note: The 2020 Civil Code reform (reform of the law of obligations) codified the completed-deliverable type for quasi-mandate contracts. When drafting a contract, it is important to clearly state whether the performance-ratio type or completed-deliverable type is adopted.

SES contracts are typically structured among the following three parties (or more, in multi-layered structures).

PartyRoleExamples
Ordering company (client)The company where the engineer is stationed and actually works. It has no authority to give direct work instructionsFinancial institutions, operating companies, SIers
SES company (receiving company)Signs employment contracts with engineers and provides services to the client. Retains the authority to give work instructionsDedicated SES companies, IT vendors
EngineerBelongs to the SES company (or works under a business outsourcing arrangement) and provides technical services at the client's siteFull-time employees, freelancers, side-job workers

What is important in this three-party structure is the location of the "authority to give work instructions." The ordering company cannot issue direct work orders to the engineer; only indirect instructions through the SES company (or through the engineer themselves in the case of business outsourcing) are permitted. Getting this wrong leads to the risk of "disguised subcontracting" discussed later.

The impression that "SES means multi-layered subcontracting with deep transaction chains" is also often heard, but looking at the 183 quasi-mandate contract projects listed on BizDev Tech that include transaction chain information (the majority of the 1,790 total do not include it), 73 were "direct with end clients" and 94 were "direct with prime contractors," together accounting for 167 projects (about 91% of the total). Only 16 projects (about 9%) were "third-tier subcontractors," and no projects with deeper transaction chains were listed (as of September 2026, according to BizDev Tech). Since this tally covers only projects with transaction chain information, it cannot be said to represent the overall reality, but at least for projects on BizDev Tech that disclose their transaction chains, the results show that projects with shallow transaction chains are the mainstream.

SES contract compensation is generally based on a "monthly rate (man-month unit price)" and varies according to monthly working hours.

Specifically, the contract specifies a "standard hour range (e.g., 140–180 hours)" and "upper and lower limit hourly rates for hours outside the range."

Working hoursSettlement methodCalculation example (rate of 600,000 yen, standard 160h)
140–180 hours (standard range)Settled at the agreed rate600,000 yen
Exceeding the standard hoursExcess hours added at the hourly rate600,000 yen + (excess hours × 3,750 yen)
Falling below the standard hoursShortfall deducted at the hourly rate600,000 yen − (shortfall hours × 3,750 yen)

When engineers belong to an SES company, roughly 50–75% of the rate paid by the client is passed on to the engineer. For freelancers taking on projects through SES companies, this payout rate becomes an important negotiation point.

When we aggregated the quasi-delegation (jun'i ninin) contract projects listed on our job site “BizDev Tech” (all 1,790 listings, including those no longer recruiting), the average rate for the 1,511 listings with a stated rate (excluding 279 listings with no rate stated or outside the ¥1 million–¥5 million range) was ¥764,000 per month, with a median of ¥720,000 (as of September 2026, based on BizDev Tech data). This is roughly the same level as the average rate of ¥756,000 for contract (ukeoi) projects aggregated under the same conditions (15 listings; a reference value only, since the sample size is small compared to SES-type projects), showing that the gap is not as large as the common perception that “contract work pays more because it carries responsibility for deliverables.”

As for working hours, looking at the 183 of the same quasi-delegation contract listings that stated both lower and upper limits, the lower limit averaged 143.7 hours and the upper limit averaged 174.1 hours. The most common combination was “140–180 hours” (121 listings), followed by “fixed at 160 hours” (32 listings). It is a good idea to use these figures as a guideline to check whether the hours presented at the time of contract deviate significantly from this range (as of September 2026, based on BizDev Tech data).

A contract for work (ukeoi keiyaku, Article 632 of the Civil Code) is a contract aimed at the “completion of work,” and compensation arises upon completion of the deliverable. In system development, this means that completion of results—such as “delivering a system that conforms to the requirements specification” or “completing the system in a state where it passes testing”—is the condition.

The differences between the two are summarized as follows.

  • SES (quasi-delegation): Compensation arises for working hours and provision of technical skills. No responsibility for completing deliverables
  • Contract for work (ukeoi): Compensation arises upon completion of deliverables. Liability for correction and damages exists if there are defects

In practice, “projects run with agile methods such as Scrum development” and “phases where requirements are ambiguous” tend to adopt SES (quasi-delegation), while “waterfall development with clearly defined requirements through delivery” tends to adopt contract-for-work arrangements.

SES contracts and dispatch (haken) contracts look similar on the surface—“the engineer works on-site at the client.” However, the most important difference lies in “where the authority to direct and command resides.”

  • SES contract (quasi-delegation): The authority to direct and command rests with the SES company (or with the contracted engineer themselves). The client company cannot give direct orders
  • Worker dispatch contract: The authority to direct and command rests with the client company (the dispatch destination). Direct work instructions can be given to dispatched staff

Dispatch contracts require a license under the “Worker Dispatch Act” and are subject to restrictions such as a maximum dispatch period (in principle, 3 years). SES contracts are not subject to the Dispatch Act, but if the form is SES while the substance is the same as dispatch, it constitutes “disguised contracting” and violates the law.

Legally, an SES contract is classified as a quasi-delegation contract; there is no independent contract type called “SES contract” defined in the Civil Code. The common understanding is that “SES” is a name attached to “quasi-delegation contracts” as an IT industry business practice.

However, within the industry, the terms are sometimes used as follows.

  • SES contract: Refers to IT-specific contract practices involving engineers working on-site with management of working hours (setting lower and upper limits)
  • Quasi-delegation contract: A broader legal concept based on the statutory definition. Includes consulting, research, and other work beyond SES

Whether the contract is titled “Service Agreement (Quasi-Delegation Type)” or “SES Contract,” the legal effect is the same as long as the content is identical. What matters is not the title but the substance and reality of the contract.

As the comparison above shows, the defining feature of an SES contract is that “there is no responsibility for deliverables, and the authority to direct and command does not rest with the client company.” This characteristic brings both advantages and disadvantages.

Many people have the image that “SES = an on-site contract requiring daily attendance at the client,” but the reality is not necessarily so. Looking at the breakdown of the 1,704 quasi-delegation contract listings on BizDev Tech (out of 1,790, including those no longer recruiting) that listed only one work arrangement (excluding 86 listings that listed multiple arrangements), hybrid work was the most common at 840 listings (about 49%), followed by fully on-site at 532 listings (about 31%) and fully remote at 332 listings (about 19%); fully on-site accounts for only about 30% of the total (as of September 2026, based on BizDev Tech data). “SES” as a contract type and “on-site work” as a working style need to be considered separately.

The strength of SES contracts lies in meeting the needs of companies that want to secure engineers without taking on recruiting costs or employment risks.

  • Securing immediately productive talent: No time needed for hiring and training. Engineers with specific skills can be assigned when needed
  • Reduced recruiting costs: No need for job advertising fees or agency fees (30–35% of annual salary) associated with hiring full-time employees
  • Converting labor costs to variable costs: Contracts can be terminated at the end of a project, so costs do not become fixed
  • Access to diverse skills: Engineers with specialized skills in AI, cloud, security, and more can be secured when needed
  • Trial-like use: Companies can first assess an engineer's capabilities under an SES contract while considering future full-time hiring

For example, a new business's MVP development phase may require 5 engineers, while the post-launch operations phase may only need 2. With an SES contract, staffing can be flexibly adjusted according to the phase.

  • No guarantee of results: Even if the project is not completed, costs accrue for the hours worked. Quality risk is borne by the client company
  • No direct authority to direct and command: Work instructions to engineers must go through the SES company, which can easily cause communication loss
  • Inability to internalize know-how: After the contract ends, the knowledge accumulated by engineers leaves with them. The risk of knowledge being tied to individuals is also high
  • High cost: Compared to full-time employees, the monthly rate is often higher (because it includes social insurance and management fees)
  • Complexity of multi-vendor management: When gathering personnel from multiple SES companies, governance and information security management become complicated
  • Acquiring diverse technology stacks: By working at multiple clients' sites, you gain knowledge of various technologies, development methods, and business domains
  • Rich portfolio: Experience with diverse projects directly becomes your resume and portfolio
  • A stepping stone to independence/freelancing: Many engineers build experience through SES and then go independent, and after independence they can win similar projects at higher rates
  • Ability to negotiate rates based on skills: It is easy to understand your market value, making it easier to negotiate rate increases as your skills improve
  • Exposure to a wide range of industries and domains: You can experience systems in various industries such as finance, healthcare, e-commerce, and manufacturing
  • Low take-home pay due to margins: Because SES companies take an intermediate margin (25-50%), only about 50-75% of the rate the client pays ends up in your hands
  • Difficulty building a consistent career: Diverse experience is a strength, but it makes it hard to "deepen specialization," and building a career as a specialist can be challenging
  • Difficulty maintaining a sense of belonging and motivation: Although you work at the client's site, you are not an "employee," so you may find it hard to feel unity with the team
  • Low freedom in choosing projects: Because the SES company selects the projects, you may not be assigned to projects in the technology or industry you want
  • Risk of waiting periods (periods without assignment): After a contract ends, a "waiting period" may occur before the next project is secured, making income unstable

Disguised contracting refers to a situation where, although the contract is nominally an SES (quasi-mandate) or service contract, the ordering company in practice gives direct work orders to the engineer. This violates the Worker Dispatch Law (Article 4, Article 24-2, etc.), and both the ordering company and the SES company risk penalties (corrective guidance, business suspension orders, etc.).

The following are examples of prohibited behaviors commonly seen in the field that constitute "disguised contracting."

SituationProhibited behavior (disguised contracting)Appropriate response
Daily workThe client's manager directly tells the engineer "Please work on this task today"Request work through the SES company's representative or team leader
Attendance managementThe client directly manages and approves the engineer's attendance and overtimeThe SES company manages attendance and reports to the client monthly
Evaluation and assessmentThe client decides the engineer's evaluation and salary raisesEvaluations are conducted primarily by the SES company
Work location and equipmentThe client says "Make them use our PCs" or "Make them wear our employee badges"Use the SES company's equipment and tools, or clarify the scope through an explicit service contract

In practice, there are many "gray zones"; for example, it can be difficult to judge whether "the client verbally communicating work priorities" constitutes direction and command. To avoid risk, it is important for both the SES company and the ordering company to regularly conduct compliance training and check whether there is any gap between the contract terms and actual practice.

"Where does an acceptable request end and direction and command begin?"—this boundary is a point of frequent confusion in the field. The Q&A collection regarding the Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare's "Criteria for distinguishing worker dispatch services from services performed under service contracts (Notice No. 37)" presents criteria for judging disguised contracting with concrete examples.

Please refer to the following practical judgment criteria.

  • OK: Information sharing or questions such as "Please review the specification for the XX feature" (providing information, not requesting work)
  • OK: Business communication and coordination such as "Please attend the weekly meeting"
  • NG: Specific work instructions such as "Please fix the XX code today"
  • NG: Direct management of working hours and location such as "Please come at 9 a.m. tomorrow"
  • NG: Temporal constraints such as "Please finish it today even if you have to work overtime"

If you are struggling with gray zones, the simplest countermeasure is to consistently "route everything through the SES company's representative (PM, etc.)."

In SES contracts, the "solo on-site assignment" situation is particularly problematic. When a single engineer is stationed at the client's site completely isolated from the SES company, the following risks arise.

  • Ambiguity of the right to direct and command: Because the SES company's manager is not on site, direct instructions from the client tend to become the norm
  • Breeding ground for compliance violations: Disguised contracting can become chronic and go undetected
  • Risk of engineer isolation and turnover: Support from the affiliated company is thin, making mental anxiety more likely

When a solo on-site assignment is necessary, it is recommended to take at least the following countermeasures.

  • The SES company's PM and sales representative visit the client's site weekly to monthly to check on the situation
  • Maintain a constant communication line between engineers and the SES company via chat tools such as Slack
  • Establish a system where the SES company reviews and approves work content through daily and weekly reports

[Pre-Contract Checklist]

  • [ ] Are the scope of work, work location, rate, and billing conditions all clearly stated in the contract?
  • [ ] Is the location of the right to direct and command clearly defined in the contract?
  • [ ] Does the contract include clauses on intellectual property rights and confidentiality obligations?
  • [ ] Are the notice period for mid-term termination and damages clauses clearly stated?
  • [ ] Are the permissibility of subcontracting and the approval procedures specified?

[Post-Contract / During-Engagement Checklist]

  • [ ] Are you regularly checking whether direct work instructions are being given by the client?
  • [ ] Is the SES company proactively managing the engineers' attendance?
  • [ ] Are monthly working-hour reports and invoices being properly reconciled?
  • [ ] Are you regularly interviewing engineers about work-related issues and concerns?

[Contract Renewal Checklist]

  • [ ] Have there been changes in the work content or rate conditions (i.e., scope creep)?
  • [ ] Are you discussing whether contract changes are needed in response to changes in the client's requirements?
  • [ ] Are you negotiating rate revisions in line with the engineers' skill improvements?

Engineers who have gained experience at multiple sites through SES contracts have a major advantage when going independent as freelancers. The reasons are as follows.

  • Diverse tech stacks: Having experienced different technologies at each site, they can handle a wide range of projects
  • Communication skills: Collaborating with various clients hones their business communication abilities
  • Market awareness: They have an intuitive understanding of how much their skills are valued in the market
  • Trust as an immediate contributor: Their wealth of experience in being able to "start working immediately" directly translates into client trust

In particular, engineers who, during their SES experience, have handled development leadership, technology selection, and architecture design in multi-vendor environments are well positioned to land projects paying over 1 million yen per month as freelance PMs or tech leads.

For engineers belonging to SES companies, the company takes an intermediate margin, so even on a project paying 600,000 yen per month, take-home pay is roughly 300,000 to 450,000 yen. With the same skills, if you can win projects directly, it is entirely possible to raise your take-home pay to 600,000 to 800,000 yen or more per month.

Ms. Suzuki: The biggest change after going independent was definitely how rates became visible. When you belong to an SES company, the company takes a margin, so even with a client rate of ¥600,000, take-home pay is around ¥300,000-450,000. After switching to direct contracts as a freelancer, the payout rate is on a completely different level even with the same skill set.

Mr. Tanaka: For BizDev Tech's quasi-mandate projects, aggregated results show an average of ¥764,000 and a median of ¥720,000 across 1,511 listings with stated rates (as of September 2026, according to BizDev Tech). There's a significant difference in take-home pay between direct contracts where this entire amount goes to you and affiliations where margins are deducted.

Ms. Suzuki: Honestly, while I belonged to an SES company, I wasn't very aware of the gray zones of disguised contracting, but after going independent, I started working with a sense that "I hold the authority to direct and command." Another change after going independent was that I began reading contract termination clauses myself, which I used to leave to the company.

Mr. Tanaka: Without my experience across multiple sites during my SES days, I don't think I'd have the breadth of technical skills I have now. The negatives tend to be overemphasized, but as a "stepping stone to independence," it wasn't a bad period.

SES contracts are one of the important options for both companies and engineers in today's era of worsening IT talent shortages. Here is a summary of the key points of this article.

  • SES contracts are a type of quasi-mandate contract, with compensation based on hours worked and technical services provided
  • They are fundamentally different from contract-for-work agreements (with responsibility for deliverables) and dispatch contracts (where command authority lies with the client company)
  • The client company cannot directly command engineers, and operational management is required to avoid disguised contracting
  • While companies benefit from flexible staffing adjustments and securing immediately productive personnel, they need to be aware of the lack of performance guarantees and the risk of knowledge leakage.
  • While engineers gain opportunities to experience diverse work environments and develop their skills, they should be mindful of reduced take-home pay due to margins.
  • Engineers and PMs with SES experience can potentially significantly increase their monthly rates by going independent as freelancers, gaining access to direct contracts and high-payout projects.
