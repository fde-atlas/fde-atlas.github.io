Many people probably have a vague image of the pre-sales role as "someone somewhat technical who sits between sales and engineering." However, now that products have become more advanced and complex with the spread of SaaS and cloud services, pre-sales professionals are rapidly increasing their market value as "proposal professionals" whose technical expertise determines the outcome of sales negotiations.

In this article, from the perspective of professionals who have handled technical proposals and PoC support in the field, we thoroughly and systematically explain the definition, job responsibilities, and essential skills of pre-sales. Pre-sales is a specialist role that supports customers from a technical perspective and leads sales negotiations to closing during the "pre-implementation" phase of IT products and software, working in collaboration with sales representatives. Its mission is to prove that the customer's challenges can be solved technically and to support their purchasing decisions. Rather than simply explaining products, pre-sales professionals function as "proposal command centers" who understand both business and technology.

With the spread of cloud and SaaS, the solutions customers demand are becoming increasingly complex. Sales representatives alone cannot fully answer technical questions, and engineers alone cannot fully grasp business challenges — the importance of pre-sales, which bridges this gap, has never been higher.

In conclusion, pre-sales is a role responsible for proposing the technical value of a company's products in connection with customers' business challenges and convincing customers that "this product can solve the problem."

The core responsibilities of pre-sales are as follows.

  • Technical discovery: Deeply exploring the customer's business challenges and technical requirements
  • Solution proposals: Designing and presenting how the company's products will solve the challenges
  • Demos and PoCs: Demonstrating and verifying that the product actually works
  • Answering technical questions: Responding immediately to technical concerns and questions raised during sales negotiations
  • Creating proposals and quotes: Preparing proposal materials based on technical configurations

As a professional opinion, the essence of pre-sales is not "selling products" but "proving solutions to customer challenges through technology." Customers want to know not about the product's features themselves, but "whether their company's challenges will be solved." Whether you can answer this question with technical evidence determines the outcome of the negotiation. Pre-sales professionals are rare individuals who possess both deep technical understanding and the ability to translate it into business value.

In conclusion, pre-sales is a role that "supports sales negotiations with technology as a weapon," and its scope of responsibility and axis of expertise clearly differ from sales, which is responsible for relationship building and winning orders, and SEs, who are responsible for implementing specifications.

Here is a breakdown of the differences between the three roles that are often confused.

The most commonly confused distinction is with "sales engineers." While pre-sales specializes in the pre-implementation proposal phase, sales engineers often handle post-contract implementation support and serve as the operational point of contact — this is the decisive difference.

As a real-world insight, pre-sales professionals often move on to the next negotiation once an order is won, making it a role where it is easy to develop expertise in proposals and verification. Pre-sales work is built around "interviews (problem discovery)" and "demos/PoCs (proving value)," supported by three core skills: "technical knowledge," "translation and presentation skills," and "trade-off judgment." Neither technical skill alone nor communication skill alone is enough; the combination of these determines your win rate in sales meetings. This section explains the job content and essential skills in concrete terms.

In conclusion, the first phase of pre-sales is drawing out the "true business problems" behind customers' surface-level requests and translating them into technical requirements. The accuracy of problem discovery here determines the persuasiveness of the entire proposal.

What an excellent pre-sales engineer does during interviews is as follows.

  • Digging into the "true problems" behind the "requests" customers voice
  • Visualizing the gap between the current state (As-Is) and the desired state (To-Be)
  • Organizing the priorities of problems and the impact of solving them
  • Understanding technical constraints (existing systems, budget, organizational structure)

A common failure in the field is taking a customer's "I want this feature" at face value and spending the whole conversation explaining features. An excellent pre-sales engineer asks "why is that feature needed?" and captures the real problem behind it. If the problem is defined correctly, the proposal will naturally resonate with the customer.

In conclusion, the second phase is demonstrating through demos and PoCs (proofs of concept) that "this product can truly solve the customer's problem" and dispelling concerns about adoption. One demonstration moves a customer more than a hundred explanations.

Key points to emphasize in demos and PoCs are as follows.

PointDescription
Fit with customer problemsNot a generic feature tour, but a demonstration tailored to the customer's specific operations
Scenario designCreating a story that lets the customer imagine "using it in our own company"
Anticipating concernsPredicting technical anxieties the customer may have and resolving them in advance
Proving feasibilityUsing real data in the PoC to show it is not just "pie in the sky"

From a professional perspective, the typical pre-sales failure in demos is "trying to show every feature of the product." What customers want to know is only "how will this solve our problem?"—explaining irrelevant features is counterproductive. Whether you can design a "compelling demo" focused on the customer's problem greatly affects your close rate.

In conclusion, what is essential for pre-sales is combining two skills: broad technical knowledge of cloud, SaaS, and related technologies, and the translation and presentation skills to convey technology to non-engineers. Either one alone is not enough.

Here is a summary of the skill set required for pre-sales.

Skill areaSpecific content
Technical knowledgeBroad understanding of cloud (AWS/Azure/GCP), SaaS, networking, and security
Product knowledgeDeep understanding of your product's features, strengths, weaknesses, and differences from competitors
Translation skillsThe ability to convert technical content into language that resonates with executives and field staff
Presentation skillsThe ability to captivate and convince customers through explanation and demonstration
Interview skillsQuestioning and active listening skills to draw out problems

From a professional perspective, when two pre-sales engineers have equal technical ability, what ultimately makes the difference is "translation and presentation skills." No matter how deep your technical knowledge, if you cannot convey it in words that resonate with the customer, the deal will not move forward. This rare combination—"understanding technology and being able to move non-engineers"—is the source of a pre-sales engineer's market value.

In conclusion, where a pre-sales engineer's skill is tested most is judging the trade-off between technical feasibility and over-specification: "how far should we accommodate the customer's requests?" Answering "yes, we can do it" to everything is not what a professional pre-sales engineer does.

The perspectives to consider in trade-off judgment are as follows.

  • Technical feasibility: Is the request truly achievable, and are you making promises you cannot keep?
  • Cost vs. benefit: Does the cost of implementing that feature match the value the customer gains?
  • Avoiding over-specification: Are you selling the customer high-end features they do not need?
  • Post-adoption operations: Is the scope within what the customer can actually handle?

A common failure: to win the order, a pre-sales engineer answers "yes" to every customer request, only for it to be discovered after signing that "actually, it cannot be done," leading to serious trouble. An excellent pre-sales engineer can proactively say, "That's excessive, so you don't need it," or "This part isn't realistic, so here's an alternative." This honest judgment builds long-term trust rather than short-term orders, which ultimately leads to a higher close rate.

Pre-sales is an attractive profession where you can directly contribute to solving customer problems using your expertise, but precisely because it stands between sales and technology, it comes with its own unique difficulties. This section explains in concrete terms the rewards and challenges that practitioners face in their daily work.

The great reward of pre-sales is that your technical proposals directly lead to solving customer problems, and you can experience the results firsthand. The sense of achievement when a system implementation contributes to improving a customer's operational efficiency or growing their sales is a appeal hard to find in other professions.

Also, because your proposals lead to closed deals and contribute to achieving the company's sales targets, your work tends to be highly valued within the organization. There is a tendency for salaries to rise in proportion to results, so you can work with clear motivation: the more you hone your expertise, the higher your market value becomes.

On the other hand, pre-sales has its own difficulties. The hardest part is having to explain complex technical requirements in plain, easy-to-understand language to executives and field staff who lack IT knowledge.

Avoiding jargon and using expressions that are intuitively understandable requires advanced communication skills. You also need multitasking ability to handle multiple sales negotiations in parallel. Since you must simultaneously handle proposal-phase support and technical training for sales teams, there is a constant challenge in prioritizing your work while managing everything.

While there are no mandatory qualifications to become a presales engineer, obtaining certifications that demonstrate broad technical knowledge or language skills gives you a significant advantage in earning customer trust and advancing your career. Here, we introduce representative certifications that are useful in practice.

To demonstrate foundational technical knowledge, IT certifications such as the IT Passport, Fundamental Information Technology Engineer, and Applied Information Technology Engineer exams are helpful. In particular, holding the Applied Information Technology Engineer certification proves that you have a certain level of expertise as an engineer, adding persuasiveness when making proposals to customers.

Also, when working with cloud products or specific vendor products, obtaining certifications such as AWS certifications or Microsoft certifications (MCP) allows you to demonstrate expertise in that area, making it easier to be evaluated as a more capable professional.

Since the latest technical information in the IT industry is often published from overseas, English proficiency is a powerful weapon for presales engineers. Especially when handling products from foreign vendors or participating in global projects, the ability to read manuals written in English is essential.

If you can prove your ability to communicate and read documents in English by achieving a certain score on tests like TOEIC, it will not only broaden the scope of your proposals but also give you a significant advantage when winning high-value deals.

In conclusion, the reason demand for freelance presales is surging lies in the market structure: "as products become more sophisticated, technical proposal talent has become indispensable, yet securing excellent presales engineers as full-time employees is extremely difficult."

Let's organize the background behind the surge in demand.

  • Expansion of the SaaS market: BtoB SaaS companies are proliferating, leading to fierce competition for talent capable of technical proposals
  • Increasing product sophistication: Products including AI and cloud have become more complex, making advanced proposal capabilities essential
  • Hiring difficulties: Professionals who combine technical and business skills are rare, making full-time hiring difficult
  • Spot demand: Ready-to-contribute talent is needed for specific phases such as major sales negotiations or new product launches

As a professional perspective, many SaaS companies face the challenge of "losing deals because they cannot fully convey the technical value to customers despite having good products." Simply bringing in an experienced external presales engineer dramatically improves the quality of sales negotiations and closing rates. The value of external presales for companies lies in being able to utilize rare talent that they cannot hire, only when needed.

In conclusion, freelance presales is a profession where flexible work styles are easily achievable, such as spot engagements of 2-3 days per week or full remote work (online sales meetings). The establishment of online sales meetings supports this flexibility.

The main working styles of freelance presales engineers are as follows.

  • Spot engagements of 2-3 days per week: Working with multiple companies simultaneously, balancing risk diversification with high income
  • Primarily full remote: With online meetings and demos becoming mainstream, location constraints are decreasing
  • Spot support for major deals: Providing one-off support solely for proposals and PoCs on important projects
  • New product launch support: Assisting with building presales frameworks and preparing proposal materials

As a real-world insight, the spread of online sales meetings now allows presales professionals to make remote proposals to customers nationwide. This means freelancers can win projects without geographic constraints. However, face-to-face meetings are sometimes required in the final stages of large deals, so it is important to confirm working conditions for each project in advance. In conclusion, the keys to success as a high-rate freelance presales professional are three points: "transitioning on a foundation of SE or IT sales experience," "combining expertise in a specific domain," and "rate negotiation based on ROI." The rarity of combining both technical and business skills underpins those high rates. This section explains concrete strategies from going independent to winning projects.

In conclusion, the shortest route to presales is to leverage a foundation in either SE (technical literacy) or IT sales (proposal literacy) and fill in the other missing skill. Most presales professionals have transitioned from these two roles.

Here is a roadmap for transitioning and going independent.

  • Leverage your foundation: SE graduates use technical strength; IT sales graduates use proposal and negotiation strength
  • Fill the gaps: SE graduates consciously acquire proposal and presentation skills; sales graduates acquire technical knowledge
  • Gain presales experience: Experience actual presales work through internal transfers or job changes
  • Establish expertise: Build a track record of proposals in a specific product area or industry
  • Go independent: Move to freelancing once you have accumulated results and connections and can foresee a steady supply of projects

As a professional opinion, what makes the biggest difference is "actual proposal and deal-closing results." Concrete achievements—what products you proposed to which customers and how you closed the deals—become the decisive factor in winning projects after going independent. Actively getting involved in large deals within your company and building up success stories is the best preparation for independence.

In conclusion, to maximize your market value as a freelance presales professional, it is extremely effective to combine presales skills with specialized knowledge in a specific domain such as generative AI, security, or ERP. The higher your expertise, the fewer your competitors and the higher your rates.

Examples of specialized domains that tend to command high rates are as follows.

Specialized domainWhy it tends to command high rates
Generative AI / LLMDemand is exploding while professionals who can propose solutions are extremely scarce
SecurityHighly specialized; deep knowledge is essential for proposals
ERP / core systemsRequires both business and technical knowledge, creating high barriers to entry
Data platforms / analyticsCentral to DX initiatives; translating into business value is difficult

As a professional opinion, the number of "people who can do presales" is growing, but "people who can do generative AI presales" or "people who can do security presales" remain rare. Simply combining general presales skills with one in-demand specialized domain dramatically increases your market scarcity. This is the classic strategy for winning projects paying over 1.5 million yen per month.

In conclusion, for freelance presales professionals to maximize income, two defensive techniques are essential: "contract design that prevents unpaid work outside scope" and "rate negotiation based on ROI (return on investment)."

Concrete measures to prevent scope creep and win fair rates are as follows.

Defensive techniqueConcrete details
Documenting scope of workClearly state "number of meetings supported and working days" in the contract and SOW
Setting workload limitsDefine a cap such as "up to X days per month," with excess compensated as additional fees
Visualizing resultsShow how much your involvement improved close rates and order amounts
Negotiating with ROIArgue cost-effectiveness, e.g., "an investment of X yen per month contributed to Y yen in orders"

A common failure case is when, as you are asked to help with more and more meetings, your scope of work expands gradually until you are effectively losing money on an hourly basis. Professionals do not "refuse"; they draw the line constructively: "That is outside the scope, so I will take it on as additional work." In rate negotiations, they justify high rates by presenting quantitative results such as "close rates on deals I supported" or "order amounts I contributed to." Because presales results directly tie to sales revenue, it is a role where ROI arguments come naturally and rate negotiations tend to go in your favor. If you consider incorporating, you can further optimize your take-home pay through tax savings.

  • What pre-sales is: A "professional of proposals" who leads deals to closure through technical proposals in the pre-implementation phase
  • Differences from other roles: Specialized in the pre-implementation phase (sales engineers also handle post-implementation support)
