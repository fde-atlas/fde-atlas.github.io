Decision Criteria for In-House Development vs. External Utilization in AI Adoption

Original title: AI導入の内製化と外部活用の判断軸

日本Fde協会 · 2026-09-10 · 6 min read

Summary

This article by the Japan FDE Association argues that companies deciding between in-house AI development and outsourcing should not rely only on cost and speed comparisons. It proposes three criteria: strategic importance, frequency of change, and control responsibility. Core business logic, access-control policy, and audit accountability must stay internal, while prototyping can leverage external speed. Outsourcing contracts should require documentation and transferable knowledge so expertise does not leave with external engineers.

Key points

  • Three decision axes: strategic value, change frequency, and control responsibility.
  • Keep core business logic and metric choices in-house even when implementation is outsourced.
  • Access control and log design authority cannot be delegated; audits hold the company accountable.
  • Contracts should specify documentation of prompts, RAG structure, and design rationale as deliverables.
  • Use external help for fast prototyping; retain daily maintenance internally.

When advancing AI adoption, many companies stall at the question of "how much to do in-house and where to rely on external help." Most discussions of in-housing end with a comparison of cost and speed, but that alone causes companies to stumble at the stage of company-wide rollout. In this article, we at the Japan FDE Association break down the line between in-housing and external use in AI adoption into concrete checkpoints, including perspectives on governance, auditing, and handover. By the time you finish reading, you will be able to propose, with supporting rationale at your next decision-making meeting, which processes your company should handle in-house and where to entrust work externally. Note that FDE in this article refers to Forward Deployed Engineer (an engineer who embeds in the customer's field operations), not the abbreviation for Full Disk Encryption.

Three Criteria for Separating In-House Work from External Use in AI Adoption

Deciding between in-house and external based only on cost and speed will cause trouble later. What we prioritize in the field are the following three criteria. First, "strategic importance" — is it central to the business and a source of competitive advantage? Second, "frequency of change" — do requirements change often, requiring repeated adjustments? Third, "governance responsibility" — who bears accountability and can maintain a state where questions can be answered at audit time? Looking through these three criteria reveals a line of demarcation different from a simple comparison of man-hours.

Keep Highly Strategic Parts Anchored In-House

Parts deeply tied to your company's own business logic will not accumulate internal knowledge if entirely outsourced. At a retail client, the demand forecasting logic was precisely the core of their competitive advantage. Even if model implementation uses external help, decisions such as which metrics to prioritize and what to set as the objective function should remain in-house. This division of labor becomes the foundation for eventual in-housing.

For Fast-Changing Parts, Work Backward from the Maintenance Setup

Prompt tuning and RAG configuration (a mechanism that retrieves external documents as the basis for AI answers) are parts that require frequent adjustments as the business changes. If you depend entirely on external parties here, every small change triggers an order and waiting time. The practical order of operations is to first decide who will maintain it day-to-day, and then judge whether to do it in-house or externally.

How to Create a Line of Demarcation That Doesn't Break Governance

The hardest issue in considering in-housing of AI adoption is ensuring governance doesn't break down even when relying on external parties. This is an area that general market commentary rarely touches, but in corporate company-wide rollouts, this is exactly where consensus stalls.

Keep Access Control and Logs In-House

For access control — who can access which data — and the design of records that allow operations to be traced later (audit trails), even if implementation is outsourced, policy decisions and final administrative authority should remain in-house. In a financial institution project, production deployment was not approved until segregation of duties and log design were in place. Even if your demos are welcomed at first, the wall of governance will inevitably appear. Leaving this to external parties creates a situation where your company cannot explain itself at audit time.

Segregation of Duties and Accountability Cannot Be Delegated

Even if implementation work is outsourced, the segregation of duties and accountability itself — "who is responsible for this decision" — cannot be outsourced. It is your company that is questioned at audit time. When signing outsourcing contracts, stipulating in advance not only deliverables but also "how much information, and in what format, can be received when responding to audits" is a primary condition for preserving governance. We recommend confirming with your own audit and legal departments whether relevant laws and internal regulations apply to governance matters.

Use External Parties in a Way That Prevents Person-Dependency and Enables Handover

The more skilled the external practitioner, the greater the risk of building a system only that person can operate. This is an issue corporations cannot afford to overlook. If the ultimate goal of AI adoption is in-housing, you need to make "handover-ready form" a condition from the external-use stage onward.

Include Documentation in the Deliverables

I myself, when serving as a systems PM at a major engineering firm, handled everything from upstream design through my own coding to operations and maintenance. What I keenly learned then is that "making things run even after the person in charge leaves" carries as much weight as making things work. Document prompts and RAG configuration, evaluation criteria, and even the reasoning behind design decisions. Specifying this explicitly in outsourcing deliverables enables later handover to your internal team. A SaaS company executive once told me, in essence: "It's great when someone understands our business better than our own employees, but the real measure is whether that knowledge remains in a lasting form."

Frequently Asked Questions

Q. What should we in-house first? Practically speaking, keep parts with high strategic importance and frequently changing requirements anchored in-house. Conversely, for processes requiring bursts of speed, such as prototype launches, borrowing external speed to shorten your internal learning period is effective. We recommend not outsourcing decisions that form the core of your company's business logic.

Q. We're worried governance will break down if we rely on external parties. Even if implementation is outsourced, the principle is to keep administrative authority over access control and logs, as well as segregation of duties and accountability, in-house. Agreeing at the contract stage on what information, in what format, can be received when responding to audits makes governance less likely to break down. Please confirm the applicability of laws and internal regulations with your own audit and legal departments.

Q. Does FDE mean disk encryption? No. FDE in this article refers to Forward Deployed Engineer (an engineer who embeds in the customer's field operations). Although it shares the same abbreviation as Full Disk Encryption (disk encryption), it is an entirely different concept.
