What is an FDE? A Practitioner Explains the Differences from SEs and SIers

Original title: FDEとは？SE・SIerとの違いを実践者が解説

日本Fde協会 · 2026-06-20 · 6 min read

Summary

The head of the Japan FDE Association explains what a Forward Deployed Engineer is, drawing on his own career across system PM work, financial trading, e-commerce, and AI product development. FDE, a term originating at Palantir Technologies, describes engineers embedded at customer sites who both write code and own business problem-solving end to end. The article contrasts FDEs with in-house SEs, SIer contractors, and IT consultants, arguing the key difference is that FDEs discover problems themselves and implement solutions, rather than executing given requirements or only advising. It attributes failed DX efforts to a shortage of people who bridge technology and business, lists required skills (technical, business understanding, communication), and notes many Japanese engineers already work this way without the title.

Key points

  • FDE is a role coined by Palantir: engineers embedded on-site solving both technical and business problems.
  • Unlike SEs, SIers, and consultants, FDEs find problems themselves and implement fixes end to end.
  • The author sees DX failures as stemming from a lack of translator-type talent between strategy and implementation.
  • Required skills: coding and system design, business understanding, and communication across audiences.
  • A key mindset is showing a working prototype fast instead of waiting for complete requirements.

"What is an FDE?"—in Japan, there are still few people who can answer this question from personal experience. I serve as the representative director of the Japan FDE Association, but it was only after looking back on my long career that I realized I was an FDE myself. System PM at a major engineering firm, trader at a financial institution, launching an e-commerce business, AI product development—though the industries changed, my way of working, "immersing myself in the field and solving problems from both technical and business angles," never changed.

By reading this article, you will accurately understand the definition of the FDE role and be able to explain to others how it differs from SEs, SIers, and consultants.

What is an FDE?—Defining the "Engineer on the Front Line"

FDE (Forward Deployed Engineer) literally translates to "forward-deployed engineer." It refers to an engineer who embeds themselves at the customer's site and solves problems using both technical skill and business understanding. The term was originally coined by the US company Palantir Technologies as the name of an internal role.

Three characteristics of an FDE

There are three points that distinguish FDEs from other roles. First, being stationed at or deeply involved in the customer's site. Second, doing the technical implementation themselves. Third, taking end-to-end responsibility from identifying business problems to designing solutions. In other words, an FDE is someone who can handle both technology and business single-handedly—not just one or the other.

When I served as PM on a system project worth around 100 million yen, I stood between a development team of 8 and more than 20 stakeholders, making technical decisions and coordinating departmental interests simultaneously. We achieved 180% of our profit target because I ran both the technical and business sides on the ground. That was when I experienced firsthand the value of having someone on-site who understands both.

The biggest difference: "finding problems yourself and solving them yourself"

SEs and SIers essentially do the work of "realizing given requirements with technology." Consultants "analyze problems and make recommendations," but they do not roll up their sleeves and solve them. FDEs do both single-handedly. Taking end-to-end responsibility from problem discovery through technical implementation to verifying results—that is the essence of an FDE.

When I embedded at a manufacturing client's site, their staff told me, "We don't know what our problem is." An SIer cannot move without a requirements specification, but an FDE walks around the site, observes business processes, and starts by defining the problem themselves. This "problem-discovery ability" is the decisive difference from traditional engineering roles.

Why FDEs are attracting attention now

The "translator shortage" happening in DX efforts

Many companies are working on digital transformation, but the biggest reason they fail is a severe shortage of "businesspeople who understand technology" or "engineers who understand business." Executives know what they want to do but don't understand the technology. Engineers have technical skills but can't understand business problems. FDEs are the people who can bridge this gap.

When I consulted with SaaS company executives, I heard many times, "We had consultants create a strategy, but no one in-house can implement it." Conversely, there are also cases where, after asking an SIer to develop something, they say, "We built it to spec, but no one uses it." The person who stands between strategy and implementation—that is the FDE.

Not just Palantir—the spread of FDE-style ways of working

Although the term FDE originated at Palantir, people doing the same kind of work have existed in Japan for a long time. I myself implemented system improvement proposals myself during my days as a financial trader, and ran marketing and system development simultaneously in my e-commerce business. At the time, I didn't know the name "FDE," but looking back, it was all FDE-like work. It's not that the name didn't exist—there are plenty of engineers working this way.

The skills and mindset required of an FDE

FDEs broadly require three capabilities.

First, technical skill: fundamental development ability, such as writing code and designing systems. Second, business understanding: the ability to understand the customer's industry structure, revenue model, and organizational dynamics, and to connect technology to business results. Third, communication skill: the "translation ability" to speak at the same level with both executives and on-site staff is essential.

From my experience building an organization from zero to 60 people at a startup, the most important thing is the attitude of "show something working first." Rather than waiting for a perfect requirements specification, build a prototype as fast as possible and decide the direction together with the customer. This sense of speed is the FDE's strength and also the biggest difference from traditional SEs and SIers.

Frequently asked questions

Q. Do I need any special qualifications to become an FDE?

A. No specific qualifications are required. However, both technical skill (programming and system design) and business understanding are required. In practice, those who have honed both skills through work experience are the ones who thrive as FDEs.

Q. What is the difference between an FDE and a full-stack engineer?

A. A full-stack engineer is "an engineer with broad technical skills," but an FDE is additionally expected to "solve business problems at the customer's site." Beyond the breadth of technical skills, business understanding and problem-discovery ability are FDE requirements.

Q. How many people are working as FDEs in Japan?

A. While the number of people working under the FDE title is still small, a considerable number of engineers are effectively working in an FDE-style role. Many people share the same style, just without the "name," and the Japan FDE Association is working to build a community for such practitioners.
