Summary

This article breaks down requirements from FDE job postings (as of August 2026) to show what the role actually demands and where preparation time is best spent. Technical requirements center on Python, TypeScript/JavaScript, LLM implementation (prompt design, agent architecture, evals), and cloud deployment, with specific frameworks rarely required. Non-technical requirements take up more space: defining ambiguous problems, cutting scope, building consensus with non-technical stakeholders, and autonomy. English proficiency is nearly mandatory at foreign AI companies' Japan offices (OpenAI, Palantir) and acts as a compensation dividing line, while many domestic startups don't require it. The author recommends prioritizing one end-to-end experience delivering a production system from an ambiguous brief, and deferring framework mastery and, for domestic targets, English.

Key points

  • Core technical stack: Python, TypeScript/JavaScript, LLM implementation with evals, and cloud deployment.
  • Non-technical skills dominate postings: problem definition, scope cutting, stakeholder consensus, autonomy.
  • Foreign AI firms' Japan offices require Japanese-English bilingualism; domestic startups often don't.
  • Top priority: one experience taking an ambiguous problem to production, however small.
  • Proof matters more than portfolios: describe what broke, how you fixed it, and current usage.

Reading a few FDE job postings, you notice a common pattern in how requirements are listed: specified programming languages, experience implementing with LLMs, cloud, and then roughly twice as much text devoted to non-technical requirements. In this article, I break down the requirements from job postings published as of the time of writing (2026-08) and organize what is demanded and to what degree, and where you should allocate your limited preparation time.

Summary First

  • The technical core is Python and TypeScript/JavaScript, implementation using LLMs (prompt design, agent architecture, evaluation), and deployment and operations on the cloud. Rather than proficiency in a specific framework, what is tested is the ability to build end to end, from frontend to backend, on your own.
  • In terms of volume, job postings devote more space to non-technical requirements. The abilities to define problems in ambiguous situations, build consensus with non-technical counterparts, and judge trade-offs to cut scope come up repeatedly.
  • English requirements vary by company between mandatory and preferred; as of the time of writing (2026-08), Japanese offices of foreign AI companies almost universally require both Japanese and English. This is the dividing line for compensation ranges.

How Are Job Posting Requirements Structured?

When reading FDE job postings, what strikes you is how short the technical requirements are. For example, the requirements section of OpenAI's Forward Deployed Engineer posting lists the ability to write production-quality code in both frontend and backend using Python, JavaScript, or an equivalent stack, and experience building systems using LLMs or generative models—but no specific frameworks or libraries are named.

Instead, the space goes to requirements about how you operate: experience scoping and delivering complex systems in uncertain, fast-moving environments; the ability to simplify complexity and make sound judgments quickly under pressure; the ability to communicate clearly to engineers, product teams, and customer stakeholders; and the ability to identify risks early and adjust without losing speed. Descriptions like these continue.

Palantir's Tokyo Forward Deployed Software Engineer posting shows the same tendency: in addition to writing strong code in Python, Java, C++, TypeScript/JavaScript, and so on, it lists the ability to collaborate on teams mixing technical and non-technical members, to work comfortably in environments where goals keep shifting, and to make decisions autonomously with minimal supervision.

In other words, the skill requirements for this role center on "how you can operate" rather than "what you can code." That doesn't mean the former is unnecessary; rather, the structure is that the former is a prerequisite for being evaluated on the latter.

Non-Technical Skills—The Core of the Role

Judging by the volume in job postings, this is the center of FDE evaluation. However, since they are written in abstract terms, you need to translate them into concrete work situations to understand them.

The top item, "the ability to define problems," is the hardest to replicate and where the biggest differences emerge. Customers arrive with something like "Could we do something with AI?" The FDE's first job is to nail down which tasks and which decisions are in scope and how success will be measured. Skip this and start building, and you end up with something that works but that no one uses.

The second item, the ability to cut scope, is also important. FDE projects can expand indefinitely if you let them. Unless you decide what not to do this time and get the customer's buy-in, you won't reach production by the deadline. The statement in OpenAI's posting to the effect of "judge trade-offs among scope, speed, and quality, and adjust plans to keep delivery on track" refers to this work.

  • Ability to define problems — From a customer's vague inquiry, determine the target business processes, how success is measured, and what is out of scope
  • Ability to cut scope — Decide what to drop in order to reach production by the deadline and secure the customer's agreement
  • Building consensus with non-technical people — Explain to counterparts from frontline staff to executives who don't speak the language of technology, and drive decision-making
  • Autonomy — Move forward and make your own judgments at customer sites without direction from a manager. Often expressed in postings as "high agency"
  • Feeding field insights back into the product — Not leaving custom work one-off, but capturing recurring patterns into the product or internal assets
  • Industry domains — Finance, manufacturing, healthcare, public sector, etc. Many postings list specific industries as preferred qualifications

How Much English Do You Need?

English is the factor that most divides paths when aiming for FDE roles in Japan. As far as I could verify as of the time of writing (2026-08), Japanese offices of foreign AI companies almost universally require Japanese-English bilinguals. OpenAI's Tokyo FDE posting explicitly states that proficiency in both Japanese and English (both speaking and writing) is a condition for success, that resumes must be submitted in English, and that interviews are conducted in both languages. Palantir's Tokyo posting likewise requires business-level proficiency in both Japanese and English.

At that level, what matters is not certification scores but actual use in practice. You will exchange written communication with engineers and product teams at headquarters, and discuss in meetings when necessary. You will work in a dual structure: customer interactions in Japanese, internal communication in English.

On the other hand, a considerable number of FDE openings at domestic AI startups do not require English. If your English is currently lacking, a reasonable path is to first build hands-on FDE experience at a domestic company, then fill in your English and aim for foreign firms.

The reason I recommend the first item is that this is what is most closely examined in FDE selection processes. A list of tech stacks can be written on your resume, but the experience of "defining things yourself from an ambiguous state and delivering them to production" can only be spoken about by those who have actually done it. The scale can be small. Whether you have taken one real business problem of someone's and carried it through from definition to operation completely changes the substance of your interviews.

Conversely, what can be postponed is proficiency in specific frameworks or tools. FDEs choose technologies to fit the customer's environment, so tools change from project to project. Rather than deeply learning one tool, training yourself to become usable with new ones in a short period is more valuable.

  • Build one experience of defining an ambiguous problem and delivering it to production — top priority. Scale doesn't matter
  • Become able to write from frontend to backend in Python and TypeScript
  • Experience implementing with LLMs including evaluation (evals) — don't stop at "it worked"; be able to explain how you measured accuracy
  • Experience deploying and operating something yourself on the cloud
  • English — essential if targeting foreign firms; can be postponed if targeting domestic companies
  • Industry domain knowledge — takes time, but is a differentiator that pays off later

How to prove your skills

What's difficult in FDE selection is proving non-technical skills. Writing "I can define problems with customers" doesn't convey anything. What works is concrete process: what the customer was saying when you were consulted, what you redefined as the problem from that, why you scoped it that way, and how what you built is used in production. If you can speak to these four points for one project, that alone covers most of the evaluation criteria.

On the technical side, consider that "something someone is actually using in production" is stronger than artifacts sitting in a repository. An FDE's job includes keeping things running, so being able to talk about what happened after it went into operation (what broke, what you fixed, and subsequent usage) conveys the resolution of your practical experience.

Frequently asked questions

  • What programming languages does an FDE need?
  • In job postings verifiable as of writing (August 2026), Python appears almost universally, with TypeScript/JavaScript also required. Some postings, like Palantir's, list Java or C++, but this stems from that company's product stack. What matters more than the number of languages is being able to write production-quality code alone from frontend to backend.
  • Is specialized machine learning knowledge necessary?
  • Knowledge of training models yourself is not required in most FDE postings. What's demanded is the ability to build systems that work in business operations using existing LLMs. Prompt design, agent architecture, search (RAG) design, and how to measure output quality (how to build evaluations) are more directly tied to practical work. However, a background in data science gives you an advantage when looking at customer data.
  • What skill are FDEs most often said to lack?
  • Comparing job posting requirements with actual career-change origins, people coming from development tend to lack "experience defining problems from customers' ambiguous requests," while those coming from consulting or SIers tend to lack "the ability to write production code themselves." Neither can be filled through short-term study, so the fastest route is to consciously take on work of that kind in your current position.
  • How are certifications and portfolios viewed?
  • FDE job postings almost never list specific certifications as requirements. For portfolios, what's evaluated is not the number of projects but "whether someone is using it in real work." Having one project you can discuss from an operational standpoint (what broke, how you fixed it, and how it's used now) is rated higher than multiple artifacts that merely run.
