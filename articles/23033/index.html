Anthropic FDE Interview: How to Prepare Round by Round

FDE Times · 2026-10-16 · 7 min read

Summary

The article argues that Anthropic's London Forward Deployed Engineer job ad should serve as your interview syllabus: map each requirement, from building MCP servers to spending 25-50% of time on customer sites, to the round most likely to test it. Citing an Exponent/Aced prep guide, it outlines four rounds: a recruiter call, a technical screen on deploying Claude with MCP tools, an incremental coding test where each step adds a requirement, and a final panel combining solution design with behavioral questions. It recommends writing pure functions so new requirements extend rather than force rewrites, asking clarifying questions before sketching architectures, and preparing honest failure stories. Anthropic's official rules allow Claude for preparation but ban it in live interviews and take-homes unless permitted.

Key points

  • Treat the job ad as a syllabus and map requirements to rounds.
  • Technical screen centers on deploying Claude with MCP tools.
  • Coding test adds requirements step by step; write extensible pure functions.
  • Design round: ask clarifying questions before drawing architecture.
  • Claude is banned in live interviews and take-homes unless permitted.

In brief

  • Anthropic's FDE job ad asks for production LLM experience, strong Python and the ability to find your way when things are unclear. Study those first.
  • One interview-prep guide describes a process with a recruiter call, a technical screen built around Claude and MCP, a coding test that adds a requirement at each step, and a design round combined with behavioral questions.
  • You may use Claude to prepare, but not in live interviews or take-home assignments unless the interviewer allows it.

Anthropic’s job ad for a Forward Deployed Engineer in London contains a line that is easy to skim past: 25-50% of the time is spent traveling to customer sites to build directly with them. It reads like logistics, but it signals that the role needs people who can build in front of a customer, not just people who are good at algorithm puzzles.

The most effective way to prepare, then, is to treat the job ad itself as the syllabus and match each requirement to the interview round most likely to test it.

The job ad is a syllabus

According to the posting, the role sits in the Applied AI team. FDEs build production applications with Claude inside customers’ own systems. What they deliver is concrete: MCP servers, sub-agents and agent skills. That is a list of things you must be able to build yourself, and knowing the names will not be enough.

The requirements are just as direct. Candidates need 4 or more years of experience in a technical, customer-facing role, such as an FDE.

Candidates need production experience with LLMs, including advanced prompt engineering and agent development. They need strong Python, and the posting wants someone highly proactive who can find their own way through large, layered and ambiguous organizations.

Each of these requirements can be matched to the round most likely to touch it. So the first job is to build that map, before opening any coding practice site.

Four rounds, each testing one capability

An interview-prep guide from Exponent/Aced sets out a sample process for the role. The first round is a 30-minute call with a recruiter about your background, motivation, references and the right level for you.

Next comes the technical screen, built around a realistic scenario: deploying Claude with MCP tools and keeping the system stable over very long contexts.

The coding round, according to the same guide, is a step-by-step practical exercise on a CodeSignal-style platform, and each step adds a new requirement.

The final panel combines two parts: solution design, meaning the architecture of a Claude system for a customer, and questions about behavior and the company’s values.

This is a sample process rather than a fixed schedule, so ask your recruiter about the order of your own rounds.

Example: a coding test that adds a requirement at each step

Take a hypothetical practice problem, not a real interview question. Step one: take a list of support tickets in JSON and group them by customer. Step two: remove tickets with duplicate content. Step three: before sending to the model, trim them so the total length for each customer stays under a limit.

The trap is writing step one as one long function. By step three you have to tear it apart and rewrite it while the clock runs. A safer approach is to turn each requirement into a pure function, so that a new requirement only asks you to add one link to the chain:

from dataclasses import dataclass
from collections import defaultdict

@dataclass(frozen=True)
class Ticket:
    id: str
    customer: str
    created_at: str  # ISO 8601, e.g. "2026-03-01T09:30:00Z"
    text: str

def load(rows: list[dict]) -> list[Ticket]:
    return [Ticket(r["id"], r["customer"], r["created_at"], r["text"].strip())
            for r in rows]

def dedupe(tickets: list[Ticket]) -> list[Ticket]:          # step 2
    seen, out = set(), []
    for t in tickets:
        key = (t.customer, t.text.lower())
        if key not in seen:
            seen.add(key)
            out.append(t)
    return out

def group(tickets: list[Ticket]) -> dict[str, list[Ticket]]:  # step 1
    groups = defaultdict(list)
    for t in tickets:
        groups[t.customer].append(t)
    return dict(groups)

def budget(groups, max_chars: int):                            # step 3
    trimmed = {}
    for customer, items in groups.items():
        total, kept = 0, []
        # newest first: ISO 8601 strings in one timezone sort chronologically
        for t in sorted(items, key=lambda t: t.created_at, reverse=True):
            if total + len(t.text) > max_chars:
                break
            kept.append(t)
            total += len(t.text)
        trimmed[customer] = kept
    return trimmed

def pipeline(rows, max_chars=4000):
    return budget(group(dedupe(load(rows))), max_chars)

In step three the code sorts by when each ticket was created, and not by id, because a higher id does not guarantee a newer ticket. If the data has no timestamp field, state your assumption clearly before falling back on the id.

Much of the credit comes from what you say while you code. Spell out the trade-off: you keep the newest tickets because you assume they are more relevant, and you would check with the customer if that assumption turned out to be wrong.

This context-limit problem is also close to the long-context stability question at the heart of the technical screen.

The design round: ask first, draw later

Because the job ad stresses finding your way when things are unclear, expect the design prompt to leave out a lot of detail, and build the habit of asking questions before you draw anything.

Say the interviewer offers: “An insurance company wants its staff to ask Claude about policy terms.” Rather than sketching a diagram with a vector database straight away, ask a few questions first.

You could open with three: “Who will use it, call-center staff or the claims department? Which system holds the policies, and does it have access controls? If Claude answers wrongly, is the consequence an annoyed caller or an incorrect payout?”

Only after those three questions do you propose an architecture: an MCP server that follows the existing access controls, a sub-agent dedicated to citing policy clauses, and a set of test cases built from real questions staff have asked.

Behavioral and deep-dive rounds: real stories beat polished ones

Educative’s guide to Anthropic’s general engineering hiring process observes that the technical deep-dive favors people who genuinely understand the systems they have built. In the behavioral round, interviewers tend to value honesty, self-awareness and reflection over polished success stories.

In practice, prepare at least one story about a deployment of yours that failed. Cover the technical cause, the decisions you made and what you did differently afterward. If you can still explain it after being pushed two layers deeper, that story is worth more than ten projects described only on the surface.

This is also the moment to fix your CV. Avoid the generic “experience with LLMs”. Write that you built an MCP server connecting a model to a specific system, who it ran for, and how you measured its quality.

Specific lines like these do not replace the 4+ years of experience the posting spells out. They simply help whoever reads your CV see at once what you did with those years.

The rules on using Claude: know them so you don’t lose points needlessly

Anthropic has published official guidance on candidates’ use of AI. You may use Claude to refine your application, but you must write the first draft yourself. You may use Claude to research the company, practice your answers and prepare questions for your interviewers.

The line is drawn at the assessments. Live interviews are “all you”, with no AI assistance unless the interviewer says otherwise. Take-home assignments are also done without Claude, unless permitted.

That may seem odd for a role that works with Claude all day, but a reasonable reading is that they want to see your underlying ability without an assistant at your side.

Mistakes that sink strong candidates

The first common mistake is studying the wrong material: spending weeks on algorithms when, in the sample process, the technical round is a Claude deployment scenario with MCP. The next is writing code as one monolithic block, so that every new requirement forces a rewrite.

Then there is jumping straight into architecture while the design prompt is still open-ended, skipping the very ability to navigate ambiguity that the job ad names.

The last two mistakes are about people. One is telling stories that are too perfect, so the listener cannot find anywhere you actually learned something.

The other is using Claude where it is banned, for instance in a take-home assignment without permission. That costs you trust, and in a role that lives on customer trust, losing it means losing everything.

An exercise for this week: ask a colleague to play the interviewer and give you only a very vague design prompt, such as “a hospital wants to use Claude for its front desk”.

Set a 30-minute timer, and for the first 10 minutes you may only ask questions, with no drawing; after the session, ask your colleague which of your questions clarified the problem the most.
