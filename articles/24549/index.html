09 Simplifying Ontology and Business Object Modeling

Original title: 09 简化 Ontology 与业务对象建模

FDE中国社区 · 2026 · 3 min read

Summary

Chapter 9 of an FDE practice series explains how to model business objects, attributes, relationships, actions, and permissions in a lightweight way during the PoC stage. Using a supply chain agent as an example, the author argues that an agent that understands language but acts incorrectly suffers from unmodeled business context, not weak language skills. The chapter provides five modeling elements, a supply chain risk example, an output template, and stresses that database table structures are not business object models—FDEs must add business meaning, responsibility flows, state-action rules, and permissions. It warns against building heavyweight ontology platforms too early.

Key points

  • Agent errors stem from unmodeled business context, not language ability.
  • Five modeling elements: objects, attributes, relationships, actions, permissions.
  • Database table structures are not business object models.
  • Permissions should bind to objects and actions, not just user roles.
  • Avoid heavyweight ontology platforms early; clarify key objects first.

Chapter Objectives

Identify business objects, attributes, relationships, actions, and permissions in a lightweight way, so that agents and workflows understand the business world instead of just processing scattered text.

Why It Matters

Enterprise processes are not a pile of documents; they are made up of objects such as orders, customers, contracts, equipment, materials, work orders, suppliers, invoices, and reports. An FDE does not need to build a heavy ontology platform from the start, but does need to model business objects clearly.

Opening Case: Why Agents "Understand the Words but Do the Wrong Thing"

A supply chain agent can understand "help me check whether this customer order has any risks" and can call inventory and logistics tools. But it often gives unstable recommendations: sometimes it mixes up customer orders and purchase orders, sometimes it does not know whether "in-transit inventory" can cover promised delivery dates, and sometimes it treats a supplier's historical delays as current delays.

The problem is not the model's language ability, but that the business world has not been modeled. The agent does not know the relationships between orders, materials, suppliers, and risk events, nor does it know which actions are merely queries and which actions mean making a commitment to the customer.

The goal of a lightweight ontology is not to build a massive platform, but to clearly define the key business objects, attributes, relationships, actions, and permissions during the PoC phase, so that system outputs are more stable and more auditable.

Five Types of Modeling Elements

Business Objects

Orders, customers, contracts, equipment, materials, work orders, suppliers, invoices, reports.

Object Attributes

Amount, delivery date, status, owner, risk level, update time, confidentiality level, version.

Object Relationships

A customer owns orders, materials affect production, work orders are linked to equipment, contracts are linked to payments, suppliers affect delivery dates.

Object Actions

Query, generate, approve, notify, create work order, update status, freeze, archive.

Permission Boundaries

Who can view which object, who can perform which action, which fields need to be masked, which actions require approval.

Modeling Output Template

## 业务对象：订单

- 核心属性：订单号、客户、SKU、数量、承诺交期、状态、金额。
- 关联对象：客户、物料、库存、物流单、风险事件。
- 可查询动作：查状态、查交付风险、查关联物料。
- 可建议动作：生成风险解释、生成客户沟通摘要。
- 禁止自动执行：修改承诺交期、取消订单、对外承诺赔偿。
- 权限边界：销售看自己客户，计划看交付字段，财务字段脱敏。

From Data Tables to Business Objects

Many enterprises already have database tables, but table structures are not the same as a business object model. The FDE needs to add three types of information:

Often found in data tablesWhat the business object model must add
Field namesBusiness meaning and definitions of fields
Foreign key relationshipsOwnership relationships in real processes
Status enumerationsWhich actions are allowed in which statuses
Permission tablesWho can view, who can modify, who can approve

The clearer this step is done, the more stable the subsequent tool schemas, prompts, eval samples, and audit logs will be.

Hands-on Task

Choose a scenario and list:

  • 5 business objects.
  • 5 key attributes for each object.
  • Key object relationships.
  • Executable actions.
  • Permission and approval boundaries.

Deliverables

  • A simplified business object model.
  • An object-action-permission table.
  • A business vocabulary the agent can use.

Common Pitfalls

  • Only mapping data tables without understanding business actions.
  • Setting permissions only by user role, not by object and action.
  • Feeding all fields to the model.
  • Building a heavy platform too early, slowing down the PoC.

Checklist

  • Are business objects and actions clearly defined?
  • Are the relationships between objects known?
  • Can permissions be bound to objects and actions?
  • Can the agent produce outputs that conform to the business object structure?
