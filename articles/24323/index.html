FDE Sales Process Breakdown: What to Do Before an AI Agent Goes Live

Original title: FDE 销售流程梳理：AI 智能体落地前要做什么

FDE Times · 2026-10-26 · 5 min read

Summary

The article opens with a hypothetical: an AI agent drafting outreach emails for a B2B sales team is abandoned within two weeks because most drafts go unread. The author argues the failure usually lies not in prompts but in the builder's ignorance of where the agent sits in the customer's actual sales process. Citing an Outreach job posting for an FDE on its AI Revenue Agents team, the piece highlights two non-coding requirements: understanding the customer's business processes and driving adoption through training and change management. It proposes interviewing sales reps, marketing, and RevOps separately across four stages—prospecting, discovery, pipeline management, and deal review—to build a process map whose real value is exposing inconsistencies between teams, such as stage fields updated only before weekly reviews. Training, on-site support, and structured feedback to product teams (observation, frequency, impact, recommendation) are framed as part of deployment, and four common mistakes are listed.

Key points

  • Outreach's FDE posting requires sales process knowledge plus training and change management, neither involving code.
  • Process maps matter most for revealing where actual practice diverges from documentation, since agents must read correct data there.
  • Interview sales reps, marketing, and RevOps separately across prospecting, discovery, pipeline, and deal review stages.
  • Training, on-site support, and structured feedback are part of deployment, not post-launch cleanup.
  • Four mistakes: interviewing only managers, copying onboarding docs, compressing training, and sending anecdotal feedback.

Key Takeaways

  • Outreach is hiring a Forward Deployed Engineer (FDE) for its AI Revenue Agents team, requiring LLM experience and sales process knowledge, plus responsibility for customer training and change management
  • The most valuable part of a process map is that it reveals where the sales team's actual practices diverge from the documentation, because that's where the agent must read the correct data
  • Training and feeding back to the product team are part of deployment, not post-launch wrap-up work

Two weeks after launch, logs showed that most of the emails drafted by the agent were deleted without being read. Imagine a team that just delivered an AI agent to draft outreach emails for sales reps at a B2B software company. The demo went smoothly, the emails read well, and the executives nodded along. This case is hypothetical, but the situation is common.

The problem rarely lies in the prompt; it lies in where the agent sits in the overall process—a process its builders never saw firsthand. A job posting from Outreach describes the work needed to avoid this quite precisely.

Outreach is hiring a Forward Deployed Engineer (FDE) for its AI Revenue Agents team, requiring experience with AI/ML systems, large language models (LLMs), or prompt engineering. But the same posting also lists two tasks unrelated to code. The first is working with sales, marketing, and revenue operations (RevOps) teams to deeply understand the customer's business processes.

The second is helping customer teams adopt the new tool and change how they work through training, documentation, and on-site support. Anyone who wants to become an FDE in AI products can start practicing these two skills this week.

Why should the process come before the prompt?

PostHog describes the FDE as the person who bridges the gap between what a product can do and what a customer actually needs. Pragmatic Engineer believes LLM integrations and AI products are especially well suited to this role.

For a sales agent, this gap shows up in three places: the prompt, the data the agent reads, and the timing of when it appears in a sales rep's day.

The prompt is under your control; the data and timing are determined by the customer's process. That's why Outreach explicitly requires "a practical understanding of the sales process," covering prospecting, discovery, pipeline management, and deal reviews. These four stages also work well as a framework for drawing a process map.

Example: Mapping the four stages

Back to the company from the beginning. Schedule three separate interviews: one with several sales reps who sell directly, one with the marketing team, and one with RevOps.

Don't ask "What is your process?"—that question only gets you a recitation of the slide deck. Ask about a specific instance: "Last time you prepared for a discovery call, which tabs did you open, and in what order?"

For the marketing team, ask what criteria a lead must meet before being handed to sales, and who has the authority to change those criteria. For RevOps, have them show you the CRM configuration, and ask directly: which fields do people actually fill in, and which are almost always empty? After the three interviews, you can compile a table like this.

StageWho does itInput dataWhere the agent can help
ProspectingSales reps, marketing teamLead lists, lead qualification criteriaDraft the first email based on lead context
DiscoverySales repsCall recordings, interaction historyPre-call summaries, suggested questions
Pipeline managementSales reps, RevOpsCRM stage fieldsFlag deals with no recent activity
Deal reviewsSales managersPipeline reportsPrepare a risk summary for each deal

This table is just a draft. Its real value lies in where the teams' accounts don't match. Suppose RevOps says the stage field is updated in real time, while sales reps say they only update it before the weekly deal review. Then an agent reading the stage field mid-week is working with data that's days old.

A finding like this changes the design. You could move deal reminders to right after the review ends, or have the agent also read email history instead of relying solely on the stage field. Writing prompts alone would never uncover this detail.

Training is also deployment

Outreach groups training, documentation, and on-site support under change management, and that classification is right. At the company above, if the new tool slows sales reps down in the first week, they will likely abandon it, even if it genuinely helps in the long run.

A simple plan can start with a small group of sales reps piloting the tool. Each stage on the process map gets a short one-page document answering three questions: what the agent does, what the sales rep does, and when the agent's suggestions should be ignored. The third question matters most, because users only trust a tool when they're allowed to disagree with it.

On-site support means being present while sales reps actually use the tool, not just showing up at the launch webinar. This aligns with Palantir's description of its Forward Deployed Software Engineers (FDSEs): working side by side with customers to quickly understand their hardest problems. For a sales agent, "side by side" means sitting with a sales rep for a whole morning doing prospecting.

That morning will teach you more than any dashboard. Track the percentage of drafts that sales reps edit before sending—this number shows whether the agent has truly been integrated into their workflow.

Bringing insights back to the product team

Pragmatic Engineer defines the FDE as an engineer who rotates between customer-facing teams and the core product engineering team. Outreach's job posting turns this rotation into a concrete responsibility: submit structured feedback to the product and engineering teams based on what you learn from customers.

"Structured" here doesn't mean leaving a complaint in Slack. Every piece of feedback should include four parts: the observed phenomenon, how often it occurs, its impact on the customer, and a suggested improvement.

Take the outdated stage field as an example. The feedback could read: the agent needs a source of activity signals in addition to the stage field, because at many customers this field only updates with the meeting cycle.

Four mistakes that make a process map worthless

The most common is interviewing only the sales leadership. Managers describe how the process is supposed to work; sales reps tell you how it actually works. The second is copying the process map from the customer's onboarding docs and treating it as fact.

Equally common is compressing training into a single session before launch, when habits take weeks of support to form. The last is sending feedback to the product team as anecdotes. Without frequency and impact, the product team has no way to prioritize.

Putting this skill on your resume

When reading FDE job postings, watch for terms like RevOps, change management, enablement, or "business processes." They signal that the company wants someone who can stand between the software and how the customer works, not someone who only integrates APIs. If you've never done sales, study the four stages above thoroughly until you can explain how a deal review actually runs.

The best proof is concrete deliverables: a process map based on real interviews, plus a one-page training plan and one piece of feedback for the product team. A hiring manager will learn more from reading those three pages than from a line like "strong communication skills."

A good prompt determines what the agent can write; the process map and training plan determine whether anyone uses what it writes.
