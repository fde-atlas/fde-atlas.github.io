FDE isn't about how fast you ship a PoC: the real battle of AI transformation begins only after the system goes live

Original title: FDE 比的不是 PoC 多快：AI 轉型真正的勝負，在系統上線後才開始

Summary

This article argues that the real test of a Forward Deployed Engineer (FDE) is not how fast a PoC is delivered, but who stays on site after launch to turn exceptions into product improvements. Citing Indeed data, it notes FDE job postings grew from 643 to 5,330 in one year (729% growth), yet recruiter Christian & Timbers estimates only about 2,000 of roughly 17,000 US-based FDEs can reliably deliver ROI. Drawing on 91APP's CDMP deployment experience, it closes with questions brands should ask when evaluating implementation partners.

FDE job postings have surged sevenfold in a year, but headhunting firms estimate that fewer than 2,000 people on the market can reliably deliver results. The gap isn't in how fast a prototype runs—it's in who stays on-site after the system goes live, turning every exception into a capability for the next version of the product. Speed is the entry barrier; what decides success or failure is the long-term work that follows.

5,330 new job postings, versus the 2,000 people estimated by headhunting firms

Over the past year, "Forward Deployed Engineer" (FDE) has gone from a niche job title to a position the entire AI industry is scrambling to fill. According to a Business Insider analysis citing Indeed job posting data, the number of FDE job postings surged from 643 to 5,330 between April 2025 and April 2026, a year-over-year increase of 729%. A TechCrunch report from July 2026 also cited a survey by the headhunting firm Christian & Timbers: in early 2026, only 5% to 10% of surveyed companies planned to hire FDEs, but by the end of Q2, that figure had jumped to 70%.

The same TechCrunch report highlighted an even more striking number: there are roughly 17,000 people in the US market with the FDE title, but Christian & Timbers estimates that only about 2,000 of them combine all three capabilities—industry knowledge, on-site trust, and the hands-on ability to take systems all the way to production—sufficient to reliably deliver ROI. This is an experiential estimate from a headhunting firm, not an objectively certified qualification threshold, but the gap itself says enough.

Job postings have grown more than sevenfold, yet this headhunting-endorsed combination of skills hasn't grown proportionally. When the market talks about FDEs, it mostly focuses on "how fast you can build a PoC," but delivering a working prototype in two weeks is becoming the most basic, most easily replicated entry barrier for this role—not the real source of its scarcity. What this article aims to unpack is precisely the difference between that barrier and the capabilities that truly determine success or failure.

This article is one piece of our team's FDE phenomenon series. Other articles in the series cover the truth about requirement translation—the fact that clients often can't articulate what they actually want—the controversy over whether FDEs are just consultants with new business cards, the last mile of making technology truly land in production, and how to tell real FDEs from fake ones. This piece focuses on the most misunderstood aspect: whether speed is really this role's talisman.

In our implementation case notes, we saw the same turning point again and again

We recently reviewed our consulting notes from the past six months of helping brands implement data and marketing automation, and noticed a common pattern: the first prototype is usually built quickly, and brands are often wowed by the demo—but the moment that truly determines whether the project succeeds tends to come three months later, not in that first week of eye-catching demos. That's when the system encounters exceptions the brand itself never realized existed—for example, a bundle discount algorithm for a particular campaign that works differently from the usual logic, or a membership segmentation condition that matches the online logic perfectly but falls apart in offline store scenarios.

At that moment, what determines the project's trajectory is who is still on-site, willing to lay the exception bare and understand it, and then actually feed what was learned back into the system. When we saw FDEs being hotly discussed, it felt familiar—because this is exactly the craft our team practices every day when delivering implementation services.

A fast PoC only solves the outermost of three layers of problems

If you break down a successful AI system implementation, there are at least three layers.

LayerWhat it determines
Surface: prototype speedWhether you can deliver a comprehensible, operable prototype in one to two weeks
Middle: depth of on-site understandingWhether you can uncover the real business rules the client can't articulate themselves
Foundation: the feedback loop into the productWhether the pitfalls encountered on-site become standard capabilities in the next version of the system

Much of the market discourse on FDEs stops at the surface layer. The GenAI Divide report released by MIT NANDA in 2025 synthesized interviews with about 150 executives, surveys of 350 employees, and an analysis of more than 300 publicly disclosed enterprise AI implementation projects. The report argues that one of the main obstacles stalling most projects is that systems "cannot retain feedback, cannot adapt to context, and cannot accumulate learning over time." The report's original wording is that these systems lack the ability to "retain feedback, adapt to context, or improve over time"—referring precisely to the foundation and middle layers in the table above, not the surface layer.

A team that only trains the surface layer can, at best, bring a brand to the "impressive-looking demo" stage. The middle and foundation layers are what truly determine whether a project can survive its first campaign, its first system revision, and its first change of client contact. Neither of these layers has much to do with "how fast the prototype was built"—what needs to be trained separately is the ability to understand the business and keep iterating.

From Palantir to AWS: what big tech's money is really buying is the people who stay

The FDE role was first introduced and popularized by Palantir; the background of this model can be found in the FDE entry on Wikipedia. Palantir's business model was never about "selling one standard software package to as many customers as possible." Instead, it deploys engineers on long-term assignments at client sites, working alongside business teams to grind the system into something usable. Many questioned this model at the time as too expensive and impossible to scale—but it was precisely this long-term presence that made it very hard for clients to switch away.

This logic is now being replicated by more companies. An official AWS announcement states that in 2026 AWS established a dedicated organization backed by a $1 billion investment, aiming to embed thousands of engineers directly into client teams; an official Microsoft announcement states that Microsoft Frontier Company is investing $2.5 billion and mobilizing roughly 6,000 industry experts and engineers to be stationed directly at client sites. OpenAI officially announced the creation of an independent Deployment Company, and an official Anthropic announcement describes a joint venture with Blackstone, Hellman & Friedman, and Goldman Sachs to form a new enterprise AI services company. What these companies have in common is that they've made "being embedded at the client site, helping the system actually get used" into a formal organizational capability—not an after-hours favor from individual engineers.

What these companies are really investing in is how to turn accompaniment into a scalable capability, not just making PoCs run faster. This closely resembles the FAE (Field Application Engineer) model long established in the semiconductor industry. An analysis by Business Next notes that MediaTek built its business precisely on the playbook of "staying with the client until success"—not on who delivered samples fastest. Looking at these cases together reveals a common thread: the more resources a company invests, the more the competition becomes about who can turn "staying" into a replicable organizational process.

Why most implementations stall after the demo: the feedback loop is the real technical barrier

If we return to the gap between "why job postings are surging while headhunting-endorsed composite skills aren't growing along with them," the answer becomes clearer. Prototype speed is a replicable skill—an engineer who masters a framework can deliver a working prototype in two weeks, and this is rapidly becoming commonplace. The truly scarce capability is being able to remember, six months after the system goes live, why it was designed that way in the first place—and when the client says "our requirements have changed," to accurately judge whether things really changed, or whether the original spec was never dug out correctly to begin with.

As one industry practitioner put it in a Digital Times quote: "Suppose the system goes live in six months and breaks down in the middle of the night—the person who originally designed the problematic architecture is the one who gets called up to fix it." This captures the most essential part of the FDE job: knowing the system's cause-and-effect relationships well enough to make the right call at 3 a.m. matters far more than who delivers fastest.

The market's rush to embrace this role is running far ahead of the supply of genuinely qualified talent.

Precisely because this bar is real, there is no shortage of skepticism about the FDE hype itself. LeadDev's analysis cites Unframe engineer Chris Slovak's view: replacing labor with expensive, hard-to-find labor doesn't sound sustainable in the first place. TechCrunch also raises one possibility: if, in a few years, agents start automating other agents, the FDE role itself could be automated away.

These criticisms actually point to two different execution models, both wearing the same FDE title. One is swapping a consultant's business card for an FDE title without actually building the ability to understand things on the ground and feed that back into the product—a model that won't last. The other treats hands-on accompaniment as a long-term capability, where every on-site lesson leaves a trace, and responsibilities and version histories are handed over to someone. What critics worry about is really the former, but the market often lumps both into the same statistics, which makes people mistakenly think the entire role is unsustainable. The difference between the two models only becomes visible after the prototype is delivered. Of course, a few teams can be both fast and deep, and simple, standardized deployments don't require long-term on-site presence. This article is about the situation most brands face: you can usually only pick one first, and it's the latter that usually determines long-term success.

91APP's approach: turning every on-site fix into the next version of the product

When our team serves brands on CDMP and personalized marketing deployments, this is exactly what we've been doing all along: we don't close the case once the first version of a segmentation logic or the first automation script is built. Instead, we stay embedded in the customer's actual operations, turning every "this doesn't match the textbook" exception into fuel for the system's next iteration.

For example, the gap most easily overlooked when brands buy a CDP AI agent is the difference between the vendor's demo environment and the real production environment. A system that answers questions accurately in a demo environment doesn't mean it will still work once connected to a brand's real, messy, legacy-laden data. Only long-term on-site presence can close that gap. This is why 91APP's CS roles have been moving forward in recent years, evolving from product consultants who simply execute client requests into growth consultants who use data analysis to create revenue opportunities for clients. The core of this shift is whether the judgment learned from every service engagement is systematically retained.

Specifically, this feedback loop is reflected in at least three places in CDMP: the thresholds for member lifecycle segmentation are fine-tuned by industry characteristics rather than applying a one-size-fits-all formula across all brands; the judgment logic for purchase-intent segmentation is calibrated to each category's purchase cycle; and coupon abuse detection rules are gradually built up from real arbitrage cases encountered on-site at brands. These adjustments depend on staying embedded long-term in the customer's data and operations, writing every pitfall into the system one by one—something a one-off consulting report simply cannot achieve.

What AI has leveled is the baseline productivity every brand can access, which isn't a new phenomenon. Everyone can buy the tools, everyone can use the models, and the bar for quickly building a prototype system is dropping fast. Once surface-level capabilities are leveled, the real differentiators move upstream—to who truly understands the customer's business, and who is willing to stay and fill in the potholes.

When evaluating an AI deployment partner, don't just look at who builds the fastest demo

If a brand is evaluating who to choose for an AI or CDMP deployment, rather than only looking at who builds prototypes fastest or who has the flashiest slides, try asking a few different questions that go straight to the point, and require verifiable evidence rather than verbal assurances.

  • What happens after the PoC ends: does the partner have a clear second-phase iteration mechanism and a post-launch operations division of labor? If the only answer is "build it first and figure it out later," it likely means they haven't thought through who will be responsible for optimization afterward.
  • Six months after go-live, who is maintaining the system: track whether, after the deployment phase ends, the same group of people is actually handling anomalies and optimizations, whether there are concrete issue records to review, or whether every incident requires re-establishing context from scratch.
  • Did the last on-site lesson make it into the product: ask the partner for specific examples of how lessons learned in past projects became a feature or a rule in the current system, rather than starting from zero every time.
  • Can they articulate what makes this industry unique and explain how they'll verify results: a team that has truly worked on the ground should be able to concretely describe the nuances unique to this category, and state the checkpoints and metrics for validating outcomes—not just apply a universally recycled slide template.

Asking these few questions usually reveals whether the partner is genuinely prepared to stay for the long haul with something verifiable to show, or has simply rebranded one-off consulting services with a trendier name.

Launch day isn't the finish line: that's when accountability begins

The market's willingness to pay high salaries for the FDE role and compete fiercely for talent shows everyone has already grasped one thing: getting AI systems truly running inside a company's daily operations is far harder than training a model. But if the industry focuses only on whose prototype runs fastest, those 5,300 new job openings may end up producing 5,300 one-off consulting reports, leaving clients with a system no one continues to care for.

What truly determines whether an AI transformation project succeeds is never how glamorous launch day looks, but whether, in the six months or year after launch, someone is still willing to stay on the ground and turn every surprise into a better next version. Speed only buys you the ticket; whether you can stay is the real test.

Frequently asked questions about FDEs and AI deployment

Q1: What is an FDE, and how is it different from a typical software engineer? A1: An FDE is an engineering role that embeds long-term at the customer's site to help AI or software systems truly land and be continuously optimized—a concept first introduced and popularized by Palantir. The biggest difference from typical engineers is that FDEs spend more time understanding the customer's business logic and on-site context, and their work often involves more than just writing code in an office.

Q2: How does an FDE differ from a traditional consultant? A2: The traditional consulting model usually ends with delivering a report or proposal, with no responsibility for subsequent optimization. The FDE model emphasizes sustained on-site presence and iteration: after the system goes live, the FDE remains responsible for handling issues, optimizing rules, and feeding lessons learned on-site back into the product.

Q3: Why have FDE job openings surged while recognized qualified talent remains scarce? A3: The skills to quickly build a prototype are relatively easy to replicate, but the ability to understand a customer's business, stay embedded long-term to solve problems, and systematically feed experience back takes time to accumulate and cannot be rushed. That is the main cause of the supply-demand gap.

Q4: With limited budgets, are small and mid-sized brands also suited to this long-term accompaniment model? A4: Long-term accompaniment doesn't mean committing massive resources from day one. You can start with a clearly defined, small-scope scenario—for example, a single segmentation communication script—confirm whether the deployment partner genuinely tracks results and keeps optimizing, then gradually expand the scope of collaboration.

Q5: How long does it take to tell whether an AI deployment is truly successful? A5: A first-version prototype can usually be seen within one to two weeks, but the real measure of success is whether, three to six months after go-live, the system can keep adjusting to the exceptions that arise in the brand's actual operations. Timelines vary depending on data quality and integration scope—it's not just about the initial demo effect.

Q6: How can a brand avoid hiring a consultant with a new business card instead of a true long-term partner? A6: Directly ask how, in their past projects, lessons learned on-site concretely became part of the product or service, and confirm that post-launch maintenance and optimization responsibilities are clearly assigned—rather than settling for a report or a one-off training session.
