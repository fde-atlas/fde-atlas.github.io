What Are Open-Weight Models? Differences from Open Source and Commercial Use

Original title: オープンウェイトモデルとは？オープンソースとの違いと商用利用

FDX株式会社 · 2026-08-24 · 12 min read

Summary

This FDX article clarifies the distinction between open-weight models and open-source LLMs: most models labeled open-source actually publish only trained weights, not training code or data. It compares three release forms (closed API, open-weight, open-source), stresses that public weights do not guarantee unrestricted commercial use, and lists five license points to verify with legal teams. It outlines four benefits of self-hosting (data stays in-house, fixed costs, version pinning, fine-tuning), cases to avoid it, and a stepwise adoption path starting with business use cases and closed-API validation before infrastructure investment.

Key points

  • Open-weight models publish weights only; most 'open-source LLMs' are actually open-weight.
  • Public weights don't mean free commercial use; licenses vary by model.
  • Check five license items: commercial use, scale limits, outputs, derivatives, prohibited uses.
  • Self-hosting benefits: data privacy, fixed costs, version pinning, fine-tuning.
  • Adoption should start with use cases and API validation, not GPU purchases.

"Open weight," "open source LLM," and "local LLM" are often used interchangeably, but they refer to different things. If you consider adoption while leaving this distinction ambiguous, it can lead to overlooked license conditions or miscalculated operational requirements.

This article organizes model release types into three categories and explains how to decide whether to use open-weight models in your organization. Performance comparisons of individual models and inference infrastructure design are covered in a separate article, "Local LLM Comparison 2026."

Who this article is for

  • Information systems staff considering in-house use of generative AI who want to clarify the differences from closed APIs
  • Legal and procurement staff who need to review model license conditions
  • Project leads preparing internal approval on the assumption that "it's open source, so it's free to use freely"

"The weights are public" and "you can use it freely" are two different things. This is the most common misunderstanding in practice.

TypeWhat is releasedRunning in your own environmentTypical usage pattern
Closed (API-based)Nothing (API endpoint only)Not possibleCalling the API with usage-based billing
Open weightTrained weightsPossibleObtaining the weights and running inference on your own infrastructure
Open sourceWeights + training code + training dataPossibleCan go as far as reproducing or modifying training

Most models commonly called "open source LLMs" are, strictly speaking, open weight. Only a limited number of models publish their training data composition and training code; in most cases, only the weights and their terms of use are released.

Why the terminology gets confused

In the software world, there has long been a convention of calling anything with published source code "open source." The equivalent of "source" for an LLM is not code but the training data and training procedure. However, because models with only published weights are also conventionally introduced as "open source," the confusion has become entrenched.

The practical implications are the following two points.

  • Overestimating the freedom to modify: With weights alone, training cannot be reproduced. Measures such as "retraining to erase" specific knowledge are not possible
  • Proceeding without checking the license: The phrase "open source" easily leads to the misunderstanding that commercial use is permitted unconditionally

Four options open weights give enterprises

1. You can run inference without sending data outside

This is the biggest advantage. Since input data never leaves your control, it can be applied even to operations handling information whose external transmission is prohibited. If contractual or policy-based data export restrictions are a barrier to adoption, this type may be the only option.

2. The cost structure shifts from usage-based billing to fixed costs

Closed APIs increase costs in proportion to usage. In-house operation of open weights centers on fixed costs: GPUs, electricity, and operational labor. Once usage exceeds a certain scale, in-house operation becomes advantageous, but the break-even point varies greatly depending on the configuration. Concrete estimation approaches are covered in "Local LLM Comparison 2026."

Designing to reduce token consumption itself works for both types. See also "5 Patterns for Saving LLM Tokens."

3. You can pin the model version yourself

With API services, models are updated or discontinued at the provider's discretion. Prompt behavior can change, and verified business workflows may require re-validation. With open weights, you can make the decision to keep operating on a specific version.

However, the flip side is that you take on the responsibility of incorporating security fixes and performance improvements yourself. Choosing not to update comes with the risk management of not updating.

4. You can make adjustments through fine-tuning

Additional training on your own data becomes possible. However, deciding to adopt primarily for this purpose should be approached with caution. Many business problems are solved not with additional training but with RAG (retrieval-augmented generation), prompt design, or redesigning business workflows. Fine-tuning presupposes an ongoing structure for maintaining, evaluating, and retraining training data.

How to read the licenses

Even when weights are public, terms of use differ by model. In practice, the following categories exist.

CategoryDescription
PermissiveBroadly permits commercial use, modification, and redistribution, such as Apache 2.0 or MIT
Custom licenseThe provider sets its own conditions. May include usage restrictions or additional licensing requirements for businesses above a certain size
Non-commercial onlyLimited to research and evaluation purposes; commercial use not permitted
License with additional policyIn addition to the license text, a usage policy (a list of prohibited uses) applies separately

The 5 items to check

Before making an adoption decision, the following 5 items should be shared with your legal team.

  • Whether commercial use is permitted: Whether business use falls within the licensed scope
  • Presence of scale conditions: Whether additional licensing requirements are imposed based on usage volume or business size
  • Handling of outputs: Whether model outputs can be incorporated into your own products and services, and whether attribution is required
  • Handling of derivative models: Whether fine-tuned models can be redistributed, and under what conditions
  • Prohibited uses: If a separate usage policy applies, whether your intended use falls under any prohibited items

The specific conditions for each model (sales/user-count thresholds, attribution requirements, separate agreements based on business type) are checked monthly in Q2 of Article 22.

Licenses are revised. You need an operational process that does not stop at checking at deployment time, but re-checks whenever the model is updated.

A common pattern of failed deployments is starting with the infrastructure before the target use cases are defined. Procuring GPUs first and then looking for ways to use them tends to result in investment running ahead with no measurable benefits. This structure is analyzed in "5 Structural Reasons Why AI PoCs Fail and How to Avoid Them".

Practical steps for the deployment decision

  • Define the target use cases first: Identify which processes of which tasks it will be used for, in terms of man-hours and expected reductions
  • Check data constraints: Confirm at the contract and policy level whether the data for that task can be sent externally
  • Validate with a closed API first: If there are no constraints, first verify quality and business fit via an API. No infrastructure investment is needed at this stage
  • Estimate the volume/cost crossover point: Compare the pay-as-you-go cost at the expected usage volume with the fixed costs of running it in-house
  • Review the license with legal: Check the five items listed above
  • Establish an operations framework: Assign responsibility for model updates, security patches, and incident response

The order matters. If you skip steps 1 and 2 and start at step 4, the usage volume that forms the basis of comparison cannot be estimated properly. The procedure for evaluating man-hours and applicability on a task-by-task basis is explained in "What Is an AI Assessment? Designing Task Inventories and ROI".

Frequently asked questions (FAQ)

Q1. What is the difference between open-weight models and open-source LLMs?

A. What is released differs. Open-weight refers to a form where only the trained weights are released, while open-source refers to a form where the training code and training data are released in addition to the weights. Many models commonly called "open-source LLMs" are, strictly speaking, open-weight. With weights alone, you cannot reproduce the training or remove specific learned content.

Q2. Can open-weight models be used commercially for free?

A. The fact that the weights can be obtained at no cost is separate from whether commercial use is unconditionally permitted. Some licenses are permissive, such as Apache 2.0 or MIT, while others are the provider's proprietary licenses that restrict uses or require additional licensing for businesses above a certain size. Some models are limited to research use and do not permit commercial use. Before deployment, you need to review the five items with legal: commercial use permission, scale conditions, handling of outputs, handling of derivative models, and prohibited uses.

Q3. Are local LLMs and open-weight models the same thing?

A. They refer to different things. Open-weight is a term for how the model is released, while local LLM refers to the execution environment (running it under your own control). Running an open-weight model in your own environment makes it a local LLM, but there are also setups where an open-weight model is used via an external cloud inference service, in which case it is not local execution.

Q4. Is running it in-house cheaper than a closed API?

A. It depends on usage volume. Closed APIs are billed per use, proportional to usage, while in-house operation is dominated by fixed costs: GPUs, electricity, and operations staffing. Beyond a certain usage volume, in-house operation becomes advantageous, but the crossover point varies greatly depending on the configuration and operations framework. The most commonly overlooked cost is operations staffing; comparing GPU costs alone makes in-house operation look disproportionately favorable. The approach to estimation is covered in "Local LLM Comparison 2026".

Q5. Where should I start?

A. Start by identifying the target use cases. Infrastructure considerations can come later. For tasks with no restrictions on sending data externally, first validate quality and business fit with a closed API, without making any infrastructure investment at that stage. Once validation gives you an estimate of usage volume, move on to comparing with in-house operation. Procuring GPUs first and then looking for ways to use them tends to result in investment running ahead with no measurable benefits.

Q6. Is it okay to keep the model version fixed?

A. It has advantages in terms of stabilizing business processes, but note that responsibility for updates shifts to your company. You will need a system to make your own decisions about security fixes and responses to known issues, and to incorporate them when necessary. If you fix the version, do not simply leave it fixed; design operations that periodically evaluate whether updates are needed.

Summary

  • An open-weight model refers to an LLM whose trained weights are published and which can be run in your own environment
  • There are three release types: closed (API only), open-weight (weights only), and open source (weights + training code + training data). Most of what is commonly called "open source LLM" is actually open-weight
  • The advantages are four: inference without sending data externally, a cost structure centered on fixed costs, version pinning, and fine-tuning
  • "Weights are published" is not the same as "free to use." Confirm five items with legal: commercial use permission, scale conditions, handling of outputs, handling of derivative models, and prohibited uses
  • Choose open weights when there are data constraints, usage volume is large, or version pinning is a requirement. Avoid them when the target use case is undecided, there is no GPU operations team, or top-tier quality is essential
  • The process is: "identify use cases → confirm data constraints → validate via API → estimate the decision point → check licenses → decide the operations structure." Do not start with the infrastructure
