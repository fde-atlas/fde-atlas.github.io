6 Key Principles for Vetting FDE Consultants | How to Identify SES Firms and Consultants Calling Themselves "FDE" Before Placing an Order | Career Support Media Specializing in FDEs, Engineers, and IT Freelancers

Original title: FDEコンサル見極め6原則|「FDE」を名乗るSES・コンサルを発注前に見分ける方法

Summary

Author Shohei Tokumitsu of DeFactory warns that many firms now calling themselves "FDE consulting" still operate like conventional SES or contracted development shops. Drawing on hands-on project experience, he proposes six principles to check before signing: don't accept instant "we can do it" answers without investigation; watch for vendors who merely execute requests without questioning goals; check whether estimates start from outcomes or person-months; verify engineers understand AI risks, not just AI usage; insist developers appear directly in discussions; and confirm deliverables, KPIs, and handover terms are written into contracts. One red flag alone isn't disqualifying, but multiple should trigger pre-contract scrutiny.

Key points

  • Many self-styled FDE consultancies are unchanged SES or outsourcing firms in practice.
  • Principle 1: instant promises without prior research or risk discussion are a warning sign.
  • Principle 3: estimates anchored to person-months reveal time-based, not outcome-based thinking.
  • Principle 5: developers who never face the client breed later disputes over what was agreed.
  • Principle 6: deliverables and KPIs must be explicitly documented in the contract.

"We're also an FDE consultancy"—lately, companies introducing themselves this way have suddenly increased. However, looking at the reality, quite a few of them are no different from conventional SES or contract development firms.

To avoid ending up with "this isn't what I expected" after placing an order, I've compiled six points to check before signing a contract, based on my experience as a practitioner. All of them are red flags I've felt from actually observing projects: "if this is present, it's dangerous."

The six principles below are not an automatic "no" if just one of them applies. However, if multiple apply, I recommend setting up a time to verify things before signing the contract.

Shohei Tokumitsu

Representative Director of DeFactory. Based on experience leading multiple 0-to-1 new business developments in software development, both in-house and at other companies, and driving business growth, he currently works as an FDE consultant/delivery lead and Product Manager (PdM), as well as handling hiring (FDE, upstream SE, PM, PMO, CPO, CTO, consultants).

1. Principle 1: Don't take "We can do it" at face value

Be wary of instant answers without prior research, and proposals that never mention risks.

If someone, in the first meeting, immediately answers "We can do it" or "No problem" without properly examining your situation, you may want to be a bit cautious. The people who truly understand the work tend to avoid instant answers. Being able to say "we can do it" without confirming the business situation, existing structure, and technical constraints is, conversely, evidence that they haven't thought deeply about what "done" even means.

Another thing to watch is how proactively they bring up risks. The better the proposal, the more risks are discussed with the same enthusiasm as benefits. Conversely, someone who never mentions risks and only lists benefits tends to hit unexpected walls once the project actually gets underway.

  • Do they conduct research and interviews before making a proposal?
  • Can they proactively and clearly state what they can't do or what's difficult?
  • Do they present an approach for handling risks?

2. Principle 2: Are they just an "order-taker"?

Someone who merely converts requests into tasks as-is lacks the ability to define problems.

They complete exactly what you asked, within the scope you specified. At first glance, this seems like the mark of a good partner, but whether they can deliver real value in practice actually can't be measured here. What to look for is whether they raise "questions."

  • Do they proactively ask about the background and purpose of the request?
  • Can they talk about what they aim to achieve after joining, with hypotheses?
  • Do they go beyond the requested scope and offer necessary recommendations?

3. Principle 3: Are they selling outcomes, not time?

If their estimates start from "man-hours," they may not have escaped time-based billing thinking.

When discussing estimates, if the first question is "How many person-months will it take?", you may want to be a bit careful. It's not uncommon for companies to advertise as "FDE consultancies" while in reality still stuck in time-based billing thinking.

  • Is the estimate based on "outcomes" or "man-hours"?
  • Will they speak up when a proposal is disadvantageous to the client, if it's necessary?
  • Do they have the ability to actually get hands-on and build things (delivery capability)?

4. Principle 4: Are they people who "solve" problems with technology?

More important than being able to use AI is understanding AI's risks when using it.

People who rely entirely on AI for development may look fast and capable at first glance. However, there are many cases where they simply adopt generated results as-is, overlooking business risks and technical debt.

  • When using AI, can they articulate where the risks lie?
  • Do they design with a long-term perspective, not just short-term fixes?
  • Do they use technology by working backward from the goal of solving business problems?

5. Principle 5: Can developers be front and center?

A structure where engineers never appear in explanations becomes a breeding ground for later "he said, she said" disputes.

The moment the conversation turns technical, only the sales rep responds, and the developers doing the actual work never show their face. Such a structure warrants some caution.

  • Can you get explanations of technical decisions directly from the developers themselves?
  • Can they explain specialized content in plain language?
  • Are the reasons for technical decisions shared in a form that leaves a written record?

6. Principle 6: Are verbal agreements documented in writing?

A verbal agreement is not an agreement. The health of the contract and structure is the last line of defense.

It's a quasi-delegated contract, yet there's no mention of deliverables. KPIs aren't stated anywhere in the contract. I've seen projects proceed in such states many times before.

  • Are deliverables and KPIs clearly stated in the contract?
  • Is a handover guaranteed when key personnel change?
  • Is there a designed forum for deciding whether to continue or revise at each milestone?
