FDE Business Acumen: Hard ROI, Budget Ownership, and Decision-Makers

Original title: FDE 商业敏感度：硬 ROI、预算归属与决策人

FDE Times · 2026-10-08 · 5 min read

Summary

This guide argues that AI projects fail mostly for organizational and financial reasons, not technical ones: IBM research cited in the article says only about 25% of AI initiatives meet expected ROI, and 95% of generative AI pilots fail. The author, writing for aspiring FDEs, explains how to convert soft ROI (saved minutes, accuracy) into hard ROI that moves a specific P&L line, using a 200-seat call center example. It advises identifying who actually owns the AI budget—often business units rather than the CIO—bringing finance in early, measuring baselines in week one, and asking four discovery questions to pin down the P&L item, baseline, budget holder, and approver.

Key points

  • Time savings only count as hard ROI if they change a P&L line.
  • AI budgets increasingly sit with business units, not just the CIO.
  • Measure a baseline in week one or lose the ability to prove impact.
  • Ask four discovery questions to find the P&L item, baseline, and approver.
  • Report financial metrics to budget holders, not technical ones like accuracy.

An IBM survey shows only about 25% of AI projects achieve their expected returns. What Forward Deployed Engineers lack isn't another framework—it's the ability to read a customer's P&L. And it's learnable.

Key Takeaways

  • Time saved is only soft ROI unless it changes a revenue or cost line on the customer's P&L.
  • AI budgets increasingly sit with business units, not just the CIO, so ask directly who holds the money.
  • ROI questions will eventually come from finance, so bring finance in early and measure the baseline in the first week.

Imagine the pilot is in week six. The AI agent is running smoothly, accuracy numbers look good, and the operations lead keeps nodding. Then someone from finance asks: "How much more money will this make the company, or save it?" The room goes quiet, and the Forward Deployed Engineer (FDE) is left holding nothing but a latency chart.

This scenario is far from rare. IBM cites an MIT report released in summer 2025 stating that 95% of generative AI pilots are failing. IBM's own CEO survey found that only about 25% of AI initiatives achieve their expected return on investment (ROI), 16% scale enterprise-wide, and only about 29% of executives are confident in measuring ROI.

IBM's conclusion is that the main challenges are organizational, not technical. For developers who want to become FDEs, that's good news: what's missing isn't another framework, but the ability to read a customer's cash flow. And that ability can be learned.

Is time saved really money?

IBM divides ROI into two categories. Hard ROI is tangible impact directly tied to profit. Soft ROI refers to benefits that don't directly map to profit but still help the organization, such as less employee burnout or faster decisions.

The trap engineers fall into is this: most of what we can measure (minutes saved, automation rates, accuracy) is only soft ROI. It only becomes hard ROI when it changes a specific line on the P&L: revenue up, costs down, or an expense that would have happened no longer needed.

There are three basic ways a customer makes money: selling more, keeping customers longer, or doing the same work at lower cost. Before writing any code, figure out which one the project affects. If the answer is "none of them," the project is just waiting in line to be cut.

Example: a 200-seat call center

Consider a hypothetical case. A consumer finance company's call center has 200 agents, each handling 60 calls a day. After each call, the agent spends 3 minutes writing notes into the CRM. You deploy a call-summarization AI agent that cuts this to 1 minute.

The technical math looks great: 200 people × 60 calls × 2 minutes = 24,000 minutes, or 400 hours a day. But turn it into a finance question: where do those 400 hours go? If employees still work full shifts at full pay and call volume doesn't increase, the P&L doesn't move a cent. That's soft ROI.

To turn it into hard ROI, there are three paths, each requiring a different person's sign-off. First, the call center takes on more sales calls with the same team: the sales director must commit to providing enough sales leads. Second, the company stops hiring extra staff for the upcoming busy season: the HR lead must confirm the hiring plan.

Third, outsourcing costs drop: the owner of the outsourcing contract must agree to scale down.

Now the answer to finance is no longer "we saved 400 hours," but: "400 hours a day equals the headcount HR had planned to hire for the busy season, and HR has confirmed that number." That's a verifiable financial statement.

Where's the money, and who signs off?

Many engineers assume the money sits with the CIO, because the CIO is the first person they meet. Futurum Group's findings are exactly the opposite: AI spending is increasingly held in business unit budgets, and budget authority is spreading beyond the CIO.

Its advice to vendors: first confirm who actually controls the budget before treating the CIO as the primary economic decision-maker.

An opinion piece on DEV Community describes a common division of labor: IT picks the technology, business units use it, and finance approves the budget. The author argues the problem is that no one is accountable for financial outcomes—that is, the gap between forecasted and actual numbers.

That's one author's view, not a research finding, but it gives FDEs a usable map.

StakeholderWhat they care aboutWhat the FDE needs to prepare
ITSecurity, integration, operating costsArchitecture, data handling, operations plan
Business unitsWhether their team can hit its targetsBefore-and-after business metrics, new workflows
FinanceMoney spent vs. money recoveredBaseline, assumptions, how hard ROI will be measured

Once costs start exceeding plan, finance deserves even more attention. Futurum reports that 46.9% of companies say AI spending has exceeded budget, and only 5.6% spent less than planned. According to Futurum, finance is also the department most likely to put this issue on the table.

If you haven't met anyone from finance yet, they will come find you—and usually at the worst possible time.

Four questions for discovery

You don't need to see the customer's financial statements. You just need to naturally ask four questions during customer discovery, ideally to the owner of the business process.

"If this project succeeds, which number in your monthly report changes?" This finds the P&L line. "What is that number today, and who tracks it?" This gets you the baseline. "Which department's budget pays for this project?" This identifies the economic decision-maker.

"If we roll this out company-wide, who has to sign off?" This tells you who to meet next.

Write the answers on one page: the P&L line, the baseline, the budget holder, the sign-off for scaling, and the person responsible for comparing forecast vs. actual. Any blank field is project risk, just as serious as an undocumented API.

Three common mistakes

The first mistake is reporting technical metrics to people who need financial ones. 92% accuracy means nothing to someone approving a budget unless you can explain what the remaining 8% costs to handle manually.

The second mistake is not measuring a baseline before deployment. Once the AI agent goes live, you can never prove "it used to take 3 minutes." Measure in the first week, even if it means timing 30 calls by hand with a stopwatch.

The third mistake is treating your friendliest user as the decision-maker. The team lead who raves about your project doesn't necessarily control the budget. If you have only one relationship line at the customer, the project's survival depends on whether that person can stay in the room at the budget meeting.

Putting this skill on your resume

When reading FDE job descriptions, look for sections about customer business outcomes or collaborating with multiple stakeholders. If they're there, that's where you put an example like the call center case above—and the same goes for interviews. Don't write "built a call-summarization data pipeline" on your resume.

Instead, write that you cut post-call processing time from one specific number to another, which department confirmed it, and which of their plans it changed.

Great FDEs don't just deploy AI agents. They can stand in the middle of the room and answer the finance question before anyone asks it.
