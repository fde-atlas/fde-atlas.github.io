Forward Deployed Engineer: the Job that Makes AI Real

Interconnected · 2025-12-03 · 10 min read

Summary

This Interconnected post, co-written with a former Palantir FDE, explains the forward deployed engineer role: its origins at Palantir serving US intelligence agencies, and its outcome-based, productization-driven model that helped push Palantir's gross margin to 82%. It argues generative AI faces two adoption problems in large organizations—hard to trust and hard to value—and FDEs address both by building deterministic guardrails and context-specific evals, while surfacing proprietary data useful for training. OpenAI's FDE team is scaling from two to 52 people this year; Anthropic and OpenAI have partnered with GSIs Cognizant and Accenture to expand capacity. The catch: FDE talent is rare, easily misused as consulting, and may become a growth bottleneck.

Key points

  • FDEs originated at Palantir around 2003, working onsite for intelligence and government clients.
  • Unlike consultants, FDEs are paid on outcomes and must productize custom solutions for resale.
  • FDEs solve enterprise AI's trust and value problems via guardrails and context-specific evals.
  • OpenAI's FDE team targets 52 heads by year-end; both OpenAI and Anthropic partner with GSIs for capacity.
  • Scarce FDE talent and consulting-mode drift could bottleneck AI enterprise adoption.

ChatGPT just had its 3rd birthday last week. With each passing birthday of this “toddler” comes heightened expectations of what its AI can do in the real world. A big part of living up to those lofty expectations comes from the frontier research that can (hopefully) make GPT models smarter. That’s why the hardest and most expensive type of talent to hire is undoubtedly AI researchers and engineers, so much so that centi-billionaire CEOs hand-cook and hand-deliver soups to poach them. The most desirable title in Silicon Valley – and a low-key flex – anointed to these AI research wunderkinds is: “member of the technical staff”. No hierarchy, pure brain.

However, if there is a second-hardest type of talent to hire, with arguably a cooler sounding title, that would be “forward deployed engineer”. These forward deployed engineers (FDEs) are as mysterious as they are influential. And their influence is growing quickly inside leading AI labs.

OpenAI’s Head of Forward Deployed Engineering, Colin Jarvis, shared with Apoorv Agrawal of of Altimeter Capital that his team is growing from a team of two at the beginning of this year to a target headcount of fifty-two (52) by the end of this year! Anthropic, OpenAI’s arch-rival, recently signed a major partnership with Cognizant, a leading GSI (global system integrator), where it will roll out Claude to all of Cognizant’s 350,000 employees. But the more important part of this partnership is Anthropic leveraging Cognizant’s employees to boost its forward deployed engineering capacity into different industries, because Dario’s own FDEs just aren’t enough and he can’t hire them fast enough. Not to be outdone, earlier this week OpenAI signed a similar partnership with another GSI, Accenture.

So what does a FDE do? Why does every AI lab want (and need) them to make AI real, especially for large enterprise customers and governments? And where did this militaristic-sounding role come from?

In this post, co-written with a good friend who was a real-life FDE himself, we will try to answer all these questions and more. As it turns out, the origin of the FDE function all started with the one tech company Silicon Valley loved to hate (so much so it moved to Denver), the same tech company that Washington, DC now loves to love: Palantir.

Unwrapping the Forward Deployed Engineer Mystery

FDE as a concept is almost two-decade old. It emerged shortly after Palantir’s founding in 2003, as it was tackling its first set of clients: agencies in the US intelligence community.

FDEs are often (mis)construed as glorified IT consultants. This confusion is understandable because all FDEs work on a client’s site – sometimes for a few weeks, sometimes up to a year – depending on the complexity, depth, and dollar value of the engagement. As Nabeel Qureshi, a former Palantir FDE, reflected, “FDEs were typically expected to ‘go onsite’ to the customer’s offices and work from there 3-4 days per week.” For anyone who has known a management consultant or any type of consultant in their life, traveling every week to work at the client’s office is the classic consultant lifestyle. That’s the “forward deployed” part of the title. However, the similarity between FDEs and consultants starts and ends there.

The major difference is the expected outcome of FDE’s work and the business model that’s wrapped around them. For most consultants, services that generate billable hours is the name of the game. The goal is to solve a specific problem or provide support to clients, but probably never completely solve the problem, otherwise the billable hours dry up. More labor means more money, just like lawyers or accountants. Maximizing billable hours is the core incentive driver.

FDEs, on the other hand, are tasked with building custom solutions for a client, while also discovering how some of those solutions can be productized and generalized into pieces of software that can be sold to other similar clients in the future. Their work is compensated as a percentage of outcome achieved, e.g. productivity gain or cost reduction, rather than billable hours. In fact, disciplined FDEs might decline client requests that are billable hours in nature, if they deem the work irrelevant or distracting from the core outcomes they need to deliver and productize, leaving some short-term money on the table in hope of long-term gains. (The best way to describe this work is context R&D.)

In hindsight, this approach made a lot of sense for Palantir in the early days. It was working with the intelligence community and the military, where needs are reticent, trust is scarce, and mission-critical problems are not readily shared out. Gaining access to the right problems to solve and requisite political support required earning trust incrementally. So going on-site, or forward deploying, became a natural initial step to start the relationship. These government clients may be sensitive about their data, but once a trusting relationship is built, they tend to be price insensitive about how much to pay for solutions. This means huge contracts with high dollar value that can last many years, leading to sticky revenue with fat margins.

After about a decade of building this motion with US government agencies, Palantir’s FDEs scaled to foreign (and friendly) governments and private companies in regulated industries, like financial services, healthcare, aerospace, and others. These FDEs fanned out and have since been deployed inside banks, insurers, hospitals, factories, power plants, etc. in London, Paris, Tokyo, Wellington, and across the US.

Slowly but surely, Palantir’s revenue, once ridiculed in Silicon Valley as low-margin consulting money, now has a gross margin of 82%! All because the FDEs distilled much of their custom-built solutions for one client into repeatable and reusable software, then packaged and re-sold to other clients. It also helps that the typical clients for Palantir are large, hard to convince, but, once you succeed, are happy to pay a premium price for many years.

It is not hard to see why any company that wants to work with deep-pocketed enterprises or governments want their own FDEs, but especially AI labs.

A forward deployed Yeti mug, only for the real FDEs.

Why Does Every AI Lab Want (Need) Them

AI adoption inside large organizations have two interconnected problems: hard to trust, hard to value.

The magic, and downfall, of generative AI is its probabilistic nature. A large language model is more nuanced, more creative, thus more “magical” than so-called traditional machine learning. But its behavior is also, by definition, impossible to predict and hard to explain. This unpredictability is simply not ok for many high-stakes, mission-critical situations.

The value, or lack thereof, of generative AI is also difficult to benchmark and measure. And inside large enterprises, you can’t adopt a new technology on virality or “vibes” – an important contrast to consumer adoption or even small and medium sized businesses adoption. Sure there are many, perhaps too many, benchmarks out there measuring various dimensions of intelligence of a foundation model. Domain specific leaderboards beyond text-based LLMs are also emerging, quantifying everything from video creation to design chops. But all these public benchmarks, or evals, can only get you so far, when large organizations have different contexts and value definition. A glowing eval score of 97.3% on a benchmark looks nice in a model card, but does little to convince large organizations to adopt; they need 100% interpretability and are worried about that remaining 2.7%!

These two problems are two sides of the same coin. With generative AI in large enterprises, you don’t know what you are getting and no single set of evals can measure what you are getting. A crack team of forward deployed engineers, in theory, can solve both problems at once.

For the problem of trust, we have already described how the first iteration of FDEs at Palantir navigated this issue from a relationship building perspective with arguably the most skeptical client type: intelligence agencies. The same lesson applies to AI labs trying to persuade a big bank to deploy more LLMs. However, given the probabilistic nature of these AI tools, a technical solution is also required to forge trust: deterministic guardrails around the probabilistic LLM. This means a set of rules and boundaries that the AI cannot trip over or its behavior is 100% predictable (aka deterministic) to get the customer comfortable. That was what Colin Jarvis did when OpenAI was trying to persuade Morgan Stanley to roll out more GPT-based AI tools for its wealth management division. And this requirement generalizes across industries, even if specific business logics differ. This is the perfect problem for FDEs to solve, then port back into the product platform to be reused for other clients. For any AI lab’s FDEs, their first order of business is likely always: build guardrails to build trust.

As for the problem of value evaluation, here is where FDEs demand discovery comes in, effectively translating public benchmarks into context-specific evals that the client would use and believe. Only if the AI tools achieve agreed-upon outcomes based on these context-specific evals would the engagement be considered successful. After all, how well a version of GPT or Claude performs on SWE-bench may get you in the door. But how well the model with its new guardrails, plus some fine-tuning and post-training on the company’s proprietary data, perform on the company’s own code base is what will let you walk out with a big contract. A competent team of FDEs can architect these context-specific evals to both make their clients happy and their bosses (and themselves) paid.

There is a bonus benefit to having FDEs for an AI lab that is underappreciated and does not quite apply to pre-AI software products. A FDE-driven discovery process can surface latent company context, data, problem sets, and other hidden institutional knowledge that are locked inside corporate silos. All this information becomes extremely valuable training data for the AI research team – all those members of the technical staff. This bonus opens up a whole new level of generalizability and re-packaging potential if, for example, OpenAI wants to train a frontier model just for wealth management in financial services. To even entertain this path of AI verticalization, it must start with the type of FDE engagement that Colin Jarvis had with Morgan Stanley.

Scalable Enablers or Growth Bottleneck?

Now that we have laid out all the reasons why forward deployed engineers are the best thing since sliced bread, what’s the catch?

They are hard to find and easy to misuse.

The venn diagram of skillsets is rare. The ideal FDE candidate, of course, has to be technical with strong engineering chops, because a big part of the job is building generalizable solutions and features. However, this person also has to be business savvy, can quickly grok a customer’s domain specific context, while having enough soft people skills to navigate the client’s internal politics and persuade all team leaders and gatekeepers to be onboard, when some of them don’t want you there! In one of the FDEs projects that Qureshi was part of, even though the client paid for a 8-12 weeks pilot, the FDEs had to spend most of that 8-12 weeks period negotiating with different teams to just gain data access from different silos, before rushing out a demo at the very last minute. Knowing how to read a room and make friends quickly is as desirable a skill as building good software.

Assuming you are able to hire a crew of qualified FDEs, it is deceptively easy to slip into consulting mode, especially if you are under short-term revenue pressure. These FDEs could also end up doing implementation work to shoehorn the customer’s problems and workflows into the AI solution, when the FDEs should be doing the opposite. This mistake is common because it is a common practice of pre-AI SaaS products, where teams of solutions engineers or solutions architects (two other titles that often get conflated with FDEs) pair up with a sales person to help a customer use their SaaS product, while doing their best to not do any customization of that SaaS product. (Otherwise, that would be considered a consulting service.)

If the pace of AI adoption and value creation is dependent on the availability of FDE-like talent, it is an open question whether the necessity of having them is a scalable enabler to wider adoption or a growth bottleneck. That is assuming you can build a team to begin with and know how to forward deploy them strategically. The shortage of FDE talent has not been much of an industry problem, because Palantir was the only game in town and cornered the market of anyone who has the traits or penchant to be a FDE.

Now that the benefit of FDE for spreading AI into the enterprises is becoming more obvious, the battle for this talent is growing fierce. And it’s not like Palantir stopped hiring for them either.

Source: https://www.palantir.com/careers/open-positions/

Where FDEs should sit inside a tech company is also a moving target. For Palantir, they are under the Engineering umbrella. For Anthropic, the FDE job openings fall under the Sales function. As for OpenAI, which has 23 FDE openings at the time of this writing, it branded the team under a unique name: “Model Deployment for Business”.

Considering how the “forward deployed engineer” title is itself a fantastic case study of clever branding, how this function is portrayed and positioned may just make the difference in attracting this rare talent. However, making AI real for all those large companies and government agencies will take a lot more than clever branding. And no one knows that better than the FDEs.
