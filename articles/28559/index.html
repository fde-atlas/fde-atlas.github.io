Team of Teams and One Mission: An FDE Reading List

Original title: Team of Teams 与 One Mission：FDE 书单

FDE Times · 2026-11-17 · 5 min read

Summary

This article recommends Stanley McChrystal's Team of Teams (Portfolio, 2015) and Chris Fussell's sequel One Mission (2017) as reading for forward deployed engineers. It draws a parallel between the task force's six-month embedding program and FDEs working inside customer teams. The most practical part is One Mission's chapter on liaison officers, who honestly relay partner reactions and extinguish small fires before they spread—a job Fussell says only experienced people should hold. The author also advises regular weekly updates to product teams to build shared consciousness, and suggests resume tips for aspiring FDEs.

Key points

  • Team of Teams argues for small-team autonomy plus organization-wide sharing.
  • One Mission details what liaison officers do daily: translate partner reactions honestly.
  • Liaison roles should go to experienced members, not newcomers, at key accounts.
  • FDEs should send regular weekly updates so product teams see the same picture.
  • Aspiring FDEs can highlight turning customer feedback into product changes on resumes.

Key Takeaways

  • *Team of Teams* (Portfolio, 2015), written by Stanley McChrystal and three co-authors, argues for giving small teams the freedom to experiment, then sharing what they learn across the entire organization.
  • Chris Fussell's sequel *One Mission* points out that liaison officers are responsible for "translating" the partner's reactions, and this role should only be given to experienced people.
  • For FDEs, the value of both books comes down to one loop: embed with the customer's team, report real reactions, put out small fires, and sync regularly with the product team.

Six months. According to Shortform's book summary, the task force commanded by Stanley McChrystal ran an "embedding" program: sending one member to work inside another team for six months.

If you are a Forward Deployed Engineer (FDE), this description will look familiar. You are likewise embedded in the customer's team, and the hardest work often comes after the embedding: connecting the customer's team with the product team back at your company, so both sides understand each other before misunderstandings turn into crises.

So this military-themed book deserves a spot on an FDE's bookshelf. It won't teach you how to write code; it teaches you how to stand at the boundary between two organizations.

What the Book Is About and Who Wrote It

The full title is *Team of Teams: New Rules of Engagement for a Complex World*. Stanley McChrystal co-authored it with Tantum Collins, David Silverman, and Chris Fussell, and Portfolio (Penguin Random House) published it on May 12, 2015. The publisher positions it as a guide to help teams become more flexible, more agile, and more cohesive when the environment changes drastically.

The core argument is simple: empower small teams to experiment, then have them share what they learn across the entire organization, and the whole organization will react faster and make better decisions. Since 2011, McChrystal Group, the consulting firm McChrystal founded, has been refining this approach and bringing it to many organizations beyond the military.

In 2017, Chris Fussell published the sequel *One Mission*. The part most worth an FDE's time is in this book: the chapter on liaison officers. The first book explains why teams need connections; the second shows what the person responsible for those connections does every day.

First Insight: Send People to Embed So They See the Other Team's Strengths

According to Shortform, the purpose of the embedding program was to build understanding and trust, because the person sent over could see the other team's strengths firsthand. The key word is "strengths." The embedded member is not there to find fault with the other side, but to stop seeing them as "the other side."

For FDEs, this point cuts both ways. First: you yourself need to see what the customer's team is good at, such as their deeper understanding of their own business data or operational processes than yours. Second, and often overlooked: the product team at your company also needs to embed, even just once, so they don't see the customer as just a string of tickets.

You can take a small step at the customer right now: invite a product engineer to sit with the customer's operations team for half a day. The purpose is the same as McChrystal's embedding program, just smaller: let that person see the customer team's strengths firsthand, rather than hearing about them secondhand from you.

Second Insight: The Liaison Officer Is a Translator—Don't Give This Job to a Newcomer

Fussell describes the liaison officer as a lifeline to the key partner, MNC-I, the person who maintains trust between both sides. The job sounds mundane. They go back and report to leadership something like: Boss, after we did X, they were extremely unhappy.

Now translate this to an FDE scenario. The product team just changed the pagination of an API, and the customer's nightly reconciliation data pipeline starts failing. Written the way a liaison officer would, the message might read:

"Tuesday's update changed the orders endpoint from offset pagination to cursor pagination. The customer's finance team's nightly reconciliation data pipeline only reads the first page, so this morning's report is missing data.

Their team lead is extremely unhappy and is already asking whether the launch should be paused. Recommendation: keep the old pagination running in parallel until they fix it; notify this team in advance of every future API change."

Four sentences, four elements complete: what changed, who was affected, how they reacted, and what to do first. Fussell calls this outcome: putting out small fires before the whole forest burns.

The most common mistake is downplaying the customer's reaction. Writing "the customer has some feedback on pagination" sounds more polite, but the product team will push it to the bottom of the backlog, while the other side is considering whether to halt the launch. The phrase Fussell relays—"they were extremely unhappy"—is valuable precisely because it offers no comfort.

Fussell also stresses that only experienced members can serve as liaison officers with the most important partners. If you lead an FDE team, don't hand your biggest customer to a newcomer.

Third Insight: Shared Understanding Doesn't Appear on Its Own

McChrystal Group calls the premise of the whole model "shared consciousness." According to the company, it can only form and be maintained through regular, transparent information sharing. If you only report to the company when something goes wrong, the first thing you lose is the "regular" part.

*One Mission* has a line that makes the division of labor clear: frontline teams fight in their own positions, while leaders and liaison officers maintain the relationships needed to support them. On a project, the customer's team and your engineers handle the deployment. Your job is to make the product team, the sales team, and leadership on both sides see the same picture.

Put into practice: send a fixed update every Friday, same template, same time, even in weeks when nothing happened. A quiet week is just as valuable, because it tells the product team: no news means everything is fine.

What Order to Read In, and What to Put on Your Resume

Read *Team of Teams* first, focusing on the chapters on shared consciousness and small-team autonomy. Then read *One Mission* to see how these ideas land in the hands of a liaison officer. The first book suits FDEs leading projects or building teams; the second suits those about to be sent to a customer site.

If you are thinking of moving into an FDE role, write on your resume about a time you turned customer feedback into a product change, and specify what was changed. When reading job postings, look for items asking you to bring customer feedback back to the product team: that's a liaison officer role under a different name.

Writing code is only half of an FDE's job. The other half is connecting two organizations, and Fussell would only entrust that job to the most experienced people.
