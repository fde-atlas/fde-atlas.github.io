How to Internalize AI Development | 5 Steps and Decision Criteria for Breaking Free from Outsourcing Dependence

Original title: AI内製化の進め方｜外注依存から脱却する5ステップと判断基準

FDX株式会社 · 2026-06-08 · 16 min read

Summary

This Japanese-language article by FDX株式会社 argues that successful in-housing of AI depends on maintaining a dedicated internal team combining business understanding and technical skill—hiring engineers alone or buying SaaS does not qualify. It lays out five sequential steps: selecting the target business process, forming a dedicated 3–5 person team, choosing the technology stack, moving from PoC to production, and handing over operations before expanding to other processes. Following the order, one process can be in-housed in roughly 6–12 months. Outsourcing should serve as a catalyst, with build-vs-buy decisions made per process across four axes: business understanding depth, improvement frequency, data sensitivity, and reusability. The article also describes three common failure patterns and recommends implementation partners who work on-site, transfer knowledge, and set explicit exit conditions, contrasting with traditional SI-style delivery.

Key points

  • In-housing means a permanent internal team with both business and technical skills.
  • Five steps must be followed in order; skipping steps stalls at production.
  • Teams of 3–5 dedicated members; part-time setups mostly fail.
  • Judge in-house vs outsourcing on four axes; keep core in-house, buy generic parts.
  • Good partners work on-site, transfer knowledge, and define exit conditions upfront.

Why "AI in-housing" is now on the executive agenda

Until 2024, generative AI adoption was in the phase of "just ask the vendor or SI for a PoC for now." But entering 2026, the tide has turned. The competitive gap three years from now between "companies that keep outsourcing AI" and "companies that build AI in-house" has become clearly visible.

With outsourcing, knowledge never stays in-house

The most valuable learnings from generative AI projects emerge in the "process of putting business operations onto AI." Which decision branches are difficult, where to hand back to humans, what to measure. You can't understand these without deep engagement with the business. The knowledge learned by outsourced vendors stays with the vendor. Even if you keep paying, your organization's capabilities never accumulate.

Many companies that fully outsourced RPA implementation three years ago have operations that have stalled today. Every time the vendor changed, systems stopped working, and on-the-ground knowledge was never passed on. We want to avoid repeating the same mistake with AI.

Both models and tools change every month

In the generative AI world, assumptions change within months. Claude versions get upgraded, new frameworks emerge, costs drop. Without an internal structure that can keep up with these changes, systems become obsolete. This is fundamentally different from traditional software, where you build once and use it for years.

With an outsourcing-based approach, you need new estimates and contract changes every time. With an internal structure for continuous improvement, you can absorb change as opportunity.

Running costs are also easier to reduce in the medium to long term

Vendor-dependent generative AI adoption can incur annual running costs on the scale of tens of millions to hundreds of millions of yen. On the other hand, as in-housing progresses, the same functionality can be covered by a combination of cloud usage fees plus personnel costs, and in some cases running costs drop significantly over a period of years. The initial investment for launch is substantial, but the more operations run in-house, the faster the payback.

What "AI in-housing" really means

The term "in-housing" is used ambiguously. Often the definitions differ between executives and practitioners. This article defines it as follows.

AI in-housing refers to a state in which a dedicated team with both business understanding and technical skills is permanently based in-house and can continuously run the planning, implementation, operation, and improvement of AI systems.

Three common misconceptions that deviate from this definition are as follows.

Misconception 1: It is not "hiring engineers"

Simply hiring engineers with technical skills does not make in-housing work. AI systems are deeply embedded in business operations, so engineers who cannot see the business context get stuck at the very first judgment: what to build in the first place. Conversely, a business expert who has only dabbled in Python on their own cannot withstand production operation.

What works is either "people who can make technical judgments with an understanding of the business" or "a small team where business experts and engineers collaborate daily." The key lies not in individual skills themselves but in the relationship where the two mesh together.

Misconception 2: It is not "signing up for SaaS"

Rolling out ChatGPT Enterprise or Microsoft 365 Copilot company-wide is "tool adoption," not "in-housing." SaaS is a convenient foundation, but the logic specialized to your business and data integration still must be designed by someone. Whether you can handle that design in-house is the essence of in-housing.

Misconception 3: It is not "building everything in-house"

The opposite misconception is "use no outsourcing at all and build everything in-house from scratch." This is also unrealistic. There is no need to train foundation models (Claude, GPT, Gemini) yourself, and there is no point in implementing observability infrastructure (LangSmith, Langfuse) yourself. What you should hold in-house is the design and operation of the junction between business and AI; for generic components, it is efficient to use the best external options.

Correct in-housing is a hybrid of "core in-house, periphery external."

Five steps to successful AI in-housing

From here, let's organize the concrete process. These five steps have meaning in their very order; steps that are skipped almost always cause bottlenecks later. The foundation of the "maintain order / judge at gates" approach common to all five steps is covered in "How to Introduce Generative AI"; this article digs deeper into it with a focus on in-house development issues.

Step 1: Selecting the Target Business Process

If you choose the wrong first process, everything downstream goes awry. Choose a process that meets the following three conditions as the first candidate for in-house development.

Condition 1: The scope of impact is limited Do not suddenly target company-wide processes. Narrow it down to one process in one department, on the scale of tens to hundreds of items processed per day. The iron rule is to start with a size where the company won't stop even if you fail.

Condition 2: Results can be quantified Avoid vague goals like "efficiency" or "quality improvement." Choose processes where you can later look back at numbers, such as "50% reduction in processing time" or "first-response rate of 70% or higher." If you start in-house development with a process whose ROI cannot be measured, you won't be able to decide whether to continue or withdraw.

Condition 3: Many decision branches, but the process can be rule-based Fully routine processes are better suited to RPA, and purely creative work should be handled by humans. AI agents show their true value in processes that can be rule-based but involve many rules, or where assumptions change easily (see: The Complete Guide to AI Agents).

As concrete candidates, cross-search of internal knowledge, research and proposal drafts for sales prospects, first-line handling of inquiries, and drafting of approval request documents tend to produce early wins as the first project.

Step 2: Building a Dedicated Team

This step has the greatest influence on the success or failure of in-house development. The moment you form a team with concurrent roles, in-house development is almost certain to stall.

Team composition (3–5 people as a guideline)

  • Business owner (the on-site manager of that process, 1 person)
  • Business engineer (business understanding plus implementation skills, 1–2 people)
  • AI engineer (prompt design and model selection, 1 person)
  • Project lead (bridge to management, concurrent role acceptable, 1 person)

Why dedicated roles matter Developing and operating an AI system does not improve in quality unless you touch the details of the process every day. With a part-time team meeting once a week, context is lost and decision-making is delayed. If you cannot assemble a team that can commit full-time for six months, it is wiser to reconsider the decision to develop in-house altogether.

A realistic approach to securing talent As of 2026, people who possess both business understanding and technical skills are scarce in the market. Relying solely on new hiring is unrealistic. Rather, reskilling—adding technical skills to internal business experts—has a higher success rate than hiring technical talent from outside. Technical skills can be acquired in 6–12 months, whereas business understanding takes years. It pays to leverage this difference in learning speed.

The decision between hiring and training is detailed in the separate article "Integrating FDEs into Your Organization."

Step 3: Selecting the Technology Stack

Do not start technology selection with "just use ChatGPT for now." Decide the following three things in order.

1. Selecting the foundation model Try major models such as Claude, GPT, and Gemini, and decide based on performance and cost for the target process. People tend to think "using the strongest model will produce quality," but for 80% of processes, mid-tier models (Claude Haiku, GPT-5 mini, Gemini Flash) deliver sufficient accuracy. Cost can vary by more than 10x depending on model selection.

2. Selecting the framework/SDK For serious agent development, choose one of LangGraph (open source), Claude Agent SDK, or OpenAI Agents SDK. The decision criteria are "tolerance for vendor lock-in," "handling of sensitive data," and "whether multi-agent requirements exist" (see: The Complete Guide to AI Agents).

3. Selecting the observability and audit platform This is the part most often postponed and most regretted later. Incorporate one of LangSmith, Langfuse, or OpenTelemetry from the start. If you defer it with "we can add it once it's running," you will get stuck on quality and audit requirements right before production deployment, resulting in rework.

Step 4: From PoC to Production

Once 80% of the design is complete, move into limited operation. Here, the attitude of designing the PoC with production in mind is important.

Clarify the purpose of the PoC What you examine in a PoC is not "does it work?" You verify "can it be integrated into the process?", "does it achieve the expected quality?", and "is the operational burden acceptable?" A PoC that is purely technical validation almost always stalls at the production stage.

Limited operation for 2–3 months Run a 2–3 month limited operation with a few people in one department, accumulating friction in business integration, patterns of quality degradation, and the actual operational burden. The on-the-ground knowledge gained here becomes the greatest asset of in-house development.

Go to production once the gate is passed Based on the limited operation data, judge "whether company-wide rollout is appropriate." Quantify the judgment criteria in advance (e.g., processing quality of XX% or higher, operational burden within XX hours per month, error rate of XX% or lower).

Detailed procedures for getting past the PoC plateau are organized in the separate article "How to Introduce Generative AI."

Step 5: Operational Handover and Horizontal Expansion

The six months to one year after production deployment is the true validation period for in-house development.

Establish the system for taking over operations Who will improve prompts, update tool definitions, and notice quality degradation? Put in place a structure where these roles are carried out continuously. If more than half a year passes without operational design, the system will surely become obsolete.

Run an improvement loop monthly Once a month, review operational logs, user feedback, and quality metrics, decide 3–5 improvement items, and implement them the following month. Continuing this is the only way to keep an AI system alive.

Horizontal expansion starts 3–6 months later Once operations are stable in the first business process, reuse the agent foundation for the next one. The ROI of in-housing grows significantly from this horizontal expansion stage onward. The second and subsequent business processes can be launched at roughly one-third the cost and time of the first.

Criteria for deciding between in-house vs. outsourcing

"Fully in-house" and "fully outsourced" are both extremes, and both fail. Use the following four criteria to decide for each business process and workflow.

CriterionLean in-houseLean outsourcing
Depth of business understandingLots of business-specific tacit knowledgeCan be handled with industry-standard procedures
Frequency of improvementRequires continuous improvement at least monthlyBuilt once and used for years
Sensitive dataMainly data that cannot leave the companyCan be processed with cloud SaaS
ReusabilityPlanned expansion to other business processesOne-off or limited use

The basic rule: if two or more of the four criteria lean in-house, build in-house; otherwise outsource. That said, avoid full outsourcing. At minimum, design decisions and operational quality checks should be secured in-house.

For details on these criteria, see the separate article "Using external FDEs vs. developing them in-house", which organizes them into five standards.

Three common failure patterns

When in-housing stalls, the way it stalls follows patterns. The following three are representative.

Failure 1: Pushing "all in-house" and hitting a wall after six months

Leadership enthusiastically orders "in-housing" and tries to handle hiring technical talent and building the foundation entirely in-house. As a result, six months pass with zero business processes in production while the budget is consumed. The dead end is visible from the moment you try to own even the generic components (foundation models, frameworks, observability infrastructure) yourself.

How to avoid it: Draw the line up front—core (the intersection of business and AI) in-house, periphery (generic components) external.

Failure 2: Starting with a team that has only one of business understanding or technical skill

A development team with technical skill but no business knowledge, or a team of business experts with no technical talent. With either composition, in-housing won't be complete even in a year. Finalize the team structure alongside choosing the first business process.

How to avoid it: Use dedicated staff, not part-time assignments, and assemble both pillars: business experts plus technical engineers.

Failure 3: Putting off audit and governance

Once a PoC gives a sense of "it works," many companies postpone the access controls, audit logs, and quality-check systems needed before production. They get stuck right before production launch, and deployment is delayed by months. If audit requirements aren't brought into the design phase, everything breaks down at the end.

How to avoid it: In the technology selection of Step 3, build observability and audit infrastructure in from the start.

How to use implementation partners to accelerate in-housing

I've emphasized "in-housing" throughout, but that doesn't mean you shouldn't use external partners. In fact, choosing the right partner can make the in-housing ramp-up 2–3x faster.

However, traditional SI-style partners (requirements definition → design → implementation → delivery → maintenance) don't work in an in-housing context. That's because the SI model assumes "build and hand over," leaving no knowledge inside the company.

Partners that accelerate in-housing need three characteristics.

1. Embedded on-site, working alongside you Not just holding meetings at headquarters offices, but getting into the actual business operations and building together. Developing side by side with the client company's engineers. This is exactly the working style Palantir calls the Forward Deployed Engineer (see: What is an FDE? / Palantir case study).

2. Building while transferring knowledge The building process itself develops internal talent. A true implementation partner's job is to "transfer capability."

3. Setting exit conditions up front Design a contract structure from the start that doesn't create ongoing dependence on the partner. Make the goal explicit, e.g., "50% in-house ratio after 3 months" or "operations fully in-house after 6 months." The moment you choose a vendor who "will always be there," the goal of in-housing recedes.

Frequently asked questions (FAQ)

Q1. What does AI in-housing specifically mean?

A. It means having a dedicated team in-house with both business understanding and technical skill, able to continuously run the planning, implementation, operation, and improvement of AI systems. Merely hiring engineers or merely contracting a SaaS does not constitute in-housing. The essence is whether your company can internally handle the design of logic and data integration specialized to your own business.

Q2. How long does AI in-housing take?

A. If you follow the five steps in order—selecting the target business process, forming a dedicated team, selecting the technology stack, moving from PoC to production, and handing over operations—you can generally in-house one business process in about 6–12 months as of 2026. Horizontal expansion starts 3–6 months after operations stabilize, and the second and subsequent business processes launch at roughly one-third the cost and time of the first.

Q3. How should you choose between in-house and outsourcing?

A. Decide for each business process and workflow using four criteria: depth of business understanding, frequency of improvement, sensitive data, and reusability. The basic rule: if two or more of the four lean in-house, build in-house; otherwise outsource. However, avoid full outsourcing, and at minimum secure design decisions and operational quality checks in-house.

Q4. Isn't it fine to stay outsourced? Is in-housing really necessary?

A. If you stay outsourced, the crucial knowledge generated in the process of putting your business onto AI stays with the vendor, and your organization's capabilities never accumulate no matter how much you keep paying. Since generative AI changes its premises every few months, without a system for continuous in-house improvement the system becomes obsolete. In the medium to long term, in-hosing also makes it easier to lower running costs as it progresses.

Q5. How should the in-house development team be organized?

A. Organize a dedicated team of 3 to 5 people, typically consisting of a business owner, business engineers, AI engineers, and a project lead. Once you assign people part-time, in-house development is almost certain to stall. For securing talent, rather than filling all roles through new hires, reskilling—adding technical skills to your in-house business experts—has a higher success rate. This is because technical skills can be acquired in 6 to 12 months, whereas business understanding takes years.

Q6. What are common failures in AI in-house development?

A. There are three representative failures: "full in-housing," where you try to own even generic components yourself; team composition where you have only one of business understanding or technical capability; and putting audit and governance on the back burner. All of these can be structurally avoided through design: drawing a clear line between core and peripheral, building a dedicated two-pillar team, and incorporating observability and audit infrastructure from the start.

Summary

The essence of AI in-house development is having a dedicated team that combines business understanding and technical capability permanently stationed within your company. Simply hiring engineers or contracting SaaS will not get you there. The process is ensured by following the 5-step sequence, and the build-vs-buy decision is made per process along 4 axes (business understanding / frequency of improvement / sensitivity / reusability). The three failure patterns (getting stuck with full in-housing / unbalanced team composition / postponing audits) can all be structurally avoided. If you use an implementation partner, choose one that meets three conditions: on-site presence, knowledge transfer, and clearly stated exit conditions. The traditional SI model of delivering and handing off does not work in this context.
