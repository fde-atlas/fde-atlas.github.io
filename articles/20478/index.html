A Breakdown of Glean's Founding FDE Role: Why 0-to-1 Experience Is Required

Original title: Glean 创始 FDE 岗位解读：为何要求 0 到 1 经验

FDE Times · 2026-10-25 · 5 min read

Summary

Glean's job posting for a Founding Forward Deployed Engineer makes clear the role is about building products, not just deploying them: what engineers create at strategic customers feeds into Glean's core product. Drawing on Thomas Otter's framing, the article argues that FDEs billed to clients serve projects, while Glean's company-funded model turns customer work into reusable roadmap items. The role requires having built production-grade software from scratch under ambiguous requirements, plus experience with agent development, evals, and scaled deployment, and 25–50% travel. It closes with advice on writing a 0-to-1 resume and a test for spotting deployment roles disguised as FDE positions.

Key points

  • Glean's founding FDE output becomes part of the core product, not a one-off deployment.
  • Otter's test: client-billed FDEs work on projects, not products.
  • Applicants must show they built production software when requirements were still ambiguous.
  • The role pairs with a Forward Deployed PM and requires 25–50% travel.
  • Resumes should cover the business problem, ambiguity, what you built, and where it was reused.

Key takeaways

  • Glean's job posting is blunt: the founding FDE's primary job is to create product from 0 to 1, and what engineers build goes into Glean's product.
  • Thomas Otter points out that FDEs billed to customers serve projects, not products. Glean chose the opposite model.
  • To apply for this role, you need to prove you have personally built production-grade software while requirements were still ambiguous. Being good at deployment alone is not enough.

"Your primary job is to create product from 0 to 1." That line comes from Glean's posting for a Founding Forward Deployed Engineer (FDE), and the emphasis falls on creating product, not just deploying what already exists.

The posting goes on to make it even clearer: what you build goes into Glean's product. So this role is not just an extension of the product team—it sits at the source of the roadmap.

This matters because the FDE title is being used loosely. In his writing on the role, Thomas Otter warns that many FDE positions are really deployment or pre-sales under a different label.

Glean's posting shows what a "real" FDE role looks for, and why experience installing off-the-shelf products, no matter how well done, is not enough to pass the screen.

Why being good at deployment isn't enough

In a deployment role, the product already exists, and the question is how to get it running in the customer's environment. Glean's posting assigns a different task: at its most strategic customers, identify the biggest unsolved business problems. At that point, there is no product to install.

So the posting sets one core requirement: you have built production-grade software from scratch in ambiguous environments. The key word is "ambiguous."

Deployment engineers work from ready-made specifications. 0-to-1 engineers have to write that specification themselves, often after just a few conversations, before the customer has even articulated their own requirements.

Otter describes exactly this kind of work. In his view, FDEs are suited to work with an undefined scope—that is, when building something that has never existed before.

Otter's test: project or product?

Otter also offers a simple test: if FDEs are billed to customers, they are working on projects, not products. The reason is not hard to see. The customer pays for the engineer's time, both sides just want to deliver per the contract, and neither has any incentive to turn the solution into something reusable.

Glean chose the opposite approach. "What you build goes into Glean's product" is a commitment about the operating model, not just recruiting copy. Underdog's 2026 career guide describes the same idea: FDEs convert customer-side chaos into product knowledge the company can reuse.

Put Glean's posting next to Otter's warning and you get a table for evaluating any FDE job posting:

DimensionDeployment role with an FDE titleGlean-style founding FDE
Starting pointA ready-made product that needs to be installed and runA business problem no one has solved yet
OutputA project completed for a single customerSoftware that later goes into the core product
Who pays for the timeUsually the customer, billed per projectThe company, because the results feed the roadmap
Experience you must proveConfiguration, integration, supportBuilding production systems from scratch amid ambiguous requirements
Who you work withSupport and sales teamsForward Deployed PMs, C-level executives

The work in the left column is not less valuable—it is simply different work, requiring a different resume and leading to a different career path.

Imagine a week at the customer site

Suppose the strategic customer is an insurance company whose legal team spends hours every week searching contracts for clauses. A deployment engineer would ask: which storage systems to connect, how to set permissions? These questions are not wrong, but they all start from the product.

A founding FDE would first ask about the business: which decision is being slowed down, what is the delay costing the company, and who makes the call. The real problem might not be search at all, but comparing new contracts against standard templates.

Next, the engineer builds a small agent, writes an eval set using the legal team's real cases, and only puts it into production once the eval scores are reliable enough.

The final step is where the two roles diverge: adapting the solution so a second and third customer can use it. Under Glean's model, it is precisely at this moment that something built for one customer becomes a feature of the whole product.

Small-team collaboration, a seat next to executives, and plenty of travel

This Glean posting appeared on General Catalyst's job board. It states that the role works as part of a small team with a Forward Deployed PM and serves as a technical partner to the customer's C-level executives.

This structure is telling. Having a PM alongside means the engineer does not have to shoulder the entire work of shaping the product alone, but still has to argue as an equal about what to build.

The posting also requires you to work with founder-level autonomy and ownership. In practice, no one will assign you tickets. You choose the problems, and you bear the consequences of choosing wrong.

The role also has a real personal cost: 25–50% travel. If your family situation does not allow for it, think it through before entering the interview process—don't wait until you have an offer in hand.

The AI part is not decoration

The posting's minimum requirement is four or more years of experience in technical roles, with a track record of shipping software to production. It also names a specific area of experience: agent development, eval frameworks, and deployment at scale.

These three go together for a reason. At a customer site, anyone can build a demo agent in a week. The difference between a real 0-to-1 engineer and a demo builder is evals: do you know when an agent is production-ready, and can you prove that to the customer's executives?

So if you are coming from backend or data engineering, don't stop at learning to call model APIs. Learn to write eval sets with real data, and learn to monitor agents as traffic grows. That is exactly what postings like this screen for.

How to prove 0-to-1 on paper?

Most engineers' resumes read like a list of technologies and systems they have touched. For a role like Glean's founding FDE, this style of writing hides exactly what the hiring manager needs to see.

They are not asking whether you know Kubernetes, but whether you have started from a problem with no existing solution and ended up with software that real users are using.

Write every significant project around four points: what the business problem was, where the ambiguity was at the start, what you personally built, and where it was later reused. Most people skip the last point, yet it is the one that answers Otter's question: were you working on a project, or on a product?

When reading job postings, apply this test in reverse. If the posting only talks about configuration, customer onboarding, and demo support, it is a deployment role, no matter what the title says.

If it talks about building from scratch, the roadmap, and working with customer executives, it is a product role, and you need to have your own 0-to-1 story ready.

The word "Founding" in the title sounds like an honorific. Read Glean's posting carefully and you'll see it is actually an interview question: have you ever created something that didn't exist before, at a time when no one could tell you what it should look like?
