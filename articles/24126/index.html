What Is Palantir's Ontology? A Practical Guide to Its Structure and FDE Design Process

Original title: Palantirのオントロジーとは？構造とFDEの設計手順を実務解説

FDE総研 · 2026-08-20 · 14 min read

Summary

This Japanese article explains Palantir Foundry's Ontology for practitioners: not an academic concept classification, but an executable business model that maps business entities, relationships, and operations onto real data. It describes the structure (object types, link types, action types, functions, plus properties) and argues that an FDE's first job in Palantir projects is ontology design, not dashboards. The recommended process extracts nouns and verbs from field interviews, identifies decision points, starts with a minimal set of roughly 3–5 object types, and validates against sample data. It catalogs common failure patterns—copying existing database tables, over-modeling, deferring permission design, stale data, and undocumented design decisions—and concludes that ontology design is largely general data modeling skill worth practicing even without Foundry access.

Key points

  • Ontology is an executable business model tied to real data, not a concept dictionary.
  • FDEs should design the ontology before visualization, or downstream apps and AI need rework.
  • Process: extract nouns/verbs from interviews, find decision points, start with 3–5 object types.
  • Common failures: copying DB tables, over-modeling, deferred permissions, stale data, knowledge silos.
  • Ontology design is general data modeling applied; practice on paper without Foundry.

What is Palantir's ontology?

Palantir's ontology is a common semantic layer that redefines business entities, relationships, and operations on a data platform.

In Palantir Foundry, the ontology refers to a translation of data scattered across tables and files into business entities (objects) such as "customer," "delivery shipment," "equipment," and "work order," along with their relationships and the operations that can be performed on them. Technically it is an abstraction layer on top of the data layer, but in practical terms its meaning is close to "the work of re-linking the words field staff use one-to-one with column names inside the system" (Source: Palantir Docs "Ontology" https://www.palantir.com/docs/foundry/ontology/overview/).

From the outside, the work of an FDE (Forward Deployed Engineer) can look like "someone who builds dashboards at customer sites," but in Palantir-style projects, the first thing FDEs spend time on is often not visualization but ontology design. The shared sense in the field is that if this foundation is broken and you proceed anyway, downstream apps and AI will likely have to be rebuilt.

How does it differ from the "ontology" of philosophy and information science?

Palantir's ontology is not a description of conceptual classification, but an executable business model connected to real data and processing.

In the context of information science, ontology refers to a knowledge system that formally describes concepts and their relationships, and is often discussed in connection with description languages such as RDF and OWL. Palantir's ontology borrows that idea but has a different purpose. The defined "delivery shipment" object type is linked to rows synced from actual data sources, and executing an action like "cancel shipment" triggers a write-back. In other words, it is not a dictionary for reading, but an entity for running the business.

This difference also shows up in the skills required. Rather than academic precision of classification, the criterion becomes "whether tomorrow's field operations can run with that definition." If you prioritize elegance of definition and discard field exception handling, you end up leaving duplicate management in Excel. Conversely, if you absorb every field exception as an attribute, the model bloats. Drawing the line between these two is, you might say, the very practice of ontology design.

Why does Palantir place the ontology at its core?

Because data integration, applications, and AI can all reference the same definitions, avoiding rebuilds for each use case.

In Palantir's product structure, Foundry handles data integration and the operational platform, with Palantir AIP layered on top as the layer that uses LLMs (Source: Palantir "Platforms" https://www.palantir.com/platforms/). This structure works because what AIP-side logic and agents reference is not raw tables but objects and actions on the ontology.

In other words, the ontology is an investment that enables "once properly defined, visualization, automation, and AI can all reuse the same foundation." Conversely, an approach that stacks up case-by-case SQL and dashboards does not benefit from this reuse. This is also the turning point that determines whether an FDE's work ends as a one-off deliverable or becomes an asset that can be carried to the next customer.

What structure does the ontology have?

The ontology consists of four elements — object types, link types, action types, and functions — with clearly separated roles.

Without grasping the structure, saying "model the business" leaves your hands unable to move. Foundry's ontology can be organized around an easy-to-understand correspondence: nouns (object types), relationships between nouns (link types), verbs (action types), and calculations (functions). It is sometimes explained with five terms by adding the properties attached to object types.

What is the difference between object types, link types, and action types?

Object types correspond to business nouns, link types to relationships between nouns, and action types to verbs that change state.

ComponentRoleBusiness correspondenceExamples (retail, logistics)Key design considerations
Object typeDefines business entitiesNounStore, product, delivery shipment, work orderWhere to place granularity and primary keys
PropertyAttributes of entitiesNature of nounsShipment's scheduled departure time, delay categoryWhether to separate measured and estimated values
Link typeRelationships between entitiesRelationshipShipment ↔ loaded products, store ↔ staffOne-to-many or many-to-many, whether to keep history

When discussions drag on in the field, it is usually about object granularity. Whether you count by "delivery run" or by "delivery run segment" changes all downstream links and actions. Since this is a business judgment rather than a technical one, it is considered safer for the FDE not to decide unilaterally, but to solidify it while showing actual reports and operational rules to field staff. Simply confirming what each line of a report represents often converges the granularity discussion quickly.

How do the ontology and data pipelines connect?

By mapping the datasets shaped by pipelines to object types, the ontology holds actual data.

In Foundry, ingestion and shaping from source systems are done with Pipeline Builder or Code Repositories, and the resulting datasets are mapped to object types on the ontology (Source: Palantir Docs https://www.palantir.com/docs/foundry/). Ontology design therefore cannot be separated from upstream data readiness.

A practical caveat: "definitions you want in the ontology" and "fields that can actually be filled with real data" will always diverge. Since rework is common—writing out the ideal definitions first and only then discovering fields that cannot be filled—it is often faster to proceed with design while simultaneously checking whether sample data fills them. Fields that cannot be filled should not be deleted but explicitly left as "on hold," and sharing with the customer which system changes would fill them becomes material for investment decisions in the next phase.

How does the FDE design and implement the ontology?

The FDE extracts nouns and verbs through business interviews, defines a minimal configuration, and then validates it through field usage.

FDE ontology design is not waterfall-style—completing documentation before implementation—but centers on building small, letting the field touch it, and trimming as you go. The big difference from traditional requirements definition is that validation is measured not by document reviews but by whether the field actually opens the screens.

What is the procedure from business interviews to object definitions?

The basics are to break down field language into nouns and verbs, prioritizing objectification where judgments occur.

  • Have the business flow described verbally — Not from procedure manuals, but have the person in charge talk about how they actually work. Exceptions not in the manuals surface here.
  • Write out nouns and verbs — Line up words like "run," "slip," "send back," "assign" exactly as they appeared in conversation as candidates. Do not paraphrase terms into your own words.
  • Identify where decisions happen — points where people hesitate, confirm, or make phone calls become candidates for action types and functions
  • Define a minimal set of object types — Start with about 3 to 5. This number is an empirical guideline and varies with business scale.
  • Try mapping with real data — Check which columns of existing systems fill the defined properties, and put unfilled fields on hold.
  • Show the field and correct terminology — If field staff read the defined object names without discomfort, the translation has succeeded.

Many say the most valuable part of this process is actually the third step: finding "where judgments occur." Visualizing data alone can be done with existing BI, but whether judgments can be put onto the platform as actions directly determines the value of using an ontology. Conversely, business processes where interviews surface no judgment points may be low priority for ontologization.

How far does the FDE go in deciding actions and permissions?

The principle is to align action execution permissions with business responsibility boundaries, with the FDE only proposing and final approval resting with the customer.

Since action types involve write-backs, designing "who can press which button" is itself security design. Technically, permission groups allow fine-grained control, but what should be judged is not technical but the business side's scope of responsibility. Issues like whether a field clerk may directly update inventory counts, or whether approval is required, are not things the FDE should decide alone.

On the other hand, throwing permission design entirely to the customer tends to stall discussions. A realistic approach for the FDE is to propose a draft that mirrors existing business rules, such as "in the current paper process this person stamps the seal, so how about assigning action permissions to the same person?" Limiting customer-side approval to only new actions that cannot be mapped onto the paper process narrows the issues.

How do you get the field to actually use the built ontology?

Rather than letting people touch objects directly, you need to embed the ontology into daily work flows via business screens or APIs.

The defined ontology can be provided as business screens using application-building features like Workshop, or referenced and operated from external applications using the Ontology SDK (OSDK) (Source: Palantir Docs https://www.palantir.com/docs/foundry/). What matters here is abandoning the idea of having field staff learn how to use data exploration tools.

A typical failure to stick is when, despite an excellent ontology, the field ends up running work through email and phone calls as before. You want to work backward from the design stage: whether the screen can be inserted into existing work flows, and whether the person in charge has a reason to open it multiple times a day. Tying it to existing habits, like "a screen you always open before the morning meeting," speeds up adoption.

Where do ontology implementations tend to stumble?

Three typical failure patterns: transcribing existing tables, over-engineering, and deferring permission design.

The ontology is easy to understand conceptually, but in implementation the same shapes of failure recur. Many can be avoided simply by knowing them as patterns beforehand.

What happens if you copy existing DB tables as-is?

System-driven normalized structures get carried over, resulting in unused models that do not match the field's language.

Core system table designs are often determined by transaction processing efficiency, not business meaning. Copying them as-is into object types leads to results like "T_ORDER_HEADER" and "T_ORDER_DETAIL" sitting side by side as two objects. Field staff treat an "order" as one thing, yet on the platform it is split in two.

Using the source system's structure as a starting point is efficient, but always include a step to rename and redefine granularity in the language of the field. If you see object types that are just table names translated into Japanese, treat it as a sign that no real translation has happened.

Why do overbuilt ontologies stall in operation?

If you expand the scope of what you define too far, data updates and maintenance can't keep up, and the platform becomes one that isn't trusted.

PitfallSymptomWhy it happensPossible countermeasures
Copying tables verbatimObject names don't match field terminologyCarrying over upstream designs as-isRedefine naming and granularity in the language of the field
OverbuildingMost defined objects go unusedPursuing completeness firstNarrow the scope to what's needed for the first single business process
Deferring permissionsDiscussions about access scope arise right before production rolloutDesigning actions only from a functional perspectiveDecide the executor at the same time as defining the action
Ignoring data freshnessOn-screen numbers don't match what the field actually experiencesUpdate frequency not pinned down on the pipeline sideWork backward from the update interval required for business decisions
Knowledge silosDefinition changes can only be made by a specific FDEDesign intent not documentedShare the reasoning behind definitions with the customer's staff

What hits hardest is data freshness in row 4. Even if the ontology definitions themselves are correct, if the numbers are half a day old, the field will trust the information in their own hands. Once the evaluation solidifies as "that screen can't be trusted," usage rarely returns even after a technical fix. It's safe to keep the initial rollout scope narrow and limit it to what you can guarantee freshness for. For the knowledge silo issue in row 5, the cause is often not the definitions themselves but the lack of a record of "why that granularity was chosen."

Where should I start to learn ontologies?

The fastest path is to learn the terminology from the official documentation while practicing modeling familiar business processes yourself.

Since Palantir Foundry environments individuals can freely access are limited, it's realistic to split your learning into "understanding concepts" and "repeated modeling practice."

Where should I start with Palantir's official learning materials?

It's efficient to start with the ontology overview in Palantir Docs and read object types and action types first.

Palantir publishes its product documentation, including explanations of each component of Foundry's ontology (Source: Palantir Docs https://www.palantir.com/docs/foundry/ontology/overview/). Since feature names and terminology can be updated, after grasping the concepts from articles or books, make a habit of always checking the current terminology in the official documentation. Also, Palantir provides information about learning and hands-on programs on its official website, so if you're looking for opportunities to work with the actual product, checking there is the most reliable approach.

As for reading order, (1) ontology overview, (2) object types and properties, (3) link types, (4) action types, and (5) relationship with AIP is easy to follow. If you jump straight into AIP or agents, the terms just pile up without seeing the foundation they reference.

What can I use as a substitute if I don't have Foundry?

Practicing defining objects, links, and actions on paper, using your own work or familiar services as material, is effective.

The core of ontology design is not operating a specific product but the thinking of decomposing business processes into nouns and verbs. This training can be done without Foundry. For example, take a company system you use regularly and try writing out 5 object types, 3 link types, and 3 action types. Then, going as far as "who executes this action?" and "which system can this attribute actually be sourced from?" lets you experience firsthand where design tends to get stuck.

On the technical side, fundamentals of data modeling (normalization, primary key design, history management such as SCD), SQL and Python, and experience with transformation tools like dbt transfer over directly. Ontology design may look like Palantir-specific work, but in reality it's a broad application of general-purpose data modeling skills. For engineers aiming to become FDEs, it's an area worth investing in with little downside, regardless of whether they can land a Palantir job offer.
