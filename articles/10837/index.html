Enterprises don't need an FDE internally—he is already an FDE.

Original title: 企业内部不需要FDE，他，本来就是FDE

53AI · 2026-09-03 · 4 min read

Summary

The author argues companies should not create a dedicated FDE role internally. FDEs mainly eliminate translation layers in the requirements chain, and with AI lowering coding barriers, business people themselves become the shortest chain—they are the requirement. IT's power shifts from being the sole builder to setting rules: toolchains, data access, permissions, and production standards, preventing fragmented shadow IT. Governance mechanisms cannot be designed upfront; they must run first and be iterated, tolerating a rough first version. Companies should also build an in-house harness to continuously accumulate business knowledge for AI to use.

Key points

  • FDE's real value is removing translation layers; inside a company, the business person is the shortest chain.
  • Teaching business staff to build with AI is easier than turning IT staff into domain experts.
  • IT shifts from translator to rule-setter and tool provider, governing what reaches production.
  • Governance must run first and be fixed iteratively; a rough first version is acceptable.
  • An in-house harness lets business knowledge accumulate and be reused by AI over time.

Some companies have already started hiring, and the job requirements all look pretty similar—understand the business, be proficient at VibeCoding, and be able to deliver independently. By these standards, many have been recruiting for months without finding anyone.

My previous article, "FDE Is the Collective Deathbed Fantasy of Chinese Software Companies," received a huge response. Some people said the title was sensationalist, but the content was still a very rational discussion of the value of FDEs on the vendor side. In this article, we'll continue the discussion with FDEs on the client side.

(FDE stands for Forward Deployed Engineer, a practice pioneered by Palantir: sending people who understand the business and can write code themselves to work on-site at customers, completing system implementation directly without passing through layers of product managers and developers.)

These are my personal views—feedback is welcome, and the truth gets sharper through debate:

On the surface we're discussing a headcount question, but the essence is: once AI flattens the barriers to many things, how should a company redivide its work? In my view, the FDE role doesn't need to be created. Not because it's unimportant, but because the person you're looking for is already inside your company.

Here are three points—some are predictions, some are already happening:

1. What an FDE Really Solves Is Only One Thing: Translation

There are many definitions of the FDE in the industry—understands the business, can write code, can handle being on-site, can make decisions on the spot. Traditional implementation consultants need all of these too; nothing new there. The only thing that truly distinguishes an FDE from traditional implementation is: eliminating translation.

How is a classic enterprise system built? The full process is: the boss has an idea → the business raises requirements → product managers/consultants design → UX/algorithms/engineering development/data development take over → testing, with project managers coordinating throughout, in continuous cycles.

Each layer is a translation. And with every translation, some of the most critical information gets lost.

The essence of the ideal FDE is compressing this entire chain into one such "superhuman."

So within a company, who is the person with the shortest requirements chain?

It's the business person themselves. They don't need to explain their requirements to anyone else—they ARE the requirement.

In the past, they couldn't do it because software itself is an extremely complex engineering endeavor that business people could never build on their own. In the future, they only need to explain one thing clearly—to AI.

The judgment behind this is: teaching business people to use AI to write programs is far easier than teaching IT people to understand the business and become business experts.

When I make this point, many IT folks are already drawing their swords.

2. So What Does IT Do?

This is the most frequently asked question, and the most easily misunderstood as "IT being sidelined." It's actually the opposite.

In the past, IT's power came from "only I can do this." Now the source of power has become "only I can decide how this is done"—which toolchains to use, how data gets connected, what permissions are granted, what kind of output can go to production.

That's far more power than "I'll develop it for you." Because what it governs isn't the scheduling of a few requirements—it's the boundaries for everyone.

And it guards against exactly what CIOs fear most: business departments going around them to do their own thing, each building their own silos, and IT being left to clean up the mess.

I know quite a few companies that provide workbuddy and Claude Code to all employees. It often happens that after a business person builds a small tool themselves, they come to IT asking for help deploying it.

Once, during a visit to a star hardware company for a discussion, when I was presenting our promotion simulation tool, the other side's business lead turned to a colleague and whispered—"Have Claude build me one tonight" (I wasn't surprised, and I think it's quite reasonable—this is the trend, and we need to embrace it).

This will only happen more and more, and IT can't stop it.

The business side will say: "I've already built this feature myself. You didn't have time to develop it for me, and now you won't even help me deploy it?"

So the question was never "whether to allow it"—it's how to regulate it: what the process should be, which tech stack to use, where the boundaries of security and permissions lie, what can go to production.

The real choice isn't "whether to let the business do it themselves"—they're already doing it. It's whether to give them a path that stays within governance.

IT needs to transform from the "translator" between requirements and programs into the provider of AI tools, the rule-maker, and the governor. This transformation is essentially what many people call the AI organizational upgrade within enterprises. In this process, IT certainly needs people who are both proficient in AI and understand some of the business—this role could be the CIO themselves, or an architect: they don't produce business systems; they produce rules and tools. Or, for a transitional period, bringing in outside help is perfectly fine too.

3. Mechanisms Aren't Designed—They Grow

IT needs to build this mechanism, but where do the standards come from? What counts as compliant, what can go to production, which data can be given to whom—these judgments themselves require business knowledge.

If you just make it up off the top of your head, you'll most likely set the rules too strict. Then the business side finds they can't do anything, goes around you again to do their own thing, and all that effort was wasted.

So the more accurate way to put it is: the mechanism has to start running first, then get revised version by version.

The first version will definitely be rough, maybe even wrong.

I think this point matters more than the mechanism itself, because what it tests isn't technology—it's the organization's tolerance. Many companies get stuck right here: the first version looks ugly, someone immediately jumps out and says "See? I told you this wouldn't work," and then nothing ever comes of it. This kind of thing isn't designed in a conference room and then pushed down. You have to allow it to grow a little slower and a little uglier.

Additionally, I believe that in the future, every company will develop a set of tools within this mechanism:

I call this kind of thing an in-house harness—the generic harness is the skeleton, and the rules each company puts into it are their own. This ensures that within a client enterprise, business knowledge is continuously accumulated over time, iterated in cycles, and efficiently retrieved, so that AI can support the business faster.

To sum up:

1. Business people are already the FDE—no need to create a separate role.

2. IT stops translating and does governance and tools instead.

3. The mechanism has to start running before it gets refined—it's fine if the first version is a bit ugly.

In practice, there are far more details than what's covered above—what the framework should constrain and what it shouldn't, how to coach business people, how to keep knowledge and tools continuously iterating. We're exploring these ourselves together with several companies we work with deeply.
