Defense FDE Role Requirements: Customer Acceptance and Security Clearances

Original title: 国防 FDE 岗位要求：客户验收与安全许可

FDE Times · 2026-11-10 · 4 min read

Summary

This article examines FDE job postings from Red Cell Partners and Vannevar Labs in the US defense sector. Both define an engineer's job as ending not at production deployment but at formal customer acceptance, meaning the client signs off on the work. The entry barrier is equally notable: postings require US citizenship and an already-held active clearance — Secret for the Red Cell version reviewed, TS with SCI eligibility for Vannevar. Citing ClearanceJobs, the author notes that even the fastest 90% of Top Secret investigations averaged 243 days in Q2 FY2024, so companies shift waiting costs to candidates. The piece advises engineers to agree on written, checkable acceptance criteria on day one and to frame resumes around ownership through customer sign-off.

Most Forward Deployed Engineer (FDE) job postings tell you what you will build. Red Cell Partners' FDE posting for its federal and national security business goes a step further: engineers are responsible for taking the product from prototype to production, through deployment, until "customer acceptance"—the stage where the customer formally signs off.

The role sits within a new national security incubation program inside Red Cell, currently in stealth mode. The company describes itself as a "company builder" that creates, operates, and scales future "mission-critical" businesses.

As a result, the person hired is more like a founding engineer at an early-stage startup than a contractor taking over a project that has been running for years.

Whether or not you plan to apply, this posting is worth a close read for two reasons. First, it redefines what "done" means for an FDE. Second, it shows that the ticket into defense is measured not just in technical skill, but in waiting days and citizenship.

"Done" means the customer signs, not that the code runs

Many engineering roles stop at production: the system goes live, dashboards are all green, tickets are closed. Deploying into a customer environment goes one step further. Acceptance is different in nature, because the person declaring the work "done" is no longer you—it's the customer.

Suppose you are building a report aggregation tool for a military unit. The prototype impresses in demos, and it runs smoothly in production with sample data.

After deployment to the customer's network, the problems appear: a legacy file format nobody mentioned, an approval step stuck in the middle of the data pipeline, and an end user who notices the summary is missing exactly the field he needs most.

If the engineer's responsibility ends at production, he hands these three problems off to someone else as "new requirements." If responsibility extends to acceptance, there is nowhere to hand them off: each one is an unmet criterion, and without the customer's signature, the project is not finished.

So the first thing to do on-site is not to draw architecture diagrams. Sit down with the person who will ultimately sign, write down in their own words the conditions that must be met before signing, and pair each criterion with a check both sides can execute. That list is the real technical contract.

Vannevar shows this is a template, not an outlier

Vannevar Labs' FDE posting describes an almost identical pattern: engineers design, build, and own software deployed directly into customer environments, while serving as the primary technical point of contact. The main difference between the two postings is the entry barrier.

The biggest commonality between the two is not technical. Both want engineers close to real users: one prefers to hire people living near military hubs, the other relies on intensive travel. Acceptance is hard to push through by email alone.

The word "valid" shifts the waiting onto the candidate

It is easy to assume all defense-related FDE roles require TS/SCI. The version of the Red Cell posting reviewed lists only Secret level, though some versions on other job aggregators reportedly list higher levels, so requirements vary by version. Vannevar is explicit: you must hold a valid TS with SCI eligibility.

The most noteworthy word in both postings is "valid." The companies do not write "able to obtain a security clearance"—they require that you already hold one. The reason is time: ClearanceJobs reports that in Q2 of fiscal year 2024, even the fastest 90% of Top Secret investigations still took an average of 243 days, with the remaining 10% taking longer.

That figure applies directly to roles like Vannevar's; Red Cell's Secret-level processing times differ. But the logic is the same. A startup that needs to put people in front of customers early and push prototypes through to acceptance will not pay for the days its engineer cannot yet be on-site.

For those without a security clearance, the conclusion is blunt: no matter how good your technical skills, roles requiring TS remain closed to you for months. If you already hold a clearance and have personally driven a project through to acceptance, you are exactly the combination both postings are looking for.

What if you are not a US citizen?

Many readers will stop here, and that is fine.

What is worth taking away is the scope of responsibility: any customer who needs to sign off in front of their own leadership or regulators needs exactly the kind of engineer Red Cell and Vannevar describe.

This changes how you write your resume. "Deployed an analytics system for a customer" tells a recruiter almost nothing. "Owned an analytics system end to end from prototype to customer sign-off, against five criteria agreed in week one" shows you understand what "done" means from the customer's perspective.

When reading job descriptions, search for "acceptance" and "own delivery" to see where the responsibility ends. Roles that stop at production hire builders; roles that extend to acceptance hire people accountable for outcomes. For the latter, prepare a story about a project that nearly failed acceptance and how you resolved it.

Security clearance depends on citizenship, and on a process you cannot control. The habit of writing acceptance criteria on day one is the opposite: no matter which customer you face or which country you are in, you can start practicing it on your next project.
