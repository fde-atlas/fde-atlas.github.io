How FDEs Say No to Customer Requests: A Six-Step Communication Method

Original title: FDE 如何拒绝客户需求：六步沟通法

FDE Times · 2026-10-09 · 6 min read

Summary

This guide explains how forward deployed engineers (FDEs) can reject flawed client requests without damaging relationships. Using a scenario where a client wants an AI agent to write directly into an ERP system without approvals, it argues that saying yes to everything causes scope creep and missed deadlines. The proposed six-step method: buy time with a concrete follow-up, ask about desired outcomes rather than features, state constraints plainly in terms of client risk, present options with tradeoffs (including the client's original idea), agree conditionally with schedule impacts, and log deferred requests in a RAID log. It also covers common mistakes and how to demonstrate this skill in FDE interviews.

Key points

  • Saying yes to every request causes scope creep and delays.
  • A good refusal includes constraints, reasons, and alternatives.
  • Distinguish what clients ask for from what they actually need.
  • Six steps: buy time, ask outcomes, state constraints, offer options, conditional yes, document.
  • Repeated client requests across deployments signal product improvements to report.

Key Takeaways

  • Saying "yes" to every request leads to scope creep and delays the delivery commitments you originally made.
  • A good "no" has three parts: clearly stated constraints, the reasoning behind them, and alternative options for the client to choose from.
  • Deferred requests should be logged in the RAID log; when multiple clients ask for the same thing, that's a signal to feed back to the product team.

Imagine a pilot project in its third week. The client's operations lead pulls you into a meeting room: "Have the AI agent write directly to the ERP and remove the approval step. I'm demoing to the director next week." You know this decision is wrong, and you also know the person across the table decides whether the project gets renewed.

Many engineers' first instinct is to nod and keep the peace; their second is to win the technical argument at all costs. Both approaches cause problems, just in different ways.

FDE Academy believes that politely declining, or negotiating a compromise where both sides give a little, is an unglamorous skill that has an outsized impact on Forward Deployed Engineers (FDEs).

The reason is practical. In their view, the habit of saying "yes" to every request directly leads to scope creep and delays the delivery commitments you originally made. Clients can accept a well-reasoned refusal, but it's hard for them to get over a missed deadline.

Saying "No" Is Part of Your Job

Software consulting firm Atomic Object writes that their job is to lead clients onto a more viable, more productive path, not to echo every idea the client has.

The company views saying "no" as a way of protecting the client's budget. It also promises: whenever technical constraints make the original request impossible, they will say so plainly.

That said, plain doesn't mean blunt. Kantata, which builds software for professional services firms, points out that nobody wants to hear a "no" and then find the problem untouched. A bare "no" leaves the client too much room for imagination, and people tend to assume the worst: "This person is lazy," or "This team can't do it."

So a good "no" always has three parts: clearly stated constraints, an explanation, and another path for the client to choose. The hardest step comes before all three: you have to first know what the client actually needs.

When the client says "remove the approval step," what do they actually need?

An analysis on techinterview.org points out that the FDE interview process tests exactly this ability: distinguishing what the client truly needs from what they ask for.

In the example above, "remove the approval step" is a solution the client came up with themselves. The real need might be "the director needs to see that the AI agent genuinely saves time," or it might be "the operations team complains about clicking through too many approvals."

These two needs lead to two different designs, and neither requires letting the AI agent write directly to the production system without any checks. Once you find the real need, you no longer have to choose between compliance and refusal; you simply propose another way to achieve the same goal.

A Six-Step Script, Broken Down Line by Line

Step 1: Buy time. FDE Academy describes how senior FDEs handle sudden requests: acknowledge the details first and reply later, rather than committing on the spot. In the meeting you can say: "Let me check on the ERP side, and I'll send you a proposal by 4 p.m. today." A concrete time keeps the client from feeling you're stalling.

Step 2: Ask about the desired outcome, not the feature. Don't ask "Are you sure you want to remove the approval step?"—that sounds like arguing. Ask instead: "At next week's demo, what does the director need to see for you to consider it a success?" Suppose the answer is: "An order goes from email into the system in a few seconds, with no manual entry anywhere."

Step 3: State the constraints plainly. "If the AI agent writes directly to the ERP, a single extraction error becomes a wrong order in the production system, and your team will have to clean it up." This sentence is about the client's risk, not about you not wanting to do the work. Atomic Object's advice is similar: don't dismiss the client's idea; instead, discuss together why it might not meet user needs, then look for workarounds.

Step 4: Offer options. Kantata believes that providing options helps both sides find common ground, and the client feels the choice remains in their hands. A short table attached to that afternoon's email might look like this (example for illustration only):

OptionWhat the client getsThe cost
A. Direct write to ERP, no approvalsFastest demo effectWrong data enters the production system and is hard to roll back
B. AI agent writes to a queue, one-click approvalDemo still shows the speedOne extra click for the approver
C. AI agent fully automates reliable order types, the rest go through approvalSome genuine automationExtra time needed for accuracy evals, schedule pushed back

Keep option A in the table. Placed alongside its cost, the client will usually rule it out themselves and feel it was their own decision.

Step 5: Agree conditionally. If the client picks C, FDE Academy suggests responding: "Yes, and here's how that affects the current timeline." Be clear about which items must be pushed back; don't quietly work overtime night after night to prop up the original schedule.

Step 6: Write it down. Kantata recommends logging deferred requests in the RAID log (Risks, Actions, Issues, Decisions) for easy reference later. A line like "Auto-write to ERP without approvals: deferred, revisit after accuracy evals for order type X are complete" turns the "no" into a "not yet." The client also knows their request hasn't been forgotten.

Ways of Saying No That Damage Client Relationships

The most common mistake is committing on the spot in the meeting, because silence makes you feel incompetent. The cost of "Let me confirm and get back to you" is always lower than a promise you later have to retract.

The second mistake is an outright refusal: "This can't be done." The client loses their options, and you lose your credibility. The opposite mistake is just as harmful: avoiding the constraints to dodge friction, leaving the client thinking it's still possible and proceeding with plans as usual.

The third mistake is treating every refusal as an internal matter of a single project. PostHog's FDE handbook describes how to distill patterns that recur across multiple deployments into reusable tools, shared skills, and product improvements.

If three clients in a row ask to remove the approval step, the problem may be that the approval step itself is too cumbersome, and the product team needs to know.

How to Show This Skill to Employers

For developers looking to move into an FDE role, this skill is hard to prove with a resume line like "strong communication skills." Better to prepare a real story: what the original request was, what real need you uncovered, what options you proposed, and how it turned out.

On a resume, a line like "Proposed an alternative to request X, preserving the original delivery date" carries far more weight than vague soft-skill adjectives.

In interviews, don't talk about how you out-argued the client; talk about the moment you understood the client's real need, because that's exactly what the FDE interview is designed to test.

Exercise: Rewrite This Conversation

Here's a hypothetical conversation. Client: "We'd like the chatbot to answer customers without citing source documents, so it looks cleaner." FDE: "No problem, I'll do it this week."

Rewrite the FDE's response using the six steps above: a buy-time sentence with a concrete time, a question about what outcome the client expects to see, a sentence explaining the risk the client would bear, a table listing three options and their costs, a conditional agreement, and a RAID log entry.

If your rewrite contains no "no" at all, yet the client still understands why the original plan was put on hold, then you've done it right.

Atomic Object says a consultant's job is to lead the client onto a more viable path. An FDE who says "yes" to every request is exactly giving up that part of the work.
