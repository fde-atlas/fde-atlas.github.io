TENT (2026) Explained for FDEs

FDE Interviews · 2026-10-18 · 4 min read

Summary

This breakdown explains the TENT paper on data transfer for disaggregated and multi-turn agent serving, where large KV cache and model weight transfers share networks with small latency-critical traffic. Existing engines bind transfers to fixed transports and stripe evenly, so one slow or failed rail stalls everything; one cluster saw 382 hardware failures a month. The paper in one minute

In disaggregated serving (prefill and decode on different machines) and multi-turn agent serving, the KV cache moves between GPUs and machines all the time, and so do model weights during online RL. These are large "elephant" transfers on the critical path to the first token. They share the network with small, latency-critical "mice" such as MoE expert traffic.

The authors ran Mooncake Transfer Engine on thousands of GPUs and found three recurring problems with the usual design, where the application binds a transfer to a transport and set of NICs at setup and the engine stripes it evenly. Silos: NVLink and RDMA descriptors don't mix, so paths like "remote DRAM to local GPU over NVLink" fall back to TCP. Stranding: even striping ignores a slow, congested or cross-NUMA rail, and the whole transfer waits for it. Fragility: one cluster logged 382 hardware failures a month, and each link fault surfaced as an application error needing manual repair.

TENT inverts the design. The application declares an intent (source, destination, length, priority) and gets a handle. The engine then works in three phases. Orchestration picks transports per request from a unified view of memory segments and their reachable links, and can stage through host or GPU memory to stitch a multi-hop path. Slice spraying cuts large transfers into slices of at least 64 KB, each carrying its absolute destination offset, and sends each slice to the rail with the lowest predicted finish time, β0 + β1·(queued bytes + L)/bandwidth, using live telemetry. Resilience marks rails healthy, degraded or paused, retries failed slices on other rails (safe because writes go to fixed offsets), and can switch transport entirely.

In SGLang's hierarchical KV cache, TENT gave 1.36x the input throughput of Mooncake TE and lower time to first token; RL weight updates ran 20-26% faster; a NIC failure caused a sub-50 ms dip with no errors. It is open source in the Mooncake repository.

Research notes: the problem, approach and results

throughput dip when a NIC fails mid-transfer; the NIC rejoined 26 ms after recovery, with no application-visible error

hardware failure events per month at one fintech production cluster, 72% of them GPU-related

Disaggregated and agentic serving moves KV caches and model weights between machines constantly, over a mix of NVLink, multi-rail RDMA and PCIe paths. Existing transfer engines bind each transfer to a backend and NIC set up front and split it evenly, so one slow, congested or failed link stalls the whole transfer, and a link fault surfaces as an application error.

Make transfers declarative. Applications state source, destination, length and priority; the engine picks transports per request, can stitch multi-hop paths through staging memory, and cuts large transfers into 64 KB+ slices with absolute offsets. Each slice goes to the rail with the lowest predicted finish time from live queue and bandwidth telemetry; failing rails are excluded and their slices retried elsewhere.

In SGLang HiCache serving Qwen3-235B over 10-turn conversations, TENT raised input throughput 1.36x and cut P90 time to first token from 0.90 to 0.67 s against Mooncake Transfer Engine. RL weight updates were 20-26% faster, and a failed NIC cost under 50 ms of reduced throughput with no application errors.

Why it matters to an FDE

Every serving optimization that moves KV between machines (prefix caching across nodes, prefill/decode disaggregation, multi-turn session caches) assumes the transfer is fast and reliable. In production clusters it often is neither: links degrade, NICs on the far NUMA node are slower, and something fails every couple of hours.

Tail latency comes from the slowest link. If a transfer is striped evenly, one degraded rail sets its finish time. When a customer's P90 time to first token jumps at peak hours while averages look fine, look at the transfer layer.

Failures should be routing events, not incidents. At 382 failures a month, a transfer layer that throws errors to the application means pages and restarts; one that reroutes means a brief slowdown. That difference shows up in on-call load and SLO attainment.

Cache hit rate is not the whole story. A cache that hits but transfers slowly can lose to recomputing. TENT's round-1 numbers show caching costing a little time when there is nothing to reuse; the gains come in later turns.
