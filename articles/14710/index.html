Government FDE Jobs: Pay, Security Clearance and Travel

FDE Times · 2026-10-09 · 6 min read

Summary

Drawing on Valletta Software's 2026 salary guide and job postings from Palantir, Anduril and OpenAI, this article argues that government and defence FDE roles pay less than commercial equivalents, contradicting the belief that harder work commands higher pay. OpenAI's government FDE band starts $39,200 below its commercial band, while Anduril's Air Defense role pays $113k–$155k despite requiring travel up to 80% of the year. The author identifies three hidden costs: clearance wait times (a first-time Top Secret takes about 243 days), heavy travel, and ethical concerns, noting internal Palantir debate over its ICE work. Practical advice: clarify clearance status, travel share and client assignments before signing.

Key points

  • OpenAI's government FDE band is $39,200 lower than its commercial band.
  • A first-time Top Secret clearance takes about 243 days to process.
  • Anduril's FDE role requires travel up to 80% of the year.
  • Palantir employees have questioned the company's ICE relationship.
  • Ask about clearance level, travel share and client choice before accepting an offer.

In brief

  • According to a 2026 salary guide, OpenAI's government FDE band is $145,800–$280,000, below the $185K–$300K band for the commercial role.
  • A first-time Top Secret clearance for a private-sector worker currently takes about 243 days, and an Anduril role requires travel up to 80% of the year.
  • Palantir employees have questioned the company's relationship with ICE, so ask plainly about clients before you sign.

According to a 2026 salary guide from Valletta Software, OpenAI pays forward deployed engineers who serve government a band of $145,800–$280,000. Those serving commercial customers, under the same job title, get $185K–$300K. At the bottom of the range, the engineer working for the state earns $39,200 less.

That runs against a common belief among engineers: work for government and defence is harder, so it must pay more. The “harder” part is true. The job postings and public data do not support the “pays more” part.

If you work as an FDE or are thinking of moving into the role, this is a career decision worth working through carefully. What you give up in this sector is not money. You give up waiting time, days at home and possibly your ethical comfort with the clients you serve.

Higher pay is a myth

The Valletta Software guide, published by a software company, is blunt. In its view, government work usually comes with security clearance requirements, more time on site and less flexibility, and still pays less.

This is analysis from a vendor, so treat it as a secondary source. Even so, the original job postings point the same way.

Palantir’s posting for a Forward Deployed Software Engineer for the US government estimates a base salary band of $135,000–$200,000 a year, with travel “as needed”. Anduril’s FDE Air Defense role lists $113k–$155k for the US market. That is below many commercial FDE bands, yet its working conditions are the toughest in the group.

Read down the columns and two things stand out. The closer a role sits to defence, the heavier the requirements. And pay does not rise with them.

Security clearance: the word “willing” hides eight months

Many people assume that without a clearance there is no point applying. The reality is more forgiving. Palantir’s US government posting accepts both people who already hold a clearance and those who are eligible and willing to obtain one, so a clearance is not always a day-one requirement.

But “willing” has a price. According to DCSA data cited by ClearanceJobs, a first-time Top Secret clearance for a private-sector worker currently takes 243 days end to end, roughly eight months.

Imagine you accept an offer for Palantir’s Federal Health and Civilian role, which targets Top Secret directly, and you have never held a clearance. If your case follows the current timeline, your clearance may not come through for almost the first three quarters.

The postings do not say what you will do in that time. For an engineer with two to eight years of experience, eight months is a significant stretch on a CV, so do not leave it as an unknown.

The question to ask at interview, then, is this: while waiting for the clearance, what project will a new engineer work on, with whom, and how will the results be assessed? Remember too that these postings require a US security clearance. If you are not eligible to apply for one, these roles are closed to you, however good your skills.

80% travel: count the days

Every FDE travels. Anduril’s posting is in a different category. Candidates must be able to travel to remote parts of the world, for up to two months at a time, for up to 80% of the year in total.

On a calendar, 80% of 365 days is about 292 days away from home. That leaves roughly 73 days for everything else. With a band of $113k–$155k, this is not a case of high pay compensating for hardship: you earn less than many commercial roles in order to spend most of the year in the field.

Some will find that appealing. Deploying air defence systems in remote locations is rare experience that is hard to gain in an office. But weigh it against your family situation and your own health, and do not take the job on the assumption that the money will make up for it.

Ethical constraints: ask before you sign

The two costs above can be counted. The third is harder to measure but may weigh more. As an FDE, you sit right next to the client, and the code you write runs on their data. In government work, the client may be an agency whose methods you disagree with.

LeadDev describes Palantir as one of the most important technology partners of ICE, the US immigration enforcement agency. LeadDev’s January 2026 article also reported growing internal frustration, with some employees questioning whether Palantir should continue its relationship with ICE.

People inside the company are debating this question themselves. For FDEs the risk is closer to home: an engineer at headquarters can stay distant from the product, whereas an FDE is in the field and sees first-hand how the system is used.

The practical advice is to set your boundaries before you sign. Ask the employer directly: which agencies does the company serve, can engineers decline an account, and has anyone done so before? If the answers are evasive, factor that into your assessment of the offer.

So is it worth it?

The answer depends on what you get in return for what you give up. If you want money, the available data suggest the commercial sector pays better: OpenAI’s salary bands as cited by Valletta, and Anduril’s pay compared with many commercial FDE bands, all point the same way.

If what you want is the kind of work these postings describe, such as deploying air defence systems in remote areas or working within a Top Secret clearance framework, that is a legitimate reason to accept the costs above.

For anyone targeting this role, every posting has three lines worth reading closely. The clearance level, and whether it says “currently held” or “eligible to obtain”. The share of time travelling and the length of each trip. And the salary band, compared with the same company’s commercial role.

On your CV, if you have worked on site under restricted conditions, followed strict security procedures or spent long stretches in the field, state the number of weeks and the context. That is the evidence employers in this sector need to see.

Government and defence do not pay extra for your sacrifices. If you still choose this path, choose it for the mission, and know exactly whom you are serving.
