Defend your take-home design · FDE hiring manager question

FDE Interview Prep · 2026 · 3 min read

Summary

This piece explains how candidates should defend a take-home design when interviewing for FDE roles at companies like Sierra and OpenAI, where interviewers probe product flows and architecture choices with repeated 'why' questions. The advice targets an architect's perspective: what the team maintains after you leave, standards, and 3 a.m. failures. It prescribes a six-step sequence—state both options, concede where the alternative wins, cite one deciding constraint backed by a number, show how you contained the accepted cost, describe the seam where a switch would happen, then invite pushback. Candidates should distinguish new constraints (adapt) from mere pressure (hold firm). The main trap is defending by accumulation, which sounds like a sales pitch.

Key points

  • Sierra's interview demos your build, then debates your key choices.
  • Frame defenses around maintainability, standards, and 3 a.m. breakage.
  • Concede where the alternative wins; cite one numeric deciding constraint.
  • Adapt to new constraints; don't fold under pure pressure.
  • Accumulating only your merits reads as a pitch and invites hole-hunting.

The architect framing matters. An architect cares about what their team will run after you leave, what their standards are, and what breaks at three in the morning. Defend the choice in those terms, not in terms of what was elegant.

Make the moves in this order.

  • State both options fairly. “I chose A. The obvious alternative was B.”
  • Say where B wins, specifically. Name the case where B is better and the cost A carries because you didn’t pick it. This is the move that earns you the rest.
  • Name the one deciding constraint, from the brief or a measurement, not from taste. One constraint said with a number beats four general advantages.
  • Show how you contained the cost you accepted.
  • Say what would change the choice, and how cheap the switch is: the seam in the code where B would plug in.
  • Invite the push. “Where would you push on that?”

Tell pressure from new information. If the architect adds a constraint you didn’t have, such as a company standard, say how it changes the answer. If they only push harder (“I’d still have built the loop”), don’t concede. Restate the deciding constraint and ask what they would do about it.

The trap is defending by accumulation: every merit of your approach, none of the alternative’s. It sounds like a pitch, and a skeptical architect answers a pitch by looking for the hole in it.

Follow-ups

What the interviewer may ask next, once your first answer is on the table.

  • Our standard is the other approach. Every team here uses it. Why should this be the exception?
  • Your deciding constraint came from the brief. What if the real customer never had it?
  • Show me in the code where switching to the alternative would start. How much would change?

Where answers go wrong

  • Listing your approach’s merits without saying where the alternative wins, so the architect hears a sales pitch and starts looking for the hole.
  • Folding at the first push (“fair, the other way would have been better”). Giving up a sound choice under pressure reads worse than defending a flawed one with its trade-off named.
