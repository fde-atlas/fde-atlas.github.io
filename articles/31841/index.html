Can LLM agents be forward deployed engineers? New study

FDE Times · 2026-11-04 · 4 min read

Summary

A November 2026 arXiv study benchmarks LLM agents against a real FDE's four-month enterprise text-to-SQL deployment, covering requirement discovery and delivery. Agents were decent at uncovering hidden requirements but poor at delivering systems that pass acceptance, and an independent Baidu benchmark (FDE-Bench) reached a similar conclusion. The authors recommend humans review discovered requirements and supervise agent execution.

Key points

  • The best model found over 80% of hidden requirements; open-weight models only 40–50%.
  • Across eight coding-agent configurations, only 25% of runs met every acceptance criterion.
  • 40% of hidden requirements were missed or misstated, and misreadings propagated to delivery.
  • Baidu's FDE-Bench found a best average delivery score of just 0.5510 out of 1.0.
  • Authors advise humans to review requirement lists and monitor agent plans during execution.

In brief

  • The strongest model found more than 80% of hidden requirements; open-weight models found only 40–50%.
  • Across eight coding-agent configurations, only 25% of runs met every acceptance criterion; 40% of hidden requirements were missed or misstated.
  • Baidu's FDE-Bench found the same gap: the best average delivery score was just 0.5510 out of 1.0.

Sent to interview an enterprise client, LLM agents do better than many expect: the strongest model uncovered more than 80% of the requirements that had been deliberately hidden. Asked to deliver a working system, they fared far worse. Only 25% of runs passed every acceptance criterion.

Those are the two numbers at the heart of “Can LLM Agents Be Forward-Deployed Engineers?”, a study of requirements discovery and delivery for enterprise text-to-SQL systems, submitted to arXiv on November 2, 2026. Marianne Menglin Liu is the first author; her co-authors include Dan Roth and Sujith Ravi.

For anyone working as an FDE, or hoping to become one, the study makes a practical point: a long list of requirements does not mean the client conversation is finished. A requirement recorded with the wrong meaning will still break the handover.

The yardstick is four months of one engineer’s work

The authors did not build a toy problem. Their reference point is a real deployment: an FDE who spent four months bringing a text-to-SQL model that writes Oracle-dialect SQL into a large enterprise.

The agents had to retrace both stages that engineer went through: discovering requirements with the client, then delivering a system that passes acceptance.

In the first stage the team tested 15 models. The strongest cleared 80%, while open-weight models found only 40–50% of the hidden requirements. Looking only at the best result, it would be easy to conclude that customer discovery, the most “human” part of the job, is close to automated.

Why 80% does not become 80%

The delivery stage tells a different story. Across eight coding-agent configurations, only a quarter of runs met every criterion. According to the authors, the first bottleneck is incomplete discovery: 40% of hidden requirements were missed or misstated.

The two figures do not contradict each other. The 80%-plus mark belongs to the best model, while open-weight models stopped at 40–50%. More important, the word “misstated” shows that a requirement can count as found and still not match what the client meant.

Take a hypothetical example. The client says “quarterly revenue”, but at their company revenue must exclude returns and the fiscal quarter starts in April. The agent records “quarterly revenue”, yet the SQL it writes is wrong on both counts, and that misreading travels all the way to the acceptance test.

A separate benchmark reaches a similar result

The finding does not stand alone. FDE-Bench, published separately by a Baidu AI Cloud team with no shared authors, comprises 49 cases drawn from real client projects, in which agents must draw out requirements nobody stated at the start.

Across 18 agent configurations, the best average delivery score reached only 0.5510 on a 1.0 scale. The Baidu team concluded that the agents tested cannot yet reliably deliver end to end at FDE level.

Two independent studies, using two different datasets, both found agents falling short at delivery. That makes it hard to dismiss the 25% figure as a quirk of one experiment.

Humans still need to hold on to two jobs

Liu and her co-authors are specific in their advice: humans should review the list of requirements the agent has found, and monitor the agent’s plan while it executes. On this view, the FDE moves into a supervisory role rather than being replaced, at least for now.

The skill worth practicing now is tracing a requirement from the client meeting all the way to the acceptance test, and writing down the business definition behind every term the client uses. That is precisely where the authors say human review belongs.

When reading job descriptions, watch for phrases such as “requirements gathering” or “acceptance criteria” appearing alongside “LLM agents”. On a CV, a project that states how many requirements were discovered, how many acceptance criteria there were and how many were met will persuade more than any adjective.

Agents have learned to ask questions. Checking whether the answers were understood correctly is still the engineer’s job.
