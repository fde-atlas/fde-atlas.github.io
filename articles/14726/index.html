FDE discovery: how to find the client's real problem

FDE Times · 2026-10-08 · 5 min read

Summary

The article argues that a client's stated request is only a hypothesis, illustrated by a case where a request to automate PR review masked the real bottleneck: only two staff engineers could approve changes, and both were overloaded. Drawing on Perspective AI's five-phase discovery playbook (Scope, Interview, Synthesize, Validate, Spec), Learn Cursor's FDE interview track, and techinterview.org's analysis of Palantir's decomposition round, it recommends event-based questions, a one-page map of people and systems, and a "handoff test" for specs. Strong candidates spend their first ten minutes asking questions rather than proposing solutions.

Key points

  • Treat every stated request as a hypothesis to test, not a spec.
  • The PR review bottleneck was two overloaded approvers, not missing automation.
  • Discovery has five phases, each with a clear completion condition.
  • Ask clients to walk through their last shipped change and find the longest wait.
  • Palantir's decomposition interview scores exactly this questioning skill.

Treat every stated request as a hypothesis to test; a few well-placed discovery questions can save three weeks spent building the wrong thing.

In brief

  • A stated request is only a hypothesis: a request to automate PR review can hide the fact that only two people are allowed to approve.
  • Discovery has five phases; Scope is done when people and systems fit on a one-page map, Spec is done when a teammate can build without another meeting.
  • The decomposition interview round scores exactly this skill: strong candidates spend the first ten minutes asking questions.

Picture a client sending you a single line: “The team needs a tool that automatically reviews pull requests.” The solution appears in your head at once: an agent that reads the diff, leaves comments and assigns a risk score. Three weeks later you demo it, the client says it looks great, and the time from opening a PR to merging it is exactly what it was before.

Learn Cursor’s FDE interview track opens with precisely this scenario. The client’s engineer hands you a problem statement, and, according to the track, it is almost never the real problem.

In that example the bottleneck is not a lack of automation. Review takes four days because only two staff engineers are allowed to approve, and both are buried in work.

For a developer who wants to move into FDE work, this is the skill that separates an FDE from someone who just takes tickets. You already know how to write code. What you need to learn is how to turn a vague request into a plan that both the client and your teammates believe is pointed in the right direction.

A request is only a hypothesis

The FDE discovery playbook that Perspective AI published in July 2026 starts from an assumption that sounds a little blunt: what the client says they need is not what they actually need. Not because clients lie. They describe the solution they can imagine, in terms of the tools they already know, rather than describing where the work is stuck.

So “we need automated PR review” should be read as “the client believes automated review will make everything faster”. That is a hypothesis, and your first job is to test it. If it holds, you build with more confidence; if it fails, you have just saved three weeks.

Which question turns the problem around?

The common mistake is to ask the client “what features do you want?”. That only gets them to repeat the original request. The question Learn Cursor proposes goes the other way: ask the client to walk you through the most recent change they shipped, and point to where it waited longest.

The question works because it pulls the conversation back to something that actually happened, with dates and real people. Here is a hypothetical exchange to show how it plays out:

FDE: Could you walk me through the last change your team shipped? Between opening the PR and reaching production, where did it sit waiting longest?

Client: Probably at review. Once a PR is opened it takes about four days for someone to approve it.

FDE: Who does the approving during those four days? What are they waiting on?

Client: Only two staff engineers can approve. They also handle on-call and system design, so they always have a dozen PRs piled up.

At this point the problem has changed. An agent that comments automatically still leaves the PR sitting in the queue of those same two people. A more sensible plan might be to help those two review faster, or to make clear which kinds of change need them and which do not, but you should only choose after validating it with the client.

Five phases, each with a sign that it is done

One good question is not yet a method. Perspective AI’s playbook divides discovery into five consecutive phases: Scope, Interview, Synthesize, Validate and Spec. Each has a condition that tells you when you can move on to the next.

Scope is done when you can draw a map of the people and systems involved on a single sheet of paper. In the PR example, that sheet holds the people writing code, the two approvers, the CI system and the repo. The phase ends by naming the decision point: whom the system will help take which action. If you cannot say that sentence, you are not ready to interview.

Interview is where you use event-based questions like the one above, with the people who actually do the work every day, not only the person who sent the request. Synthesize means grouping what you heard into opportunities and ranking them. A bottleneck that many people mention is usually more worth attention than a complaint raised by one.

Validate is when you go back to the client’s domain experts to confirm that the opportunity at the top of the list really is the most valuable thing to build. With a ranking in hand you will be strongly tempted to skip this step, which is exactly why it should stay. Finally, Spec is judged by the “handoff test”: a teammate holding the spec must be able to build it without any further meeting.

The mistakes that make discovery fail

The first and most common mistake is jumping straight to a solution. techinterview.org’s analysis of FDE interviews describes exactly this: candidates hear the problem and rush straight into a solution. In real work, this mistake becomes a polished demo nobody uses.

The second is talking only to the person who made the request. The engineer who filed the ticket sees the symptom; the two approvers are the ones who know why the queue is long. If your one-page map has only one name on it, you are almost certainly missing people.

The third is writing a spec that needs an explanatory meeting attached. When a teammate needs a meeting to understand the spec, the decisions are still in your head. Go back and check whether you have written down the decision point and why you chose this opportunity.

Interviews score exactly this skill

This is not only a skill for after you are hired. Palantir’s decomposition round, according to techinterview.org, assesses the ability to break an ambiguous real-world challenge into smaller parts and then propose a solution. Strong candidates spend the first ten minutes asking questions.

So when you practise for interviews, train yourself to hold back the reflex to sketch an architecture straight away. Ask who uses it, where the work gets stuck, which decision is waiting, then say the map of people and systems out loud before proposing anything.

On your CV, instead of writing “built tool X”, write one line about a time you discovered the original request was wrong and how you changed course. A line like that shows precisely the skill the decomposition round scores.

Next time you receive a one-line request, don’t open your editor yet; open a blank sheet of paper. If you cannot yet draw on it who is waiting on whom, you still don’t know what you need to build.
