From Customer Business Context to Production: A Seven-Step Method for FDE Delivery

Original title: 客户业务上下文到生产：FDE 交付的七步方法

FDEr.ai · 2026-08-02 · 2 min read

Summary

FDEr.ai outlines a seven-step delivery method for moving enterprise AI projects from impressive demos to production systems. Each step requires documented evidence: defining business outcomes with an owner, mapping real workflows, inventorying data and systems, freezing agent actions into control tiers, building production-shaped slices, staged rollout with pre-agreed go/pivot/stop criteria, and operations with handover. The article lists three signals that should stop a project and argues that production means real users, metrics, exception handling, and rollback paths—not merely deployment.

Key points

  • Seven steps span business outcome definition through operations and handover.
  • Every step must produce evidence; unmet premises mean scope reduction or stopping.
  • Agent actions are classified as read-only, reversible, approval-required, or forbidden.
  • Stop signals: no owner, hard data blockers, or unauditable critical actions.
  • Production requires real users, metrics, exception handling, and rollback—not just deployment.

What problem does this methodology solve

Enterprise AI projects often start with an impressive Demo, but expose data, permissions, accountability, and adoption issues when connected to real workflows. This Playbook uses seven steps to translate "what the customer says they want" into "what a production system can safely do." Leave evidence at every step; if a key premise doesn't hold, narrow the scope, go back a step, or stop.

The Seven-Step Delivery Path

Step 1: Define Business Outcomes and Owners

Clearly document the business outcome to improve, the current baseline, target users, the business Owner, and the observation window. Don't treat "building an Agent" as an outcome, and don't enter development without an Owner.

Deliverables: a one-page problem definition, baseline metrics, the Owner, and out-of-scope items.

Step 2: Map the Real Workflow

Follow users through a complete task, recording trigger conditions, inputs, decisions, system actions, human handoffs, and exception branches. Break down the user's "automatic handling" into verifiable steps.

Deliverables: a flow diagram, a role list, exception branches, and existing system boundaries.

Step 3: Inventory Data and Systems

Confirm data sources, update frequency, field meanings, permissions, quality, and deployment locations, then determine which data the Agent needs to read and which tools to call. When data is inaccessible or ownership is unclear, don't use mock data to mask blockers.

Deliverables: a data and system inventory, access ownership, data risks, and a minimal viable scope.

Step 4: Freeze Actions and Control Boundaries

Write the Agent's capabilities as verbs, and label each action as read-only, reversible, requiring approval, or forbidden. Actions involving external communication, funds, permissions, and irreversible changes must first have human confirmation, auditing, and rollback designed.

Deliverables: an action matrix, a least-privilege plan, approval paths, and a rollback plan.

Step 5: Build a Production-Shaped Minimal Slice

Don't just build a pretty chat window. Pick a real workflow with clear boundaries, connect a real data subset, permissions, logging, error handling, and human fallback, so the technical validation stays as close as possible to future production constraints.

Deliverables: a runnable slice, call logs, known limitations, and deployment notes.

Step 6: Roll Out Gradually with Evidence

First run with a small group of low-risk users within a clearly defined observation window. Track task quality, system stability, human takeovers, user adoption, and business metrics separately; Go, Pivot, and Stop conditions must be frozen before observation begins.

Deliverables: an evals report, user feedback, an issue list, and the rollout decision.

Step 7: Operate, Hand Off, and Feed Back

Launch is not the end of delivery. Clarify who handles exceptions, who updates rules, who reviews metrics, when to roll back, and when the FDE exits. Recurring issues should enter a fix queue and product feedback loop, not remain in personal chat logs.

Deliverables: an operations responsibility table, handoff records, retrospective conclusions, and candidates for reusable assets.

Three Signals That Require Stopping

  • No business Owner, or an Owner unwilling to take responsibility for outcomes and risks.
  • Hard blockers in data, permissions, or compliance, while trying to declare success based on Demo results.
  • The Agent's critical actions cannot be audited, stopped, or recovered.

How to Tell You've Truly Reached Production

Production is not synonymous with deploying to servers. At minimum, there must be real users, real workflows, clear business metrics, a continuous operations Owner, exception handling, and a rollback path. If the project only proved technical feasibility, it should be honestly labeled a POC; if it was only observed in a small scope, it should be labeled a Pilot.

After completing the seven steps, the team should be able to answer four questions: What business improvement was achieved? What states did the Agent change? Who takes over when errors occur? What can be reused in the next delivery? When these can't be answered, continuing to expand scope usually amplifies risk rather than accelerating delivery.
