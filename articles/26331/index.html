FDE Skill Requirements: What Should a Forward Deployed Engineer Learn First

Original title: FDE 技能要求：前线部署工程师先学什么

FDE Times · 2026-10-19 · 5 min read

Summary

Drawing on Bloomberry's analysis of 1,000 FDE job postings, this article ranks which skills to learn first. Python appears in 66% of postings, nearly double the next skill, while TypeScript, AI agents, AWS, and LLM experience each cluster around one third. The author warns against adding percentages together, recommends mastering one cloud platform (AWS by default), and uses OpenAI and Palantir postings to show that shipping real LLM or agent systems and client-facing communication separate strong candidates. Paraform data shows FDE postings grew 350% year over year.

Key points

  • Python is required in 66% of 1,000 analyzed FDE postings, nearly twice TypeScript's 35%.
  • TypeScript, AI agents, AWS, and LLM experience each appear in roughly one third of postings.
  • Skill percentages overlap and cannot be summed; deep mastery of one cloud platform beats shallow coverage.
  • 68% of postings require on-site travel, and client-facing evidence is a key differentiator.
  • 60% of postings target mid-level candidates with 3-5 years of experience; junior roles are only 12%.

Key Takeaways

  • Of the 1,000 FDE job postings analyzed by Bloomberry, 66% mention Python. TypeScript and AI agents are tied at 35%, LLMs at 31%, and AWS at 32%.
  • These percentages overlap and cannot be added together. Deep mastery of one cloud platform is worth more than a shallow brush with three.
  • According to Paraform, Python and JavaScript are just the entry ticket. What truly sets candidates apart is having shipped real LLM or agent systems and understanding customers.

Open a Forward Deployed Engineer (FDE) job posting and you can count a dozen requirements: Python, TypeScript, some cloud platform, LLMs, agents, stakeholder communication, willingness to travel. At that point, the question is no longer "am I qualified?" but "what should I learn first?"

Guessing by instinct is an easy way to go wrong. Bloomberry analyzed 1,000 FDE job postings and counted how often each skill appears — a far more reliable basis.

Knowing which skills appear in two-thirds of postings and which appear in less than a fifth gives you a priority order for both learning and resume writing.

Reading job postings systematically is rising in value. Paraform's recruiting data shows FDE postings grew 350% from Q1 2025 to Q1 2026. The more postings there are, the more noise there is — only those who can filter out the core requirements will apply to the right places.

Two-thirds of postings require the same language

The table below shows the technical skills in Bloomberry's sample, ranked by frequency. The third column converts the percentages into the number of postings out of 1,000, to make the scale tangible.

AI agents35%350 postings

Python leads by a wide margin: 66%, nearly twice TypeScript in second place. Next comes a cluster concentrated around one-third: TypeScript, agents, AWS, and LLMs. In other words, Python is nearly an entry ticket, while the second cluster is where candidates start to differentiate themselves.

Beyond technical skills, there is one more number worth remembering: 68% of postings require travel to customer sites.

Why the percentages can't be added up

This is the most common mistake when reading this data. AWS, GCP, and Azure add up to 72%, but you cannot conclude from that that 72% of postings require cloud skills. A single posting may mention both AWS and GCP, and that same posting gets counted twice.

Each skill should be viewed on its own, calculated independently against the 1,000 postings. The right question is: if you could learn just one more thing, which one would open the most doors? For cloud, the answer is AWS, appearing in 320 postings, versus 220 for GCP.

Reading it this way also prevents spreading yourself too thin. An engineer who has truly mastered AWS — deployed on it themselves, debugged their own IAM issues, watched their own costs — is more convincing than someone who has dabbled in three clouds. Once you deeply understand one platform, switching to another at a customer site becomes much faster too.

Breaking down a real posting: OpenAI's New York role

Now let's apply the frequency table to OpenAI's FDE posting in New York. It requires 5+ years of technical or deployment experience, including some work directly facing customers. Candidates need to write production code in Python or JavaScript, and must have built or deployed systems powered by LLMs or generative models.

Check it against the table line by line. Python is the most common skill (66%). JavaScript has no separate number because Bloomberry only tracked TypeScript (35%), but Paraform classifies both Python and JavaScript as "table stakes" — the entry ticket. The LLM requirement (31%) is where this posting starts to screen candidates.

The next line is the easiest to skim past. OpenAI's posting wants candidates who can communicate clearly with engineers, product teams, and customer-side stakeholders.

Paraform also believes what differentiates candidates is having deployed real LLM or agent systems, plus soft skills like understanding customers. OpenAI's posting maps to both points: it requires having built or deployed LLM systems, and requires communicating well with customer-side contacts.

Palantir's Forward Deployed Software Engineer (FDSE) posting for its US government business shows a different pattern. It wants candidates proficient in Python, Java, or C++, and requires a Top Secret security clearance.

That clearance is tied to the role's government work, so the most reasonable interpretation is to treat it as a company-specific requirement rather than a general market signal.

Palantir's posting also reveals a useful insight: the FDSE works independently, makes their own decisions, and frequently negotiates with customers. When you see a description like that, prepare an interview story about a time you had to make the call alone, with no one to sign off for you.

From frequency table to learning plan

The table above can be split into three tiers. The first tier is production-grade Python: with tests, packaging, and solid error handling.

The second tier is one or two items from the one-third cluster: choose TypeScript if you often build interfaces for customers; choose agents and LLMs if you want to target AI companies.

The third tier is one cloud platform — AWS, unless you have a specific reason to choose otherwise.

Running through all three tiers is evidence of having worked directly with customers. Since 68% of postings require travel, and OpenAI's posting explicitly states the experience must include customer-facing work, employers most likely want to see that you have built things for real users on-site at a customer.

When writing your resume, compare two phrasings. The weak version: "Experience with LLMs, agents, and AWS." The stronger version, using a hypothetical example: "Built an invoice-recognition agent for a 12-person accounting team, running on AWS Lambda, cutting reconciliation time from two days to half a day."

The second phrasing answers exactly the three things employers want to know: what you did, who uses it, and whether it actually runs.

Three mistakes that make a skills list backfire

The most common mistake is trying to check every box on the list. The frequency table is for deciding what to skip, not for collecting everything. A candidate with solid Python, fluency in one cloud platform, and one shipped agent project that actually works will rank ahead of someone with a passing acquaintance with seven skills.

Another mistake is treating years of experience as a wall. Bloomberry found that 60% of postings target mid-level candidates with 3 to 5 years of experience, while entry-level roles account for only 12% — about 120 out of 1,000 postings.

If you have less than three years of experience, aim directly at that small slice of roles while building customer-facing experience in your current job.

The last mistake is just listing keywords. An ATS might catch "LLM" on your resume, but interviewers will ask what you shipped to production, where it broke, and how you fixed it. One well-prepared story with real numbers beats ten keywords.

Exercise: count five job postings yourself

Pick five FDE job postings from companies you genuinely want to join. Copy each requirement into its own row in a spreadsheet, merge different phrasings of the same skill, then count them. Compare against the table above: where they agree is a market signal; where they differ is specific to the type of company you're targeting.

The Bloomberry table gives you the full market picture. The five job postings you count yourself tell you what to learn next Monday.
