Forward Deployed Engineer vs Contractor: Which One Gets Your AI Into Production

Hayat Amin · Operator · 2026-11-03 · 10 min read

Summary

Author Hayat Amin, a former technology CFO now working as a forward deployed engineer, compares FDEs with contractors: hire a contractor when the spec fits on one page; hire an FDE when the real problem is discovering the spec, such as pilots that worked in demos but fail on real data. He cites the MIT NANDA study (about 5% of AI pilots achieve rapid revenue acceleration) and S&P Global data on rising abandonment rates, lists US pay benchmarks (median total pay around $220,000; his firm's fractional option from $5,800/month), and flags two legal points: custom software is not a work made for hire, so IP needs written assignment, and IRS classification may treat a closely directed "FDE contractor" as an employee. A six-step checklist helps readers decide. Note the piece doubles as promotion for his own services.

A contractor builds what you specify and leaves. A forward deployed engineer works inside your company until the system runs on your real data, then hands it to someone on your team. If you can write the spec on one page, hire the contractor. If you can't, you need the FDE.

I'm Hayat Amin. I spent twenty years as a technology chief financial officer, sold three companies in that seat, and now work as a forward deployed engineer, so I've signed off contractor invoices and I've done the FDE work myself. Below is how the two differ, where the contractor wins, what each costs, who owns the code, and six things to do this week before you hire either.

The difference, line by line

Who writes the spec You do, before they start They do, with your team, in week 1
Where they work Mostly outside, on a ticket Inside your meetings, your inbox and your systems

When a contractor is the right call

I'd hire a contractor, and I have, when four things are true. The work is one integration or one feature. Both systems have documented APIs. You can say what done looks like in a sentence, for example "every paid Shopify order creates an invoice in QuickBooks within five minutes". And somebody on your team already knows the process well enough to test the result.

In that case an FDE is overkill and you'll pay for judgement you don't need. Paraform's hiring guide, dated 20 October 2026, makes the same point from the recruiting side. It cites First Round Review reporting that founders who start out asking for an FDE often turn out to need a software engineer or a post-sales role. Run that check before you open anything.

When you need a forward deployed engineer

You need the FDE when you can't write the spec, because the hard part is finding out what the spec is. The usual signs are a pilot that worked in the demo and fell over on real data, three systems that don't talk to each other, a person retyping data between them every week, or a board asking for an AI plan the chief executive can't write yet.

The failure numbers explain why a ticket doesn't fix this. Fortune's report on the MIT NANDA study, drawn from 150 leader interviews, a survey of 350 employees and 300 public AI deployments, says about 5% of AI pilots achieve rapid revenue acceleration and the rest mostly stall. The study's lead author put the core problem down to tools that don't learn from or adapt to a company's workflows, not the quality of the model. Buying from specialist vendors and building through partnerships succeeded about 67% of the time, and internal builds about a third as often. Paraform also cites S&P Global: companies abandoning most of their AI initiatives before production rose from 17% to 42%.

A contractor working to a spec will build exactly what the spec says. If the spec was written before anyone looked at the data, they'll build the demo again. I wrote up that pattern in why do my AI pilots never reach production.

If they won't fix the fee, that tells you the spec isn't finished, and you're back in FDE territory. Who owns the code, and who counts as an employee

I'm a CFO, not a lawyer, so take these two points to yours. They're the ones I see founders miss.

First, ownership. The US Copyright Office lists nine kinds of commissioned work that can be a work made for hire, such as a translation, a test or an atlas, and only with a signed written agreement. Custom software isn't on that list. Under 17 U.S.C. section 204, a transfer of copyright is valid only in writing and signed by the owner. So if a contractor builds your agent and the contract has no signed IP assignment, you may be paying for code you don't own. On my own engagements the client keeps everything, the code, the agents, the accounts and the documentation, and it sits in their repositories from the first day.

Second, classification. The IRS doesn't care whether you call someone a contractor. It weighs behavioural control, financial control and the type of relationship, and it says a remote worker is your employee if you control what is done and how it's done. An "FDE contractor" who joins your standup every morning and takes direction from your CTO may be an employee in the IRS's eyes. Write down the facts behind your decision, which is what the IRS tells businesses to do.

Six things to do this week, before you hire either

  • Write the spec on one page: the two or three systems involved, the input, the output, and the sentence that says what done means. If you can finish it in an hour, call a contractor.
  • If you couldn't finish it, list every place someone copies data from one system to another, for example HubSpot to QuickBooks, and the hours a week each one takes. Ask the person who does it.
  • Export 50 recent records from the source system and count how many are complete and consistent. If fewer than 45 are, fix the data before anyone builds anything.
  • Pick the one number that should move, such as hours retyped a week or days to send an invoice, and write down today's value.
  • Name the person on your team who will own the system after it's built. If nobody can, stop and fix that first, whichever route you take.
  • Before you sign anything, check the contract for a signed IP assignment, code in your own repository, accounts in your company's name, and a written handover. Then check the working arrangement against the three IRS categories with your accountant.

Plenty of companies finish step 1 and find it's a contractor job, or a native integration they already pay for. That's a good result. Spend the money there.

If step 2 shows three systems that won't join, a pilot that never left the demo, or a number the chief executive can't see until month end, the job is bigger than a weekend fix, and that's when you bring in a forward deployed engineer. Questions people actually ask

What is the difference between a forward deployed engineer and a contractor?

A contractor delivers a piece of work you have already defined, to a spec, and the engagement ends when that work is accepted. A forward deployed engineer starts before the spec exists. They sit with the people doing the work, find where it breaks, build the fix into your own systems on your real data, and hand it to a named owner on your team. You hire a contractor for an answer you can describe and an FDE for a problem you can't.

Can I hire a forward deployed engineer as a contractor?

Yes. A forward deployed engineer is a way of working, and you can engage one as an employee, through a firm, or as an independent contractor. The IRS doesn't go by the title. It looks at behavioural control, financial control and the type of relationship, so if you direct how and when someone works every day, they may be your employee whatever the contract says. Check the arrangement with your accountant before you sign.

Is a forward deployed engineer the same as consulting?

No. A consultant leaves a recommendation and a contractor leaves a deliverable. A forward deployed engineer leaves a working system in your stack with someone on your team who runs it. I wrote the longer answer in is a forward deployed engineer just a consultant.

Who owns the code a contractor writes for my company?

Under US law, often the contractor, unless your contract moves it to you. The US Copyright Office lists nine kinds of commissioned work that can be a work made for hire, and custom software isn't one of them. A transfer of copyright is only valid in a signed written document, under 17 U.S.C. section 204. Put a signed IP assignment in the contract, and keep the code in your own repository from day one.
