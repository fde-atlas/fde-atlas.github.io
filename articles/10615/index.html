What Talent Does the AI Era Need? A One-Stop Guide to the Roles and Skills of FDEs, AI Trainers, and Narrative Designers

Original title: AI時代需要哪些人才？一次看懂FDE、AI訓練師、敘事設計師的工作與能力

While one side of the market debates how many people AI will replace, the opposite phenomenon is happening. In July, India's largest IT services provider TCS announced plans to build a team of up to 8,900 Forward Deployed Engineers (FDEs) dedicated to helping customers bring AI into their enterprises. In August, Meta was revealed to have planned early in the year to drastically shrink teams through AI agents, only to hit the brakes when productivity fell short of expectations.

Companies are beginning to discover that the real difficulty lies in whether people know what problems AI should solve, and how to judge whether it's doing things right. The answer is hidden in the kinds of talent the AI industry has been most aggressively competing for this year.

What 3 key types of talent do companies need to adopt AI?

Forward Deployed Engineer: clarifying real needs to make AI land in enterprises

The Forward Deployed Engineer (FDE) is becoming a new battleground for talent among Silicon Valley AI companies. According to Business Insider, citing data from the job platform Indeed, demand for FDE positions has clearly heated up, with job postings in April 2026 growing about 7-fold compared to a year earlier—a staggering rate of growth.

AI Trainer: applying professional judgment to vet model answers

AI Trainers, who safeguard the quality of AI models, are shifting from gig work anyone could take on to specialized talent that AI companies are competing to find. OpenAI, Anthropic, and Meta are all looking for people with domain knowledge in law, medicine, finance, and other fields to train models. This demand has also supported a massive industry—matching platform Mercor alone pays contractors more than $1.5 million a day.

Narrative Designer: making choices from a sea of content to tell a clear story

And as AI content generation becomes easy, the last mile comes back to human storytelling. As a result, a role that few paid attention to in the past is gaining recognition: people who specialize in making complex things clear. As tech giants like NVIDIA, SAP, and AMD successively write "narrative" and "visual storytelling" into official job titles, this capability has become a formally defined profession.

These three roles sit at different stages of AI's journey from technology to value, yet together they confirm one thing: as AI makes execution increasingly cheap, what is truly valuable is the work that "only humans" can do.

When customers can't articulate their needs, how does an FDE find the real problem?

For AI to be truly useful in real-world settings, the key is often not the technology. Summit, an FDE Lead at the Taiwanese software company Linker Vision, has observed that model capabilities are mostly strong enough now; what really gets stuck is deployment. The most common difficulty is that customers themselves can't clearly articulate what they want, or the needs they voice aren't actually the real pain points. Figuring out how to turn vague needs into problems AI can solve is often where the most effort goes.

"An FDE is like a combination of consultant, engineer, and PM," Summit describes. This role must simultaneously understand the business, handle complex technical deployments, and keep projects on track—in short, a "problem solver who uses AI tools."

He also uses "multiplication" as a metaphor for AI: the stronger the model, the higher the amplification factor, but if the problem being amplified is defined wrong from the start, even the strongest model will only magnify the error. "AI can't define problems on its own. The ability to converge on the right problem is the part of humans that's very hard to replace."

Who judges whether AI's answers are good?

Many of AI's answers have no absolute right or wrong, good or bad, and require further human judgment. AI Trainer Carol also assumed before entering the field that it was just a rule-following job anyone could pick up, but doing it turned out to be a completely different matter, because an AI response can read fluently while hiding critical factual errors, and the same scoring guidelines can be interpreted differently by different people.

"The essence of this job is really more like being a referee," Carol describes. Trainers must make consistent and precise judgments of AI's performance in ambiguous territory where there are no standard answers, and they must be able to explain their reasoning clearly. This judgment relies on human experience and an understanding of context and culture. It is precisely because such professional judgment is needed that this profession is gradually shedding its "gig work" image and moving toward professionalization.

PwC's latest AI Jobs Barometer also observed a similar shift: in AI-affected roles, newly added tasks lean more toward human capabilities like empathy, judgment, and creativity. The study also divides roles into two categories: one where AI taking over routine tasks makes human professional judgment more prominent, and another where AI lowers the barrier so non-experts can perform the work. Job postings in the former category are growing twice as fast as the latter, and wage growth since 2021 has been 42% higher.

When everyone can generate content, storytelling is what matters

AI can generate piles of content quickly and well, but it can't decide for you which parts truly matter—something especially evident in presentations. That's why tech companies have started offering high salaries for dedicated presentation and narrative designers.

Huang Fei-Long, who previously worked in creative marketing and art direction at Yahoo in the US and recently sparked discussion by sharing that an NVIDIA recruiter invited him to work on presentations, believes the point of this role was never making slides look pretty, but first understanding what a company truly wants to convey, then selecting and shaping a clear storyline from the engineers' technical details, executives' vision, and marketing goals. "When everyone can generate content, what's truly scarce is no longer content, but perspective." In his view, when everyone has the tools, people who can create direction for something become even rarer.

Why do Taiwanese companies get stuck with AI even after buying the tools?

According to 104's AI Talent Trends Report, Taiwanese companies' average monthly hiring of AI talent surpassed 100,000 people in 2026, with demand growing 97.6% over the past five years—far outpacing the overall recruitment market. The job categories with the highest demand growth were, in order, project/product management, sales, and marketing. This may reflect that companies need not only people who can build AI, but also people who can make AI run smoothly within organizations and workflows.

Yet strong demand doesn't mean smooth transformation. The Artificial Intelligence Foundation (AIF)'s 2026 Taiwan Industry AI Adoption Survey observed that many companies adopt AI by "buying the tools first, then looking for uses," rather than starting from their real pain points, leaving investments without visible results. Summit's frontline observations echo this: he has found that Taiwanese companies are more accustomed to ready-made, standardized products and are still adapting to the collaborative model that FDEs require. The successful adoptions he has seen were usually ones where the boss first thought through what problem to solve, then led the push themselves.

In the end, Taiwan's challenge isn't technology, but whether companies can first think clearly about what problem they want to solve. As AI tools become easier to obtain, what's lacking instead are people who can define problems and lead the organization to implement them.

Working inside AI, they see human value more clearly

Returning to the original question: when AI can do almost everything, what human value remains? Interestingly, these people who work closely with AI every day are the least anxious, because they are increasingly certain that humans still have irreplaceable strengths.

"As long as you're passionate about something and have strong ideas, you really shouldn't be afraid of having nothing to do," Summit says, because AI happens to help you realize your ideas quickly. Huang Fei-Long's view is more direct: "In the future, the most valuable people won't be those who know how to use AI, but those with experience and a sense of responsibility." In Carol's view, rather than staying stuck in anxiety, it's better to step in proactively: "Go see where this industry has gotten to, and then think about what you can do." Only by truly participating can you see AI's limits clearly—and know which abilities it lacks that you should cultivate.
