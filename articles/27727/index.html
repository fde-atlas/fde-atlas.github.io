How to Use Loom Async Video: FDE Cross-Timezone Customer Communication

Original title: Loom 异步视频怎么用：FDE 跨时区客户沟通

FDE Times · 2026-11-07 · 4 min read

Summary

This article explains how FDEs working across time zones can use Loom, a screen-recording tool acquired by Atlassian in 2023, to replace late-night meetings with five-minute async video updates. It argues video suits one-way information like demos and milestone updates, while scope negotiations and bad news still require live meetings. The free tier allows 25 videos of up to 5 minutes each, roughly five weeks of daily updates. The recommended structure: state the result in the first 30 seconds, demo the running system, then end with a concrete task and deadline for the customer. It also warns about data privacy, quota limits, and pairing videos with written documentation.

Key points

  • Use video for one-way updates; use meetings for scope talks, bad news, and decisions.
  • Free tier: 25 videos, 5 minutes each — enough for about five weeks of daily updates.
  • Open with the result, close with one concrete customer task and a deadline.
  • Add 3-5 lines of text under the link so updates remain searchable.
  • Check customer data policies before recording; mask or anonymize sensitive fields.

One-way information like demos and milestones is well suited to video; negotiating scope, delivering bad news, and making joint decisions still call for a meeting.

Key takeaways

  • Loom records your screen and voice, letting you update clients on progress without finding a mutually free time slot
  • The free plan offers 25 videos per person, each up to 5 minutes: enough to try it out, but may not last a whole project
  • Lead with the outcome at the start of the video, end with what the client needs to do, and add a few lines of text

Loom's free plan gives each person 25 videos, each up to 5 minutes long. That limit sounds annoying. But for an FDE who needs to sync progress with clients on the other side of the world, five minutes is exactly the discipline worth keeping.

Loom is a free screen recording tool for Mac and PC, acquired by Atlassian in 2023. It was built for exactly this: record your screen, talk over it, then send the video message to colleagues or clients.

For a Forward Deployed Engineer (FDE), the challenge is pacing, not tooling. Your client is in another time zone, and every question that has to wait for the next joint meeting pushes the deployment back another cycle. A good video walkthrough lets the client keep moving without waiting for you to wake up.

When should you record a video instead of holding a meeting?

Loom's website states the problem plainly: getting everyone in the same place at the same time takes real effort, especially across time zones. Loom positions asynchronous video as something that can both replace and complement real-time meetings, with viewers watching whenever they like. Remember the word "complement."

When information flows mainly one way, record a video: demo a feature you just built, explain why you chose a particular approach, show how to rerun a step. Atlassian's blog notes that Loom uses asynchronous video internally for major milestones and roadmap updates. On the client side, that maps to completing a deployment milestone, or a change in plans.

When you need to renegotiate scope, deliver bad news, or make a decision that requires multiple parties' agreement, you should still meet. Video conveys information quickly, but it won't help both sides reach consensus.

What does five minutes actually look like?

Imagine this scenario: you're heading home from work while the client team is about to go to sleep. The data ingestion pipeline you built for them just completed its first run on real data, but there's still a bug in the reconciliation step. By tomorrow morning, they need to know three things: what worked, what didn't, and what they need to do.

TimestampContentPurpose
0:00–0:30State the outcome in one sentence: the pipeline ran, one reconciliation bug remainsEven someone who only watches the first 30 seconds knows the status
0:30–2:00Share your screen and show data flowing through each stepProve it with something actually running, not just describe it
2:00–3:30Point out the bug and your hypothesis about the causeHelp the client understand why the work isn't done yet
3:30–4:30One thing the client needs to do, with a deadlineTurn the video into action
4:30–5:00What you'll do over the next dayLet the client know when the next update is coming

This structure fits neatly within the free plan's 5-minute cap, and that's no coincidence. If you can't say it in five minutes, it usually means you haven't figured out what you want the client to do.

The bad version usually starts with small talk

Now imagine the wrong version of the same video: it opens with "Hi everyone, I just wanted to give a quick update today," then spends two minutes recapping project background the client already knows, then shares the screen and scrolls through code. The reconciliation bug doesn't appear until minute four, and it ends with "Let me know if you have any questions."

Someone on the client side opens the video at the start of their day, in the gap between processing a batch of emails. They'll most likely close it before minute four, without knowing what they're supposed to do. The fix is simple: cut all the pleasantries and background, say "the pipeline ran, one reconciliation bug remains" in the first second, and replace the closing niceties with a specific task and deadline.

Atlassian's blog argues that video messages are faster and more personal than plain text. That comparison implicitly assumes one thing: video should complement text, not replace it.

Write three to five lines below the video link: the outcome, the decision the client needs to make, and the ticket link. When someone needs to look things up later, they'll search these lines; nobody will rewatch the whole video.

Think through these limitations before you hit send

Do the quota math first. If you send one update video per working day, the free plan's 25 videos run out in about five weeks, far shorter than many projects' timelines. Before your entire handoff process depends on Loom, ask whether your company or client already has a paid plan.

The bigger risk is data. Everything on your screen goes into the video, and the video lives on a third-party service. Before recording real client data, check their policies, mask sensitive fields, or demo with a sanitized copy of the data.

Finally, video cannot replace handoff documentation. Runbooks, configurations, and architecture decisions still need to be written down. Video is an explanation layer on top of the docs, helping readers understand them faster.

What to practice first, and how to show it to employers

The first skill to practice isn't hitting the record button; it's writing a five-line script before you record. Then rewatch the video from the client's perspective: the moment you hit jargon only your own team understands, re-record it. After a few rounds of this practice, your written updates will get sharper too.

If an FDE job description you're targeting mentions collaborating with clients across time zones or asynchronous communication, don't just write "strong communication skills" on your resume. Make a five-minute video walkthrough using the structure above and attach it to a GitHub project. Interviewers can see directly how you hand off work, instead of just taking your word for it.

When your client is on the other side of a time zone difference, sometimes the best meeting is the one nobody has to stay up late for.
