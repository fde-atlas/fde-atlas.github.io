Introduction to Implementing FDE Internal Controls | How to Build a System That Doesn't Break Down

Original title: FDE内部統制の導入入門｜崩れない仕組みの作り方

日本Fde協会 · 2026-11-14 · 5 min read

Summary

This article by the Japan FDE Association argues that AI deployments often break down months after launch not for technical reasons but because internal controls were never designed. It explains the three-part framework of segregation of duties, approval flows, and audit trails, stresses drawing a clear boundary between what AI agents may execute automatically and what requires human approval, and offers a checklist of control points to verify in the first week of a project. It also notes that even small organizations reduce incidents simply by separating approval from record-keeping.

Key points

  • Breakdowns surface after handovers, not during prototyping.
  • Core questions: who approves, who executes, who records.
  • Combining approval and execution in one person is riskiest.
  • Set automation boundaries by impact level, documented in advance.
  • A six-item control checklist covers approvals, audit trails, permissions, and handover.

This is the Japan FDE Association. In AI implementation work, we often receive consultations saying, "We built something that works, but operations broke down six months later." In most cases, the cause is not technical but rather a missing design for FDE internal controls. By reading this article, you will grasp the overall picture of segregation of duties—who approves, who executes, and who records—along with audit trails, and be able to identify the control checkpoints you should verify first in your own projects. Note that FDE in this article refers to Forward Deployed Engineer (a practitioner who embeds themselves in the customer's site), and is distinct from Full Disk Encryption, which refers to encryption.

What Actually Happens When FDE Internal Controls Break Down

Most FDE explanations on the market stop at "engineers who embed themselves in the field." However, what we keenly feel in practice is that what matters after implementation is the design of controls. When AI is introduced into business processes, parts of what humans used to judge are replaced by automated processing. If controls are not designed at this point, it becomes unclear who was responsible for what was executed.

Why Things Break Down After Implementation, Not at the Start

At the stage when a prototype is working, the person who built it understands the entire process, so problems don't surface. Things break down when the person in charge is transferred and operations are handed over to someone else. If there is no record of approvals and no rules about who can update data, explanations become impossible during audits or incident response. When Maekawa was in charge of a shipping and receiving management system for petrochemical complexes at a major engineering company, seeing the project through from upstream design to operations and maintenance gave him the sense that "it's not about building and finishing; a system only becomes a system once records are kept."

Sketching the Big Picture with the Three-Piece Set: Segregation of Duties, Approval, and Records

"Internal controls" sounds difficult, but in FDE practice it can be broken down into three questions: "Who approves?" "Who executes?" and "Who keeps records?" The structure most prone to breakdown is when the same person handles all three.

Segregation of Duties: Separating Approval from Execution

Segregation of duties is the concept of dividing a single process among multiple roles so they check and balance each other. For example, even if AI generates a draft of billing data, the approval to finalize it as official data is handled by a different person. At financial institutions, it was standard practice to separate the person who initiates transactions from the person who records them. This thinking doesn't change with AI implementation. In fact, the wider the scope in which AI agents operate automatically, the more crucial the line-drawing of "how far AI may execute automatically, and from where human approval is required" becomes to controls. The technical design of permissions themselves is covered in detail in Designing AI Agent Permission Management.

Approval Flow: Determining the Boundary Between Automated and Manual

In designing approval flows, it is practical to set tiers based on the impact of the process. Low-impact processes can be left to AI, while high-impact processes involving amounts or personal information require human approval. If this boundary is agreed upon and documented in advance, you can answer when later asked, "Why is this one automated?"

Audit Trails: Keeping Records That Can Be Explained Later

An audit trail is a record that allows you to trace later when, by whom, what was executed, and with what input. When AI is involved, you need to retain the prompts entered, the data referenced, and even the output results. For a client in the medical field, we established operations where AI-generated drafts were not used as-is, and records were kept up to the point of human review. Knowing "who gave the final confirmation" builds trust in the system.

Control Checkpoints to Verify at Project Kickoff

We have organized into a table the points we check regarding controls during the first week when starting a new project. Addressing these at the requirements definition stage dramatically reduces rework later.

PerspectiveWhat to Verify
Location of approvalIs there a designated approver who finalizes AI output as official?
Segregation of dutiesAre approval, execution, and recording concentrated in a single person?
Boundary of automationIs there agreement on the scope AI may execute automatically and the scope requiring human approval?
Audit trailAre inputs, outputs, and reviewers retained in a traceable form?
Permission managementAre data viewing and updating permissions restricted to the minimum necessary?
Handover of recordsAre operational rules and records retained in a form that survives personnel changes?

There are situations involving laws and standards related to internal controls (such as J-SOX), but whether they apply varies by company circumstances, so please treat this as general guidance here. From my experience of founding my own e-commerce business, even in small organizations, simply "separating approval from recording" greatly reduces incidents. For record maintenance and handover perspectives, also see FDE Handover and Documentation. For establishing operations grounded in controls, Operational Establishment After AI Implementation is a helpful reference.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q. From what company size should internal controls be a concern? Regardless of size, if approval, execution, and recording are all handled by one person, it is worth addressing. Even in small organizations, simply separating approval from recording reduces incidents.

Q. Is control design for AI implementation within the scope of FDE work? Yes. The Association views FDEs as practitioners who support not only field interviews and development, but implementation grounded in segregation of duties and audit trails.

Q. Are control-related skills covered in the FDE Certification? The FDE Certification exam domains include internal controls and auditing, information security, ethics, legal matters, and customer accountability. The FDE Certification is a private certification operated independently by the Association, and holding or not holding the credential does not legally restrict engagement in such work.
