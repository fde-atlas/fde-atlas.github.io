Differences Between Palantir Gotham and Foundry | Use Cases, Customers, and FDE Practice

Original title: Palantir GothamとFoundryの違い｜用途・顧客・FDE実務

FDE総研 · 2026-10-21 · 10 min read

Summary

This article compares Palantir's two main platforms: Gotham, built for defense, intelligence, and law-enforcement agencies, and Foundry, aimed at commercial enterprises. Both share the same design philosophy of turning raw data into permissioned objects for decision-making, but differ in customers, purpose, and operating requirements. It also explains how AIP (announced April 2023) layers LLMs on top of the Ontology, how Apollo delivers updates even to air-gapped environments, and how FDE work differs between the two: Gotham projects are shaped by security clearances and classified environments, while Foundry projects reward business modeling and fast delivery. Notable Foundry deployments include Airbus Skywise, the UK NHS Federated Data Platform (2023), and a 2019 joint venture with SOMPO. For engineers in Japan, Foundry and AIP commercial projects are the more realistic entry point.

Key points

  • Gotham serves defense and intelligence agencies; Foundry optimizes commercial operations via Ontology.
  • The two products share one design philosophy, not separate codebases or concepts.
  • AIP connects LLMs to Ontology-defined actions; Apollo automates delivery across isolated environments.
  • Gotham FDEs face clearances and air-gapped constraints; Foundry FDEs focus on business modeling.
  • Case studies: Airbus Skywise, NHS England platform (2023), SOMPO joint venture (2019).

What are the differences between Palantir Gotham and Foundry?

Gotham is an analysis platform for government, defense, and intelligence, while Foundry is a platform for integrating and operating business data for private companies.

Palantir Technologies was founded in 2003 and developed Gotham, its first flagship product, for government agencies. It later began offering Foundry for private companies. Both share the same purpose of integrating data for decision-making. The difference lies in "who uses it and for what kind of decisions." Palantir itself explains on its official website and in its annual reports (Form 10-K) that its main platforms are the four: Gotham, Foundry, Apollo, and AIP.

How do the target customers and purposes differ?

Gotham is a platform for "finding threats and targets and making judgments," while Foundry is a platform for "optimizing and executing business operations."

Are the technical foundations shared?

Much of the technical foundation has become common. Even so, the approach to data models and the requirements of the operating environment differ by product.

Gotham and Foundry are closer in reality to products that extend the same design philosophy to government and private sectors, rather than being entirely separate products. Both are built around the flow of "converting raw data into meaningful objects, presenting them according to permissions, and recording decisions." Foundry productizes this flow explicitly as the Ontology, and the later AIP is also built on the Ontology.

From a practitioner's perspective, the skill required in both types of projects is "the ability to define the meaning of data." I often feel that outcomes are determined more by whether you can correctly model the customer's business than by the pipeline-building technology itself.

What kinds of organizations use Palantir Gotham and for what?

Gotham is mainly used by defense, intelligence, and law enforcement agencies in the US and allied countries, supporting intelligence analysis and operational decision-making.

It is widely said that Gotham's name "Gotham" was taken from the fictional city that serves as Batman's setting. Palantir also raised part of its early funding from In-Q-Tel, a venture capital firm affiliated with the US CIA, which well reflects the fact that Palantir started with government-focused work.

What are the main areas of use?

The main areas of use are four: intelligence analysis, defense operational planning, investigative support for law enforcement agencies, and disaster response.

  • Intelligence analysis: linking communication records, reports, and open-source information to visualize relationships between people and organizations
  • Defense and operational support: integrating units, equipment, and geographic information to support commanders' situational awareness and decision-making
  • Law enforcement and investigations: searching across case records and investigative materials to find connections
  • Disaster response and public health: tracking the status of supplies and facilities to make allocation decisions

Specific contract names and amounts can only be confirmed to a limited extent through government procurement information and Palantir's financial disclosures. Most projects are not public. Information circulating in articles and on social media such as "Gotham was used in Operation XX" needs to be verified against primary sources.

What requirements are unique to Gotham?

In Gotham, access control by classification level, operation in environments isolated from networks, and auditability take top priority.

A major difference from civilian systems is that being able to later verify "who viewed which information and how they judged" is mandatory. In addition, there are cases where Gotham is deployed in air-gapped environments with no internet connection, or in frontline environments with limited communication bandwidth. Engineers involved in developing or deploying Gotham must design with security and operational constraints as prerequisites before adding features.

How is Palantir Foundry used by companies?

Foundry is used to connect data scattered across a company as business objects via the Ontology, and to build applications that support frontline decision-making.

Foundry's distinguishing feature is that the data platform and application development environment are integrated. Unlike deploying a data warehouse and BI tools separately, data ingestion, transformation, modeling, business application development, and write-back can all be handled under the same permission management.

What are some notable publicly known deployments?

Famous public examples include Airbus's aviation data platform "Skywise," the UK NHS's federated data platform, and the joint venture with Japan's SOMPO.

The NHS case is a public institution, but the platform used is Foundry-based. It's useful to remember this as an example showing that things can't simply be divided into "Gotham = government, Foundry = private sector."

Which business areas are commonly used in enterprises?

Commonly used areas are operations that span multiple systems, such as supply chain, production and quality control, equipment maintenance, and supply-demand planning.

What these operations have in common is that data is scattered across ERPs, MES, spreadsheet files, paper records on the shop floor, etc., and a single decision requires multiple data sources. Foundry's value is not in creating dashboards per se. It lies in being able to handle "which orders are affected by this part's delivery delay, and who should decide what" on a single Ontology.

From a practitioner's perspective, whether a Foundry implementation succeeds tends to be determined less by technology than by "the first use case you choose." Rather than starting with company-wide KPIs that management wants to see, choosing one decision-making task that frontline staff use daily and building it out thoroughly tends to lead to better adoption.

How do AIP and Apollo relate to Gotham and Foundry?

AIP is a layer for running generative AI on top of Gotham and Foundry, while Apollo is the platform that continuously delivers and updates both products to each environment.

The four products are not side by side; they are layered on top of each other.

LayerProductRole
AI utilization layerAIP (Artificial Intelligence Platform)Connects LLMs to the Ontology and business actions, embedding them into agents and business applications
Application & data layerGotham / FoundryData integration, modeling, analysis, business applications
Delivery & operations layerApolloContinuous delivery and updating of software to cloud, on-premises, air-gapped environments, etc.

How does AIP differ from Gotham and Foundry?

AIP is not a standalone analytics tool. It is a product for safely embedding LLMs into business operations on top of the existing Ontology and permission management.

AIP was announced in April 2023. After that, "AIP Bootcamp," an onboarding approach where customers prototype use cases in a few days, became the central method of Palantir's sales and implementation. The key point is that the LLM does not directly touch databases; it operates through objects and actions defined in the Ontology. As a result, Gotham and Foundry's permission model of "who is allowed to do what" applies to the AI as well.

Why is Apollo important?

Thanks to Apollo, Palantir can consistently update the same software from government isolated environments to private-sector clouds.

Gotham customers have environments isolated from networks, or multiple environments with different security classifications. Apollo is a mechanism that automates software delivery while absorbing those environmental differences. Palantir has been offering Apollo as an external product since around 2021. For FDEs, it serves as the foundation for avoiding the state of "manually maintaining forked code for each customer."

How does the work of an FDE differ between Gotham and Foundry projects?

In Gotham projects, security constraints and confidentiality shape the way you work; in Foundry projects, business understanding and speed of results shape it.

The role corresponding to FDE (Forward Deployed Engineer) at Palantir is called "Forward Deployed Software Engineer" internally, and involves embedding into customer sites to handle everything from problem discovery to implementation. In both types of projects, the essence of "building alongside the customer" is the same. However, daily constraints differ greatly.

How do the working environment and required conditions differ?

In Gotham projects, security clearances and workplace restrictions are significant; in Foundry projects, flexibility to embed into customer companies' sites is required.

For engineers aiming to become FDEs in Japan, the most realistic opportunities are in Foundry/AIP-related private-sector projects. Gotham-related defense projects limit who can participate due to nationality and clearance requirements.

Which is better for a career?

Which is advantageous depends on the direction you want to pursue. Foundry projects make it easier to hone versatile FDE skills that apply across industries.

In Foundry projects, you can repeatedly gain experience translating operations from different industries into the Ontology. This "ability to model business operations" is a skill that transfers easily to FDE roles outside Palantir, data platform implementation support, and internal DX promotion roles. On the other hand, Gotham projects offer rare experience in design, operations, and audit response in high-security environments. This will be a strength for those who want to build a long-term career in defense and the public sector.

To add from a practitioner's perspective, what is valued in both types of projects is "being able to restate the customer's problem in the customer's own words." Being familiar with Palantir products is only the entry point. Whether you can deliver value as an FDE depends on whether you can fully determine, together with the customer, "what decision this data is for." Understanding the difference between Gotham and Foundry is the first step toward reading the context of that decision.
