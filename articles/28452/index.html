FDE interview prep: are courses and coaching worth it

FDE Times · 2026-11-17 · 6 min read

Summary

The article compares three FDE interview prep products: Educative's 138-lesson subscription library, Exponent's self-paced course plus 1:1 coaching, and Interview Kickstart's 23-week live cohort requiring roughly 230-322 hours total. It argues that knowledge and answer frameworks can be self-taught, but the customer-simulation round requires a real person playing a difficult buyer—something a friend can do for free. The advice: run at least three timed mock sessions before paying, buy only for your specific weakness, and show accepted proposals on your CV rather than course certificates.

Key points

  • FDE job postings on Indeed rose more than tenfold in 2025 versus 2024.
  • The three vendors sell different things: a library, coaching, and a 23-week cohort.
  • A friend playing a pushy customer against the clock can replace a paid coach.
  • Buy coaching only for the live-pushback layer you cannot self-practise.
  • A thin candidate pool means real customer work evidence beats certificates.

Courses run to 138 lessons or 23 weeks, yet the scarce thing is a real person playing a difficult customer, and they need not be a paid coach.

In brief

  • Educative, Exponent and Interview Kickstart sell three different things: a lesson library, 1:1 coaching and a 23-week cohort programme.
  • Most of the knowledge and answer frameworks can be self-taught, but the customer-simulation round needs a real person pushing back on you.
  • Before buying a coach, practise with a friend against the clock, and pay only for the part you cannot yet do on your own.

An FDE course on Educative has 138 lessons across 17 modules, plus 5 AI-scored mock interviews graded against a rubric. Interview Kickstart sells a programme that runs for 23 weeks.

Exponent sells a self-paced course and then invites you to book a coach. Three names, three very different levels of commitment, all aimed at the same buyers.

The reason is easy to see. According to The Pragmatic Engineer, FDE job postings on Indeed rose more than tenfold in 2025 compared with 2024. When demand grows that fast, course sellers follow.

But if you are thinking of opening your wallet, ask a narrower question: which part of the package can you not do yourself? The answer is far shorter than the courses are thick.

Three sellers, three different products

Lumping them together as “interview prep” invites confusion, because these three sell different things. Educative sells a library. Its general FDE course is open only to subscribers, at a list price of $59 a month, and includes dedicated preparation for the interview loops at Anthropic, OpenAI, Google and Palantir.

It also sells a separate course on the Google FDE interview: 48 lessons in 9 modules, roughly 12 hours, written by Fahim ul Haq, Educative’s co-founder and chief executive. That course also sits behind the subscription. The real cost therefore depends on how many months you study, not on the price of a single course.

Exponent, which has since moved to the aced.io domain, pairs a self-paced course with 1:1 coaching from people who have worked at FAANG+ companies. The model is stated plainly: learn first with the self-paced course, then book a coach for live practice. The coaching page does not list prices.

Interview Kickstart is the heaviest: 23 weeks, split into four blocks, taught live in cohorts and designed to be taken alongside a full-time job. Its course page does not list a price either, but it does state the workload: about 4-6 hours a week of live classes and 6-8 hours of assignments, projects and self-study.

Do the multiplication. Ten to 14 hours a week over 23 weeks comes to roughly 230 to 322 hours. That is the time you have to put in, before tuition. For someone in work, hours can be harder to find than money.

Reading cannot replace someone who pushes back

To see which part is worth paying for, look at the interview itself. Educative describes the FDE loop as including a customer-simulation round, in which the interviewer plays an enterprise buyer. A hundred and thirty-eight lessons will not train the reflex you need when that buyer cuts you off mid-sentence to ask about data security.

Exponent’s blog guide to the decomposition round makes the same point. The round lasts 45-60 minutes and, according to the guide, practising alone cannot reproduce the ambiguity of a live conversation. You need someone who knows how to needle you.

The interesting part is that the same guide points to a free way to practise: timed 45-60 minute sessions with a friend playing the interviewer and constantly following up, while you record yourself.

In other words, even a seller of coaching concedes that the core ingredient is someone who pushes back, not necessarily a paid coach.

What a self-run session looks like

Imagine you enlist a colleague who has worked with customers. The prompt: a logistics company wants to use an LLM to read supplier invoices. Your colleague plays the head of operations and reveals information only when you ask the right question.

By minute 10 you are already thinking about a RAG architecture. The “buyer” asks: “If an invoice is wrong, who is accountable?” If you freeze, the recording will show it clearly. That is the most valuable data from the whole session, and it cost you nothing.

The session should end with a proposal. Interview Kickstart’s free blog offers a test concrete enough to grade yourself against: a good proposal must be clear enough to evaluate and small enough to deliver.

In the invoice example, “automate the entire accounting function” fails. “Extract five fields from invoices from the three largest suppliers and measure accuracy on 200 samples” passes.

So what is worth paying for?

Map the offerings onto the layers of practice and the boundaries become fairly clear.

Practice layer Who sells it How to do it yourself
Knowledge and answer frameworks Educative’s library, Exponent’s self-paced course Free blogs, technical documentation, writing your own proposals
Rubric-based scoring Educative’s 5 AI mock interviews Write your own rubric, review the recording
Live pushback Exponent’s 1:1 coaches, Interview Kickstart’s live classes A colleague playing the buyer, against the clock
Study discipline Interview Kickstart’s 23-week cohort schedule A self-set schedule with a practice partner

The first row barely needs buying. The third row is where money may be worth spending, but only if you cannot find anyone to play a sufficiently difficult customer, or if you need someone who has sat on the interview panel at the exact company you are targeting.

The last row is a question about you rather than the course. If you know you will give up when studying alone, a fixed cohort schedule may be worth the money. If not, those 230 hours could go into a real project with real data.

Do not let the course market set your pace

There is one more reason not to rush. A recruiter quoted by The Pragmatic Engineer said every company wants to hire FDEs, but perhaps only around 10% of the market wants to do the job. With a candidate pool that thin, you are competing against fewer people than the course adverts make it feel.

That is no excuse for sloppy preparation. It means what makes you stand out is evidence of working with customers, not a certificate for completing 17 modules.

The practical advice: practise the decomposition and customer-simulation rounds at least three times with a real person before buying anything. By then you will know where you are weak, and you can pay for exactly that weakness.

On your CV, include a short proposal that a customer or an internal team actually accepted, stating the metric and how long it took to deliver.

Read job postings carefully, too. Educative describes the customer-simulation round as part of the FDE interview, so if a posting stresses working directly with customers, prepare for a role-play round. That is the round where you need someone to argue back, and that person can just as well be the colleague at the next desk.

The course market will keep growing as long as postings do. But what decides the customer-simulation round is still how many times you have been grilled, not how much you have paid.
