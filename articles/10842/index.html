FDE is the collective dying fantasy of Chinese software companies.

Original title: FDE是中国软件公司的集体临终幻想

53AI · 2026-09-02 · 7 min read

Summary

The author, a founder of an AI sales-forecasting startup, argues that while Chinese ToB software companies must adopt the FDE (Forward Deployed Engineer) model, they should not expect it to command premium pricing. True FDEs compress the delivery chain and reduce information loss, but most self-styled FDEs in China are just renamed on-site outsourcing staff. Palantir's FDEs are valuable because the company is valuable, not the role itself. Certifications cannot create scarcity, and clients won't pay for a vendor's cost structure. As AI drives coding costs toward zero, the value of accumulated standard products erodes; real premium lies in turning consultants' experience into callable, testable knowledge assets built over time.

Key points

  • FDE is necessary, but most Chinese FDEs are rebranded outsourced staff.
  • Palantir's FDE premium flows from the company, not the role.
  • Certifications and artificial scarcity cannot win client premiums.
  • AI erodes the value of accumulated standard products and code.
  • Durable premium comes from knowledge assets refined over time.

Full text

I've also fantasized that we ourselves are a ToB AI software company. So let me say this upfront, to avoid being misread as doom-mongering: FDE is something that must be done, but don't fantasize that it will bring a premium. This article will discuss the FDE model and the real sources of premium for ToB software companies.

I. FDE must be done, but it may not be the same thing as what most people call FDE

Palantir has been doing FDE for twenty years, and the two AI companies with the strongest product capabilities in the world almost simultaneously founded enterprise services companies and sent people to customer sites. OpenAI's venture raised $4 billion in funding and also absorbed an entire 150-person British AI consulting firm.

They have strong product capabilities, and they are all doubling down on FDE. Why? As I understand it, FDE has two functions:

1. Bridging the organizational gaps beyond product and technology: why existing processes look the way they do, who has the final say on rules, who sets the standards, why the data is dirty, why this role is the one doing this work, and what pitfalls the previous owner left behind. Of course, these are things classic implementation consultants should cover; it's just that some giant companies have begun to recognize the value of this dirty, exhausting work, taking it back from the traditional software vendors' partner ecosystem — the so-called "sacrificing profit to build a moat."

2. What truly distinguishes FDE from implementation consultants is that they must bridge another gap: "information loss. Traditional delivery is a serial chain: customer → Sales Engineer → business consultant → product manager → developer → tester. Every layer is a translation, and every translation loses information. The more people involved, the greater the loss. The essence of FDE is compressing this chain onto one person or a very small number of people."

On one hand, only AI makes this possibility a reality — it allows one person to potentially cover the skills of an entire team. But it must also be acknowledged that such people are extremely hard to cultivate — business, technology, communication, on-the-spot decision-making; if any one of these is even slightly weak, the chain breaks, and you have to add another person, and the translation layer comes back. That's why there simply aren't that many real FDEs on the market.

Today, the vast majority of so-called FDEs on the market are still outsourced on-site staff with a new name, with just one added requirement — knowing how to use AI tools. Not a single layer of the chain has been removed; the translations are still the same translations.

Moreover, Palantir's FDEs are valuable not because the "on-site engineer" role is somehow noble, but because Palantir itself is valuable — extremely high deal sizes, a strong position in front of customers, and an acceptance rate so low that getting in is itself a filter. The halo flows from the company to the role, not the other way around.

So when hundreds of thousands of people in China suddenly call themselves FDEs overnight, what happens is not "this role has become widespread," but that the word has lost its source of value.

By the way, let me mention how hot the FDE concept has gotten lately — so hot that even Zhang Qi is talking about it, which I never expected. It shows it has gone mainstream.

From the demand side, this role does make sense. It is a path software companies must take in the AI era — a form of self-discipline. But "must do it" and "can sell it at a premium" are two different things.

II. Premium comes only from scarcity, and scarcity never comes from a job title

So can scarcity be artificially created? There are three paths; let's see whether they are viable:

In my personal view, this path is very hard — it's not an execution problem, it's a structural one. Certifications with real value — CPA, the bar exam, physician licensing, construction engineer — all share three traits: slowly changing knowledge, legal liability, and license-based monopoly. Software has none of the three: tech stacks change every three years, and programmers don't go to jail for writing bad code. Exam syllabi take years to go from initiation to release; by the time they're printed, what's being tested is already outdated.

Many tenders require certified project managers. But a certificate that can be earned in a few months stops no one — the height of a barrier equals the cost of clearing it. It won't make FDEs more valuable; it will only add an expense for companies doing FDE.

And what's crueler: even if you truly achieve talent scarcity in FDE, customers can't see it when signing the contract. The FDE transformation improves delivery quality and efficiency. But in China's ToB market, delivery quality is something "you're supposed to do," not something "worth paying extra for." Sending people on-site, in the customer's mind, is a cost you paid to win the deal, not value added for them. Trying to package it as a new model and charge a premium is extremely hard. Customers don't pay for your cost structure; customers only pay for their own gains.

So FDE is more like a self-imposed standard for software companies, or a long-term trend, rather than a bargaining chip for negotiating high premiums with customers. That's why I said, somewhat clickbait-y, that FDE is the collective deathbed fantasy of Chinese software companies — not that we shouldn't do it, but that we can't count on it to save us or make us rich. (As some say: we must give it our all just to stay in place.)

The FDE concept is actually common knowledge in the industry; it also carries the responsibility of going on-site and bringing knowledge back to be consolidated into the product. Cosmic, Xinghan, BIP — these are star products built with real money, and the direction isn't wrong. For the past twenty years, "consolidating standard products" has been the only correct answer in this industry.

Because the value proposition of standard products is clear: customers don't have to build from scratch, so they're willing to pay for something ready-made. The development costs you save them are your pricing space.

The problem is, this premise may no longer exist in the future. When AI drives down the development cost of "building a set from scratch," is the part that standard products save customers still worth anything?

So the real question to ask is: in an era when writing code is no longer a cost, what kind of consolidation is worth anything?

IV. Why is money in this industry so hard to earn

Actually, in China, among legitimate businesses, which kind of money is easy to earn? This isn't sarcasm; it's a very real reality. China's talent density is too high — any direction proven to make money will quickly attract enough people coming in at cheap enough prices.

And what have software companies consolidated over the past twenty years? Standard products, configurable platforms, component libraries. At the end of the day, it's stockpiling the act of "writing code," which constitutes an important part of the moat.

But China has no shortage of programmers. Software companies are stockpiling something that isn't scarce, and you can't build a premium from that — that's the past of Chinese software companies. And AI makes software assets even less valuable: as the cost of writing code approaches zero, what we've stockpiled over the years will only depreciate faster.

So the real question isn't "how to sell at a higher price," but: what is something that more people cannot solve? That's what's worth consolidating.

Customers have always been willing to pay for knowledge and experience. What they buy has never been software, but "you've done fifty of these and know where the pitfalls are." Software is the vehicle, not the value itself. We pour the vast majority of our costs into the vehicle and then charge based on the vehicle. In an era when development was expensive, this business worked.

Meanwhile, knowledge itself is intangible; the vast majority of it sits in consultants' heads and leaves when they do. The company's books are left with a pile of customer logos and documents, but the implementation experience and the judgment for different scenarios all leave with the consultants to competitors.

  • Definition capability: concepts, standards, relationships, etc.
  • Evaluation capability: when to use which method, in what situations humans must intervene, how to judge whether a result is correct, and what lessons from successes and failures can be referenced
  • Component capability: consolidating the capabilities we can use for different scenarios into components that can be flexibly invoked (which can be code or prompts)

In the past, knowledge sat in people's heads or in documents, lying there unread; if it becomes an invocable skill, it gets invoked with every delivery, and being invoked means being tested, and being tested means being corrected.

That's what it means for knowledge to enter the production process. As for whether to use ontologies, knowledge graphs, documents, or vector databases to store it, each has its own scenarios — that's the vendor's own business and doesn't constitute a selling point to customers.

The ideal form is: when arriving at the next customer, the AI first understands the company's situation, then matches it against the knowledge we have accumulated, and figures out on its own the best-fitting components and path. People are no longer just carriers of knowledge; they also become users and collectors of it. And that person can be an FDE, or go by any other name.

To sum up: after all this discussion, we come back to two rather clichéd words: knowledge + time

Come to think of it, the ToB industry has no shortage of time. A company that has spent twenty years in a vertical domain—the pitfalls it has stumbled into, the exceptions it has seen, the boundaries it has judged—these are inherently the scarcest resources in the entire industry. Code isn't scarce, people aren't scarce, and tools are even less scarce; only "the knowledge accumulated after serving 100 customers" is scarce. Everyone can say "be a friend of time." But in an era where everything is accelerating, many say it, few truly believe it.

So how to build an organization, a mechanism, and a set of tools to ensure that knowledge is continuously accumulated over the timeline, efficiently retrieved, and iteratively refined in a loop—I believe this is where the future competitiveness of software companies lies, and the source of their premium.
