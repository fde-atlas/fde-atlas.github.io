0.2

The one-paragraph definition

The definition

A Forward Deployed Engineer is a software engineer who is embedded with a specific customer, writes production code against that customer's real data and real environment, and is measured on whether the customer's outcome improves — not on tickets closed or features shipped. You are the same engineer you were before; what changes is that the requirements now arrive as a person's frustration instead of a Jira ticket, and the definition of done is "they use it and it worked".

Two halves matter equally, and most people are strong at one:

The engineering half

You write and ship real code — services, pipelines, UIs, infrastructure.

You debug systems you did not build, with logs you cannot see, in a cloud account you do not own.

You pick boring, provable technology because you will be the one carrying the pager.

You leave behind something another engineer can run — not a demo held together by your laptop.

The forward half

You sit with the people who do the work and watch them do it, on their screen.

You translate "it takes forever to route these" into a schema, a metric and a scope.

You say no to the wrong feature in front of the person who asked for it, and keep the relationship.

You feed reality back to product engineering so the next customer gets it for free.

The title comes from Palantir, which built the whole company around it: engineers deployed into a customer's building, working alongside analysts, writing code that got used the same afternoon. The model spread because it works on a specific class of problem — powerful software that nobody can use out of the box. That description now fits almost every serious AI product, which is why OpenAI, Anthropic, Sierra, Scale AI, Applied Intuition, Ramp and a long tail of AI-native startups all hire FDEs, sometimes under the names Forward Deployed Software Engineer (FDSE), Deployment Engineer, Solutions Engineer (technical) or Applied AI Engineer.

The structural fact that defines the job: you are the only person who is in both rooms. The customer's ops lead does not talk to your product manager. You do. Every advantage and every difficulty of the role comes from that.

The tell in a job description

Read the JD for who owns the code after the project. If the answer is "we hand it to the customer and leave", that is consulting. If it is "you'll build POCs to support deals", that is pre-sales. If it is "you'll own the deployment, keep it running, and drive what we build next", that is a real FDE role. Also look for on-call, customer VPC, you'll be in their systems and a real engineering interview loop — those are the honest signals.

Quick check

A job ad says: "You'll partner with our largest accounts, build custom integrations against their systems, own the production deployment in their cloud, and feed requirements back to our platform team." Which role is this?

Owns the production deployment plus feeds requirements back to the platform team is the FDE fingerprint. A sales engineer would not own production; a consultant would not have a platform team to feed; an architect would design rather than build.

0.4

The forward-deployed loop

Every FDE engagement — a two-week pilot or a three-year account — runs the same six-stage loop. The rest of this course is a module per stage. Learn the loop now and every later module has a place to sit.

You will run this loop dozens of times with one customer, at shrinking amplitude — the first turn takes a quarter, the twentieth takes an afternoon.

1 Listen — in their words, at their desk

Watch someone do the work. Not a requirements call: a screen-share of the actual job. You are looking for the copy-paste, the second monitor, the spreadsheet nobody mentions. Fails when you accept a written requirements list and build it.

2 Scope — one painful, provable slice

Pick the smallest thing that (a) hurts measurably, (b) you can prove moved, and (c) does not need six other teams. Write it down with a number attached. Fails when the scope is "modernise the workflow".

3 Build — a working thing, fast, boring

Days, not quarters. Real data, real edge cases, a real UI even if it is ugly. Choose technology by how fast you can debug it at 6pm on their network. Fails when you build a beautiful platform nobody has used yet.

4 Deploy — into their environment, not yours

Their cloud, their SSO, their firewall, their change window. This is where most prototypes die, and why Module 06 exists. Fails when "it works on my laptop / our staging" was the plan.

5 Observe — usage, errors, and value

Two dashboards: one for you (health) and one for them (value). If you cannot show adoption and a moved metric, the renewal conversation has no evidence. Fails when nobody notices it stopped being used.

6 Generalise — bespoke becomes product

Push what you learned up the ladder: hard-code → config → plug-in → core feature. Your long-term output is the product improvement, not the branch. Fails when you accumulate 14 customer forks and become the only person who can deploy anything.

0.5

A week in the life

The honest version, from a mid-engagement week on a single enterprise account. Roughly 55% building, 25% customer contact, 20% internal and operational — the ratio shifts hard toward customer contact in week one of an engagement and hard toward building by month three.

Note what is not here: long sprint ceremonies, refinement meetings, and someone else deciding what you work on. Note what is: a fixed deploy window you do not control, and support triage that is genuinely your job.

From the field

The hardest adjustment for a strong product engineer is that interruption is the job, not a distraction from it. A customer message at 4pm that changes tomorrow's priority is not a failure of planning — it is the signal you were deployed to receive. Engineers who protect deep-work blocks above all else are miserable in this role; engineers who like context-switching and closure thrive.

Levels, compensation and career shape

FDE ladders map onto normal engineering ladders, but the scope axis is customer and outcome rather than system and blast radius. Titles vary; the shape does not.

Level

Scope

What separates them

FDE I / Deployment Engineer

One workstream on one account, with a lead

Ships the thing that was scoped for them. Learning the customer's domain.

FDE II / Senior

A whole account, or the hardest workstream on a big one

Scopes their own work. Says no. Owns the deploy and the pager.

Staff / Lead FDE

Multiple accounts, or one strategic account plus a pattern

Turns repeated custom work into product. Other FDEs reuse what they built.

Principal / Head of Deployment

The deployment practice itself

Decides which customers are winnable, sets the engagement model, hires.

On compensation: FDE roles at AI-native companies generally pay at or slightly above the equivalent product-engineering band at the same company, and frequently include a variable or bonus component tied to account outcomes. That premium is real but it is paid for travel, on-call and customer pressure. Public ranges move fast enough that any number written here would be wrong within a year — use levels.fyi and the company's own posted ranges, and ask directly in the first call whether the role carries variable comp and travel expectations.

The career argument for doing this

Three years as an FDE gives you something very few engineers have: you have seen how software actually fails to get adopted, across many real organisations. That converts unusually well into founding a company, into product management, into staff-level product engineering, and into being the person a startup hires to save its first enterprise deal. The exit options are wider than the title suggests.

0.7

Is this you? An honest checklist

You will probably hate it if…

You need a protected four-hour block to feel productive.

You want the requirements settled before you write code.

Being the least knowledgeable person about a domain makes you anxious rather than curious.

You judge work by the elegance of the system rather than whether it got used.

You cannot stand being blocked by someone else's IT ticket queue.

You will probably love it if…

You get energy from watching someone use the thing you built.

You are comfortable saying "I don't know, let me find out" to a VP.

You like the whole stack — schema to CSS to Terraform — more than depth in one layer.

You would rather ship something imperfect that changes a Tuesday than something perfect in six months.

Ambiguity reads as opportunity, not as poor management.

Quick check

Your prototype is working beautifully in your own cloud. The customer's security team will not approve outbound traffic to it for at least six weeks. What is the FDE response?

The environment is a requirement, and it was one on day one — you just did not treat it as one. Escalating may help in parallel, but the six weeks are a fact and building more features into an undeployable prototype makes the eventual migration worse. Module 06 is entirely about discovering these constraints before you write the code.

0.8

Assessment — the role brief

Coding assessment

Write the engagement one-pager

Before any code, an FDE writes the document that keeps everyone honest. You are joining Northwind Logistics (the fictional customer used throughout this course): a mid-size freight broker, 240 staff, whose support team manually triages ~700 inbound customer emails a day into six teams. They bought your product because a competitor promised "AI triage". Nobody has defined what success means. Your job this week is only to write the one-pager.

⏱ 45–60 minNotionLinear

Build it

Write a Current state section: the workflow as steps, with a time estimate per step and a named person who does it. Invent plausible detail — the exercise is the structure.

Write a Wedge section: the single narrowest slice you would automate first, and explicitly the three things you are not doing.

Write a Success metric section: one primary number, its current value, its target, and exactly how it will be measured. "Faster triage" is a fail; "median time-to-route drops from 11 min to under 2 min, measured from received_at to routed_at in our own DB" is a pass.

Write a Risks section with at least four, each with an owner and a date by which it must be resolved. At least one must be about their environment and one about their data.

Write a Kill criteria section: what would make you recommend stopping. If you cannot write this, you have not scoped it.

Done when

The whole thing fits on one page. If it does not, the scope is wrong, not the page.

A stranger can read it and tell you what will exist in six weeks and how you will know it worked.

The success metric is a number that exists in a system you control, not a survey.

Every risk has a human name and a date, not a status.

The "not doing" list is longer than the "doing" list.

Reveal a reference solution

A reference shape — yours should differ in the details, not in the bones. Keep this file; Module 02 refines it and the capstone in Module 11 builds exactly this.

What background do I need?

Working software engineering experience. You should be comfortable with Python, SQL and HTTP APIs, and able to read TypeScript. You do not need prior customer-facing experience, LLM experience or Kubernetes expertise — those are taught here at the depth the role actually requires.

Which companies hire forward deployed engineers?

Palantir originated the role. It is now common at AI labs and AI application companies including OpenAI, Anthropic, Sierra, Scale AI and Applied Intuition, at complex-infrastructure vendors, and at Series A to C startups with early enterprise customers. Titles vary: Forward Deployed Software Engineer, Deployment Engineer, Applied AI Engineer or technical Solutions Engineer.

Is a forward deployed engineer a real software engineering job?

Yes. A forward deployed engineer writes production code most of the week — services, integrations, interfaces and infrastructure — and typically carries the pager for their deployment. The difference from product engineering is that requirements arrive as a person's frustration rather than a ticket, and the software runs in the customer's environment.

What is a forward deployed engineer?

A forward deployed engineer (FDE) is a software engineer embedded with a specific customer who writes production code against that customer's real data and environment, and is measured on whether the customer's outcome improves. The role was popularised by Palantir and is now common at AI companies such as OpenAI, Anthropic, Sierra and Scale AI, sometimes under the titles Forward Deployed Software Engineer, Deployment Engineer or Applied AI Engineer.

Is a forward deployed engineer the same as a solutions engineer?

No. A solutions or sales engineer supports deals with demos, proofs of concept and architecture advice, and is measured on pipeline. A forward deployed engineer ships and operates production software inside the customer's environment after the deal, owns the deployment and is measured on adoption and outcome. FDE roles carry on-call and a real engineering interview loop; pre-sales roles usually do not.

Do forward deployed engineers write real code?

Yes — most of the week. A typical FDE writes backend services (usually Python with FastAPI), data integrations, front-end interfaces (usually TypeScript and React or Next.js), infrastructure as code, and increasingly LLM-backed features with their evaluation harnesses. The difference from product engineering is the requirements arrive as a person's frustration, and the code runs in the customer's environment.

Do you need to travel as a forward deployed engineer?

It depends on the company and the customer. Palantir-style engagements historically involved being physically on site for extended periods. Most AI-company FDE roles today are remote-first with periodic on-site visits — typically a kickoff week, key milestones and quarterly reviews. Ask directly about travel expectations in the first interview call; it varies from near zero to more than 50%.
