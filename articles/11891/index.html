The FDE Canon: The Reading That Actually Explains the Role

Vibe Engines · 2026-08-01 · 14 min read

Summary

This piece is a curated reading list and method for understanding the forward deployed engineer role, arguing that most writing on the topic is recruiting copy or speculation. It orders ten sources: first-hand accounts (Nabeel Qureshi on Palantir, PostHog, Baseten), job descriptions read as documents (OpenAI, Anthropic, Palantir's Dev-versus-Delta split), and market analysis (a16z's margin-for-defensibility trade, MIT NANDA's widely reported ~95% figure on pilots, Bloomberry's analysis of 1,000 postings). Its core method: treat postings as commitments and interrogate them. For example, if Anthropic's published 40/30/30 time split slips so customer work reaches 60%, the product-feedback share in a 13-week quarter falls from 3.9 to 1.3 weeks, drifting the role toward consulting. It warns that one title covers builder, pre-sales and internal jobs (roughly a third of postings are pre-sales-shaped), posting counts are not filled roles, and the most time-consuming parts of the job—identity, security review, commercial layer, evals—are almost never written up.

Key points

  • Read first-hand accounts before job descriptions to avoid recruiting framing
  • Treat postings as commitments: scope promises and published time splits are askable
  • If the 40% grows to 60%, the product-feedback 30% shrinks from 3.9 to 1.3 weeks per quarter
  • One title covers three jobs; about a third of postings are pre-sales-shaped
  • Identity, security review, commercial layer and evals are the unwritten parts of the job

Most writing about forward deployed engineering is recruiting copy or speculation. A small number of pieces are genuinely load-bearing: first-hand accounts by people who did the job, primary documents worth reading as documents, and market data. Here is the shelf, in reading order, with what to take from each — and what each one is silent about, because that matters as much.

Start here: what the job feels like

Two first-hand accounts. Read these before any job description, or you will read the job descriptions wrong.

Nabeel Qureshi — “Reflections on Palantir”14

Take: the texture of forward-deployed work at the company that invented the title — the Airbus engagement, the culture, what it feels like to be dropped into an organisation and expected to produce. It is the best answer available to “what is this actually like?” and it is unusually honest about the parts that were bad.

Silent about: the modern AI-native version of the role, and compensation. It describes one company, one era, one person’s experience — which is its strength as a document and its limit as a guide.

PostHog — “WTF is a forward deployed engineer?”15

Take: the same role at a product company rather than a platform company, written first-party and plainly. It is the clearest available account of the travel reality, which most sources skip and which is the single most common reason people leave the role.

Silent about: enterprise-scale deployment concerns — security review, air-gapped environments, the commercial layer. A smaller-company view, and the more relatable one.

Baseten — “What I learned as an FDE at an AI startup”31

Take: the AI-native version, at the stage where nobody has patterns yet. Useful precisely because it is not from a mature FDE organisation — it describes the improvisation that a polished job description hides.

Silent about: what scales. One engineer’s tour at one startup; read it for the shape of the days, not the method.

Job descriptions describe the role a company wants to hire. First-hand accounts describe the role as lived, including the parts nobody would put in a posting: the travel, the loneliness of being the only engineer who understands the system, the weeks where you are a project manager with a laptop. Reading the postings first calibrates you to the marketing.

Primary documents: read the job descriptions as documents

Not to apply — to see what companies are committing to in writing.

Read them for what a company is committing to rather than what it is asking for. “Owns the full arc of a deployment” is a promise about scope; a published time split is a promise about how your quarter is allocated. Both are legitimate things to ask about in an interview, and asking signals that you read carefully — which is the trait the job is about.

The commercial argument, and the market data

a16z — services-led growth8

Take: the reason the role exists, stated as a trade — margin for defensibility. If you understand this essay you can predict most decisions your employer will make about your team.

Silent about: the failure mode it walks into. It argues for the trade and does not dwell on organisations that pay the margin and never receive the moat. Read alongside our breakdown.

MIT NANDA — the 95% figure17

Take: the demand-side explanation. Capability is available and outcomes are not, which is a deployment gap rather than a model gap.

Silent about: its own methodology — the figure circulates through press coverage rather than a broadly available paper, so quote it as approximate. Our breakdown handles the caveats.

Bloomberry — 1,000 FDE jobs analysed1

Take: that one title covers three different jobs, and roughly a third of postings are pre-sales-shaped. This single fact will save you from accepting the wrong role.

Silent about: filled roles. Posting counts measure demand signals, and title inflation is real in a hot market. Breakdown here.

First Round Review — hiring an FDE11

Take: the hiring side, which tells you what is actually being assessed. It puts the engineering bar at staff level plus a temperament most strong engineers have not been asked to develop — useful because it names the second half explicitly.

Silent about: what the role does to a career over five years, which is the question candidates should be asking.

The Pragmatic Engineer — forward deployed engineers12

Take: the industry-wide framing, written for engineers rather than for investors or recruiters. The best single orientation piece if you read only one.

Silent about: the practitioner detail — evals, identity, the security review — which is where the work actually lives.

Where the role is going

Latent Space — forward deployed engineers at the AI Engineer World’s Fair30

Take: the 2026 shift some teams describe — from writing feature code to writing the systems that write feature code, with engineers supervising agent harnesses. If that direction holds, it changes what the deliverable is, and the agent harness design is our attempt at what it implies.

Silent about: whether it generalises. One company’s direction of travel, described at a conference, is a hypothesis rather than a trend.

Palantir blog — a day in the life33

Take: the concrete shape of a week at the origin company, which is a useful anchor against which to read every newer description.

Silent about: everything after 2020-era Palantir. Read it as a historical baseline, not a current spec.

There is very little good writing on the parts of the job that consume the most time: enterprise identity, the security review, the commercial layer, and evaluation for client work. That is not an accident — those topics are unglamorous, company-specific and rarely written up by the people doing them. It is also why most of this site exists. If you know of a genuinely good first-hand piece on any of them, it belongs here.

The reading is the cheap part

Every source above describes the same underlying skill: sitting with ambiguity in front of someone who is paying, and producing a next step rather than a caveat. That is practisable.

The picture: the shelf in reading order

Worked example: reading one posting as a document

Apply the page’s method to a posting that uses the phrases quoted above. For each line, ask what it commits to and what it leaves out. The quarter is 13 weeks; the overrun in step 3 is an illustrative scenario.

StepThe posting saysWhat it commits toWhat to ask
1“Owns the full arc of a deployment”Scope from first meeting to running in productionWho owns it after go-live, and for how long?
2A 40/30/30 time splitOver 13 weeks: 5.2, 3.9 and 3.9 weeksWhich share gives way when the 40% overruns?
3(Scenario) the 40% grows to 60%7.8 weeks, 2.6 more than promisedIf it all comes out of the product-feedback 30%, that share falls from 3.9 to 1.3 weeks: two-thirds gone
4Nothing on customer countSilentHow many customers at once?
5Nothing on pay structureSilentIs any of the comp variable?
6Air-gapped or impact-level deploymentA regulated jobWhat do travel, on-site work and clearance cost me?
7Quota, demos, “partner with sales”Pre-sales-shaped, like roughly a third of postingsIs this the builder variant at all?

Step 3 is why the page calls the 30% that feeds back into the product the part that separates the role from consulting. It is also the share that disappears first under customer pressure. Asking steps 2 to 5 in an interview costs nothing. The answers tell you which of the three jobs behind the title you are actually being offered.

Common mistakes

  • Reading postings firstJob descriptions calibrate you to recruiting language. Read two first-hand accounts first, then the postings, and the gaps between them become your interview questions.
  • Quoting the 95% as a precise factThe figure reaches most readers through press coverage, not the method. Say “roughly 95% of pilots, in one widely reported study” and use it as a direction, not a statistic to build a case on.
  • Counting postings as jobsPosting counts measure demand signals. In a hot market titles inflate, and some postings are never filled. Growth in postings is not growth in people doing the work.
  • Treating one title as one jobThe same word covers builder, pre-sales and internal roles. Accepting an offer on the title alone is how people end up in a sales role they did not want; step 7 above is the check.
  • Reading a baseline as a current specThe Palantir pieces describe one company in an earlier era. Use them as an anchor to compare newer descriptions against, not as a description of the job at an AI company today.

Where this shows up: interviews and the first month

  • In the interviewThe steps 2 to 5 questions show the careful reading the role is hired for. “How many customers at once?” and “what gives when the 40% overruns?” are fair, specific and rarely asked.
  • Choosing between offersPut each posting through the same seven steps. Two offers with the same title can turn out to be a builder role and a pre-sales role once you look at quota language and the time split.
  • Explaining the role to a clientThe a16z trade is the short answer to “why is an engineer from the vendor sitting with us?”: the vendor spends margin on deployment depth because depth is what keeps the account.
  • Filling the missing shelfIdentity, the security review, the commercial layer and client evals are learned on the job. Writing up what you learn in your first month, with client details removed, is the scarce document every newcomer wishes existed.

Check yourself

A friend has two hours before an FDE interview. What should they read, in what order?

One or two first-hand accounts (Qureshi, PostHog) for the job as lived, then that company’s own posting read as a document. The gaps between the two become their questions. Market data can wait: it explains the industry, not this role.

A posting promises 40/30/30, and the hiring manager says the 40% “sometimes runs over”. Over a 13-week quarter, what is at stake if it reaches 60%?

7.8 weeks instead of 5.2, so 2.6 extra weeks must come from somewhere. If they come from the product-feedback 30%, it falls from 3.9 to 1.3 weeks, and the role drifts towards consulting. Ask which share gives.

A slide says “FDE postings are booming, so FDE employment is booming.” What is wrong?

Postings measure demand signals, not filled roles, and title inflation is real in a hot market. Roughly a third of postings are also pre-sales-shaped, so even the postings are not all the same job.

A conference talk says engineers will soon supervise agent harnesses instead of writing features. How should you weigh it?

As a hypothesis. It is one company’s direction, described at a conference. Take the implication seriously (the deliverable may change) but wait for other teams to report the same before planning a career on it.

Quick answers

What should I read first to understand forward deployed engineering?

First-hand accounts before job descriptions. Nabeel Qureshi’s "Reflections on Palantir" for the texture of the work at the company that invented the title, PostHog’s first-party account for the travel reality that most sources skip, and Baseten’s account for the AI-native version at a stage where nobody has patterns yet. Reading postings first calibrates you to recruiting language rather than to the job.

Why read FDE job descriptions as documents?

Because they show what a company is committing to in writing, not just what it wants. "Owns the full arc of a deployment" is a promise about scope; a published time split such as Anthropic’s 40/30/30 is a promise about how your quarter is allocated. Both are legitimate to ask about in an interview, and asking demonstrates the careful reading the job itself requires.

What explains why demand for the role grew?

Two sources together. a16z’s services-led-growth argument gives the supply-side logic — deliberately trade gross margin for deployment depth because depth is defensible. The widely reported finding that roughly 95% of enterprise AI pilots showed no measurable P&L impact gives the demand-side logic: capability is available and outcomes are not, which is a deployment gap rather than a model gap.

What is missing from the available writing about this role?

Almost everything about the parts that consume the most time: enterprise identity and SSO, the vendor security review, the commercial layer of SOWs and pricing, and evaluation for client work. Those topics are unglamorous, company-specific and rarely written up by the people doing them, which is why most practitioner material has to be assembled rather than found.

Where is the role heading?

One direction described at the 2026 AI Engineer World’s Fair is a shift from writing feature code to writing the systems that write feature code — engineers supervising agent harnesses rather than authoring every change. Treat it as a hypothesis rather than an established trend: it is one company’s direction of travel, described at a conference, and it has not yet been shown to generalise.
