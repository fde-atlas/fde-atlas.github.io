02 The Boundary Between FDEs and Traditional Roles

Original title: 02 FDE 与传统岗位的边界

FDE中国社区 · 2026 · 4 min read

Summary

This chapter clarifies how FDEs differ from development, algorithm, pre-sales, implementation, consulting, customer success, and operations roles. Using a case where a successful AI demo led to a signed deal but stalled in production due to intranet systems, permissions, and audit requirements, it argues that FDEs must own an end-to-end loop from pre-sales to production rather than a single function. It lists core FDE responsibilities, four roles an FDE plays during a PoC, a minimal agile team with an AIBP, and five signals for judging whether a role is truly an FDE regardless of title.

Key points

  • FDEs own the cross-boundary loop from pre-sales through production, not one function.
  • A demo-driven sale failed in production due to permissions and audit needs.
  • Core duties span discovery, scoping, architecture, coding, integration, evals, and adoption.
  • Judge FDE roles by responsibility signals, not job titles.
  • A minimal team pairs FDE with AIBP, data engineer, and domain expert.

Chapter Objectives

Understand the differences between FDEs and developers, algorithm engineers, sales engineers, implementation engineers, consultants, customer success managers, and operations consultants; clarify the FDE's scope of responsibility and ways of collaborating.

Why It Matters

FDEs are easily misunderstood as "sales engineers who can code" or "on-site implementation engineers." This misunderstanding leads organizations to place FDEs in the wrong roles: only having them do demos, firefighting, or custom development, without building reusable products or measurable delivery.

Opening Case: Why a "Sales Engineer Who Can Code" Isn't Enough

A SaaS company assigned an engineer to build an AI demo during the pre-sales stage with a major client. The engineer quickly connected the model to the client's sample data, the demo went well, and the deal was signed. But once the project entered implementation, problems emerged: the client actually needed to integrate with an intranet system, the knowledge base had multi-level permissions, the approval workflow spanned three departments, and the "automatic report generation" in the demo required human confirmation and audit logs in production.

If the organization treats this person as a sales engineer, their goal is "a successful demo"; if treated as a traditional implementation engineer, their goal is "launching according to requirements." The FDE's goal is different: they must keep asking the same question across pre-sales, PoC, Beta, and production: Has this capability truly entered the business process and produced measurable results?

This is also the core difference between FDEs and traditional roles: they don't stand within a single functional boundary, but take responsibility for cross-boundary closed loops.

Role Boundaries

RoleTypical FocusHow the FDE Differs
DeveloperImplementing features per requirementsThe FDE participates in defining what's worth building and is accountable for business adoption
Algorithm EngineerModel metrics and training performanceThe FDE focuses on business outcomes, system integration, and operability
Sales EngineerPre-deal demos and solution presentationsThe FDE owns the delivery loop from prototype to production
Implementation EngineerConfiguration, deployment, launchThe FDE co-creates requirements, designs architecture, and feeds capabilities back
ConsultantReports, diagnostics, recommendationsThe FDE delivers working systems and evaluated results
Customer SuccessUsage, renewals, satisfactionThe FDE focuses on whether the system's capabilities truly solve the problem
Operations ConsultantProcess optimization and strategy recommendationsThe FDE turns strategy into data, tools, permissions, and system actions

Core Responsibilities of the FDE

  • Discover high-value business processes.
  • Define technical scope and launch boundaries.
  • Design the AI system architecture.
  • Write or review production code.
  • Integrate client data, tools, and permissions.
  • Establish evals and observability mechanisms.
  • Drive user adoption and business retrospectives.
  • Feed on-site client experience back into the product roadmap.

Relationship with the Applied AI Engineer

The Applied AI Engineer emphasizes applied AI engineering skills, while the FDE emphasizes on-site client work and end-to-end delivery. The two overlap heavily at many companies.

The criterion isn't the job title, but whether the responsibilities cover discovery, scoping, system design, build, rollout, and eval-driven feedback.

The FDE's Four Roles (Working Mindset)

Within a PoC cycle, the FDE often plays four roles simultaneously—one person can wear multiple hats, or they can be divided within a small team:

RoleCore QuestionTypical Outputs
Business ConsultantWhich processes are worth changing, what counts as successInterview notes, JTBD, scenario cards
Architecture OperatorWhat architecture to use, how to integrateArchitecture diagrams, tool lists, permission schemes
AI TamerHow to make the model stable and usablePrompts, RAG, eval samples, failure taxonomies
Monitoring OperatorHow to launch, observe, roll backTraces, alerts, launch gates, operations manuals

Together, the four roles form a closed loop of "understanding the business, building systems, optimizing performance, and operating in production."

The FDE + AIBP Agile Deployment Team

AI projects shouldn't be built by a lone hero. The recommended minimal agile team:

RoleResponsibilities
AIBP (AI Business Partner)Define what a good business outcome is, Ground Truth, and acceptance criteria
FDEDelivery of intelligent capabilities, integration, evals, demos, and rollout
Data EngineerData supply, ETL, permissions, and quality
Frontline Business ExpertReal samples, edge cases, adoption judgment

Scenario cards are the shared agreement between the FDE and the AIBP; without an AIBP, the FDE lacks Ground Truth; without an FDE, business ideas stall at the requirements document.

Diagnostic Questions for Role Boundaries

To judge whether a role is truly an FDE, don't just look at the title—check whether the JD or project responsibilities include the following signals:

SignalIf PresentIf Absent
Participates in client discoveryNeeds to understand real processesMay just be delivery development
Can write or review codeNeeds to personally build the minimal pipelineMay lean toward solutions/consulting
Owns launch adoptionCares whether users actually use itMay only be responsible for demos
Builds eval samplesCan prove effectivenessMay rely only on subjective acceptance
Feeds back into the productDistills field experience into platform capabilitiesRisks becoming one-off outsourcing

Using this table to assess a role is more reliable than agonizing over names like "FDE, Applied AI Engineer, AI Solutions Engineer."

Hands-On Exercise

List the roles in AI projects at your organization or a target company, and note:

  • Who is responsible for discovering requirements.
  • Who is responsible for system design.
  • Who is responsible for writing code.
  • Who is responsible for acceptance metrics.
  • Who is responsible for user adoption.
  • Who is responsible for post-launch issues.

Deliverables

  • A role boundary map.
  • An FDE collaboration interface table.
  • Three key checkpoints requiring FDE involvement.

Common Pitfalls

  • Treating the FDE as a post-sales firefighting role.
  • Requiring the FDE to do custom development without requiring them to build reusable product capabilities.
  • Holding the FDE accountable for business metrics without granting engineering authority.
  • Leaving on-site client experience in individuals' heads without feeding it back into the product.

Checklist

  • Can you explain the difference between an FDE and a Sales Engineer?
  • Can you explain the difference between an FDE and an Implementation Engineer?
  • Can you explain why an FDE must understand both business and engineering?
  • Can you judge whether a role is truly an FDE?
