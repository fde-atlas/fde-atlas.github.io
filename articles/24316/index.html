You may be hearing the term "MVP development" more often, but many people likely have questions such as "How is it different from a prototype?" or "What development methods are available?"

MVP development is an approach for new business and product development that involves "releasing with minimal functionality and quickly gauging market response." In particular, from 2024 to 2026, the rise of generative AI has dramatically improved the speed and cost efficiency of MVP development, and it is becoming established as a standard method not only for startups but also for new business divisions of large enterprises.

MVP (Minimum Viable Product) translates literally into Japanese as "minimum viable product." What matters here is balancing both "Minimum" and "Viable."

It refers not to a mere "unfinished product" or "non-functional prototype," but to a product with the minimum functionality needed to solve a specific user problem, in a state where it can actually be tested in the market.

MVP development is a development approach that intentionally and systematically builds this MVP and rapidly cycles through market launch → feedback collection → improvement.

TermFull NameMeaning
MVPMinimum Viable ProductMinimum viable product
MVP development—A hypothesis-testing development method using MVPs
Lean Startup—A business development methodology centered on MVPs

The concept of MVP development originates from the methodology proposed by Eric Ries in his 2011 book "The Lean Startup." The Lean Startup applies the philosophy of Toyota's "Lean Production System" to software development and business development, with its central concept being the rapid cycling of the BML (Build-Measure-Learn) loop of "Build → Measure → Learn." Specific design methods for rapidly cycling this BML loop are explained in Practical Guide: Lean Development and MVP | Design and Methods for the Fastest Hypothesis Testing.

In this BML loop, what is built in the first "Build" phase is the MVP. Rather than a complete product, by focusing on the minimum functionality needed to validate the most important hypothesis, you can quickly discover "what the market truly wants" without wasting development effort.

Before the Lean Startup, waterfall-style development typically involved spending months to years building a "finished product" before releasing it, at which point market response was finally confirmed. With this method, even if you noticed a mismatch with user needs, corrections required enormous cost and time. MVP development rapidly spread as an approach that fundamentally solves this problem.

Behind the current attention on MVP development are the following three structural changes.

① Acceleration of market change

With the advancement of digitalization, competitors can now release similar products in short periods. The risk of competitors seizing first-mover advantages while you spend a long time building a perfect product has increased. Getting to market first and iterating through learning has become the source of competitive advantage.

② Lowered barriers to prototyping through the spread of cloud and SaaS

With cloud infrastructure like AWS and GCP, APIs like Stripe and Twilio, and the emergence of no-code tools, an environment now exists where functionality that once took engineering teams weeks to build can be implemented in days.

③ Dramatic changes in MVP development cost and speed brought by generative AI

By incorporating generative AI such as ChatGPT, Gemini, and Claude into MVP development, cases have been reported where development effort is reduced by 30-50% compared to conventional methods. In addition, the category of "AI-native MVPs," which use AI itself as the core functionality of the MVP, is rapidly expanding, and this is putting MVP development back in the spotlight.

The purposes of implementing MVP development can be broadly organized into the following three.

① Hypothesis validation (exploring Product-Market Fit)

The greatest risk in business is "building something nobody wants." In MVP development, hypotheses such as "these users must have this problem" and "this feature should solve it" are validated with actual user behavior data. If the hypothesis is wrong, you can pivot; if the right hypothesis is found, you can scale.

② Minimizing development costs

Compared to traditional development, where a full-featured product is built before release, MVP development minimizes costs by skipping the development of unnecessary features. Startup research shows that 60-80% of product development costs are spent on "features users never actually use," and MVP development fundamentally eliminates this waste.

3. Securing First-Mover Advantage

Another important goal is to enter the market ahead of competitors and gain brand recognition, user data, and know-how first. Especially in niche markets, word-of-mouth and community building from early entry become entry barriers that later entrants cannot overcome.

MVP development is not a silver bullet. It is important to understand it properly before applying it.

Drawback 1: Risk to Brand Image

Because the product is released with minimal features and quality, early users may have a negative experience. Especially for BtoC consumer services, first impressions tend to persist as word-of-mouth and reviews, which can hinder subsequent improvements.

→ Countermeasure: Start with a closed beta or interview-based validation, and make the public release only after ensuring a certain level of quality.

Drawback 2: The Definition of "Minimum" Tends to Be Vague

If the interpretation of "Minimum" varies within the team, unnecessary features end up being added and the MVP stops being an MVP—a phenomenon known as "feature creep."

→ Countermeasure: Narrow the hypotheses to be validated down to one or two, and prepare a "feature scope definition document" in advance that lists only the features directly related to them.

Drawback 3: Accumulation of Technical Debt

If implementation is done with sloppy code in the name of shipping fast, problems can surface later as technical debt when adding features or scaling.

→ Countermeasure: Design the architecture with future expansion in mind even at the MVP stage, and set a certain code quality standard for the core domain.

These three terms are often confused, but they differ in purpose, target, and level of completion. Understanding them accurately allows you to choose the right approach at each phase of a project.

PoC (Proof of Concept) is a validation to confirm "whether something is technically feasible." It is not intended to be delivered to users; it is built for engineers or researchers to verify internally. The pros and cons of PoC and keys to success are explained in detail in What Is PoC? Pros, Cons, and 3 Keys to Success.

For example, when running a PoC to answer the question "Can AI detect anomalies in our manufacturing line data?", you would actually build a machine learning model and measure its accuracy, but there is no need to turn it into a UI that external users can use. It is merely a stage for confirming "whether the technology works."

The biggest difference from MVP is that PoC aims at technical validation and does not assume delivering value to users. MVP aims to validate business hypotheses and must be a minimal product that real users find valuable.

A prototype is "a mock-up for checking a product's form, behavior, and UX." It is mainly used to validate design and UI; it does not need to process real data, and its main purpose is to reproduce appearance and feel.

Typical examples include a UI prototype made in Figma or a 3D-printed model for checking the appearance of hardware.

The biggest difference from MVP is that a prototype aims to "confirm how it would work" and is not intended to be used by real users in a production environment. An MVP is actually used by users, and business hypotheses are validated from their behavior data.

Agile development is a "process methodology" that advances development through repeated short iterations (sprints). Its purpose differs from MVP development, but they are highly compatible and are often used together.

Agile development = the process and approach of development (short iterations) MVP development = the strategy of what to build and where to start (minimal hypothesis validation)

The most common combination is to release with MVP development and then iterate improvements using Agile while incorporating feedback.

MVP development offers several approaches depending on the nature of the product and the hypotheses to be validated. Here we explain six representative approaches along with real-world examples of each.

The most common MVP approach: release a working prototype implementing only the core features. The UI can be rough, but the features must be at a level users can actually use.

Characteristics

  • Actual usability and UX can be validated
  • Technical feasibility can also be confirmed at the same time
  • Easy to obtain concrete feedback from users

Representative success story: Dropbox In its early MVP development, Dropbox released a 3-minute demo video showing "how files are automatically synced" before building an actual file synchronization system (combined with the demo video MVP described later). It then released a prototype MVP with actual sync functionality, acquiring thousands of beta users on the first day. At this stage, the supported OS was Mac only and sharing features were limited, but this was sufficient to validate the core hypothesis of "solving the file synchronization problem."

This is a method of validating "whether demand exists" by having humans manually provide the service without using any digital systems or automation. The service appears normal to users, while the backend is handled by humans.

  • Development cost is nearly zero
  • You can directly hear what users truly want
  • You can deeply understand business workflows before automating them

Representative success story: Food on the Table This is a service that provides users with weekly meal plans and the necessary grocery lists. Initially, without using technology, the founder manually created meal plans for a single user each week, checking supermarket flyers to build the lists, offering the service in a concierge style. Through this manual validation, they deeply understood user needs and later moved to systematization.

Before building the actual product, this method involves creating only a landing page, running ads, and measuring user responses (click rate and registration rate). It allows you to validate the most fundamental hypothesis—"does demand exist?"—fastest and at the lowest cost.

  • Development effort is nearly unnecessary (only LP creation)
  • Demand can be measured quantitatively with numbers
  • Multiple patterns of target audience, messaging, and price ranges can be A/B tested

Validation process

  • Create an LP with a tentative service name, catchphrase, and benefits
  • Reach target users via Google Ads or social media ads
  • Measure the click rate and registration rate of CTAs such as "pre-registration" or "waitlist signup"
  • If sufficient demand is confirmed, proceed to development (or revise the messaging and retest)

This is a method where the system appears to be operating automatically on the surface, while in reality humans are manually operating and making decisions behind the scenes. The name comes from the movie "The Wizard of Oz," from the scene where a real person is behind the curtain.

  • You can validate demand before implementing AI or complex algorithms
  • You can minimize development costs while faithfully reproducing the user experience
  • By exploring "where human involvement is needed," the priorities for automation become clear

Representative success story: Zappos Zappos, now known as one of the world's largest online shoe retailers, initially operated without holding any inventory. When a user ordered shoes, the founder would actually purchase them at a nearby shoe store and ship them—a fully manual operation. Through this Wizard of Oz-style MVP, they validated that the behavior of "buying shoes online" would actually occur, and later built a formal e-commerce system.

Rather than developing a system from scratch, this method builds a service by combining existing SaaS tools and APIs. By leveraging tools such as Airtable, Notion, Zapier, and Slack, you can validate the service's "mechanism" with almost no development cost.

  • Minimal development effort (often achievable with no-code or low-code)
  • You can leverage the reliability and stability of existing tools
  • You can focus validation on the product's core value

Use cases

  • Reservation management: Accept bookings with Calendly, manage data with Notion
  • E-commerce: Payments combining Shopify and Stripe
  • Internal tools: Build a database with Airtable, automate with Zapier, send notifications via Slack

At a stage when the actual product does not yet exist, this method involves creating a demo video showing "how the product works" to validate demand. It has strong visual appeal and can gather reactions from prospective users in a short period.

Representative success story: Dropbox (revisited) As mentioned earlier, Dropbox released a simple 3-minute screencast. The video demonstrated "what it would be able to do" and directed viewers to a pre-registration form; immediately after release, the waitlist reached 75,000 signups. This became the basis for the decision to invest in actual development.

The rapid spread of generative AI (LLMs) since 2023 is fundamentally changing the nature of MVP development. In particular, the following three points are cited as practical observations from development teams.

1. Dramatically faster development

By using AI coding assistants such as GitHub Copilot and Cursor, you can significantly reduce the time spent writing routine code. The effects are especially notable in CRUD API implementation, automatic generation of test code, and documentation generation. It is not uncommon for a single engineer to complete initial implementation that previously took two weeks in under one week.

2. The "manual validation" phase can be replaced with AI

Traditional concierge and Wizard of Oz MVPs assumed that "humans would manually perform operations." However, by leveraging generative AI, tasks requiring natural language processing (document summarization, recommendations, Q&A responses, etc.) can be handled by AI, allowing services to be provided in an almost fully automated state. This has made it possible to validate hypotheses at a scale far beyond the cost and limitations of manual validation.

3. AI itself has become the core feature (AI-native MVP)

Instead of "using generative AI to make existing systems more efficient," products are rapidly increasing where "the AI's reasoning capability itself is the core value of the service." This is the area known as "AI-native MVPs," with representative examples including natural language interfaces built on the ChatGPT API or Anthropic API, document search using RAG (Retrieval Augmented Generation), and creative support tools using image generation AI.

Pattern 1: Leveraging the ChatGPT API — Natural Language Interface MVP

This is a product where the user enters questions or instructions as text, and the AI provides appropriate responses or processing. At the MVP stage, it can be implemented with just a combination of prompt engineering and the API, so you can build a "working product" without writing almost any complex backend logic.

Concrete examples:

  • AI legal document summarization tool (SaaS for law firms)
  • First-line customer support chatbot
  • Automated screening tool for recruitment documents

Pattern 2: Document Search MVP Using RAG (Retrieval Augmented Generation)

In this configuration, a company's internal documents (manuals, meeting minutes, policy collections, etc.) are stored in a vector database, and the AI answers user questions by searching and referencing relevant documents. Using frameworks such as LangChain or LlamaIndex, MVP-stage implementation can be achieved in a few days to about a week.

  • AI search system for internal policies and FAQs
  • AI support desk for product manuals
  • Information extraction tool from past proposals and contracts

Pattern 3: Leveraging Image Generation AI — Creative Support MVP

This provides a feature that generates images from user text input by incorporating APIs such as Stable Diffusion or DALL-E. Its strength is that visual content can be generated even without a designer, and demand is growing in the advertising, e-commerce, and publishing sectors.

  • Automatic product image generation tool for e-commerce sites
  • Automatic banner generation service for social media posts
  • Automatic architectural and interior perspective rendering tool

Pattern 4: Leveraging Multimodal AI — Image and Voice Input MVP

This is a product where the AI processes not only text but also images and voice as input. By leveraging multimodal models such as GPT-4 Vision or Gemini Pro, you can realize a UX of "just take a photo to get a diagnosis or analysis."

  • For manufacturing: AI inspection tool that detects equipment defects just by taking photos of the equipment
  • For food service: chef assistant that suggests recipes from photos of ingredients
  • For healthcare: screening tool that indicates the priority for a dermatology visit from photos of skin (under physician supervision)

Case 1: Notion AI (Notion)

Notion released its AI writing assistant feature as an MVP in limited form in early 2023. It rolled out gradually to existing users via a waitlist approach, expanding features while collecting feedback. The MVP, centered on AI-powered text generation, summarization, and translation, was well received and led to the subsequent official release.

Case 2: Cursor (AI Code Editor)

This is an editor based on VS Code in which AI suggests code autocompletion, refactoring, and bug fixes. Its initial MVP had only a simple code completion feature, but features were expanded after confirming strong demand from engineers. It is now growing rapidly as a tool that lets you develop with AI in a "pair programming" style.

Case 3: Domestic SaaS Example: AI Contract Review Service

In Japan's LegalTech space, multiple SaaS products for contract review using the ChatGPT API have emerged. The initial MVP had a simple configuration of "upload a PDF → GPT analyzes the clauses → risk areas are highlighted," but it directly addressed the pain points of legal staff, and many corporate customers signed paid contracts even at the beta stage.

The first step in MVP development is narrowing down "what you want to validate" to one or two things. Skipping this step will cause every subsequent stage to drift off course.

It is recommended to document your hypothesis in the following format.

"Because [target users] have [a problem or situation], providing [a solution] should enable [a behavior or outcome]."

Example: "Because legal staff at small and medium-sized businesses struggle with contract reviews taking too much time, providing an AI contract review tool should reduce review time by 50%."

It is important to quantitatively define "which metrics must change for the hypothesis to be considered proven."

Once the hypothesis is set, classify features into those "absolutely necessary to validate the hypothesis" and those "that can be validated without."

Feature Classification Criteria (MoSCoW Method)

ClassificationMeaningTreatment at the MVP Stage
Must HaveFeatures essential to hypothesis validationAlways implement
Should HaveNice to have but not essentialKeep out of scope
Could HaveImplement if there is roomKeep out of scope
Won't HaveNot needed in this versionExplicitly exclude

Build the MVP based on the selected approach (the six approaches from the previous chapter). The key principle at this stage is "don't aim for perfection."

MVP Build Checklist

  • Does the core functionality actually work?
  • Can a user complete the flow on their own?
  • Are there any critical bugs or security risks?
  • Are data collection and measurement mechanisms in place (Google Analytics, Mixpanel, etc.)?
  • Is a feedback collection path designed?

After the MVP is released, "measurement" and "learning" are the most important phases. Decide the next actions based on data, not gut feeling.

Key Metrics to Measure at the MVP Stage

MetricDescriptionExample Measurement Tools
Activation ratePercentage of users who use the core feature for the first timeMixpanel, Amplitude
Retention ratePercentage of users who return after a certain periodMixpanel, Amplitude
NPS (Net Promoter Score)Rating of "Would you recommend it to a friend?" on a 0-10 scaleTypeform, Delighted
Conversion rateConversion rate such as free-to-paidStripe, in-house DB
Churn ratePercentage of users who stopped using the serviceChartmogul

Alongside quantitative data, it is important to also collect qualitative data through user interviews. This is because the reasons behind "why users behaved that way," which numbers cannot reveal, often become the core of the next improvement.

Based on the collected data, decide whether to "continue improving as is (iterate)," "change direction (pivot)," or "move on to full-scale development and marketing (scale)."

Guidelines for Deciding to Move to Scale

  • Retention rate exceeds a certain level (for B2C, weekly retention above 20% is one guideline)
  • NPS for the core feature is 40 or higher
  • Multiple users have responded with "I would pay to use this"
  • Unit economics (LTV/CAC) are expected to be viable

Failure Pattern 1: Underestimating the "M" and Overemphasizing the "V"

This is a pattern where, despite intending to stay "Minimum," features keep getting added until it becomes a "full-featured product." Scope locking and regularly asking "Is this feature really necessary for hypothesis validation?" are important.

Failure Pattern 2: Repeating Improvements Without Validating Hypotheses

This is a pattern where, by continuously responding to user requests of "I want more ○○," "responding to customer requests" becomes the goal instead of hypothesis validation. It is important to prioritize all improvement requests based on data and prioritize those directly tied to the core hypothesis.

Failure Pattern 3: Postponing the Measurement Setup

This is a pattern where, prioritizing "release as soon as possible" and postponing analytics tool setup, no data can be collected after release, forcing decisions based on "gut feeling." Treat implementing Google Analytics or Mixpanel as a mandatory requirement before release.

Let us summarize the key points once again.

  • MVP (Minimum Viable Product) is a "minimum viable product" and a strategic approach for validating business hypotheses as quickly and cost-effectively as possible.
  • It is important to understand the differences in roles—PoC for technical validation, prototypes for UX confirmation, and MVP for market validation—and to choose the appropriate method for each phase.
  • The six MVP methods (prototype, concierge, smoke test, Wizard of Oz, piecemeal, and demo video) should be chosen based on the hypothesis you want to validate.
  • With the advent of generative AI, MVP development speed and cost efficiency have improved dramatically, and a new domain called "AI-native MVPs," where AI itself is the core feature, is rapidly expanding.
  • Engineers and PMs who can handle MVP and AI development are in a position to easily win high-rate projects in the freelance market.

MVP development skills are not merely about learning a development method; they form the core of the "ability to build a business from scratch."
