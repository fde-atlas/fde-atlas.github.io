FDE Observation Methods | Capturing "Undocumented Procedures" through Shadowing and Contextual Inquiry

Original title: FDEの観察法｜シャドーイングと文脈的質問法で「説明されない手順」を拾う

TIMEWELL · 2026-09-15 · 14 min read

Summary

In this TIMEWELL practice article, Hamamoto argues that interviews capture only half of frontline work; the rest consists of undocumented procedures—duplicate data entry, pre-input phone confirmations, printed-paper approvals—that workers cannot articulate because they consider them trivial. He details three observation methods used in sequence: shadowing on day one to map the workflow, contextual inquiry from day two to probe reasons and judgment criteria, and think-aloud testing after a working prototype is delivered. Practical guidance covers seating diagonally behind the worker, recording five columns (time, action, screen, speech, anomalies), and mitigating the Hawthorne effect. He contrasts FDE observation with commissioned As-Is analysis: the observer is also the builder, so a 20-minute approval wait seen Friday becomes a working prototype Monday. Citing a former Palantir FDE and Taiichi Ohno's chalk circle, he also admits limits: observation is time-consuming, day-specific, and requires consent and confidentiality agreements.

Key points

  • Interviews miss undocumented procedures; observation is required to capture them.
  • Three staged methods: shadowing, contextual inquiry, and think-aloud after delivery.
  • Record five columns—time, action, screen, speech, anomalies—to spot gaps.
  • Unlike commissioned As-Is analysis, the FDE who observes also builds, continuously.
  • Limits include time cost, Hawthorne effect, and consent/confidentiality constraints.

Hello, this is Hamamoto from TIMEWELL Inc.

This is the second installment in our FDE practice series. In the previous article on interview skills, I wrote that "interviews are the opening act of observation." This time, I'll talk about the main event: observation.

If listening on-site were enough, engineers wouldn't need to keep sitting in factories and offices. The reason we keep sitting there is that there are things that don't come out even when you ask. Procedures that the person in charge does every day but can't recall when asked. These are the exception rules written on sticky notes, the hidden columns in Excel, and the extra step of confirming by phone before entering data into the system. In this article, I'll describe observation methods for picking up such "undocumented procedures," in the order our engineers use them in the field. If you want to check first how well your own team can see what's happening on the ground, please use our AI literacy diagnostic.

Listening alone gets you half. The "undocumented procedures" that observation picks up

Why isn't listening alone enough? The reason is that people cannot accurately describe their own behavior. This is not laziness on the part of the person in charge; it's a property of memory. In user research practice, the value of observing actual behavior in the field is repeatedly emphasized, and the basis for this is that what people say they do often diverges from what they actually do 1.

A person who served as an FDE at Palantir for eight years expressed the fundamental idea behind this role in one phrase: "context is the scarce resource." By going to the customer's site, you go to capture tacit knowledge about how people actually work, not the flat form of a list of requirements, he writes 2. At the aircraft manufacturer where he was first assigned, he commuted to the factory for a year and spent four days a week on the manufacturing floor. The reason the engineer spent a year in the factory was not to listen, but to see.

There are even older examples. Taiichi Ohno, who created the Toyota Production System, is said to have drawn a circle in chalk on the factory floor, made managers stand inside it, and had them watch the work for hours. He would ask "What did you see?" and if they hadn't seen anything, he would say "Keep watching" 3. Our engineers don't draw chalk circles in the field, but what they do is the same. They sit diagonally behind the worker, don't interject, and watch the same work over and over.

I divide what can be picked up through observation into four categories: the order of procedures, the waits and movements between procedures, the confirmations outside the procedures, and the decisions to skip procedures. Interviews only surface the first one; the remaining three can't be known without watching.

Three observation methods: shadowing, contextual inquiry, and think-aloud

Even if we call them all observation methods, what you can pick up differs depending on how you do it. Here is a table of the three methods we use and distinguish between.

MethodWhat you doWhat you can pick upWhen to use it
ShadowingFollow behind the person in charge and observe a whole day without interjectingThe overall picture of the work, movements and waits, confirmations that don't come up in explanationsDay one
Contextual inquirySit in on the work and ask questions on the spot while watching the workThe reasons for procedures, the criteria for decisions, how exceptions are handledDay two onward
Think-aloudHave the person speak aloud what they're thinking while workingWhere on the screen they're looking and what they're hesitating aboutAfter putting something working in place

Shadowing is, literally, a method of following behind like a shadow. It's used on day one. The goal is to build a map, so you don't interject. If the person in charge gets up and goes to the neighboring department, you go along. If they make a phone call, afterward you ask just one question—"What was that confirmation for?"—and don't dig any deeper. Digging starts on day two. What matters in shadowing is recording the movements and waits. "20 minutes waiting for approval," "carrying a printed sheet to another floor." This time never comes up in the person's explanations. For them, it doesn't count as work. Yet what the FDE should build first is usually found within this time.

Contextual inquiry, when rendered in Japanese, is "contextual questioning," and it has been systematized as a user research method. Its distinguishing feature is going to the place of work and asking questions on the spot while watching the work being done. It's often compared to the relationship between a master and an apprentice. The apprentice watches the master's work from beside them and asks, "Why did you do that just now?" The master answers without stopping the work. When you establish this relationship, the person in charge becomes the "teacher" and shows you the actual procedures, not the ideal ones 4. This is the method our engineers use from day two onward; you can think of it as the deep-dive interviews I wrote about in the previous article, conducted at the place of work rather than in a meeting room.

Think-aloud is a method of having people speak aloud what they're thinking while working, and it's known as a fundamental technique in usability research 5. In FDE practice, it's used after you've put something working in place. "Try doing your usual task using this screen. Say out loud whatever comes to mind." You can see where on the screen the person stalls, what they search for, and where they say "huh?" It's a way of capturing the "what's different?" from the validation interviews I wrote about last time, not through words but through actions.

What the three methods have in common is that they're done at the place of work, not in a meeting room, and that they don't take up much of the person's time. Shadowing barely uses the person's time at all. Contextual inquiry accumulates dozens of seconds between tasks. Think-aloud needs only ten minutes. As I wrote last time, the people on the ground don't have sixty minutes.

FDE observation is "observation for building." How it differs from contracted current-state analysis

Here, let me make the difference from contracted development clear. Contracted development also has current-state analysis (As-Is analysis). You draw business flow diagrams, compile a list of issues, and put it into a proposal. Looking only at the actions, it's the same as observation. The difference lies in what you do after observing.

PerspectiveContracted development current-state analysisFDE observation
PurposeTo create the basis for proposals and estimatesTo decide the next "working thing" to put in place the following day
DeliverablesBusiness flow diagrams, issue lists, proposalsAn exceptions notebook and the next version of the working thing
DurationA bounded research phase; once done, move to designContinues in parallel with implementation; it never ends
Handling of observed exceptionsAgreed upon as requirements and included in the estimateBuilt on the spot; if it's a pattern, it goes into the product; if it's a branch, it's managed as a branch
Who observesThe research staff (usually not the implementers)The engineer who will do the implementation themselves

The current-state investigation in commissioned work is done for later phases. That is why the person investigating and the person implementing can be different. An FDE's observation is done to decide what to put in front of the customer the next day. That is why the person watching and the person building must be the same person. The "20 minutes waiting for approval" found during observation on Friday becomes something working, placed in front of the person in charge, on Monday. This round trip is possible because the engineer who observed is the one who builds. In a January 2026 essay, a16z cites "whether hours are decreasing at mature customers" as a test for identifying the FDE archetype 6, but if observation feeds directly into implementation, the same kind of observation becomes unnecessary at the next customer. At the next customer, the product already knows those procedures.

Another difference is how observed exceptions are handled. In commissioned work, exceptions are agreed as requirements, put into the estimate, and fixed in the contract. In FDE work, exceptions are built on the spot. Then, once a week, you sort whether each exception is a branch unique to this company or a pattern that exists at other companies too. Patterns go back into the product as standard features; branches are recorded as branches. A former Palantir FDE wrote that the FDE's job is "to solve that problem, without worrying about generalization; generalization is the product team's job" 2, but since we are a small company, the engineer who observes also writes the product. The sorting decision is made weekly, by the same person.

On-site procedure: where to sit, what to watch, what to write

I will write this concretely. This is the procedure our engineers follow when observing on site.

Where to sit is diagonally behind the person in charge. Directly behind feels oppressive, and beside them you cannot see the screen. From diagonally behind, you can see the screen, the papers in their hands, and their facial expressions all at once. The first thing to ask on day one is a single sentence: "Please show me your usual work, done the way you always do it, while I sit beside you." Before they show you, tell them that you have come to learn, not to evaluate, and that what you see will be returned to them as something working.

What to watch is four things: the screen, the hands, the paper, and movement. For the screen: which system and which screen, the order of switching, the copy-and-paste actions. For the hands: what they touch besides the keyboard and mouse—calculator, sticky notes, printouts. For paper: the forms on the desk and in the drawers, and the notes written on them. For movement: the reason for leaving the seat and where they go. Of these four, interviews surface only part of the screen.

What to write is five columns: time, action, screen, utterance, and discrepancy. Time is recorded by the minute, so that later you can see the length of waits and movements. Actions are written as verbs, like "opens the approval screen." Screens are recorded as system name and screen name. Utterances are the person's words exactly as spoken. In the discrepancy column, you write the gap between what you were told and what you saw, like "they said section-chief approval, but the name of an assistant manager is in there." This fifth column is the candidate list for what to build the next day. The eye for noticing drift, which I described in the previous article as "not something you gain from training," was developed by writing this column every day.

The trouble with observation is that people's behavior changes when they are being watched. This is a phenomenon called the Hawthorne effect; its very existence and magnitude are still debated in research, but the fact that behavior can change under observation is considered something to account for in research design 7. We handle it in three ways. Tell them on day one that this is not an evaluation. Watch the same work again on another day. And return the results of the observation to them the next day as something working. The third is the most effective. When people see their own work turned into something working, they stop showing you work performed for the observer and start showing you their real work.

Whether to interject is decided by the method. In shadowing, you do not interject. In the contextual inquiry method, you do—but you must not make them stop working. Questions are short, at breaks in the work, ending with "Who did you check with just now?" and if the answer looks like it will run long, you cut it off with "Please tell me later." Breaks and lunch are made time for casual conversation, not observation. The remark that comes out in casual conversation—"actually, nobody ever looks at that form"—cannot be captured by any observation method.

Four patterns frequently seen on site

For confidentiality reasons I cannot write about individual cases, but I will generalize and list four patterns our engineers have repeatedly seen on site. None of them come out in interviews with the person in charge; all were found for the first time through observation.

The first is double entry. Content entered into the core system is entered again into a separate Excel file. When you ask why, the answer is that Excel is easier for aggregation, or that it is a habit from the previous system. The person does not think of this as "work," so it never comes up in explanations.

The second is pre-entry confirmation. Before entering data into the system, they confirm with the person at the next desk or by phone. If the person they confirm with is off, entry stops for the day. The content of this confirmation is the decision criteria that are not written in the system.

The third is printing and carrying. An approval that should be completed on screen is printed on paper, carried to another floor, stamped, and brought back. The approval screen exists but is not used. When you ask why, the answer is that the approvers do not look at screens.

The fourth is the decision to skip. The procedure manual includes a verification step, but experienced staff skip it. They know from experience that skipping causes no problems. This judgment is written neither in the procedure manual nor in any documentation, but when that person is off, a newcomer follows the manual and it takes longer.

What the four have in common is that they are too obvious to the person in charge to put into words. And the first thing an FDE should build is usually one of these four. Eliminate the double entry, surface the confirmation criteria on screen, put the approval where the approver will see it, make explicit the conditions under which skipping is allowed. None of these is a big feature, but each changes the person's day.

Our practice and its limits: observation takes time

I will write this honestly. Observation takes time. Shadowing takes a day, contextual inquiry several days, and watching the same work twice takes several more. Resistance to spending engineers' time on site is only natural. For an engineer who wants to build something working, a day spent sitting and building nothing feels long. Even so, we keep doing it because one day of observation eliminates a week of rework afterward. What is built without watching gets the response "this won't work in our workplace" the moment it is placed in front of the person in charge. What is built after watching gets only "this part is different."

There are limits, too. Observation covers only the work of the people observed, on the days observed. Processes that happen only at month-end, the annual closing, the backup procedures for when that person is off—these cannot be observed. That is why we combine observation with the interviews from the previous article. About the procedures you saw, you ask "do you do this every day, or is month-end different?" and about procedures you could not see, you ask about them as events.

Confidentiality and consent also have their limits. Observation means watching how the person in charge does their work, so it requires the approval of both the individual and their manager. At our company, before entering the field, we hold an initial meeting to decide whose work we will observe, when, for what purpose, and how the records will be handled. We commit in advance to never writing anything in the records that could lead to personal evaluations, and to using the records solely for building a working artifact. This commitment becomes even more important in the video recording and AI analysis covered in the next article.

Summary

Interviews can only reveal half of the work on the ground. The other half lies in procedures that the people in charge cannot explain, and can only be seen. There are three observation methods: on the first day, create a map through shadowing; from the second day onward, dig into reasons and criteria with the contextual inquiry method; and after a working artifact is in place, capture actions with think-aloud protocols. Where you sit is diagonally behind; what you watch is the screen, hands, paper, and movement; and what you write down is a five-column table of time, action, screen, utterances, and anomalies.

What distinguishes FDE observation from contracted research is that the person who observes is the one who builds. The "20 minutes waiting for approval" found on Friday becomes a working artifact on Monday. Because of this back-and-forth, observation is not a research phase but part of the implementation.
