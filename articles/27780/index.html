Forward Deployed Infrastructure Engineer at Palantir

FDE Times · 2026-11-04 · 6 min read

Summary

The article examines Palantir's Forward Deployed Infrastructure Engineer role, including a new grad track. In air-gapped customer environments, clusters cannot pull images from public registries or send telemetry back to the vendor, so the on-site engineer effectively becomes the operations team. The author argues this is more than renamed DevOps: Palantir requires strong coding in Java, Go, Python or JavaScript, while Ultimate Knowledge's posting mandates Kubernetes, CNI familiarity (Calico, Cilium, Multus), GitOps, and air-gapped delivery experience. Practical advice includes pre-rendering Helm charts to verify all images and building reproducible offline lab projects to demonstrate readiness.

When a vendor cannot see inside a customer's cluster, the engineer on site becomes the operations team, and Palantir's infrastructure FDE role is open to new graduates.

In brief

  • Palantir has a dedicated Forward Deployed Infrastructure Engineer title, including a new grad version, with duties that cover deployments, migrations and on-call at customer sites.
  • In an air-gapped environment the cluster cannot pull images from public registries or send telemetry back to the vendor, so the engineer on site is the operations team.
  • This is more than DevOps with a new name: Palantir demands strong coding skills, and Ultimate Knowledge's FDE posting asks for Kubernetes, CNI, GitOps and experience delivering software into air-gapped environments.

Palantir does not put all of its FDEs under a single title. Its careers page lists a role called “Forward Deployed Infrastructure Engineer”, and the job description is blunt: you will develop software and also be responsible for the reliability and operation of software systems. There is a new grad version of the role as well.

That detail matters because the popular image of an FDE today is someone who builds agents, wires up data and sits with the customer. Engineers whose strengths are Kubernetes, Terraform or networking may never have considered FDE work a path open to them.

The job postings suggest otherwise. There is a branch of FDE work in which infrastructure skills are the main thing the customer needs, not a sideline. It exists for a specific reason: some customers need software to run in places the vendor cannot reach.

The customer’s environment creates the role

Documentation for Apollo, Palantir’s deployment platform, says it supports every major cloud environment, on-premises installations and disconnected (air-gapped) environments. A community-compiled FDE roadmap on GitHub describes the infrastructure FDEs typically meet as “hostile, legacy, air-gapped, or hybrid”.

On an isolated cluster, even the most ordinary things are missing. CoreWeave’s air-gapped deployment documentation warns that the cluster cannot pull images from public registries, so every container image it needs must already be in the customer’s internal registry before deployment.

The second problem is the one that changes the nature of the job. TrueFoundry, describing how LLMs are brought into heavily regulated industries, says the common approach is to install with Helm into a Kubernetes cluster the customer owns, turn off automatic updates, and send no telemetry back to the vendor’s control plane.

Without telemetry, the SRE team at headquarters sees nothing: no dashboards, no alerts, no logs. When a pod dies at two in the morning, the only person who can look at the system is whoever has access on the customer’s side. That is why the title exists: the engineer on site is also the operations team.

An installation without internet

Take a hypothetical case: you bring a Helm chart to the cluster of a financial services customer. The chart references nine images: seven for the application, one for a log-collecting sidecar and one for an init container that runs migrations. You have mirrored the seven main images to the internal registry but missed the other two, because they sit in a subchart.

On a cluster with internet access, almost nobody would notice, because the node pulls the images itself. In an air-gapped environment, the pods get stuck in ImagePullBackOff. To fix it you have to find a way to get those two images into the internal registry, on a cluster with no route to the internet.

The lesson is about working habits more than Kubernetes. Before arriving at the customer, render the entire chart, extract the list of every referenced image, and write a script that checks that list against the target registry.

This is the kind of “runbook automation” Palantir lists in its job description, alongside deploying new products across multiple production environments.

More than DevOps with a new name

Ultimate Knowledge’s FDE posting on Workable states plainly that you will work across both cloud and on-prem, and that this is not a traditional DevOps or IT role. The same posting lists Kubernetes as mandatory.

The two statements sound contradictory but are not. A DevOps engineer usually looks after their own company’s platform, where they choose the tools and have full access. A forward deployed infrastructure engineer carries one product through many environments that belong to someone else, each with its own configuration and its own rules.

That is why Palantir puts its coding requirement on the same level as the US government clearance requirement: you must be a strong programmer, proficient in languages such as Java, Go, Python or JavaScript. Running a runbook by hand across ten different environments will produce errors sooner or later. Turning it into a tool is a software engineer’s job.

Set the postings side by side and the shape of the role becomes clear, along with what each requirement turns into on a customer site.

Requirement in the posting Where it appears What it means on site
Strong coding in Java, Go, Python or JavaScript Palantir Turning runbooks into repeatable tools
Deploying products across multiple production environments Palantir One release has to run on many different configurations
Migrations to the latest infrastructure, on-call Palantir, new grad version Upgrading live systems and handling incidents
Kubernetes is mandatory Ultimate Knowledge The product runs on the customer’s cluster, not yours
Familiarity with CNIs such as Calico, Cilium, Multus Ultimate Knowledge Working with whatever network layer the customer chose
GitOps, ArgoCD preferred, Flux accepted Ultimate Knowledge System state lives in a repo, not in someone’s memory
Experience delivering software into air-gapped or heavily regulated environments Ultimate Knowledge Preparing images, charts and file-transfer processes in advance

Networking separates the strong candidates

In the table above, the CNI line deserves the closest reading. Ultimate Knowledge’s posting does not pick one plugin; it names all three: Calico, Cilium and Multus. The reasonable reading is that the choice is not yours. The cluster belongs to the customer, and the network layer was decided before you arrived.

So when one service cannot reach another, you need to tell whether the fault lies in the application, in a network policy or in the way the customer’s CNI handles traffic. Someone who only knows a default configuration gets stuck right there. Someone who understands networking can isolate the fault before the shift ends.

GitOps plays a similar role. Say you leave the project and a colleague takes over. With no telemetry coming back, keeping the system’s desired state in a repo and letting ArgoCD or Flux sync it means the next person can understand the environment without guessing.

New grads can get in, with proof

Palantir’s new grad version does not shrink the scope of the work. The duties are still deploying new products at customer deployments, carrying out migrations to the latest infrastructure and taking part in on-call. Even recent graduates are expected to work directly on real production systems.

The way to show you can do that is a lab project that others can reproduce, which counts for more than another certificate. Build a cluster, block its route to the internet, mirror images into an internal registry, install a real chart with Helm, manage it with ArgoCD, and switch the CNI to Cilium to see how network policies behave differently.

On a CV, write about outcomes rather than listing tools. A line such as “installed a nine-image system into a cluster with no internet access, with a pre-deployment check script” carries far more weight than one that just reads “Kubernetes, Helm, ArgoCD”.

When reading job postings, look for the words air-gapped, on-call and migrations. They signal the infrastructure branch of the work, as opposed to the demo-building branch.

Many infrastructure engineers have heard of FDE work as a job that requires learning a whole other skill set. In places without internet access, infrastructure skills turn out to be what customers need most.
