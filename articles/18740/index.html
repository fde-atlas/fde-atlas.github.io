AI-Native Services vs IT Outsourcing: What Differs

MarsLab · 2026-11-01 · 7 min read

Summary

MarsLab contrasts AI-native services firms with traditional IT outsourcing: the former sells a scoped outcome, fields a small senior team, and shares outcome risk, while the latter sells hours and capacity under time-and-materials billing, which US federal rules (FAR 16.601) note gives no incentive for cost control. Citing Sequoia Capital's claim that services spend is six times software spend, the piece argues AI makes outcome-based pricing viable. Case studies include StudyBuddy (seven-department ERP) and Sanjeevani Group (sugar recovery lifted from 8.0–8.3% toward 9.0–9.5%), with a standard eight-week engagement. Outsourcing still suits stable maintenance, large staff augmentation, and commodity run work. Ownership of code and models depends on the contract and should be secured in writing.

Key points

  • Outsourcing sells hours; AI-native firms sell a system in production and share outcome risk.
  • Time-and-materials billing rewards more hours; outcome pricing benefits from AI-driven speed.
  • Sequoia frames AI-native services as a vendor swap, since services spend is six times software spend.
  • Outsourcing remains better for stable maintenance, large-scale staff augmentation, and commodity run work.
  • Code, model, and data ownership depends on contract terms; demand export rights in writing.

How an AI-native services firm is staffed, paid and held to account compared with a traditional IT outsourcing company, and when outsourcing is still the better buy.

7 min readPublished Nov 2026

A traditional IT outsourcing company supplies people, usually billed by time and materials, and you manage the work. An AI-native services firm fields a small senior team, scopes it to an outcome in production and is accountable for the result. The first is paid for effort. The second is paid for a working system.

How do an AI-native services firm and an IT outsourcing company differ?

They differ in what is sold, who carries the risk and how the work is measured. Outsourcing sells capacity. An AI-native firm sells a result, and takes on the operating problem instead of leaving it with you.

  • What you buy: outsourcing sells engineers and hours. An AI-native firm sells a system in production.
  • Who manages the work: with outsourcing you set the priorities and review the output. With an AI-native firm the firm owns delivery against an agreed result.
  • How it ends: an outsourcing contract usually continues as long as the work does. An AI-native engagement is scoped to reach production and then hand over.
  • Where the risk sits: in outsourcing the client carries the risk of the outcome. In an AI-native engagement the firm shares it.

MarsLab’s definition of an AI-native services firm puts the line in one place: a staffing firm supplies people by the hour and the client carries the risk of the outcome, while an AI-native firm is accountable for the result.

How is each one staffed and paid?

Outsourcing is staffed by headcount and paid by the hour or the month. An AI-native firm is staffed by a small senior team and paid for a scoped outcome. The payment model decides what each side is rewarded for.

Under time and materials, more hours mean more revenue. US federal procurement rules say as much: FAR 16.601 states that a time-and-materials contract “provides no positive profit incentive to the contractor for cost control or labor efficiency.” That is a government document and many outsourcers manage the problem well, but the incentive is built into the model.

AI changes the arithmetic. If a tool lets an engineer finish in a day what took a week, an hourly vendor earns less. A firm paid for the outcome earns the same and finishes sooner. Sequoia Capital’s essay Services: The New Software makes the point in its own terms: “if you sell the work, every improvement in the model makes your service faster, cheaper, and harder to compete with.” Sequoia is an investor in this category, so read it as an argument and not as neutral research.

MarsLab’s definition makes the same link. Services used to scale with headcount, and capable models changed the ratio between people and output, which made it possible to sell the outcome rather than the tool.

Why did the AI-native category appear?

Because spending on services is far larger than spending on software, and AI made it possible to sell the work itself. The same Sequoia essay puts it this way: “For every dollar spent on software, six are spent on services.”

The essay also argues that replacing an outsourcing contract with an AI-native provider is a vendor swap, because the buyer already purchases an outcome and already has a budget line for it. That is one investor’s view. It holds best for well-defined, repeatable work, and less well for work that needs deep knowledge of your own operation.

What is traditional IT outsourcing good at?

Predictable, high-volume work that runs for years: keeping systems up, handling tickets, patching, testing and staffing large programmes. It is mature, well understood and built for scale.

  • Large pools of engineers that can be added or removed to match demand.
  • Round-the-clock support with defined service levels.
  • Established procurement, security review and contract templates that your legal team already knows.
  • Cost control through volume, standard processes and offshore delivery centres.

None of this is what an AI-native firm is built for, and a firm that claims to do everything is worth questioning.

What is an AI-native services firm good at?

Building a specific system around how your operation actually runs, getting it to production quickly, and staying through adoption. It suits problems where the hard part is your data, your older systems and your people, not the model.

MarsLab’s evidence is in its case studies. For StudyBuddy it built an AI-native ERP across seven departments, from a sales enquiry to salary in the bank. At Sanjeevani Group it rebuilt procurement, connected plant controllers to live monitoring and moved sugar recovery from between 8.0 and 8.3% toward 9.0 to 9.5%. The standard engagement runs from strategy to production in eight weeks.

When is traditional outsourcing the better choice?

When the work is large, stable and ongoing, or when you need many engineers for a long time. An AI-native firm is a poor fit for both.

  • Large stable maintenance: an ageing system that needs steady upkeep, not change. A support contract with fixed service levels fits this better than an engagement designed to end.
  • Staff augmentation at scale: if you need thirty engineers inside your own teams, directed by your managers, you are buying capacity. A small outcome-based team cannot supply that.
  • Commodity run work: service desks, testing farms and routine operations where the process is known and cost per unit is the main measure.
  • Procurement rules that require an approved vendor panel or a framework contract.

If your need is capacity and not a result, buy capacity. Paying an outcome-based premium for work that has no outcome to define is waste.

When is an AI-native firm the better choice?

When you can state the result you want, when the work crosses several systems, and when you want a system to run in production by a set date instead of a pool of people.

It also fits when the cost of the workaround is already being paid every day and the system will be reused. If nothing built will be reused, it is closer to consulting, and our guide to forward deployed engineers says it should be priced that way. The delivery model most such firms use is described on the forward deployed engineers page.

Who owns the code and the models?

It depends entirely on the contract, in both models. Never assume that paying for the work means you own it.

The US Copyright Office’s Circular 30 says a work made for hire can arise “when a certain type of work is created as a result of an express written agreement between the creator and a party specially ordering or commissioning it.” Rules differ by country, so have a lawyer check the terms. Then ask for the following in writing:

  • Source code in a repository in your account.
  • Cloud accounts and credentials in your name.
  • Models and fine-tunes you hold, or a clear list of the third-party APIs you depend on.
  • Your data, exportable in full.
  • Documentation that lets another team run the system.

On MarsLab engagements the client owns the system, the cloud account and the models. Outsourcers vary, so check the terms of each contract. See if you can’t export it, you don’t own it for a longer checklist.

How do you tell a real AI-native firm from a rebranded one?

Ask for evidence that the firm is paid for outcomes and that a small senior team does the work. A rebrand shows up in the contract and the staffing plan, not in the website.

  • Named clients and the figure each engagement moved. Anonymised tiers and vague gains are a warning.
  • Who owns the system and the stack at the end.
  • How long it takes to reach production, and whether that means a prototype or a system the business depends on.
  • Who is on the team, how senior they are and whether they stay for the whole engagement.
  • What is billed. If the invoice is still hours multiplied by a rate with an AI label on the proposal, the model has not changed.
  • What happens to the fee if the work finishes early.

MarsLab’s own definition ends the same way: an AI-native services company that cannot answer these with specifics is selling consulting with a new label. Hold us to the same test.

Can you use both?

Yes, and for many companies that is the sensible split. Keep a traditional provider on stable run and maintenance work, and bring in an AI-native team to build and hand over a new capability.

The handover is the part to plan. Decide who runs the new system once it is live, and make sure the build team documents it for them. If you are unsure which problem you have, an AI discovery and ROI roadmap is the usual way to find out before committing to either.

When should you talk to MarsLab?

Talk to us when you have a defined result in mind, systems that do not talk to each other, and a date you want it running by. Do not talk to us if you need a large block of engineers for steady maintenance.
