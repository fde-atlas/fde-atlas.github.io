Every customer is different—how does the product grow?

Original title: 每个客户都不同，产品怎样成长

pafa.github.io · 2025-06-11 · 17 min read

Summary

This chapter examines how forward deployed engineering work can feed back into a growing product rather than just accumulating client-specific commitments. The author argues that revenue growth does not equal product growth, and that companies must distinguish reusable capability from promises requiring ongoing care. Drawing on vendor accounts from Baseten (a 70% product-return target), PagerDuty, Atlassian, SAP, OpenAI, and Palantir, the chapter stresses these sources cannot prove actual reuse returns. It proposes four layers of reuse—knowledge, engineering components, workflow structure, and full product—and uses a hypothetical dispatch-system scenario to show how to separate generic capabilities from client rules, verify them with a second team and compatibility matrices, and decide who pays for generality.

Key points

  • Revenue growth does not guarantee product growth; the distinction is reusable capability versus ongoing commitments.
  • Baseten's 70% product-return target is a goal, not an audited result, and percentages alone do not measure retained value.
  • Reuse has four layers: knowledge, engineering components, workflow structure, and full product.
  • A hypothetical dispatch scenario shows how to draw the line between generic capabilities and client-specific rules, verified via compatibility matrices.
  • Delivery costs and product investment should be priced separately, and not all field feedback should become features.

Picture a team like this: the first customer is willing to pay, and everyone is excited. The second customer has similar needs, making the team feel the product has found its direction. But when the third customer goes live, the engineers find themselves once again copying code, changing fields, and re-explaining the same set of rules.

At this point, revenue is growing, but the product may not be.

A company offering FDE services can keep solving hard problems with excellent people. But if every new dollar of revenue requires hiring another batch of equally excellent people, management will sooner or later have to answer a question: is the company accumulating capabilities that can continue to be used, or accumulating commitments that must be continuously cared for? Both kinds of business can work, but their economic structures differ. Presenting the second as the first misleads customers, product teams, and investors all at once.

This chapter starts from this fork in the road. The question is not "whether to customize," but how one customization can become a help to the next delivery—and when one should admit that it is simply a requirement of a specific customer.

Why one number is not enough

Vlad Shulman, who leads FDEs at Baseten, gave a clear direction in an article last updated on June 11, 2025: have 70% of the work built by FDEs flow back into the product. This is a team goal, not an audited achievement rate, nor does it mean that seven out of every ten lines of customer code should be merged directly into the shared product. 1

This goal is worth caring about because it pushes the location where work is completed one step further. Restoring things to normal at the customer site only completes the delivery; whether the company subsequently makes the same mistake one less time or writes one less set of duplicate code is what begins to answer the product question.

But the percentage itself does not tell us how much value is retained. A piece of code merged into the main repository may never be maintained again. A page turned into a configuration option may add dozens of hard-to-test combinations. Conversely, a project that leaves no reusable code may have proven that a product hypothesis does not work, stopping more expensive development in time. That, too, can be valuable accumulation.

Therefore, managers cannot only ask "how much was merged back"; they must also ask: which specific step did the next project skip? Who maintains this capability? Is the second customer actually willing to change their practices for it? These questions are closer to whether the product can grow than where things sit in the repository.

From customer problems to product: what can be seen

PagerDuty runs incident and alerting collaboration. Its engineer Doug McClure recalled on March 24, 2026: in 2019, he was embedded for six months at an unnamed broadcast media customer, working with a product manager to extend the ServiceNow incident management integration, and the changes entered the official product that year. In his recollection, he called it an early FDE project, before the term existed. 2

One instance of something entering the product does not automatically become a replicable method. The same article recalls that subsequent services teams kept building customer customizations in isolated environments, lacking unified on-call ownership and service commitments, with the same kinds of problems being re-solved for the next customer. After shifting to an FDE organization, the team entered the product development system and had engineers join feature teams, learning their code, testing, and release standards. 2 This is one provider's recollection; per-customer costs were not disclosed. It still reveals the real cost: delivery at the customer site can succeed, but maintainers and product standards do not automatically follow.

What is worth taking away from this is not "move the department into R&D and you will succeed," but that the receiving side must participate in shaping the solution. Having field engineers finish first and then asking the shared product to absorb the work may leave differences that could have been discovered earlier until the very end. This also sets a stricter question for the product material below: a capability must not only enter the shared entry point, it must also withstand continued use by existing customers.

In Atlassian's FDE methodology description dated October 4, 2026, the starting point of co-building with customers is the desired outcome: engineers first probe the participants, related systems, handoff breakpoints, and permissions before writing code, connecting systems, or adjusting processes. 3 SAP, on October 13, described its own FDE model: co-developing specific capabilities with customers, then converting the validated solutions into industry-facing software capabilities. 4 The former shows how to find problems worth solving; the latter shows how field results might enter the product. Both are provider statements about how they work; neither proves that a given change has been adopted by other customers or generated calculable reuse benefits.

On September 17, 2026, Boaz Francis, who leads FDEs at Wonderful, described another episode: after the company opened its early product to collect feedback, FDEs did not just report problems—they directly proposed fixes for concurrency performance issues and rewrote unstable upload handling. The article also stressed that code still has owners, and changes still require review and must meet engineering standards. 5 This self-account did not publish patches or itemized merge results; what it can support is that the mode of contribution changed—not that reuse benefits can be calculated from it.

In the feedback process this company describes, problem reports began to arrive with proposed changes for the core team to judge. This is not the first time field engineers could contribute code—Chapter 3: Palantir's Field and Product already covered earlier arrangements. What is worth observing here is that submitting candidates can become easy, while the product team still must judge whether it only fits the current customer, whether it will affect others, and who will maintain it going forward. Shortening the distance does not require handing discovering problems, writing code, and approving shared behavior all to the same person.

Baseten's Chains offers an example where the product shape can be observed. Its introduction explains that beyond its single-model deployment tool Truss, the company faced workflows composed of multiple models and processing steps. Customers found it hard to manage compute resources for each step separately and tended to bind different requirements together. Chains splits steps into composable components, letting different parts choose hardware and scaling approaches independently. 6

Judging by feature names alone, it is easy to read this as just another programming tool. From the user's situation, the change is more concrete: if one step needs more powerful hardware, there is no need to require the entire pipeline to use equally expensive hardware; if one step gets busy, there is no need to scale all steps at once.

This is this book's explanation of the product's mechanics. In the description of moving from beta to general availability (GA), the vendor lists further changes: results can be streamed to users as they are generated, large chunks of data can be transferred in formats better suited for direct programmatic processing, different workflows can share components, and developers can more easily inspect and modify things. These changes do not mean every customer is already using them, but they show that productization still has to keep solving how results are passed between steps and how they are maintained. 7

In October 2026, OpenAI's head of FDEs recalled another level of reuse: the team first built custom tools for different validation tasks for an unnamed semiconductor customer, later migrated those tasks to a shared runtime framework centered on Codex, and organized some of the capabilities as shareable skills and plugins. 8 This is a vendor's own account of moving from multiple custom applications to a shared framework within the same customer; it cannot prove that total maintenance costs fell or that reuse has happened at other customers. Extending to new teams may still require swapping connectors and building evals for new tasks.

These materials can support the claim that "customer problems were expressed as general product capabilities"; they cannot prove that all improvements were invented independently by FDEs, much less allow calculating the profit FDEs create for the company. The public articles do not provide sufficient project costs, internal decision records, or counterfactual comparisons. With that boundary kept in place, the cases are still useful: they make "coming back from the customer to build the product" no longer just an organizational slogan, but something that lands on a discussable change.

Two customers—what is truly the same?

To lay out the judgment process, the following uses a teaching scenario; the companies, contracts, and feature requirements are all hypothetical.

A device service provider asks a team to build a repair dispatch system. The first customer requires the system to read repair tickets, identify device types, and recommend engineers, with dispatch after supervisor approval. The second customer also calls it "smart dispatch," but requires the on-duty manager to approve night tasks, and special zones must be entered only by people with specified certifications.

Two pages may look very similar. If a team starts abstracting from the interface, it is easy to turn "approver" into a configurable field and then declare that both customers are supported. The real difference may lie in the nature of the decision: one always requires supervisor approval, while the other also judges by location, time, and personnel qualifications. A configurable list does not mean the rules have been correctly expressed.

The common ground a product team should look for first is stable business actions. For example, "decide whether approval is needed based on current conditions," "record the task version associated with an approval," and "invalidate old approvals after task conditions change." These actions can be reused. As for which qualification applies to which region, that should usually be maintained by the customer.

With this division, the product sells a set of capabilities for expressing and executing decisions, while the customer retains the content of the decisions. The FDE's task is to identify the boundary between the two and verify that this boundary can withstand real change.

If the second customer only needs to add one rule, layering may work. If every new rule requires changing the core flow, the so-called generic capability may simply be hiding the first customer's habits more deeply. At this point, the product's generalization of business rules should be re-examined: whether the first customer's specific regulations were mistaken for a universal structure. The problem is not necessarily that the second customer's "requirements are too special."

There are at least four kinds of reuse, and they cannot be mixed into one diagram

In this scenario, the things that can be retained do not all sit at the same level.

The first level is knowledge. The team knows that repair descriptions often lack equipment numbers, and that engineer qualifications must be confirmed before dispatching. Knowledge helps ask the right questions faster next time, but it is not yet a runnable product.

The second level is engineering components. Reading work orders, identifying duplicate requests, and saving task states can become code reused next time. However, after switching to a different work order system, the interfaces may differ, so components must specify inputs, outputs, and failure conditions.

The third level is workflow structure. Proposing suggestions, requesting approval, executing tasks, and confirming results may hold across multiple customers. What is reusable here is the order of responsibilities; the specific rules still differ.

The fourth level is a complete product. Another team can deploy, configure, upgrade, and troubleshoot according to documentation, without the original authors being present long-term to explain every detail. Only at this level does it come closer to a capability that can be continuously sold and maintained.

All four kinds of accumulation have value, but the cost saved in the next delivery differs. When a lead says "this project produced a lot of沉淀," they should further specify at which level the沉淀 sits. Otherwise, some industry knowledge that was learned may be recorded as a completed standard product, and the gap only shows when the next customer arrives.

Palantir's Marketplace documentation presents some practical mechanisms for product distribution and maintenance: product installation, updates, maintenance windows, and release channels. It shows that once reuse targets continuous operation, one must handle "how to deliver to the next environment" and "how to change safely." The documentation itself does not prove that a particular customer application has been successfully rolled out, nor does it provide a reuse return rate. 9

The scope of shared configuration is also changing. On October 6, 2026, Palantir announced that Workshop's Ontology interface support is generally available: multiple business object types can share partial interfaces and workflow configurations based on common attributes and relationships, without building them separately. The announcement also lists components not yet supported; maps, pivot tables, and Gantt charts are still pending. 10 This adds a product-level reuse entry point; the business meaning of new objects, permissions, and uncovered parts still need to be checked item by item, and how much delivery effort is actually saved still needs separate measurement.

The second use is a new test

The first success can easily make a team overestimate generality, because requirement interpretation, code, and operational habits all come from the same group of people. They know which places cannot be touched and whom to ask when something goes wrong. Many dependencies therefore go unwritten.

Handing this system to a second team can expose these hidden conditions. Still using the repair scenario above: in a test environment isolated from the real dispatch system, prepare an approved daytime ordinary-zone work order. The second team changes it to a nighttime special zone and then attempts dispatch. The expected result is that the old approval becomes invalid, the system re-checks personnel qualifications, requests approval from the on-duty manager, and leaves a modification record; if it is still dispatched directly using the original approval, the association design between task versions and approvals should be revisited.

The second team should also be able to restore the test records to their pre-change state according to the documentation, confirming that the old approval only applies to the original task conditions. This is a constructed check that does not involve injecting faults into a customer's real production system. The original authors may observe, but should not reveal all the answers in advance. If the team can only find the recovery entry point after the author's hints, another dependency in the product or documentation that needs filling has been discovered.

This only tests the new customer. After adding the second customer's rules, whether the first customer still works normally must be placed in the same compatibility table. In the following, A and B are both the teaching customers above; the old core v1 only supports A, and the new core v2 is intended to support both. The table shows expectations that must be verified, not real customer test results.

B, nighttime special zone, qualifications valid and on-duty manager has approvedDoes not support this rule combinationAllow dispatch after verifying the current task version

B, missing time zone, cannot determine whether it is nighttimeNot supportedStop at pending verification; must not default to daytime
Any customer, time or zone changed after approvalOriginal approval only applies to the original taskThe new task version must not reuse the old approval

An easily missed regression is: v2 adds a "daytime exempt from approval" default, causing A to suddenly lose a check that should have been preserved. The solution is not to require customers to remember to change the default every time, but to make the upgrade process explicitly choose the customer strategy and stop migration if the configuration cannot be identified. In the teaching arrangement, the shared dispatch product group receives core v2 and this compatibility table; A's and B's owners confirm their strategies respectively, the deployer retains the original configuration and corresponding versions, and on upgrade failure restores the matching set—never swapping back only the program while leaving the new configuration.

Two-customer compatibility and migration package provides the filled matrix, strategy JSON, and a missing-configuration counterexample. Readers can first judge v2 against A's old behavior, then test B's new requirements; this comes closer to the real increment of the product's promise than only showing that the second customer's demo passed.

If the second team frequently stops to ask "why is this written this way," the problem may be in the documentation; if they must redefine business objects, the problem may be in the abstraction; if they know what to do but lack the corresponding permissions, the problem is in the connection between the product and the customer's organization. The three situations require different improvements and cannot all be attributed to insufficient training.

This kind of verification also has costs. To make a requirement that has appeared only once into a shared feature, one may need to design configuration, maintain compatibility with old versions, add tests, and train support staff. If it will only serve one customer in the future, maintaining a clearly scoped extension separately may be more reasonable.

Productization is not better the earlier it happens. Too late, and duplicated effort accumulates; too early, and the team solidifies differences it does not yet understand into the product, after which every customer pays the price of working around them. What an FDE can contribute is not just proposing features, but bringing back enough usage conditions for the product team to judge when it is worth taking on this long-term commitment.

When a product consists of multiple pieces of software, one must also check whether they can be installed together. Palantir introduced product groups on September 15, 2026; the documentation read on October 1 is explicitly marked Beta, can check dependency cycles, missing inputs, and incompatible versions, and saves immutable snapshots containing versions and check results. 11 This provides another layer of tooling for the teaching compatibility table above: first ensure the combination can be installed, then confirm that A's and B's business rules still hold. Installation checks do not prove for the customer that "nighttime requires approval" is correctly enforced.

The review entry point is also changing. The Changes interface on September 17 consolidated cross-resource changes in one view, but diff viewing does not cover Workshop, Pipeline Builder, or AIP Logic. 12 The owner must specify who will separately review these uncovered objects. Consolidating proposals onto one page reduces the burden of looking things up; it does not mean changes that are not displayed automatically pass acceptance.

Who pays for the "generic" part

Customers are willing to pay for their own problems, but not necessarily to bear the full cost of the vendor building a platform for its next hundred customers. Nor can the vendor promise a low-priced project and then expect the field engineers to produce a generic product through overtime.

A reasonable discussion should separate the parts required for delivery from the parts that are product investment. Suppose the current customer only needs one approval path, while the product team judges that a composable approval capability will be needed in the future. The former determines whether this engagement can be accepted; the latter is the company's product choice. Both can proceed at the same time, but the owners, scope, and resources should be explicit.

Otherwise a common communication mismatch occurs: sales believes a feature has been promised, the FDE believes a direction is being piloted, core R&D believes it is just customer customization, and the customer believes it has entered standard support. When the system is upgraded, all four understandings enter production together, and the disputes are often harder to handle than technical failures.

Therefore, when a feature moves from a project into the product, a genuine transfer of responsibility should take place. Who accepts it, which behaviors are maintained, which changes are allowed, and whether the current customer needs to migrate should all have clear answers. A merged code change that can still only be explained by the one person on site means the organizational transfer is not yet complete.

There is also another option here: a company can explicitly offer high-quality custom services. As long as the price covers maintenance, the contract is clearly worded, and the customer understands the exit conditions, there is no need to force a packaging of reusability just to look more like a software company. The most dangerous position is promising both the flexibility of customization and the low maintenance cost of a standard product while giving neither side sufficient resources.

Not all feedback should become features

The loudest requests from the field often have a clear requester and an urgent deadline. People who have not yet bought the product, frontline employees operating the system, and those who will later take over maintenance usually speak much more quietly. If the product roadmap is ordered only by the volume of requests, the team will get better and better at satisfying whoever currently holds the most power, without necessarily getting closer to the target users.

This does not mean rejecting large customers' demands. It means rewriting the request as a question: who cannot complete which action under what conditions? What does the current workaround cost? Does this difficulty exist elsewhere? Which existing users would find the product harder to understand if the new capability is added?

There should also be room for a harder-to-refuse kind of request: legitimate, technically feasible, and paid for by the customer, yet not necessarily worth becoming a public product. Teaching customer A wants repair dispatches printed synchronously on an old device, with dedicated paper placement, internal numbering, and frequently changing layouts. It does not request privilege escalation, and there is no fundamental technical obstacle; the problem is that no other customer has the same need, and if the public core took on every layout change, it would have to maintain long-term a set of behaviors used only by A.

The product decision in this case is to keep the standard export interface and make printing a customer extension with its own maintenance owner and cost. If A does not accept the ongoing maintenance conditions, the existing manual printing continues, with no commitment to merge into the public core. If more customers later develop the same need, it can be revisited whether a standard template mechanism is worth adding. Declining this particular productization is not the same as declining to help the customer; it gives a clear answer to "who pays for future changes."

Some feedback is best turned into documentation fixes, some into deployment diagnostics, and some calls for adjusting pricing or service scope. Only a portion belongs in the product. Only when all of these are treated as responsive actions does the field team no longer need to prove it values the customer by "adding a feature."

Once the product gets stronger, should FDEs become less busy?

If a class of problems has already been stably solved by the product, continuing to send the most experienced people to handle it repeatedly does not prove a good customer relationship. Product growth should make some field work disappear, or hand it to support channels whose cost and responsibilities fit better.

But total workload may not decline, because the team will take on new customers, new tasks, or more complex scenarios it previously could not serve. Management should look at two things separately: whether the same kind of task is getting easier, and whether the company is deliberately taking on harder tasks. Looking only at FDE headcount growing in step with revenue risks mistaking exploratory investment for inefficiency, or repetitive toil for expansion.

A useful retrospective question is: if the three engineers most familiar with the current systems were reassigned tomorrow, which customers would still run stably, and which capabilities would immediately fall back to manual work? The answer helps the company see whether growth rests on the product that has been left behind, or on the people still in the field.

The most valuable connection between FDEs and core R&D is ultimately more than a feedback channel. It should make the company's commitments increasingly accurate: knowing which problems can be delivered directly, which require joint exploration, and which should be declined. Such a product may not have the most features, but it knows more clearly what it can sustainably take on.
