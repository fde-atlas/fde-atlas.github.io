Machine learning interview questions in 2026 still centre on classic ML: whether you can pick a sensible model, validate it without leaking information, choose a metric that matches the business goal, and explain why a model that looked great offline is failing in production. Interviewers for ML engineer, applied scientist and data scientist roles use these questions to separate people who have run

model.fit()

from people who have shipped and debugged models. This guide covers 75 high-value questions with model answers, from bias-variance and regularisation to gradient boosting, imbalanced data, A/B testing, ML system design and ten production scenarios.

How to use this guide

  • Freshers and data science graduates: most first-round questions come from the fundamentals, algorithms and metrics sections. Interviewers check that you know why a technique works, not just its name.
  • Working ML engineers and data scientists: expect feature engineering, imbalance, evaluation and debugging questions with "what went wrong when you tried that?" follow-ups.
  • Senior candidates: system design, MLOps and scenarios carry most weight. A good answer states the objective, label, baseline, metric, failure modes and monitoring plan.
  • This guide is about classic ML and core deep learning. Transformer internals, fine-tuning and LLM evaluation live in the separate LLM interview questions guide.

Contents

ML fundamentals

1. What is the bias-variance trade-off, and how does it affect model selection?

Answer: Expected error on unseen data splits into bias (error from overly simple assumptions), variance (sensitivity to the particular training sample) and irreducible noise. Linear models on few features tend towards high bias and low variance; deep trees or k-NN with small k towards low bias and high variance. More capacity usually trades bias for variance, so you pick the capacity that minimises validation error, not training error.

You read it from symptoms: high training and validation error means bias (richer features or model, less regularisation); low training error with much higher validation error means variance (more data, regularisation, bagging). Bagging mainly cuts variance; boosting mainly cuts bias.

2. How do you diagnose overfitting and underfitting in practice?

Answer: Compare training and validation scores, and plot learning curves (score against training-set size) and validation curves (score against a hyperparameter such as depth). A large persistent gap means overfitting; both scores low and close means underfitting. If validation score is still improving as data grows, more data will help; if both curves have flattened at a poor level, you need better features or a more expressive model. Also check the spread of cross-validation scores: wide swings across folds mean an unstable model even when the mean looks fine.

3. Explain L1 and L2 regularisation. When would you use each, or elastic net?

Answer: Both add a weight penalty to the loss. L2 (ridge) penalises squared weights, shrinking coefficients smoothly and spreading weight across correlated features. L1 (lasso) penalises absolute weights, pushing some exactly to zero, which performs feature selection. Elastic net combines them, keeping sparsity while staying stable when features are correlated (lasso tends to pick one of a correlated group arbitrarily).

Use L2 as a stable default, L1 for sparse interpretable models, elastic net for high-dimensional correlated data. Scale features first, or the penalty treats them unequally.

LogisticRegression

C

is the inverse of regularisation strength: smaller

C

means stronger regularisation. Candidates often get this backwards.

4. How does k-fold cross-validation work, and when do you need stratified k-fold?

Answer: Split data into k folds; each fold serves once as validation while the model trains on the rest, and you report the mean and spread of the k scores. It uses data efficiently and shows variance, which a single split cannot. Stratified k-fold keeps class proportions equal in every fold, which matters for classification and especially for rare positives, where plain folds may contain almost no positives. When you also tune hyperparameters, use nested cross-validation or a separate test set, or the reported score is optimistic.

5. When is random k-fold cross-validation the wrong choice?

Answer: Whenever rows are not independent:

  • Time series: random folds train on the future. Use forward-chaining splits (
    TimeSeriesSplit
    ), with a gap if features use lagged aggregates.
  • Grouped data: many rows per customer, patient or device. Use 
    GroupKFold
    or 
    StratifiedGroupKFold
    so one entity never sits on both sides.
  • Site effects: a model rolled out to new hospitals or stores should be validated on held-out whole sites.

Interview tip: The rule that answers most follow-ups: the validation split must mimic how the model meets data in production.

6. What is data leakage? Give examples you would look for.

Answer: Leakage is information unavailable at prediction time, or from the validation set, reaching the model during training. It produces excellent offline scores that collapse in production. Common forms:

  • Target leakage: a feature caused by the label, such as "closure reason" in a churn model or a chargeback flag in a fraud model.
  • Temporal leakage: aggregates whose window extends past the prediction time.
  • Preprocessing leakage: fitting scalers, imputers, target encoders or feature selection on all data before splitting.
  • Duplicate or group leakage: the same customer or near-identical rows on both sides of the split.

Real-world example: Consider a hospital readmission model that scored suspiciously well. Its strongest feature was a code clinicians entered only after a readmission was scheduled. Removing it lowered the score and made the model useful. The most effective single check is asking, for every feature, exactly when its value becomes known.

7. Why keep a separate test set if you already cross-validate?

Answer: Every decision made from validation scores (features, hyperparameters, model family, threshold) slightly fits the validation data, so after many iterations the cross-validation score is optimistic. A test set touched once, at the end, gives an unbiased estimate of the chosen pipeline. Reuse it repeatedly and it becomes another validation set. For time-dependent problems, make it the most recent period, which also reveals how the model copes with drift.

8. How do you choose a baseline, and why does it matter?

Answer: A baseline is the simplest credible approach: majority class or mean, an existing business rule, last period's value or a seasonal naive forecast, or a regularised linear model. Without it a metric means nothing: a fraud model with high accuracy may be no better than predicting "not fraud" everywhere. Baselines also expose leakage (an implausible margin over a strong baseline deserves investigation) and tell you whether ML is worth its operating cost. In design rounds, name your baseline before any complex model.

Algorithms and when to use each

9. What are the assumptions of linear regression, and what happens when they are violated?

Answer: OLS assumes a linear relationship, independent errors, constant error variance (homoscedasticity), no perfect multicollinearity and, for valid intervals and p-values, roughly normal errors. Non-linearity biases predictions, so add transformations or interactions. Heteroscedastic or correlated errors leave coefficients unbiased but standard errors wrong, so use robust errors or model the structure. Multicollinearity makes individual coefficients unstable even when predictions are fine; regularisation helps. Squared error is also outlier-sensitive; Huber or quantile regression are robust alternatives.

10. How does logistic regression work, and how do you derive the gradient of its loss?

Answer: It models the log-odds as linear: p = σ(w·x + b). Training minimises log loss, the negative Bernoulli log-likelihood: L = −[y log p + (1 − y) log(1 − p)]. Using σ'(z) = σ(z)(1 − σ(z)) and the chain rule, dL/dz simplifies to (p − y), so the weight gradient is (p − y)·x averaged over the batch, plus the regularisation term. The loss is convex, so there are no spurious local minima.

Interview tip: Add that coefficients are changes in log-odds and outputs are reasonably calibrated when the model is well specified, which is why lenders still use it for scorecards.

11. How does a decision tree choose splits? Compare Gini impurity and entropy.

Answer: At each node the tree greedily picks the feature and threshold that most reduce size-weighted impurity in the children. Gini (1 − Σp²) and entropy (−Σp log p, giving information gain) usually produce near-identical trees; Gini is slightly cheaper. Regression trees minimise squared error. Trees need no scaling and are readable, but they are high-variance: a small data change can alter the top split. Control this with maximum depth, minimum samples per leaf and cost-complexity pruning, or use ensembles.

12. Explain bagging and boosting. How do they improve weak learners?

Answer: Bagging trains models independently on bootstrap samples and averages them; partly independent errors cancel, reducing variance, so it suits deep, high-variance trees. Boosting trains models sequentially, each correcting the current ensemble (re-weighting samples in AdaBoost, fitting the negative loss gradient in gradient boosting), reducing bias by combining many shallow learners.

13. How does a random forest work, how does it handle missing data, and which parameters do you tune?

Answer: It bags deep trees and also samples a random subset of features at each split, de-correlating the trees so averaging removes more variance. Out-of-bag rows give a free validation estimate. Missing-value handling depends on the implementation: Breiman's original method used proximity-based imputation, many libraries required imputation, and recent scikit-learn versions support missing values natively in some tree estimators, so check your version's documentation. Imputation plus a "was missing" indicator is the explicit, portable option. Tune

max_features

(the main de-correlation lever),

min_samples_leaf

and

max_depth

(regularisation, model size, latency), tree count until gains flatten, and

class_weight

for imbalance.

14. How does gradient boosting work, and how do XGBoost, LightGBM and CatBoost differ?

Answer: Gradient boosting builds an additive model: each round computes the negative gradient of the loss at the current predictions (for squared error, the residuals), fits a small tree to it and adds it scaled by a learning rate. Any differentiable loss works, so the same framework covers regression, classification and ranking.

  • XGBoost: second-order (gradient and Hessian) optimisation, explicit regularisation of leaf weights, sparsity-aware splits that learn a default direction for missing values, histogram mode for speed.
  • LightGBM: leaf-wise growth with histogram binning, fast on large data; can overfit small data unless leaves and depth are capped.
  • CatBoost: ordered target statistics for categorical features without target leakage, and symmetric trees that are fast at inference.

Tune learning rate with early stopping, leaves or depth, minimum child weight, row and column subsampling, and L1/L2 on leaves.

15. Explain SVMs and the kernel trick. When would you choose a linear kernel over RBF?

Answer: An SVM finds the maximum-margin hyperplane; only support vectors (points on or inside the margin) define it. The soft-margin form uses hinge loss, with C trading margin width against training errors. The kernel trick computes inner products in a high-dimensional feature space through a kernel function without constructing that space. Use a linear kernel for many sparse features, such as TF-IDF text, which is often near-linearly separable and scales well. Use RBF for fewer dense features with a non-linear boundary, tuning C and gamma together (large gamma overfits). Kernel SVMs scale poorly with row count, need scaled inputs and do not output calibrated probabilities by default.

16. How does k-nearest neighbours work, and why does it struggle in high dimensions?

Answer: k-NN stores the training data and predicts by majority vote or average of the k closest points. Training is free; prediction is expensive, so large-scale use relies on approximate nearest-neighbour indexes. Small k is low-bias and high-variance. In high dimensions distances become similar to one another (the curse of dimensionality) and irrelevant features dominate, so k-NN needs scaling, feature selection or dimensionality reduction and a suitable metric, often cosine similarity for embeddings. The same idea underpins vector search, covered in the embeddings explainer.

17. Explain k-means clustering. How do you choose the number of clusters?

Answer: k-means alternates assigning points to the nearest centroid and moving centroids to their points' mean, minimising within-cluster sum of squares. It finds local optima, so use k-means++ initialisation and several restarts. Choose k with the elbow of inertia against k, silhouette scores, stability across bootstrap samples and, above all, usefulness: a marketing team that can act on five segments gains more than from fourteen. k-means assumes roughly spherical, similar-sized clusters, uses Euclidean distance (scale features first) and is pulled by outliers.

18. When would you use hierarchical clustering, DBSCAN or Gaussian mixtures instead of k-means?

Answer:

  • Hierarchical clustering builds a dendrogram, so you choose the cluster count afterwards and see nested structure. It suits small to medium data; linkage (Ward, average, complete) shapes the result.
  • DBSCAN finds dense regions of any shape, needs no k and labels sparse points as noise, which suits geographic hotspots. It struggles with clusters of varying density; HDBSCAN eases that.
  • Gaussian mixture models fit Gaussians with expectation-maximisation, giving soft assignments and elliptical clusters of differing size. Choose components with BIC or AIC.

19. Explain PCA. How do eigenvectors relate to it, and how do you choose the number of components?

Answer: PCA finds orthogonal directions of maximum variance. After centring (usually also standardising), the components are eigenvectors of the covariance matrix and eigenvalues give the variance along each; libraries compute this via SVD for numerical stability. Choose components by cumulative explained variance, the scree-plot elbow or, most reliably, downstream validation performance when PCA is preprocessing. Remember it is linear, high variance is not always predictive, and components are harder to explain than raw features.

20. How do t-SNE and UMAP differ from PCA, and what should you not use them for?

Answer: t-SNE and UMAP are non-linear methods that preserve local neighbourhoods, which makes them good for 2D or 3D visualisation of embeddings. PCA is linear, deterministic and preserves global variance. In t-SNE plots, cluster sizes and inter-cluster gaps are not meaningful, and results change with perplexity and seed. UMAP is faster, keeps somewhat more global structure and can transform new points. Use both to explore; do not treat a visual cluster as proof of a real group without other validation.

21. Given a new tabular problem, how do you decide which algorithm to try?

Answer: Start from constraints: data size, feature types, latency, interpretability and regulation. Then climb a ladder: baseline, regularised linear or logistic model, then gradient-boosted trees, which are typically strong on structured data. Deep learning earns its place with unstructured data, very large datasets, or a need for learned embeddings of high-cardinality entities.

22. Compare grid search, random search and Bayesian optimisation for hyperparameter tuning.

Answer: Grid search tries every combination of fixed values; cost grows exponentially and many trials vary unimportant parameters. Random search samples from distributions and, for the same budget, usually explores the important dimensions better. Bayesian optimisation (Gaussian processes or tree-structured Parzen estimators, as in Optuna) models score against hyperparameters and proposes promising trials, which pays off when each run is expensive. Successive halving and Hyperband stop weak trials early. Whatever the method, tune inside cross-validation and keep the test set out.

Feature engineering

23. When do you standardise versus normalise features, and which models need scaling?

Answer: Standardisation (subtract mean, divide by standard deviation) suits linear models, SVMs, PCA and neural networks, and copes with outliers better than min-max normalisation, which squeezes everything when one extreme value exists. Min-max fits when a bounded range is needed, such as pixel values; robust scaling with median and IQR helps heavy outliers. Distance- and gradient-based methods need scaling; tree models split on thresholds and do not. Fit scalers on training folds only (Q28).

24. How do you handle high-cardinality categorical features?

Answer: One-hot encoding thousands of merchant IDs or PIN codes creates huge sparse matrices and rare categories with too little data. Options:

  • Count encoding: simple and leak-free, though categories can collide on the same count.
  • Target encoding: a smoothed per-category target mean; powerful but leaks unless computed out-of-fold. scikit-learn's 
    TargetEncoder
    cross-fits internally; CatBoost uses ordered statistics.
  • Grouping: rare values into "other", or up a hierarchy (PIN code to district to state).
  • Hashing into fixed buckets, or learned embeddings (Q27).

Define the fallback for categories never seen in training; production will produce them.

25. How do you handle missing data beyond mean imputation?

Answer: First ask why values are missing; missingness is often informative (a test not ordered because the doctor saw no need). Options: drop rows or columns when missingness is small and random; median or mode imputation plus a "was missing" indicator; model-based imputation such as k-NN or iterative imputation (each feature predicted from the others, in the spirit of MICE); or models that handle missing values natively, such as many boosting libraries. Fit imputers on training data only, and monitor null rates in production: an upstream system that starts sending nulls is a classic silent failure.

26. What are feature interactions, and how do you capture or select features?

Answer: An interaction exists when one feature's effect depends on another, such as an amount normal at a jeweller but odd at a pharmacy. Linear models need explicit interactions (products, ratios, "amount relative to the customer's average"); tree ensembles learn them through successive splits, and factorisation machines and neural networks learn them in embedding space. For selection, use filters (mutual information) to screen, embedded methods (L1, tree importance) and wrappers (recursive feature elimination) when compute allows. Run selection inside cross-validation; selecting on all data first is leakage.

27. What is an embedding, and how is it useful for categorical data?

Answer: An embedding maps a discrete item (user, product, merchant, word) to a dense low-dimensional vector learned so that similarly behaving items sit close together. Compared with one-hot, embeddings are compact, generalise across similar categories and can be reused across models. Tabular neural networks give each high-cardinality column an embedding layer; recommenders rely on user and item embeddings. Pretrained text and image encoders also produce embeddings, the basis of semantic search; see embeddings explained.

28. How do you build preprocessing so it cannot leak into validation?

Answer: Put every learned transformation and the model into one pipeline and cross-validate the pipeline, so each fold fits imputation, scaling and encoding on its training portion only. Serve the same fitted object, which also prevents training-serving skew from re-implemented preprocessing.

from sklearn.compose import ColumnTransformer
from sklearn.pipeline import Pipeline
from sklearn.impute import SimpleImputer
from sklearn.preprocessing import (
    OneHotEncoder, StandardScaler)
from sklearn.linear_model import LogisticRegression
from sklearn.model_selection import (
    StratifiedKFold, cross_val_score)

num = Pipeline([
    ("imp", SimpleImputer(strategy="median",
                          add_indicator=True)),
    ("sc", StandardScaler()),
])
cat = OneHotEncoder(handle_unknown="ignore")
prep = ColumnTransformer([
    ("num", num, num_cols),
    ("cat", cat, cat_cols),
])
clf = Pipeline([
    ("prep", prep),
    ("lr", LogisticRegression(
        C=1.0, class_weight="balanced",
        max_iter=1000)),
])
cv = StratifiedKFold(n_splits=5, shuffle=True,
                     random_state=42)
scores = cross_val_score(
    clf, X, y, cv=cv, scoring="average_precision")
print(scores.mean(), scores.std())

handle_unknown="ignore"

for unseen categories and

scoring="average_precision"

Imbalanced data

29. Which metrics would you use for imbalanced classification? How do precision-recall and ROC curves differ?

Answer: Accuracy misleads: predicting the majority class everywhere scores high and catches nothing. Use precision, recall and F-beta at the operating threshold plus a threshold-free summary. ROC-AUC (true positive rate against false positive rate) measures ranking and ignores prevalence, which hides problems with rare positives: a tiny false positive rate on millions of negatives can still swamp the true hits. The precision-recall curve and average precision (PR-AUC) focus on the positive class and reflect how many alerts are real. A random model's PR-AUC equals the positive rate, a useful baseline to quote. Operations teams also care about precision at a fixed alert budget.

30. Compare resampling, SMOTE and class weights. When do they help?

Answer: All shift the learner's attention to the minority class. Undersampling the majority is fast and works well on large data but discards rows. Oversampling duplicates minority rows and can overfit. SMOTE interpolates synthetic minority points, which helps some models but can create unrealistic points in high-dimensional or categorical data. Class weights (or

scale_pos_weight

in boosting) do the same inside the loss. Three rules matter more than the choice: resample training folds only; recalibrate afterwards, because resampling and weighting distort probabilities; and consider skipping resampling altogether, training a good ranker on the natural distribution and setting the threshold from business costs.

31. Scenario: your fraud model produces so many false positives that the review team cannot keep up. What do you do?

Answer: Treat it as a threshold and capacity problem first. The threshold should come from error costs and review capacity, not a default of 0.5.

What I would check:

  • Precision at the number of alerts the team can actually review per day, on recent data.
  • The cost of a missed fraud versus a false alert (review time, a blocked genuine customer).
  • Whether alerts can be tiered: auto-block at very high scores, review in the middle, step-up authentication such as an OTP for borderline cases.
  • Whether false positives cluster in a segment (merchant category, new customers, festival spending) that hints at a missing feature, or whether precision dropped recently, which suggests drift.

Production consideration: Make the threshold a versioned, configurable parameter agreed with the business, and monitor alert volume and precision daily.

Metrics: classification, regression and ranking

32. Explain the confusion matrix, precision, recall and F-beta. How do you choose between them?

Answer: The confusion matrix counts TP, FP, TN and FN at a threshold. Precision = TP / (TP + FP): of flagged items, how many were right. Recall = TP / (TP + FN): of real positives, how many were caught. F1 is their harmonic mean; F-beta weights recall beta times as much, so F2 favours recall and F0.5 precision. Choose by the costlier error: missing cancer or a large fraud favours recall; blocking a genuine card or paying for an unneeded retention offer favours precision. When stakeholders cannot state costs, show the trade-off at several thresholds in their own units and let them choose.

33. What is calibration, and when do log loss or Brier score matter more than AUC?

Answer: A model is calibrated if about 20 in 100 cases scored 0.2 are positive. AUC measures only ranking, so a high-AUC model can have poor probabilities. Calibration matters whenever the probability drives a decision: expected loss in credit, insurance pricing, combining model scores, or risk shown to a clinician. Log loss punishes confident mistakes; the Brier score is the mean squared error of probabilities. Inspect a reliability diagram, and fix miscalibration (common in boosted trees, SVMs and resampled training) with Platt scaling or isotonic regression on held-out data, for example

CalibratedClassifierCV

.

34. Compare MAE, RMSE, MAPE and R² for regression. Which would you report?

Answer: Pick by error cost. If large misses hurt disproportionately (under-stocking a high-margin item before a festival), use RMSE or a quantile loss. If stakeholders think in percentages and actuals are never near zero, MAPE or WAPE communicates well. Always show a naive baseline's error alongside.

35. How do you evaluate a ranking or recommendation model? Explain precision@k, MAP, MRR and NDCG.

Answer: Ranking metrics focus on the top of the list. Precision@k is the share of the top k that is relevant; recall@k the share of all relevant items found in the top k. MAP averages precision at each rank where a relevant item appears, rewarding early hits. MRR uses only the first relevant item's position, suiting search with one right answer. NDCG handles graded relevance (purchase over click over view) with a logarithmic position discount, normalised by the ideal ordering. These are proxies: also check coverage and diversity, and confirm online, because logs only contain items the old system chose to show.

36. Scenario: a retailer asks you to "predict churn with high accuracy". How do you choose the metric?

Answer: Translate the request into the decision. Churn prediction only pays off when someone acts on it, and that action has a cost and limited capacity.

What I would check:

  • The churn definition and prediction horizon.
  • The intervention, its cost per customer and how many customers it can reach each month.
  • The base churn rate, which shows how misleading accuracy will be.
  • Whether the goal is finding likely churners or customers an offer can actually change; the second is an uplift problem.

Production consideration: Report precision and recall at campaign size and retained revenue minus offer cost from a holdout. Agree this metric before modelling.

Deep learning basics

37. Explain backpropagation. How does the chain rule apply?

Answer: A network is a composition of functions. The forward pass computes activations and the loss. Backpropagation applies the chain rule in reverse: each layer receives the loss gradient with respect to its output, multiplies by its local derivative to get its weight gradients, and passes the gradient with respect to its input to the previous layer. Forward activations are cached because local derivatives need them, which is why training needs much more memory than inference. Frameworks implement this as reverse-mode automatic differentiation; an optimiser then applies the gradients.

38. Compare batch, stochastic and mini-batch gradient descent.

Answer: Batch gradient descent uses the full dataset per update: exact and stable but slow. Stochastic gradient descent updates per example: cheap and noisy, sometimes escaping poor regions, but erratic and hardware-inefficient. Mini-batch, with batches from tens to a few thousand examples, is the standard because it vectorises well on GPUs and gives a reasonable gradient estimate; "SGD" in frameworks usually means this. Batch size interacts with learning rate: larger batches allow higher rates but may need warm-up to generalise as well.

39. How do momentum, RMSProp and Adam differ from plain gradient descent?

Answer: Momentum follows an exponential average of past gradients, damping oscillation and speeding consistent directions. RMSProp divides each parameter's step by a running root-mean-square of its gradients, giving per-parameter adaptive rates. Adam combines both moments with bias correction for early steps; AdamW decouples weight decay from the adaptive update and is a common default. Well-tuned SGD with momentum still competes in some vision work, and learning-rate schedules (warm-up, cosine decay) often matter as much as the optimiser.

40. How do you prevent overfitting in neural networks? Explain dropout, early stopping and weight decay.

Answer: Dropout randomly zeroes activations during training, preventing co-adaptation and acting like an ensemble of thinned networks; frameworks rescale during training so inference uses all units unchanged. Early stopping halts when validation loss stops improving and restores the checkpoint with the lowest validation loss. Weight decay penalises large weights. Data augmentation, smaller architectures, more data and label smoothing also help.

Interview tip: Mention the bug of evaluating in training mode, which keeps dropout on and batch-norm statistics updating; in PyTorch,

model.eval()

versus

model.train()

.

41. What does batch normalisation do, and why does it help?

Answer: It normalises a layer's activations with the current mini-batch's mean and variance, then applies a learned scale and shift. It permits higher learning rates, reduces sensitivity to initialisation and adds mild regularisation. The original "internal covariate shift" explanation is debated; later work points to a smoother optimisation landscape, so describe the mechanism rather than over-claiming why. Inference uses running averages. It degrades with tiny batches, one reason transformers use layer normalisation, which normalises across features within each example.

42. What causes vanishing and exploding gradients, and how is gradient clipping used?

Answer: Backpropagation multiplies many local derivatives. Consistently below one, gradients shrink exponentially and early layers stop learning; above one, they blow up and the loss turns NaN. Saturating activations, poor initialisation and long recurrent sequences make it worse. Fixes: ReLU-family activations, He or Xavier initialisation, normalisation layers, residual connections and gated recurrent units. Gradient clipping rescales gradients whose norm exceeds a threshold; it is standard for RNNs and a common safety net elsewhere, but it addresses exploding gradients only.

43. What is a CNN, and how does it differ from a fully connected network?

Answer: A CNN slides small learned filters over the input, so each output depends on a local region and the same filter is reused everywhere. Weight sharing needs far fewer parameters than dense layers on raw pixels and gives translation equivariance. Stacked convolutions and pooling widen the receptive field: early layers learn edges, deeper ones parts and objects. Practical image work starts from a pretrained backbone and fine-tunes it, which needs far less labelled data. Vision transformers are an alternative; CNNs remain efficient, especially at the edge.

44. How do RNNs, LSTMs and GRUs work, and when would you prefer one over another?

Answer: An RNN processes a sequence step by step with a hidden state, but plain RNNs lose long-range information to vanishing gradients. LSTMs add a cell state governed by input, forget and output gates. GRUs use update and reset gates without a separate cell, so they have fewer parameters and often train faster with similar accuracy; LSTMs may edge ahead on long complex sequences, so try both if it matters. Recurrent models are hard to parallelise and transformers have largely replaced them for language, but they still suit compact streaming and sensor models.

45. At a high level, how does attention work, and why did transformers displace RNNs?

Answer: Attention lets each position build a weighted mix of all positions: each token produces a query, key and value, and query-key similarity decides how much of each value to blend. Transformers stack self-attention and feed-forward layers with residual connections and normalisation, adding positional information because attention has no inherent order. They won because any position reaches any other directly and whole sequences are processed in parallel on GPUs; the cost is attention that grows quadratically with length. For LLM depth, see the LLM interview guide and the what is an LLM explainer.

46. What is an autoencoder, and how is it used for anomaly detection?

Answer: An autoencoder reconstructs its input through a narrow bottleneck: the encoder compresses, the decoder rebuilds. The latent vector is a learned non-linear compression, a generalisation of PCA; variants include denoising and variational autoencoders. For anomaly detection, train on normal data only and flag inputs with high reconstruction error, which suits machine sensor data or network traffic where labelled anomalies are rare. Set the threshold on validation data and compare with simpler detectors such as isolation forest first.

Model evaluation and A/B testing

47. How would you design an A/B test for a new ML model?

Answer: Define a primary metric tied to the goal (conversion, fraud loss, retention), guardrails that must not worsen (latency, complaints, revenue per user) and the minimum effect worth detecting. Use a power calculation to fix sample size and duration in advance. Randomise by user rather than request, so nobody sees both variants, and run across full weekly cycles. Before trusting results, check sample ratio mismatch and logging parity. Ramp through shadow mode, a small canary, then the full split. For ranking, interleaving is often more sensitive than a standard split.

48. Scenario: three days into an A/B test, the new model shows a strong lift and the product manager wants to ship. How do you respond?

Answer: Wait for the pre-registered sample size. Repeatedly checking and stopping at the first significant reading ("peeking") inflates false positives far beyond the nominal rate, and early lifts are prone to novelty and day-of-week effects.

What I would check:

  • Planned sample size and duration reached, and how wide the confidence interval still is.
  • Sample ratio mismatch and logging parity between arms.
  • Guardrails, including lagging costs such as returns or chargebacks.
  • Whether the lift comes from one segment or a few heavy users, and whether it is decaying day by day.

Production consideration: If early looks are needed, agree a sequential testing method with adjusted boundaries before the test starts.

49. How do you handle trend and seasonality in forecasting? Where do ARIMA, Prophet and gradient boosting fit?

Answer: Decompose first: trend, seasonality (weekly, yearly, festivals such as Diwali) and noise. ARIMA models a series from its own lags, differencing and past errors; SARIMA adds seasonal terms and SARIMAX adds exogenous regressors such as promotions, price and holidays. Holt-Winters exponential smoothing is a strong simple baseline. Prophet, an open-source library from Meta, fits additive trend, seasonality and holiday components and is analyst-friendly, though not always the most accurate. For thousands of related series, one global gradient-boosting model on lags, rolling statistics, calendar and exogenous features often wins. Deep sequence models such as LSTMs pay off with many long series and rich covariates. Always compare against a seasonal naive forecast.

50. How do you validate a forecasting or time-dependent model properly?

Answer: Backtest with rolling or expanding origins: train up to time t, forecast the horizon, move t forward, repeat and average errors. Leave a gap when features use overlapping aggregates, and evaluate at the horizon the business actually uses. Keep features point-in-time correct, and if production will only have forecasts of exogenous inputs such as weather, validate with forecasts rather than actuals. Report error by segment, since aggregates hide poor performance on low-volume or new items.

51. How do you decide whether model B is genuinely better than model A offline?

Answer: Evaluate both on identical folds or the same test set and analyse paired differences: per-fold differences, or a bootstrap of per-example loss differences. Pairing accounts for shared hard and easy examples. If the gap sits within fold-to-fold noise, treat it as noise; repeated cross-validation with different seeds tightens the estimate. Then weigh practical significance (latency, cost, complexity, interpretability) and check key segments for hidden regressions. An online test usually has the final word.

ML system design

52. How do collaborative filtering, content-based filtering and matrix factorisation differ?

Answer: Collaborative filtering learns from user-item interactions alone: people who bought similar things will like similar things. It needs no metadata but suffers cold start and popularity bias. Content-based filtering recommends items similar to past likes using attributes or embeddings, handling new items but narrowing variety. Matrix factorisation approximates the sparse user-item matrix as a product of low-rank user and item embeddings, trained with alternating least squares or SGD on observed entries with regularisation; implicit feedback (views, clicks) uses weighted variants that treat unobserved entries as weak negatives. Hybrids combine interactions with user and item features for cold-start coverage.

53. Design a product recommendation system for an e-commerce platform.

Answer: Clarify the objective (purchases or revenue, not just clicks), surfaces (home feed, similar items, frequently bought together), latency and catalogue size. Large systems use a funnel:

user + context
     |
     v
candidate generation  (thousands of items)
 - two-tower embeddings + ANN search
 - co-purchase, trending, category rules
     |
     v
ranking model  (hundreds of items)
 - gradient boosting or deep ranker
 - user, item, context, cross features
     |
     v
re-ranking  (tens of items)
 - diversity, stock, business rules
     |
     v
serve + log impressions and outcomes

Handle cold start with regional or category popularity for new users and content embeddings for new items. Evaluate candidates with recall@k and ranking with NDCG offline, then A/B test conversion and revenue per session with diversity and latency guardrails.

Interview tip: Raise the feedback loop: the model learns only from what it showed. Mitigate with a small exploration share, position-bias correction and impression logging.

54. Design a real-time card fraud detection system for a bank.

Answer: Requirements: a decision inside the payment authorisation path (tens of milliseconds), extreme imbalance, adaptive adversaries, labels that arrive weeks later via chargebacks, and explainability for audit.

card transaction
     |
     v
feature lookup (online store)
 - velocity: txns in last 1h / 24h
 - amount vs customer baseline
 - merchant, device, location risk
     |
     v
rules engine + ML score (GBM)
     |
     +--> approve
     +--> step-up auth (OTP)
     +--> decline / queue for review
     |
     v
labels: chargebacks, analyst outcomes
     -> retraining + monitoring

Gradient-boosted trees on behavioural features are a strong, explainable start; graph features (shared devices, linked accounts) catch rings. Use time-based splits and evaluate PR-AUC, recall at a fixed false-positive budget and value-weighted recall. Rules handle known patterns and fast response; the model catches subtle combinations.

Production consideration: Batch and online feature computation must match exactly, and score distributions and alert rates need hourly monitoring because fraud shifts quickly.

55. Design a churn prediction system for a telecom or subscription business.

Answer: Define churn (port-out, cancellation or inactivity), the horizon (say, the next 30 days) and a feature window ending at a clear cut-off so nothing leaks. Features: usage trends rather than levels, recharge gaps, complaints and tickets, local network quality, tenure, plan changes and payment delays. Gradient boosting or logistic regression scored in a weekly batch job is enough, since retention is not real-time. Validate on a later period, output a ranked list sized to the retention team's capacity, and attach reason codes from SHAP so agents know what to address.

Production consideration: Keep a no-contact holdout to measure incremental retention, and move to uplift models once campaign data exists. Without a holdout, a churn model can look successful while changing nothing.

56. When do you choose batch inference versus real-time inference?

Answer: Batch scores a population on a schedule into a table: cheaper, simpler, easy to audit, and right for churn lists, credit limit reviews, forecasts and nightly recommendations. Real-time scores each request behind an API and is needed when the latest event matters: payment fraud, search ranking, dynamic pricing. It adds latency budgets, autoscaling, an online feature store, fallbacks for missing features and stricter monitoring. A common hybrid precomputes features or candidates in batch with a light real-time step; streaming inference covers near-real-time cases such as IoT alerts.

MLOps touchpoints

Most ML engineer loops include a few production questions. For pipelines, registries, CI/CD for models and LLMOps in depth, use the MLOps interview questions guide; these are the touchpoints every ML engineer should handle.

57. Walk through an end-to-end ML pipeline for production. What are the key challenges?

Answer: Ingestion with data validation (schema, ranges, null rates, freshness) feeds a feature pipeline that builds point-in-time correct training sets. Training is a reproducible job that records code version, data snapshot, parameters and metrics, and registers the artefact with its evaluation. Promotion requires beating the current model on a recent holdout and key segments, then a staged rollout (shadow, canary, A/B). Serving, batch or API, reuses training's feature logic, and monitoring of data, predictions, outcomes and system health triggers retraining or rollback. The hard parts are rarely the algorithm: skew, unreliable upstream data, delayed labels and unclear ownership. For packaging, see Docker for AI applications.

58. What is the difference between data drift and concept drift, and how do you monitor them?

Answer: Data drift is a change in P(X), such as a new customer segment or a recalibrated sensor. Concept drift is a change in P(y|X), such as fraudsters changing tactics so safe-looking patterns become risky. Prior shift changes the base rate P(y). Data drift is visible immediately; concept drift usually needs labels. Monitor feature distributions against a training reference (population stability index, Kolmogorov-Smirnov, Jensen-Shannon), prediction distributions, data-quality signals and, as labels arrive, the real metric by segment. On large volumes tests flag harmless shifts, so set thresholds by impact and prioritise important features.

59. How do you handle model versioning, rollbacks and training-serving skew?

Answer: Register each model with its code commit, data snapshot, feature definitions, hyperparameters, threshold and evaluation report. Deploy by registry reference so rollback is a pointer switch to a previous version that stays deployable. Skew arises when features are computed differently offline and online: SQL in training versus re-implemented service code, time-zone or null-handling differences. Prevent it with shared feature definitions (a feature store or common library), logging served features and training on those logs, and a parity test that recomputes a sample of online features offline and compares.

60. How would you check that a model is not biased, particularly in healthcare or lending?

Answer: Define the relevant groups and harms, then measure per group: selection rates (demographic parity), error rates (equalised odds) and calibration. These criteria can conflict, so choose them with domain, legal and compliance teams. Dropping a protected attribute is not enough when PIN code, name or school act as proxies, and historical labels may encode past policy. Mitigations include reweighting, training constraints, lawful threshold adjustment and human review of borderline cases. Document the analysis and keep it in monitoring; the bias and fairness testing guide covers test design.

Debugging and interpretability

61. Scenario: your new model's validation AUC is far higher than anything the team has seen. What do you do?

Answer: Treat it as a bug until proven otherwise; leakage is the usual cause.

What I would check:

  • Feature importance or SHAP: one dominant feature is a red flag. When is its value known?
  • The split: random versus time-based, and whether one customer or patient appears on both sides.
  • Duplicates across splits, and preprocessing fitted on all data.
  • A fresh later period nobody has looked at.

Production consideration: Make time-based, group-aware validation the pipeline default and auto-flag any feature with extreme importance.

62. Scenario: your neural network's training loss is not decreasing. How do you debug it?

Answer: Simplify until it works, then add complexity back.

What I would check:

  • Can it overfit a handful of examples? If not, the bug is in the model, loss or data pipeline.
  • Inputs and labels: shuffled together, normalised, in the format the loss expects (indices versus one-hot, logits versus probabilities, no double softmax).
  • Learning rate: too high diverges or gives NaN, too low looks flat; run a range test.
  • Per-layer gradient norms for zeros (dead ReLUs, detached graph, frozen layers) or explosions.

Production consideration: Fix seeds and log every run's configuration so regressions after a refactor can be bisected.

63. What is SHAP, and how is it used to explain model predictions?

Answer: SHAP attributes a prediction to features using Shapley values from cooperative game theory: each feature's average marginal contribution across feature combinations, summing to the gap between the prediction and a baseline expected output. It gives local explanations (why this applicant was declined) and, aggregated, global importance and dependence plots. TreeSHAP is exact and fast for tree ensembles; KernelSHAP is model-agnostic, slower and approximate. It explains the model, not causation, and correlated features can split or swap credit.

Real-world example: Consider an insurer's claims-triage model. Showing adjusters the top three SHAP contributions per claim built trust and exposed a feature that unfairly penalised one region.

64. How does LIME differ from SHAP, and what do partial dependence plots show?

Answer: LIME explains one prediction by perturbing the input nearby, scoring the perturbations and fitting a simple weighted surrogate, usually sparse linear. It is fast and intuitive but unstable across random perturbations and kernel widths. SHAP has firmer theoretical properties (additivity, consistency) and is usually preferred for tabular models. A partial dependence plot shows the average prediction as one feature varies, revealing global effect shape, but it averages over unrealistic combinations when features are correlated. ICE curves show per-instance lines; accumulated local effects handle correlation better.

65. How do you interpret a random forest? What is wrong with default feature importance?

Answer: Impurity-based importance sums each feature's impurity reduction across splits. It favours high-cardinality and continuous features with more split points and is computed on training data, so it can reward overfitting. Prefer permutation importance on validation data, or SHAP. Both share credit awkwardly between correlated features: shuffling one of a correlated pair barely hurts, so both may look unimportant. Combine global importance with dependence plots and local explanations, and sanity-check with domain experts; an important feature that makes no domain sense is often leakage.

Real-world scenarios

66. Scenario: a model that performed well at launch has gradually degraded in production. How do you investigate?

Answer: Separate pipeline breakage from genuine drift, then choose between fixing data, retraining and redesigning.

What I would check:

  • Whether the drop is real: metric definition, label pipeline, and label delay that makes recent windows look worse.
  • Data quality around the onset: null rates, new categories, unit or schema changes upstream.
  • Drift in the most important features and in the prediction distribution.
  • Segments: is the drop concentrated in a new product, region, channel or cohort?
  • Concept drift: a model trained on recent data that recovers performance means retraining is warranted.
  • Feedback effects, such as fraudsters adapting to what the model blocks.

Production consideration: Turn these checks into monitoring, retrain on a sliding window with validation gates, and keep the previous model ready for rollback.

67. Scenario: the model scores very well offline but shows no improvement, or gets worse, in the online test. Why?

Answer: The offline evaluation did not represent the online decision: leakage, skew, a proxy metric that does not track the business outcome, or bias in logged data.

What I would check:

  • Training-serving skew: compare logged online features with offline features for the same entities and timestamps.
  • Offline features that are unavailable or staler at serving time.
  • Metric mismatch: better AUC or NDCG may not mean more purchases or less fraud loss.
  • Selection bias: logs only hold outcomes for what the old system chose, so the new model's choices are partly unevaluated.
  • Latency or timeouts that cancel accuracy gains or trigger fallbacks.
  • Experiment health: sample ratio mismatch, low power, interference between arms.

Production consideration: Shadow-deploy first to compare live features and predictions, and trust only offline metrics shown to track past online results.

68. Scenario: you are building a fraud model where far fewer than one in a thousand transactions are fraudulent. Walk through your approach.

Answer: Extreme imbalance changes evaluation, training, thresholds and operations, so address each explicitly.

What I would check:

  • Labels: how fraud is confirmed, how late labels arrive, and how much unlabelled fraud hides in the genuine class.
  • Time-based splits with enough positives per split, grouped by card or customer.
  • Metrics: PR-AUC, recall at review capacity and value-weighted recall, never accuracy.
  • Class weights or majority undersampling on training folds only, then calibration if probabilities feed loss estimates.
  • Behavioural, velocity, device and graph features, plus unsupervised anomaly scores for novel patterns.

Production consideration: Set tiered thresholds (decline, step-up, review) from cost and capacity, monitor precision and alert volume daily, and feed analyst decisions back as labels.

69. Scenario: in a loan-default model, labels arrive months after the decision. How do you monitor and retrain?

Answer: Use early indicators and design retraining around the label lag.

What I would check:

  • Input drift, approval rate and score distribution by segment, available immediately.
  • Early proxy labels such as missed first instalments, validated against final default on past cohorts.
  • Vintage analysis: each monthly cohort compared with earlier cohorts at the same age.
  • Selection bias: outcomes exist only for approved applicants; discuss reject inference or controlled exploration with the risk team.

Production consideration: Train only on matured labels, and expect lending model changes to need documented validation and model risk approval before deployment.

70. Scenario: a model trained on three hospitals performs much worse at a fourth. What happened and what do you do?

Answer: Distribution shift across sites: different equipment, lab ranges, coding practices, populations and workflows, plus shortcuts the model learned from site-specific processes.

What I would check:

  • Feature distributions and missingness patterns at the new site.
  • Encoding differences: units, codes, device-specific values.
  • Whether original validation held out whole sites; random splits would have hidden this.
  • Base-rate differences that break calibration even when ranking holds.

Production consideration: Validate on held-out sites, recalibrate or fine-tune locally before go-live, and pilot with clinician review; see human-in-the-loop AI for review designs.

71. Scenario: a bank wants a more accurate credit model, but the risk team needs clear explanations. How do you approach it?

Answer: Treat explainability as a requirement and compare options on accuracy and governability together.

What I would check:

  • What must be explained: reason codes for declined applicants, validation documentation, or both.
  • How much a complex model actually gains over a well-built logistic scorecard on an out-of-time sample.
  • Whether monotonic constraints (risk never falls as missed payments rise), which major boosting libraries support, make behaviour defensible.
  • Stability of SHAP reason codes for similar applicants, and fairness across groups (Q60).

Production consideration: A frequent outcome is a constrained boosted model with SHAP reason codes, or a scorecard using features the complex model surfaced, documented for model risk review.

72. Scenario: a retail demand forecast fails badly during the festival season. How do you fix it?

Answer: Festival demand is rare, large and moves in the Gregorian calendar (Diwali and Eid shift each year), so weekly and yearly seasonality alone will miss it.

What I would check:

  • Festival features with before and after windows aligned to each year's actual dates.
  • Promotions, prices and marketing calendars as known future inputs.
  • How many past festivals the data covers; pool across stores and products if few.
  • Stock-outs that capped recorded sales, understating demand, and regional differences between states.

Production consideration: Backtest specifically on past festival periods, forecast quantiles so planners can cover upside risk, and track the accuracy of manual overrides too.

73. Scenario: you have only a few hundred labelled examples. What are your options?

Answer: Use simple regularised models, get more from each label, and collect labels efficiently.

What I would check:

  • Whether a regularised logistic baseline is already adequate, using repeated stratified cross-validation because single splits are noisy.
  • Pretrained image or text encoders as feature extractors (transfer learning).
  • Active learning, so experts label the most informative examples next.
  • Weak supervision from expert heuristics, and realistic augmentation; synthetic data is better for testing than as a substitute for labels (see synthetic data for AI testing).

Production consideration: Report confidence intervals rather than single scores, and plan a feedback loop that turns production cases into labels.

74. Scenario: a stakeholder says "the model is a black box and it is wrong too often". How do you handle it?

Answer: Turn the complaint into evidence and give explanations at the level the stakeholder works.

What I would check:

  • Specific cases they consider wrong: true errors, label errors, or cases outside the model's scope?
  • The model's error rate against the current process on the same cases.
  • Patterns in the errors (segment, data source, recent period) that point to a fixable cause.
  • Whether the threshold reflects their costs.

Production consideration: Add per-case explanations and a feedback path whose flags become labelled data; adoption improves more from transparency than from another point of AUC.

75. Describe a machine learning project you worked on. What were the key challenges, and how did you solve them?

Answer: This tests depth, ownership and honesty. Cover the business problem and users, the data and label definition, the baseline, what you tried and why, the metric and its link to the outcome, one or two real problems (caught leakage, a skew bug, imbalance, a stakeholder disagreement) and how you solved them, deployment and monitoring, and the outcome, stated qualitatively if you cannot share numbers.

Interview tip: Expect "why that metric?", "how did you rule out leakage?" and "what would you change?". Choose a project you can defend at that depth and never inflate your role. Early in your career, a documented portfolio project with a deployed API, a model card and monitoring is a credible substitute.

Key takeaways

  • Validation must mimic production: time-based, grouped or site-held-out splits, with all preprocessing inside the cross-validation pipeline.
  • Results that look too good are usually leakage; ask when each feature's value is actually known.
  • Gradient-boosted trees are the usual strong choice for tabular data; deep learning earns its place with unstructured data or very large scale.
  • Choose metrics from the decision: precision and recall at real capacity, calibration when probabilities drive actions, ranking metrics for lists.
  • For imbalance, PR-based metrics and cost-based thresholds matter more than the resampling method.
  • Offline wins need a properly powered online test, without peeking.
  • Production performance depends on feature parity, drift monitoring, versioning and rollback as much as on the algorithm.

Interview preparation checklist

  • Derive the logistic regression gradient and explain bias-variance with your own example.
  • Explain bagging versus boosting and how XGBoost, LightGBM and CatBoost differ, without notes.
  • Write a scikit-learn pipeline with a 
    ColumnTransformer
    , proper cross-validation and a sensible scoring metric from memory; if your Python is rusty, the Python training course covers the foundations.
  • Prepare one leakage story and one drift or degradation story.
  • Practise one design each for recommendations, fraud and churn: objective, label, features, model, metric, serving, monitoring.
  • Read a confusion matrix, PR curve and reliability diagram and say what you would change.
  • Design an A/B test covering power, randomisation unit, guardrails and peeking.
  • Review backprop, optimisers, dropout and batch norm at the level of what goes wrong when misused.
  • Rehearse a two-minute project story with business framing, your specific role and honest outcomes.

FAQ

What skills are required for a machine learning engineer interview?

You need solid Python and SQL, statistics and probability, classic algorithms such as linear models and gradient boosting, validation and evaluation methods, feature engineering, basic deep learning, and an understanding of how models are deployed and monitored. Clear communication matters because many questions ask you to justify trade-offs.

How should I prepare for machine learning interview questions?

Explain concepts out loud, build small models end to end on public datasets, and practise system design with a fixed structure. Prepare real stories about leakage, imbalance and drift, because interviewers probe experience rather than definitions.

Are ML interview questions in 2026 still about classic machine learning?

Yes. Teams that build LLM applications still use classic ML for ranking, fraud, forecasting and churn, and they test validation, metrics and debugging fundamentals. Many interviews add a separate LLM or GenAI round rather than replacing classic ML questions.

How is a data scientist ML interview different from an ML engineer interview?

Data scientist interviews lean towards statistics, experimentation, metrics and business framing. ML engineer interviews lean towards production: pipelines, serving, latency, monitoring and code quality. Both expect strong validation and model selection fundamentals.

What is asked in an ML system design interview?

You are usually asked to design recommendations, fraud detection, search ranking or churn prediction. Interviewers look for clear objectives, label definitions, a baseline, features, model choice, offline and online metrics, serving architecture and monitoring, with trade-offs stated.

Do I need deep learning for machine learning interviews?

You need the basics: backpropagation, optimisers, regularisation, batch normalisation and a high-level view of CNNs, RNNs and transformers. Deeper expertise is expected mainly for vision, speech or NLP roles.

How much mathematics do machine learning interviews require?

Expect working-level linear algebra, calculus for gradients, probability and statistics. You should be able to derive simple gradients, explain PCA and reason about distributions and hypothesis tests, but rarely to prove theorems.

Is machine learning a good career for Indian engineers in 2026?

Machine learning remains a core skill in product companies, services firms and global capability centres in cities such as Hyderabad and Bengaluru. Engineers who combine ML fundamentals with production and cloud skills tend to have the widest range of roles open to them.

Can freshers crack machine learning interviews?

Yes, with strong fundamentals and two or three well-documented projects that show validation discipline, sensible metrics and a deployed model. Freshers are judged more on understanding and clarity than on years of experience.
