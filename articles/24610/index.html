FDE Prototyping Techniques | Show a Working Prototype in One Day

Original title: FDEのプロトタイピング術｜1日で動く物を見せる

日本Fde協会 · 2026-06-21 · 8 min read

Summary

The head of the Japan FDE Association argues that FDE prototyping differs from standard software prototyping: the goal is not technical polish but showing something working within a day to raise the resolution of customer problems, since people speak candidly in front of running software. He describes three methods—paper prototypes, no-code tools, and throwaway code—matched to situations, a five-step process (pick the single most painful problem, use existing data, show at 60% completeness, incorporate feedback on the spot, agree on next actions), and common failures like overbuilding or building without the customer.

Key points

  • Working prototypes make customers speak honestly and speed decisions, reportedly 3x faster meetings.
  • Choose among paper protos, no-code tools, or throwaway code depending on the situation.
  • Show prototypes at 60% completeness to invite concrete feedback.
  • Fix feedback on the spot rather than taking it away for a week.
  • Spending over three days on a prototype leads to technical debt.

FDE prototyping differs in purpose from typical software development prototyping. When we "show something working in one day," it is not to show off our technical skills. By showing something working right in front of the customer, we rapidly raise the resolution of problems that words alone cannot convey. I serve as the representative director of the Japan FDE Association, and the biggest lesson from my career has been that "in front of something working, everyone speaks their true mind."

By reading this article, you will learn concrete steps for quickly building prototypes at customer sites and gathering feedback. If you want to learn the basics of FDE first, also see "What is FDE? A Practitioner Explains the Differences from SEs and SIers".

Why Prototyping Is the Most Important Skill for FDEs

The Choice Not to Wait for Requirements Documents

In traditional system development, the flow is typically requirements definition → high-level design → detailed design → implementation. However, in environments where FDEs work, "what should be built" is usually not even decided. In many cases, the customers themselves cannot accurately articulate their problems. That is exactly why starting by building something working and showing it is the starting point.

When I joined a manufacturing client, they came to me with a vague request: "We want to make use of our production data." Writing a requirements document would have taken half a year. That same day, I built a simple dashboard using their existing CSV data and showed it to them. Their factory manager immediately reacted: "If we could see these numbers in real time, it would change our decision-making." That one sentence instantly set the direction for what was truly needed.

Prototypes Change the "Quality of Meetings"

In meetings without prototypes, each participant is talking while picturing something different in their head. Discussions based on words alone tend to become air battles. With something working in front of you, you can have concrete conversations like "I want this changed like this" or "We don't need this feature." In my experience, meetings where a prototype is brought in reach decisions more than three times faster than meetings without one.

Three Prototyping Methods—Choosing the Right One for Each Situation

The prototyping methods used in FDE work fall broadly into three categories. What matters is not "which one is superior" but choosing the right one for the situation.

Paper Prototypes: "Draw Together with the Customer"

The greatest value of paper prototyping is that it involves the customer. I bring whiteboard markers and sticky notes to interviews and draw the screen flow on the spot as I listen. When I proceed while checking "Is this the kind of image you have?", the customer starts making modifications with their own hands. By the end of this collaborative work, half of the project's direction is already decided.

No-Code: A Tool for Building "Something You Can Touch Today"

No-code tools are used to have non-engineer customers "actually operate" something. In a project for a SaaS company, I organized the problems in a morning interview and built a simplified version of the business flow with no-code that same afternoon. The moment a customer asks "Can we start using this tomorrow?" is when I feel the strongest sense of reward as an FDE.

Code Implementation: Write It with the Assumption You Will Throw It Away

The most important thing when writing code as a prototype is deciding from the start that "this code will be thrown away." Prioritize showing something working as fast as possible over clean design or test coverage. In AI product development, I often build a demo API with a few dozen lines of Python and let the customer try it. The production implementation is rewritten from scratch after gathering customer feedback. This "courage to throw things away" determines the speed of prototyping.

The FDE Approach to Prototyping—Five Steps

Step 1: Choose the Customer's "Most Painful Problem"

What a prototype should show is a solution to the problem the customer is struggling with most. Trying to show the whole picture results in something half-baked. Ask, "Of your ten problems, which one do you want solved tomorrow?" and narrow it down to one. This question itself also helps the customer organize their thinking.

Step 2: Build as Fast as Possible Using Existing Data

Prototype data does not have to be production data. The fastest approach is to borrow the Excel or CSV files the customer uses daily and pour them in as-is. In a project for a financial institution, I used a spreadsheet the client had been using for traders as the data source and built an analytics dashboard prototype in one day.

Step 3: Show It at "60% Complete"

If you show something 100% complete, the customer thinks it is "already decided" and stops giving feedback. By deliberately showing it in a rough state, you draw out concrete opinions like "I want this changed like this." When I showed a prototype to the owner of a small business, a single comment—"Couldn't this screen be simpler?"—led me to cut half the features and dramatically improve usability.

Step 4: Reflect Feedback "On the Spot"

Taking feedback home and fixing it the following week is too slow. Whenever possible, fix it on the spot and show it again. By repeating exchanges like "Like this?" or "No, a bit more like this" right in front of the customer, the gap in understanding between you approaches zero.

Step 5: End by Deciding Concrete Next Actions

The goal of a session where you show a prototype is not to impress but to decide "what to do next." "We will implement this feature at production level by next week" or "First, we will verify the accuracy of this data"—once concrete actions are decided, the project moves forward reliably.

Common Prototyping Mistakes

Mistake 1: Overbuilding Until You Can't Throw It Away

If you spend more than three days on a prototype, it's no longer a prototype. The more time you invest, the stronger the "waste" mentality becomes, and you end up carrying code that should be discarded straight into production. This becomes technical debt down the road.

Failure 2: Building without the customer

If you build a prototype purely inside your own head, there's a risk it will diverge from what the customer actually wants. I made this mistake myself when launching an e-commerce business. Convinced that "if we build something technically good, it will sell," I proceeded with development without any customer feedback, and sales were nearly zero for the first six months. The business only got on track after I made thorough market research and interviews a habit. FDE prototyping should always be done together with the customer.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q. How is a prototype different from an MVP (Minimum Viable Product)?

A. A prototype is a "mockup for validation" and is meant to be disposable. An MVP is "a product with minimal functionality that is actually delivered to users." In FDE practice, the common flow is to confirm the direction with a prototype and then move on to MVP development.

Q. What no-code tools do you actually use?

A. It depends on the project, but for validating business workflows we often use Bubble or Retool, and for data visualization we often use Streamlit or Google Sheets + Apps Script. What matters more than tool selection is the criterion of "can we show it by the end of the day?"

Q. When we show a prototype, won't people misunderstand and think "so this is the finished product"?

A. That's a common concern, but simply saying "this is a mockup to confirm the direction" before showing it resolves the issue. In fact, showing something rough has the positive effect of making people feel comfortable that "it can still be changed," so they share their opinions more freely.
