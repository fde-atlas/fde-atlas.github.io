What Are the Methods for Visualizing Business Processes? Procedures Used in the Field by FDEs and BPMN Notation

Original title: 業務フロー可視化の方法とは？FDEが現場で使う手順とBPMN記法

Summary

This Japanese article from FDE総研 explains how forward deployed engineers (FDEs) visualize business processes at customer sites. It argues the primary goal is not proposing improvements but building a shared understanding of the current state among frontline staff, managers, and developers, which reduces requirement rework and helps identify where software should be deployed. The author outlines a five-step process—define scope, interview, observe, draw, verify with the field—and compares flowcharts, swimlane diagrams, and BPMN 2.0, recommending swimlanes for consensus-building and BPMN only for system design and automation. Common pitfalls include drawing idealized flows, inconsistent granularity, and diagrams nobody maintains; FDEs should hand over diagrams as lasting customer assets.

Key points

  • Visualization's first goal is shared understanding of the current state, not improvement proposals.
  • FDEs follow five steps: define scope, interview, observe, draw, and verify with the field.
  • Choose notation by reader: swimlanes for cross-department consensus, BPMN 2.0 for system design.
  • Five BPMN element types—events, activities, gateways, flows, pools/lanes—cover the basics.
  • Key failures: mixing As-Is with To-Be, uneven granularity, and unmaintained diagrams.

What Is Business Flow Visualization? Its Purpose and Benefits

Business flow visualization means diagramming who does what, when, and in what order, so that everyone involved shares the same understanding of the current state.

Business flow visualization has long been done as a preliminary step for creating manuals and defining system requirements. In the work of an FDE (Forward Deployed Engineer), it carries a somewhat more practical meaning. An FDE is a role that embeds itself in the customer's workplace and drives software to the point where it is actually "in use." It is also a working style popularized by Palantir. For this reason, business flow diagrams are used less as reporting materials and more as a "shared map" that lets the field and engineers point at the same thing when talking.

The primary purpose of visualization is to build "a shared understanding of the current state"

The primary purpose of visualization is not to propose improvements. It is to create a state where the field, managers, and developers can all talk about the same current state.

In actual workplaces, even the same word "reception" often refers to different scopes depending on the person in charge. It is not uncommon for the procedures managers believe are in place to differ from what is actually done in the field. Diagramming turns those gaps into concrete questions: "Who performs this step?" or "What are the criteria for this branch?" From my experience working in the field, I often find that a single remark during a discussion over the diagram—"actually, this part is..."—is more valuable than the completeness of the diagram itself.

For FDEs, the benefits are reduced rework on requirements and identifying the scope of implementation

For FDEs, business flow visualization pays off in two ways: it reduces rework on requirements, and it narrows down where software should be introduced.

BenefitDescriptionWho it mainly helps
Discovering gaps in understandingDifferent understandings of the process across departments and roles surfaceField staff and managers
Reducing requirements reworkException handling and branches can be identified before developmentFDE and development team
Identifying implementation scopeReveals where manual work, duplicate data entry, and waiting time concentrateFDE and decision-makers
Creating a baseline for measuring resultsRecording the process before implementation enables comparison afterwardDecision-makers
A handover assetEven after the FDE leaves, the customer can trace the correspondence between the process and the systemCustomer organization

For decision-makers at companies considering implementation, a major point is that it provides material for judging "where investment will produce results."

What Are the Steps to Visualize a Business Flow? What Is the FDE Field Process?

The basic approach for FDE business flow visualization is a five-stage process: "define the scope → interview → observe → draw → validate in the field."

Rather than starting to draw a diagram at a desk, you gather facts from the field first, draw, and then bring the drawing back to the field to verify it. This back-and-forth is a hallmark of the FDE approach.

StepWhat to doDeliverable
1. Scope definitionDecide the start and end points of the process and the target departmentsScope memo (start point, end point, list of stakeholders)
2. InterviewAsk each person in charge about procedures, decisions, and exceptionsInterview records
3. Field observationWatch the actual work firsthand and record differences from the interviewsObservation notes and screenshots
4. DiagrammingChoose a notation and draw the As-Is (current) flowBusiness flow diagram v0.x
5. ValidationReview the diagram together with the people in charge and make correctionsAgreed-upon business flow diagram

Steps 1-2: Define the scope before interviewing

Defining the "start point" and "end point" of the process in one sentence at the outset keeps interviews on track and makes the diagram's level of detail consistent.

For example, make the trigger and completion condition explicit, such as "from when a customer inquiry email arrives until sending the reply is finished." If you start without defining the scope, related processes keep surfacing one after another, and the diagram never gets finished.

In interviews, asking in the following order makes it less likely that things get missed.

  • Have them walk through the normal flow (the most common pattern) from start to finish
  • At each step, ask what they look at to make decisions
  • Ask "what do you do when things don't go well" to capture exception handling
  • Have them concretely list the systems, Excel files, paper forms, and chat tools they use
  • Ask how long each task takes, based on their own sense

The time figures don't need to be exact. They serve as clues when prioritizing later.

Steps 3-5: Get closer to "the actual process" through observation, diagramming, and validation

A diagram drawn from interviews alone tends to represent "the process as the person perceives it." Field observation and review sessions bring it closer to "the actual process."

During field observation, pay attention to actions the person doesn't consider "work," such as switching screens, copying and pasting, and checking with others. In practical experience, manual steps and back-and-forth confirmations that never came up in interviews are often found through observation.

For diagramming, you don't need to use a polished tool from the start. It usually goes more smoothly to sketch a rough draft with sticky notes, a whiteboard, or online tools like draw.io or Miro, and refine it during review sessions. In the review, have the person in charge explain the diagram while tracing it with their finger. Watching where they stumble over words makes it easier to spot errors in the diagram or ambiguities in the process.

Which Notation Should You Choose for Business Flows? Differences Between Flowcharts, Swimlanes, and BPMN

As a practical guideline, choose swimlane diagrams for building consensus among stakeholders, and BPMN for system implementation and automation design.

When you're unsure which notation to choose, work backward from "who will read the diagram and what it will be used for." Diagrams shown to frontline staff and diagrams handed to development teams or workflow engines require different levels of rigor.

Comparing flowcharts, swimlane diagrams, and BPMN

The three notations differ stepwise in expressive power and learning cost. It's easiest to choose by first asking whether the notation can express "who is responsible."

AspectFlowchartSwimlane diagramBPMN 2.0
Representing responsible partiesNot possible (requires separate notes)Can be shown with lanesCan be shown with pools and lanes
Representing branches and exceptionsMainly conditional branches (diamonds)About the same as flowchartsCan be expressed in detail with gateways and events
Interactions between organizationsHard to representShown with arrows crossing lanesCan be distinguished and shown with message flows
StandardizationJIS X 0121 and othersNo fixed standard; many schools of practiceOMG standard specification (ISO/IEC 19510)
Learning cost for readersLowLow to mediumMedium to high
Best suited forProcedures and decision logic handled by a single personCross-departmental business sharing and consensus buildingSystem design and workflow automation

BPMN 2.0 basics: five element types are enough to read it

BPMN 2.0 has many elements, but you can read the basics once you know five types: events, activities, gateways, flows, and pools/lanes.

BPMN (Business Process Model and Notation) is a notation developed by the industry standards body OMG (Object Management Group). BPMN 2.0 was officially released in 2011 and became the international standard ISO/IEC 19510 in 2013.

ElementShapeMeaningExample
EventCircleSomething that "happens," such as start, intermediate, or endReceiving an inquiry, deadline arrival
ActivityRounded rectangleWork (task) performed by a person or systemChecking content, drafting a response
GatewayDiamondBranching and merging (exclusive, parallel, etc.)Branching on "Is it in stock?"
Sequence flow / message flowSolid arrow / dashed arrowOrder of work / interactions between organizationsInternal procedure / sending to a customer
Pool / laneWide horizontal frameOrganization / roles or departments within an organizationA sales lane within your company's pool

One thing to watch out for is not trying to use all BPMN elements from the start. At the stage of showing diagrams to frontline staff, we believe keeping it to a swimlane-style BPMN drawn with just the five types above makes review sessions go more smoothly.

Criteria for choosing: "the reader" and "the next step"

Even for the same business process, it's realistic to switch notations by stage. There's no need to draw a perfect BPMN diagram from the beginning.

  • Understanding the current state and building consensus: Draw with a swimlane diagram (or only basic BPMN elements)
  • Identifying issues: Overlay marks on the same diagram showing manual work, waiting time, and duplicate data entry
  • Design and implementation: Redraw only the scope to be automated rigorously in BPMN 2.0 (using a BPMN-compatible tool such as Camunda Modeler makes it easier to connect to a workflow engine)

From an FDE's perspective, there is value in the same person being able to move between stages 1 and 3. Translating diagrams drawn in frontline language into a level of detail engineers can implement is one of the core roles of the FDE profession.

What are common failures in business process visualization and how to prevent them?

Common failures are three: "drawing the ideal," "inconsistent granularity," and "drawing and stopping there." All can be prevented by deciding procedures and rules in advance.

Failure patternWhat happensCountermeasure
Drawing the ideal process as the current stateAfter rollout, people say "it doesn't match reality"Separate As-Is and To-Be into different diagrams
Inconsistent granularityDiagrams become unreadable and discussions don't alignDefine one task as work one person can complete in a single sitting
Drawing and stopping thereDiagrams become outdated and no one looks at themAssign an owner and set update timing
Omitting exception handlingException handling remains as manual work after implementationAlways ask "what happens when things don't go well"

Failure 1: Drawing the ideal process (To-Be) as the current state

The most common failure is drawing the "ideal procedure" from manuals as the current state. Always separate As-Is and To-Be into different diagrams.

This failure is more likely when you interview only managers or draw based on policy documents. The countermeasures are not to skip the frontline observation in step 3, and to clearly state the state and date in the diagram title, such as "As-Is (as of September 2026)." Including a date also helps when comparing improvement effects later.

Failure 2: Inconsistent granularity makes diagrams unreadable

When a large box like "order processing" sits next to a small box like "press the button," the diagram instantly becomes hard to read. Decide granularity rules in advance.

A practical and useful definition is: "one task is work that one person can complete in a single sitting." More detailed operating procedures should be attached as notes to tasks or split into separate detail diagrams. Keep the overall diagram to a number of elements that fits on one page, and extract details as subprocesses; this serves both decision-makers and practitioners.

Failure 3: Drawing it and stopping, with no one updating it

A business flow diagram starts becoming outdated the moment it's finished. You should assume that a diagram without an assigned owner and update timing will stop being used within a few months.

FDEs eventually leave the frontline once implementation is complete. That's why it's important to set things up so diagram maintenance can be handed over to the customer. Specifically, it's good to agree in advance that the customer will appoint someone responsible for updates, that the tool will match what the customer normally uses, and that diagrams will be reviewed when systems are modified or the organization changes. Leaving the business flow diagram as "a customer asset" rather than "an FDE's working document" helps preserve the long-term value of projects involving FDEs.

Business process visualization is less about the technique of drawing diagrams and more about the technique of going into the field to gather facts and bringing the drawn diagrams back to the field for verification. Start with a swimlane diagram, and draw rigorously in BPMN 2.0 only for the scope that needs automation. We believe this staged approach is the realistic one in FDE practice.
