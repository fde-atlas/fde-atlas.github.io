How to Identify Business Systems You Can Build In-House | Thinking in Terms of Data Update Frequency

Original title: 自前で作ってよい業務システムの見分け方｜データの更新頻度で考える

TIMEWELL · 2026-09-29 · 27 min read

Summary

TIMEWELL's Ryuta Hamamoto proposes a two-axis framework—speed of change plus "weight" (complexity, transaction volume, regulatory burden)—to decide which SaaS to replace with in-house builds. He notes Gartner's Pace-Layered model actually recommends standard packages for slow-changing core systems, so low update frequency alone gives the wrong answer. But generative AI lowers build costs, making slow-and-light systems (ledgers, FAQs, internal portals, approvals, quote ledgers) candidates for self-building, while slow-but-heavy systems like accounting and payroll should stay on SaaS. The article includes a per-function decision table, five pre-build checks, and describes how the company's FDEs deliver fixed-duration projects in customers' cloud environments.

Key points

  • Two-axis framework: speed of change plus weight (complexity, volume, regulation)
  • Gartner's original model says slow-changing core systems should be bought, not built
  • Slow-and-light systems (ledgers, FAQ, portals) are now build candidates thanks to AI
  • Accounting and payroll stay on SaaS due to frequent regulatory changes like Japan's invoice system
  • Company FDEs charge by duration, deploy in customer clouds, and finish when customers can self-maintain

Hello, I am Ryuta Hamamoto of TIMEWELL, Inc.

This is the second in a five-part series on canceling expensive SaaS and rebuilding in-house. In the first article, on decision criteria, I listed the items to look at when considering cancellation. This time I address the question that comes before that: among the dozens of SaaS tools used in-house, which should be the first candidates for rebuilding?

My view is simple: start with the ones whose data is updated infrequently. Contract ledgers, internal FAQs, internal portals, requests for equipment and purchases, quotation ledgers. These systems rarely change in the shape of their fields, and the number of entries written per day is small. Now that generative AI has reduced development effort, I feel that building and maintaining these in-house has become less likely to cause problems.

However, there is a pitfall in this way of putting it. The representative framework for classifying business systems by their pace of change is Gartner's pace layering, but if you read the original source plainly, it concludes that systems of record, which change slowly, should fundamentally be held as standard packages. If you simply say "slow-changing means we can build it," you end up saying the opposite of the source. So this time, I add another axis—weight (complexity, transaction volume, regulatory compliance)—to the pace of change, and sort business functions into four categories. Things that are slow and light, like ledgers and FAQs, are candidates for in-house building; things that are slow but heavy, like accounting and payroll, remain on SaaS. I have also included a quick-reference table by business function.

To state upfront: within the scope of my research, I could not find any public framework that separates build-versus-buy by data update frequency. The two-axis classification is our own view. Even when we enter the field as FDEs, we select candidates in this order.

SoR, SoE, SoI, pace layering. First, let's align the terminology

There are three main sources for the terms used to classify business systems by their nature.

The oldest is SoR (Systems of Record) and SoE (Systems of Engagement), presented by the American management consultant Geoffrey Moore in a 2011 white paper. Moore explained SoR as the systems, tools, and data repositories onto which companies have loaded their business processes over the past several decades. SoE was positioned as something layered on top of those large SoR investments, providing access from the web, usability across various devices, and collaboration across organizations 1. SoR is what correctly keeps records such as accounting and order entry/receipt; SoE is the venue for interactions with employees and customers. If you remember it that way, you won't be far off.

In 2015, Brian Hopkins of the American research firm Forrester added SoI (Systems of Insight). It is defined as the business discipline and technology for extracting insight from data and consistently converting it into effective action 2. Think of it as the layer that connects the records accumulated in SoR and the interactions that occur in SoE to decisions and actions.

Pace layering differs in its approach from these. It is a framework announced by Gartner in February 2012 that divides applications into three layers by pace of change 3. The first layer, SoR, consists of established packages or long-standing in-house systems that support core transaction processing and manage the organization's critical master data. The pace of change is low, and the source explains the reason: the processes are established and common to most organizations, and they are often subject to regulatory requirements. The second layer, Systems of Differentiation, supports processes unique to the company and industry-specific functions; its lifespan is medium, one to three years, and it requires frequent configuration changes to match business practices and customer demands. The third layer, Systems of Innovation, is built on the spot to address new demands or opportunities and serves its purpose within 0 to 12 months.

Moore's SoR and Gartner's SoR share the same name but differ in their axes. Moore classifies by role (record or engagement), while Gartner classifies by pace of change. If you search for "SoR SoE difference," you often find explanations that mix the two, but when considering build-versus-buy, it is Gartner's pace-of-change axis that matters.

These terms have also taken root in Japan. Materials presented by the Ministry of Economy, Trade and Industry at the first Legacy System Modernization Committee in September 2024 organized the idea that data from core systems (SoR) can also be used in front-end systems (SoE) and in data analysis and insight (SoI), and stated that front-end services are increasingly using SaaS and the like along with the spread of cloud computing 4. SoR and SoE also appear as example terms in the IT Passport exam syllabus 5.

Read the source plainly, and it says "the slower the change, the more you should buy"

Here, it clashes with the view at the beginning. The source defines SoR as established packages or long-standing in-house systems, and demands safety and cost-effectiveness for environments that support core business 3. Following this definition, precisely because slow-changing SoR is common to most organizations, it is rational to hold it as standard packages, whose costs can be shared across many companies, rather than building it in-house at each company. The claim that low update frequency means you can build it yourself reads in the opposite direction if you take the source at face value.

Domestic public guidelines point in the same direction. The Ministry of Economy, Trade and Industry's DX Report 2 (December 2020) stated that companies should reject self-reliance in collaborative areas and promote the standardization of business processes in order to make use of SaaS and package software 6. The Basic Policy on Cloud Utilization for Government Information Systems (DS-310), revised by the Digital Agency in May 2025, states that SaaS should be broadly and preferentially considered from the perspective of reducing development volume, and even includes an item on "thoroughly enforcing a don't-build approach" 7. The modernization committee materials mentioned earlier also discussed the importance of Fit to Standard—aligning business processes to new systems or SaaS and migrating onto them 4.

The reality of companies reflects this too. According to IPA's "DX Trends 2025," as sourcing methods for non-core businesses and non-competitive areas, Japanese companies reported 33.8% for package software adoption, 24.4% for SaaS adoption, and 16.6% for in-house development. For the same question, the US reported 40.1% for in-house development and 11% for SaaS 8. It can be said that Japanese companies have covered the peripheral parts of their business with SaaS and packages.

Even so, based on the original source and official guidelines, where is there room to still say "you can build the slow things"? The clue lies within DS-310 itself. While DS-310 strongly recommends SaaS, it emphasizes that the recommendation is not unconditional. It states that when the number of users is expected to grow in stages, SaaS usage fees can become expensive at the operational stage, so from a lifecycle cost perspective, you need to carefully evaluate whether it truly delivers cost savings. It also says that if similar functionality to SaaS can be achieved with other managed services, both approaches should be compared and evaluated, and it urges particular caution with SaaS that is billed per account or that is expensive 7. The same document also calls for using generative AI in development.

The government's own guidelines lay out both the reasons to choose SaaS and the conditions under which not to choose it. I believe the key to discernment lies in how these exceptions are written.

Adding a "weight" axis to the speed of change

Rereading the reasons Gartner gave for why SoR changes slowly, there are two: it is common to most organizations, and it is often subject to regulatory requirements. Both are reasons to choose a package, but their natures are completely different.

"Buying because it's common" is the logic of splitting development costs. It is cheaper for one company to build something and sell it to 100 companies than for 100 companies to each build the same thing separately. This logic works more strongly the higher the cost of building. When generative AI lowers the effort of development, the simpler the mechanism, the smaller the benefit of cost-sharing, and the monthly fees paid in proportion to the number of users become more conspicuous.

"Buying because it's subject to regulation" is the logic of splitting the cost of tracking changes in laws and regulations. This barely shrinks even with generative AI. You must read the amendments and translate them into calculation specifications, finish testing by the deadline, and bear responsibility when you get it wrong. All of these are work that exists apart from the speed of writing code.

So I think about it by adding a weight axis to the speed-of-change axis. Speed of change is measured by dividing data update frequency into two: how many records are written per day, and how many times per year you want to change fields, screens, or rules. Weight is judged by three things: the complexity of calculations and exceptions; transaction volume (number of records, number of concurrent users, and which business processes stop when it goes down); and regulatory compliance (how often changes are forced from outside, and the responsibility for errors).

軽い（単純、少量、法令が薄い） 重い（複雑、大量、法令が厚い）
Slow change① Candidates to build in-house (ledgers, internal FAQ, internal portal, applications, quote ledgers)② Continue with SaaS or packages (accounting, payroll, master data referenced as the source of truth by core systems)
Fast change③ Build it, and discard it when its purpose is served (prototypes, temporary aggregations, campaigns)④ Set up an organization and decide carefully (customer-facing services, CRM core, production planning)

Overlaying this on Gartner's three layers, ② roughly corresponds to SoR as in the original, ③ to Systems of Innovation, and ④ to Systems of Differentiation. ① is a category that would fall under SoR in the original, but it is the area where only one of the reasons for choosing a package—"because it's common"—remains. Our view is that when generative AI lowers the cost of building, this is where the cost-benefit balance flips first.

The research numbers do not contradict this line of demarcation either. A 2023 experiment showed that a group using GitHub Copilot completed a task implementing an HTTP server in JavaScript 55.8% faster—a small task with clear specifications 9. On the other hand, in a randomized controlled trial conducted by METR in 2025, 16 experienced developers—who on average had contributed for years to large open-source repositories with over 22,000 stars and over a million lines—took 19% longer on tasks when they were allowed to use AI. They had predicted beforehand that they would be 24% faster, and even after finishing, they felt they had been 20% faster 10. In February 2026, METR published an estimate that tools as of the end of 2025 reduced required time by 18%, but the confidence interval ranged from -38% to +9%, crossing zero, and they noted themselves that bias in how participants were selected made the true effect hard to see 11. Google's DORA 2025 report also found that AI use has a positive relationship with development throughput and a negative relationship with delivery stability, concluding that AI does not fix teams but amplifies what already exists 12.

My reading is this: small, simple things get faster, while for large, intricate things neither speed nor stability is guaranteed. This is why ① is easy to build, and why caution is warranted for ② and ④.

Previously, in an article about how much of AI in-housing to do yourself, I wrote that the more frequently something changes, the more it should be kept in-house. That may sound like the opposite of this article, but the question is different. That one was about "who holds the adjustments," such as prompts and review procedures, with the logic that if you outsource every adjustment externally, improvement stops. This article is about "whether to keep paying monthly fees or build once and own it." Slow-changing systems are touched less often after they are built, so even if you borrow external help for the building part, the ongoing burden of ownership stays small. Putting the two together, the division of labor becomes: hold fast-changing adjustments in-house, and build slow-changing vessels cheaply and own them.

The tasks listed under ① are all areas that are often already covered by SaaS. According to the Ministry of Internal Affairs and Communications' Reiwa 7 Communications Usage Trend Survey, among companies using cloud services, 61.4% cited "internal information sharing/portals" as a service they use, the second highest after "file storage/data sharing" at 73.3%. "Payroll, financial accounting, and HR" also stood at 56.3% 13. The former is a large population that could become candidates for rebuilding, while the latter is an area better left as-is and paid for — that is the conclusion of this analysis.

The reason accounting and payroll were placed under ② is the frequency of legal revisions. In Japan, the invoice system started on October 1, 2023 14. From June 2024, fixed-amount tax reductions were implemented in payroll withholding, and payers of wages were required to respond in calculating withholding tax amounts 15. For the December 2025 year-end adjustment, it became necessary to respond to revisions of the basic deduction and employment income deduction under the Reiwa 7 tax reform 16. In roughly two years, there have been at least three dated changes. SaaS vendors take on this tracking collectively for all their customers. If you build it yourself, you must employ people to read the revisions, translate them into specifications, and test them by the deadline. AI only shrinks the implementation part of this; the reading and the responsibility do not shrink.

The judgments in the table are only a starting point. Gartner also writes that the same application can be classified into different layers depending on the company, based on how it is used and its relationship to the business model, and that it migrates between layers as it matures 3. Quotations are an easy example to estimate. A quotation ledger that merely manages numbers and history is ①, but a quotation for manufacturing that builds up costs from drawings and processes falls under ④, where the calculation itself is the competitive edge. Even with the same name "quotation," where it is placed varies by company.

Accounting and HR/labor also do not need to be entirely placed in SaaS

In the quick-reference table, accounting, payroll, and attendance were placed under "keep the SaaS," but to be precise, what remains in the SaaS is the source of record and statutory processing. Accounting and HR/labor SaaS contain both the parts determined by law and the parts determined by your company. What you may build yourself is the part your company determines.

TaskParts to leave to the SaaS (or specialists)Parts you may build yourself
AccountingJournal entry source of record, closing, tax filings, storage of electronic transaction dataExpense claim entry points, budget management, management accounting aggregation and screens

Quotations and invoicingInvoice issuance, payment reconciliation, storage of transaction documentsHow quotations are calculated, price tables, ledgers of deals and quotations

In form, you build your own screens that all employees touch, and pass the data via API to the accounting or HR/labor SaaS. Only accounting or HR staff will open the SaaS screens, so you may no longer need seats for other employees. The more an account-based SaaS bills proportionally to the number of users, the greater the effect of this approach. In Moore's terms, it means leaving the SoR untouched and layering your own SoE on top of it 1.

Quotations are particularly suited to this form. The invoice system requirements apply to invoices and the like 14, and what is mainly required of quotations is the storage requirements when exchanged electronically 17. And how quotations are issued — how prices are set and how discounts are thought about — differs by company. As written in the previous section, for manufacturing quotations the calculation itself is the competitive edge. Leave invoicing and payment reconciliation to the SaaS, and keep the quotation creation part in-house. We believe this division is realistic.

One caution. Some SaaS or package contracts include clauses that charge for indirectly allowing people without licenses to use the service via APIs or integrations (called indirect access or multiplexing). Before building your own screens and reducing seats, check the contract for this clause. If it exists, the number of seats you can reduce changes.

Our company also reads accounting SaaS data via API and displays it on our internal portal, while keeping the journal entry source of record on the accounting SaaS side.

Distinguishing what looks light but is heavy

Even if something looks like ①, there are five things to verify before building it. In each case, heaviness hides behind apparent simplicity.

First, whether you store the electronic documents themselves. According to the National Tax Agency, when you send or receive electronic data corresponding to paper documents that must be retained — such as purchase orders, contracts, quotations, and invoices — you are obligated to store that electronic data. As a rule, three things are required: measures to prevent tampering, provision of displays for verification, and searchability by the three elements of date, amount, and transaction partner 17. A contract ledger that only manages "which contract expires when" stays light. If it also serves as a storage location for electronic contracts, it needs a design that keeps a history of corrections and deletions. It is not that you cannot build it. Whether you include the requirements from the start makes an enormous difference in later rework.

Second, whether that ledger is the source of record. In Gartner's definition, managing an organization's critical master data is the role of the SoR 3. If you move masters that accounting and ordering reference — such as items, business partners, and account codes — into your own ledger, you take on ② while intending ①. It is safer to make your own ledger read-only from the source of record and leave the writing to the core system side.

The third criterion is the granularity of permissions and whether people outside the company will use it. In an experiment presented at ACM CCS, an international security conference, in 2023, participants who used an AI assistant wrote more insecure code than those who did not, and were more likely to believe their code was secure 18. Although this was a result from the models of that time, I believe login, permissions, and audit logs are not something you should have AI write from scratch every time. They should be placed on a verified common foundation. If it will be opened externally, like a portal used by business partners, raise the weight assessment by one level.

The fourth criterion is what stops when it stops. If the internal FAQ is down for half a day, business continues. If the ledger used for shipping instructions goes down, shipping stops. Even with few write operations, anything whose downtime has a large impact belongs on the heavy side.

The fifth criterion is whether there is someone who will maintain it over time. In the same IPA survey, the top challenge cited by Japanese companies pursuing in-house development was "difficulty securing and training personnel," at 82.3% 8. Even if the cost of building goes down, if no one is there to add fields, answer user inquiries, and make the few changes per year, you simply end up with one more ownerless application. DORA's point that AI "amplifies what already exists" means that in an organization without owners, it amplifies the ownerless state.

Rank the items that pass these five criteria. The inventory procedure we recommend is as follows.

  • Add five columns to the list of SaaS you use: daily write count, number of times fields or rules changed in the past year, relevant laws and regulations, number of connected other systems, and user count and billing model
  • Place them in ① through ④ based on speed of change and weight
  • From ①, first select those billed per account whose costs balloon as users increase (this is exactly the type DS-310 warns about 7)
  • Check whether data can be taken out, and verify the format and procedure (Practical guide to SaaS cancellation and migration)
  • Compare the five-year total cost of building and owning versus continuing to pay (Estimating the five-year total cost of SaaS)
  • Decide where to place it (Designing where to host business apps on your own cloud)

The five columns in step 1 are limited to items that can be filled in from invoices and each SaaS's admin console, plus brief interviews with the field. If by step 3 the candidates have narrowed down to one or two, I consider that an excellent result.

How this differs from contract development

When you decide to build a ①-type system, who you ask changes the outcome.

When contract developers build a ①-type system, the norm is to build each company's ledger or FAQ from scratch and bill for the hours spent. Yet the skeleton of a ①-type system is remarkably similar across companies: login and permissions, change history, search, and approval steps. What differs is little more than field names and approval routes. If the skeleton is rebuilt every time, the customer pays for those hours every time.

Our FDEs hold this skeleton as a shared mechanism on the product side, and place only the fields and approval routes that differ per customer as that company's configuration. Common patterns found in the field are fed back into the product, so the next company can be built faster and more cheaply by that margin. That is why, for us, field hours are an investment, not revenue. The fewer hours per customer, the better. The ending is different too. It does not end at acceptance; it ends when the person in charge can add fields and change approval routes on their own. The finished state is one that runs without us.

I believe this model is close to the relationship the Ministry of Economy, Trade and Industry anticipated in DX Report 2. That report stated that when user companies move toward in-house development, internal staff often cannot respond immediately, so demand will grow for vendors to support the transition to in-house development and transfer skills while working alongside them. It further stated this should not become a business of on-site vendor staffing, but a relationship of growing together (co-education) and building together (co-creation) 6. Rebuilding a ①-type system is just the right size for testing this relationship on a small scale.

What our FDEs can do

Here is the scope our FDEs (Forward Deployed Engineers—engineers who go into the customer's site and turn problems into working software) take on when rebuilding SaaS.

First, we sit in on the inventory. Together we review the SaaS list and invoices, fill in the five columns from the previous section, and place items in ① through ④. Things that should not be built are also made clear at this stage. Anything placed in ② is excluded from the rebuild scope.

Next, we select just one item from ①, and on day one you see a working prototype running on real data with personal information and the like masked. We never replace multiple systems at once. The period is 45 to 90 days, and the completion conditions (e.g., the person in charge can add fields and change approval routes themselves) are decided up front. Cost is set by duration, not by accumulated hours.

The hosting location is, in principle, the customer's cloud environment. The processing region is designed to be selectable according to the customer's requirements. The AI models and services used are limited to those that do not train on customer data. We are certified under ISO/IEC 27001 (scope of registration: planning, development, and provision of SaaS products leveraging AI technology).

We also plan the cancellation process together: the notice deadline calculated backward from the renewal date, data export testing, and the parallel operation period. The content described in the fourth piece in the series, Practical guide to cancellation and migration, is used directly as the procedure. The overall approach is summarized on our FDE service page.

Our practice and its limits

We ourselves divide our internal systems along these same lines. Our internal portal is built and operated in-house, and it hosts the contract ledger and deadline management, the PC and equipment asset ledger, the ledger of SaaS in use, expense approval requests and approvals, travel requests, policies, security training, weekly goals, and daily reports. The items placed in ① in the quick-reference table line up almost exactly. On the other hand, accounting, HR and labor, and quote issuance remain on cloud SaaS. The asset ledger is in-house, but the accounting fixed-asset ledger lives on the SaaS side, and our in-house ledger merely records its numbers. This is us practicing, within our own company, the second checkpoint from the previous section: treat the system of record as read-only.

Attendance tracking takes a slightly different form. The clock-in entry point sits on our in-house portal, while the original records and evidence remain on the SaaS side. This is exactly Moore's pattern of layering a SoE on top of a SoR. Quote issuance stays on SaaS because billing through payment collection is one continuous flow, and invoice requirements also apply. What we keep in-house is only the ledger portion that links deals to quotes.

Not everything went well. We built a daily report entry screen on our internal portal, but right after launch almost nobody used it. Later, we added a mechanism that prepares drafts from the previous day's plans and records, plus an evening nudge. Even if the cost of building goes down, the effort required to make something actually used does not. If you build it yourself, you need to budget for that effort as well.

We also note what we will not take on. We do not accept projects that replace the systems of record for accounting, payroll, or attendance from SaaS with in-house builds. In the area of ②, this would force customers to keep up with regulations themselves. The same applies to projects that migrate the master data that core systems reference as the authoritative source. If not even one person in the company can be assigned to maintain what is built, we do not start. Systems used by many external parties such as business partners may look like ①, but we treat them as the heavy side and discuss timelines and staffing separately.

The two-axis framing itself has limits. The idea of separating build vs. buy by data update frequency appears in neither public guidelines nor research firm frameworks; it is only our own assessment. The quick-reference table's judgments are also rough guides assuming a typical company. And since we are a small company, the number of sites we can take on simultaneously is limited.

Conclusion

  • SoR and SoE are a classification by role (Moore, 2011), while Pace Layering is a classification by speed of change (Gartner, 2012); the axes differ
  • Whether you read Gartner's original sources directly or Japan's public guidelines, slow-changing core systems are fundamentally packages or SaaS. "It's slow, so we can build it" alone contradicts the original sources
  • The original sources cite two reasons why SoR changes slowly: "it is common" and "it is subject to regulation." Of these, only the former weakens as a reason to choose packages in the age of generative AI
  • That is why we add an axis of weight (complexity, transaction volume, regulatory compliance) to speed of change. Slow and light ledgers, FAQs, internal portals, application forms, and quote ledgers are candidates for in-house builds
  • Accounting and payroll are slow but heavy. From 2023 through 2025, deadline-bound regulatory revisions have continued, so continuing with SaaS is the more rational choice
  • However, what remains in-house should be only the systems of record and statutory processing. Screens touched by all employees can be built in-house, reducing the SaaS seats down to those of the responsible staff (check the indirect access clauses of your contract first)
  • Before building, verify five points: document retention, whether it is a system of record, permissions and external use, the impact of downtime, and who will maintain it

Looking back, "build or buy" is not a question to be decided all at once. Start by adding five columns to your SaaS inventory, find one item that falls into ①, and check whether it is truly light. If you start there, even a failure stays small.
