How to Write a Resume for FDE Roles | Structure and Examples of Tailoring Your Achievements to Boost Your Pass Rate

Original title: FDEの職務経歴書の書き方｜通過率を上げる構成と実績の書き分け例

FDE総研 · 2026-08-18 · 10 min read

Summary

This Japanese guide from FDE総研 explains how to write a resume (rirekisho/shokumukeirekisho) for Forward Deployed Engineer (FDE) applications. It argues that FDE screening evaluates whether a candidate turned vague customer requests into defined problems and shipped working solutions, not lists of languages and frameworks. Recommended structure: a 3-5 line summary leading with customer-facing delivery, then skills, then project details in reverse chronological order taking over half of an A4 2-3 page document. Each project follows a five-part template: customer, problem, role, actions, results. Achievements should show before/after states; when NDAs block numbers, use ratios, frequency, or headcount instead. The article also gives tailored advice for SWE, consulting, sales, and analytics backgrounds, and separates roles between resume (judgment history) and portfolio (implementation evidence).

Key points

  • FDE resumes are judged on problem definition and personal decisions, not tech stacks.
  • Open the summary by stating you delivered working software from undefined requirements on-site.
  • Give over 50% of space to the 2-3 most recent projects, each in a five-part format.
  • Replace confidential figures with reduction rates, frequency, or people affected.
  • Resume carries judgment and context; portfolio carries code and working demos.

What Gets Evaluated in an FDE Resume?

In an FDE resume, what gets evaluated is not the breadth of technologies you have handled, but "how you turned vague requests into concrete problems and what you moved." FDE (Forward Deployed Engineer) is a role defined by Palantir, involving embedding in the customer's site and taking responsibility through implementation. Therefore, in document screening, it does not end with checking a list of languages and frameworks as in typical software engineer (SWE) hiring. In reality, resumes from which "whose problem was solved, and how" cannot be discerned are unlikely to pass, even if they meet the technical requirements.

Where Do Recruiters Read First in a Resume?

Recruiters first read the 3-5 line career summary and use it to judge whether you have experience building things with customers. Resumes that open with a list of technology stacks tend to be treated as SWE applications. If you are targeting FDE roles, it is effective to state your positioning in the first line of the summary, such as "I have embedded at customer sites and delivered working products starting from undefined requirements."

Practitioners in the field say, "Most of the impression is formed by the career summary and the description of the most recent one or two projects, so it is more efficient to emphasize recent work rather than writing older projects in detail." Readers' time is limited, and writing all projects with equal weight blurs the focus (this is not an absolute rule, as emphasis varies by company and hiring stage).

What Gets Points Added or Deducted in an FDE Resume?

Descriptions that show "judgment" and "customer interaction" earn points, while descriptions of only task content lose them. Even for the same project, the amount of information conveyed varies greatly depending on how it is written.

PerspectiveDescriptions that earn pointsDescriptions that lose points
Problem definition"Interviewed the customer about their requests and redefined the root cause as XX""Participated in requirements definition"
Implementation"Presented a prototype in one week and finalized specifications through dialogue""Responsible for development in Python"
Judgment"Proposed cutting two features to prioritize the deadline and obtained agreement""Managed the schedule"
Results"Automated manual data aggregation, greatly reducing the workload of the staff in charge""Delivered the system"
Role"Coordinated directly with non-engineer staff on the customer side""Joined as a team member"

What descriptions that lose points have in common is that the subject is "the project," making "you" invisible. In FDE selection, what is asked is not the team's achievements but what the applicant personally decided. The full picture of required skills is organized in Skills Required for FDEs, and the definition of the role itself in What Is an FDE?.

What Structure Should an FDE Resume Follow?

The basic structure is four blocks: career summary, skills, project details (newest first), and self-promotion, with the most space devoted to project details. No special format is needed; a standard Japanese resume format works fine. What matters is the order and allocation of space. Keep it to 2-3 A4 pages, devoting more than half to descriptions of the most recent 2-3 projects, which is an easy-to-handle format.

In What Order Should the Overall Structure Be Arranged?

An inverted pyramid structure, placing the conclusion (what kind of person you are) first and then arranging supporting projects from newest to oldest, is the most readable. Writing in chronological order from oldest delays the reader's arrival at what they most want to know: your current abilities.

BlockApproximate lengthWhat to write
Career summary3-5 linesExperience with customer interaction, technical areas of strength, patterns of roles you have taken on
Transferable skills10-15 linesLanguages, platforms, and data processing, plus experience in business understanding and negotiation
Project details (recent)Over 50% of the totalCustomer, challenge, your judgment, implementation, and results for each project
Project details (past)3-5 lines eachOverview and role only. Keep it to demonstrating the breadth of your technical experience
Self-promotionAbout 10 linesWhy you aspire to be an FDE and first-hand experiences that support it

What Is the Template for Describing Each Project?

Writing each project in five items: customer, challenge, role, approach, and results, makes it easy for readers to extract the material for judgment. Fixing the order below in bullet points makes your resume easy to compare across projects.

  • Customer and background: Industry and scale, and why the request arose (company name within the disclosable range)
  • Challenge: The gap between the customer's initial stated request and the actual root cause
  • Your role: Team size and the scope you were responsible for and decided on within it
  • Approach: The technologies used and the design trade-offs you made there
  • Results: Quantitative or qualitative changes and subsequent operation and rollout

Of these five items, those from SWE backgrounds tend to omit 1 and 2, while those from consulting backgrounds tend to omit 4. After finishing, check which one is thin in your own resume; this also makes it easier to predict where interview questions will land. Preparing for the interview stage together with FDE Interview Preparation reduces overlap.

How Should FDE Achievements Be Written to Convey Them Effectively?

Achievements are best conveyed by writing "before and after the change" as a set; even without numbers, the difference in state can serve as a substitute. Writing only "built ◯◯" fails to tell the reader what it meant for the customer. Presenting the state before and after the work side by side is the most concise way to demonstrate value.

How do you express achievements when you can't provide numbers?

When confidentiality obligations prevent you from disclosing numbers, substitute one of the following: percentages, frequency, time required, or number of people involved. It's not uncommon to work on projects where you can't write actual amounts or counts, but writing only "contributed to efficiency improvements" conveys zero information.

Information you can't discloseAlternative ways to write it
Actual sales or cost figures"Reduction rate" or "approximate payback period"
Actual processing counts"Number of processing runs per day" or "percentage reduced from manual work"
Client name"Manufacturing company (several thousand employees)" or "a municipal government's front-office department"
Specific work detailsRaise the level of abstraction one step, e.g., "an approval flow spanning multiple departments"
Numbers never measuredChanges in state, e.g., "the daily work of three staff members became unnecessary"

Among practitioners, a common view is that "rather than inflating numbers, it's safer to honestly state facts you haven't measured and instead describe changes on the ground concretely—that holds up better in interviews." This is because numbers fabricated on paper become impossible to explain the moment interviewers probe the underlying assumptions.

How do you articulate achievements in client negotiations?

For negotiation achievements, writing "the situation where conflict arose" and "the decision you made" conveys the substance of your coordination skills. Descriptions like "built a good relationship with the client" can be written by virtually every applicant, so they don't differentiate you.

The following four topics are easy to write about.

  • A situation where you declined a client's request or replaced it with an alternative
  • A situation where you accepted a specification change but negotiated to drop something else in exchange
  • A situation where you explained technical constraints to a non-engineer stakeholder and gained agreement
  • Mediating when opinions diverged between frontline users and the management department

In each case, three sentences suffice: "the other party said X → I decided Y → the result was Z." Some also say that including one case that didn't go well, paired with what you learned afterward, increases your credibility.

How should you write if you're aiming for FDE roles without direct experience or from a different profession?

When applying without direct experience, the basic strategy is to translate experiences from your own career history that structurally resemble FDE work and rearrange them. Even without experience under the FDE title, many professions include experiences of "turning someone's words into a problem, then building and delivering a solution yourself." The key is to rewrite in terms of actions, not job titles.

What should SWE and SIer backgrounds add?

For those from SWE or SIer backgrounds, since implementation skills already come across, the quickest improvement is to add client interactions and your own decisions. While they often have experience across the full development lifecycle, their career documents tend to focus on which phases they were responsible for.

Candidates to add include: experience turning requests from other internal departments into requirements, experience explaining things to non-engineers, and experience interacting directly with clients during incident response. The differences between contract development and FDE work are organized in Differences between FDE and SES/contract development, and the differences from SWE in Differences between FDE and SWE.

How should people from consulting, sales, or data analysis write theirs?

For those from non-engineering backgrounds, the top priority is writing the concrete names of deliverables you built with your own hands. Even if your problem definition and negotiation descriptions are solid, the selection process won't advance unless you convey that you're "someone who can build."

BackgroundAlready strong areasAreas to reinforce in your career document
ConsultantProblem definition, structuring, documentationCode you wrote yourself and the scope in which it ran
B2B sales / customer successClient understanding, expectation managementHands-on experience with technical verification and automation
Data analystSQL and analysis, understanding of business dataImplementation experience putting analysis results into production
In-house ITBusiness understanding, interdepartmental coordinationExperience proposing to and negotiating with external clients

Reinforcement doesn't mean fabricating achievements. Even a small tool built outside of work can serve as material if you can write "whose problem it solved and what it solved." For an overview of switching from another profession, see Aiming for FDE without prior experience.

How should you divide roles between the career document and the portfolio?

The principle is to have the career document carry the "history of decisions" and the portfolio carry the "evidence of implementation," without duplicating content. Writing the same project description in both only increases the length without giving the reader more information. Divide the roles, and make the career document link to the portfolio for easier handling.

What is each document's role?

Putting context and decisions in the career document, and working products and code in the portfolio, ensures the necessary information is available at each stage of selection.

ItemCareer documentPortfolio
Main readersHR, hiring managersFrontline engineers, technical interviewers
Core contentClients, problems, decisions, resultsWorking products, code, design intent
Work projectsDescribe within what can be disclosedIn principle, do not include
Personal development / OSSA 1-2 line mention with a linkInclude the full work; explain the problem and design in the README
Length2-3 A4 pagesNarrow down to 3-5 items (quality over quantity)

If you prepare a portfolio, as an FDE candidate it's considered effective to include at least one project that "solved someone's problem." This is because the validity of the problem setting demonstrates fit for the role more readily than the polish of a technical demo.

How much can you write about projects under confidentiality obligations?

Work projects can be described if you omit the client name, specific figures, and screens or design documents, and abstract them into the industry and the type of problem. When in doubt, the criteria are two questions: "Can the client be identified from this description?" and "Does it include information not found in public materials?"

Examples of abstraction include replacing the company name with "a manufacturing company with about a thousand employees," the system name with "the inventory management area of a core system," and actual figures with "reduction rate." Depending on the contract, even mentioning the industry may be restricted, so if you're unsure it's safest to check your previous or current employer's policies. For parts you can't judge, it's better to leave them blank and supplement verbally in the interview; this is unlikely to disadvantage you in selection.

A resume is not a document you write once and consider finished. If you adopt a workflow where you only adjust the first line of your professional summary and how you present your most recent project for each application, you can reduce the burden of updating while making it easier to improve your pass rate.
