FDE Travel Ratio and Travel Expense Reimbursement: Must-Ask Questions Before Comparing Offers

Original title: FDE 出差比例与差旅报销：比较 offer 前必问

FDE Times · 2026-11-10 · 5 min read

Summary

This article compares four FDE job postings and finds a consistent gap: travel percentages (25–50%) are stated, but who pays for client travel is almost never specified. Only Genlogs commits to 100% reimbursement of approved travel; Foxglove, Relevance AI, and Tungsten Automation list perks like retreats or commuter budgets that don't answer the question. In the US, unreimbursed travel costs are largely non-deductible for employees, so unclaimed expenses directly cut take-home pay. The author recommends emailing recruiters, getting the policy in writing, and comparing offers net of estimated out-of-pocket travel costs.

Key points

  • Only Genlogs of four postings promises 100% reimbursement of approved travel.
  • Perks like retreats and commuter budgets don't cover client travel costs.
  • US employees generally cannot deduct unreimbursed travel expenses anymore.
  • At 30–50% travel, ten weeks of self-paid dinners could cost $600–1,000.
  • Ask five questions in writing and get reimbursement terms into the offer.

Before comparing two offers, ask about the travel reimbursement policy first—every unreimbursed expense comes out of your own pocket.

Key Takeaways

  • FDE job postings typically state a 25–50% travel percentage, but rarely say who pays; Genlogs is a rare example that promises 100% reimbursement.
  • Perks like a commuting budget or all-expenses-paid team offsites do not answer the question of who pays for client travel.
  • In the US, unreimbursed business travel expenses are now nearly impossible to deduct for most employees, so ask explicitly and get the answer in writing.

Foxglove's FDE job posting is straightforward: this role requires 25–50% travel to client sites.

The benefits list that follows includes all-expenses-paid company offsites and a monthly $300 commuting or office space budget.

But not a single sentence explains: when you fly to a client, who pays for the flights and hotel.

Foxglove is not alone. Tungsten Automation's FDE (Churn Prevention) posting states the role requires 30–40% travel for strategic, client-facing onsite projects, yet says nothing about expenses. Relevance AI puts "Travel Required" right in the job title and may station you onsite for up to three months, likewise without explaining who covers the costs.

For Forward Deployed Engineers (FDEs), travel is a core part of the job. An industry guide on fde.academy estimates that travel typically ranges from 25–50%, depending on the company and industry.

So if you compare two offers without understanding the travel reimbursement policy, the salary numbers you're comparing may themselves be wrong. Ask about travel costs first, then discuss salary.

Every posting states the percentage; almost none says who pays

Put four FDE job postings side by side and the pattern is obvious: travel percentages are stated quite specifically, but only one posting spells out an expense policy.

GenlogsUp to 30%100% reimbursement for all approved company travel and expensesYes
Foxglove25–50%, to client sitesAll-expenses-paid company offsites, monthly $300 commuting or office space budgetNo

Genlogs stands out because both pieces of information appear together: a travel cap ("up to 30%") and a promise of 100% reimbursement for approved travel. With both a cap and a clear payer, candidates know exactly what they're signing up for. The other three only give half the picture.

The way the percentage is expressed also varies. "Up to 30%" is a cap; "25–50%" is a range whose upper bound is double the lower bound. In the comparison table in the fde.academy guide, Palantir has roughly 25% of time at client sites, but that page likewise doesn't say who covers travel costs or whether a daily allowance is provided.

Benefit items can mislead you

The trap here isn't that the posting says nothing—it's that some items look like they answer the question. Relevance AI's posting includes one expense-related item: public transit fares and parking can be pre-tax deductible. That's a daily commuting benefit, not the cost of flying to a client and living near them for three months.

Foxglove is the same. "All-expenses-paid offsites" refers to internal company gatherings, and the monthly $300 budget is for commuting or office space. Both are genuine benefits, but neither touches on the travel that could take up half your working time.

The skill needed here is exactly what an FDE uses daily when facing clients: separating what's written from your own assumptions. When reading a client requirements document, you'd probe every line for what it actually promises. The benefits section of a job posting deserves the same scrutiny.

Unreimbursed expenses are a real loss

US tax rules show why this is no small matter. According to IRS Publication 463, if an employee fully accounts to the employer, is fully reimbursed, and returns any excess amounts, neither the expenses nor the reimbursements need to appear on the tax return. This is the logic of an "accountable plan"—a reimbursement policy backed by complete documentation.

When the company doesn't reimburse, two rules in the same publication lead to the same outcome: you bear the costs yourself. Since miscellaneous itemized deductions subject to the 2% floor have been suspended, unreimbursed travel expenses are no longer deductible for most employees.

For meals specifically, Publication 463 notes that unreimbursed meal expenses remain subject to the 50% limit, including meals during business travel.

Consider a hypothetical calculation. Based on a five-day work week, a 30% travel percentage is roughly one and a half days per week. If you pay out of pocket for a $40 dinner on every travel day without reimbursement, over ten weeks that's 15 days and $600—not counting taxis and overweight baggage fees.

At 50%, the same calculation over ten weeks comes to 25 days and $1,000; a three-month onsite engagement like the one in Relevance AI's posting would push that number even higher.

Tax rules differ by country; if you work outside the US, consult a local tax professional. But the core conclusion holds everywhere: unreimbursed expenses come straight out of your real income.

Five questions to ask before comparing salaries

The first question is the simplest: who covers client travel costs—the company or the client? Is it fully reimbursed? Genlogs has written "100%" into its posting, so that standard is a reasonable expectation, not an extra favor. If the recruiter answers "it depends on the project," follow up by asking how the most recent project handled it.

The second question is about cash flow: do you have to pay upfront and wait for reimbursement, or does the company book travel and issue a corporate card? How long does reimbursement take? An engineer who just changed jobs and has to front several flights in a row is effectively giving the company an interest-free loan.

The third question is per diem versus actual-expense reimbursement. A per diem is a fixed daily amount—simple to calculate, but potentially insufficient in high-cost cities. Actual-expense reimbursement is more accurate, but you must keep receipts and submit claims according to the prescribed process—that is, the accountable plan approach described earlier.

The fourth question probes the word "approved." Genlogs's promise applies only to "approved" travel and expenses, so for any offer with similar conditions, ask: who approves? Before or after the trip? What kinds of expenses are typically rejected?

A policy that looks generous on paper but has an opaque approval process can still leave you paying out of pocket.

The fifth question targets long-term onsite engagements. Spending three months at a client site means asking about long-term accommodation, flights home mid-engagement, and whether "25–50%" is calculated weekly, quarterly, or annually. An annual average can mask a quarter where you're barely home.

Asking the right questions is itself a demonstration of competence

Many candidates feel awkward discussing money, fearing they'll seem difficult. For FDE roles, it's the opposite: the person who clarifies terms before committing is exactly who the company wants to send to clients. The way you ask about travel policy—calm, specific, with a written record—shows employers in advance how you'll conduct client requirements discovery.

In practice, email your questions to the recruiter rather than just asking verbally, and ask for the travel reimbursement policy to be written into the offer letter or attached as a policy document. When comparing two offers, don't just put the two salary figures side by side—compare salary minus the out-of-pocket costs you estimate based on each company's travel ratio.

If you're preparing a portfolio or interviews, you can use this approach as an example: read the requirements and clarify the scope before getting started. This is exactly the skill FDE employers value, and you just applied it to your own contract.

The travel ratio tells you how long you'll be away from home; who covers the costs usually only becomes clear if you ask.
