What a forward-deployed engineer is

A forward-deployed engineer is a software engineer who works inside a customer’s business to make a product actually work there. They sit with the people who will use it, connect it to the systems already running and ship changes until the problem is solved.

The defining feature is close involvement in a customer’s deployment. The engineer needs to understand both the software and the operation it serves: the people, records, access rules and exceptions. The role may involve on-site work, remote collaboration or a combination.

“Forward deployed” means the engineer works where the software is used, inside the customer’s operation, rather than back at headquarters. Palantir calls the role a forward deployed software engineer (FDSE); AI companies such as OpenAI use FDE.

Here’s where the role came from, what the work looks like day to day, the skills it takes, how it compares with similar roles, what it pays and when a company should bring one in.

How companies use the role

Many people tie the idea to Palantir, and AI companies such as OpenAI now hire for it. The details differ by employer, but the job is the same: own the result inside one customer’s operation, not just the code.

Palantir: many capabilities for one customer

Palantir describes its forward-deployed engineers as engineers responsible for technical and operational customer outcomes. Its distinction is between developing a capability used by many customers and applying multiple capabilities to a particular customer’s needs.

Inside Palantir the two jobs have nicknames. A product engineer is a “Dev” and an FDSE is a “Delta”. Devs sit in product development and Deltas in business development. Deltas still do code reviews and monitor production, and they carry what they learn in the field back to the product teams.

OpenAI: from prototype to production adoption

OpenAI’s forward-deployed engineering role spans discovery, technical scoping, system design, implementation and production rollout alongside customer teams. These role descriptions illustrate the delivery responsibility; the exact remit depends on the employer and engagement.

Its San Francisco posting-sf-san-francisco/) measures success by production adoption, measurable workflow impact and evaluation feedback that changes product and model roadmaps. A demo that nobody uses does not count.

Anthropic: Claude in production workflows

Anthropic’s FDE posting puts the engineer inside strategic customers to build production applications with Claude. The deliverables are concrete: MCP servers, sub-agents and agent skills that run in production workflows. Travel to customer sites runs 25 to 50 percent.

Google Cloud: from prototype to production-grade agents

Google Cloud’s generative AI FDE role takes work from rapid prototypes to production-grade agentic workflows, such as multi-agent systems and MCP servers. It names the blockers outright: integration complexity, data readiness and state management.

Salesforce: a builder paired with a strategist

Salesforce hires FDEs to design, build and deploy agentic AI inside enterprise customer environments. Each works with a Deployment Strategist, a customer-facing advisor who shapes the AI strategy and drives adoption, on solutions that go live in production from day one.

Every one of these postings measures the job in production, not prototypes.

Why the model works beyond Palantir

Marty Cagan of SVPG argues that the idea applies far more broadly than the hard cases Palantir is known for. The condition is that field learning flows back into the platform. Otherwise a company piles up large one-off builds it has to maintain forever.

Why companies are hiring forward-deployed engineers now

MIT NANDA’s 2025 report on AI in business found that 95 percent of organizations were getting zero return on generative AI, despite $30–40 billion in enterprise investment. Just 5 percent of integrated pilots were extracting millions in value. The divide, the report says, does not seem to be driven by model quality.

That matches what we see. Late-2025 models could not finish many jobs; today’s frontier models hold far more context, far more accurately. The bottleneck has moved from the model to the deployment: access, integration, QA and trust. That work happens inside the customer’s business, so the engineer has to be there too.

Investors noticed. Andreessen Horowitz called it “trading margin for moat” in June 2025: hands-on implementation costs margin early, as it did for ServiceNow and Workday at their IPOs, then becomes the reason customers stay. Most stalled pilots never reached production. Ours are built to: Clive Collective’s platform was working in five days, then spent two weeks in quality assurance and security before it carried real work.

What a forward-deployed engineer does day to day

A forward-deployed engineer moves between understanding the job, implementing the system and checking it with the people who will use it. Planning and delivery inform each other. Here’s the work in five steps:

Sit with usersLearn the real job

Connect systemsCRM, phones, data, identity

Ship smallA scoped working version

MeasureTests, review queues, monitoring

Hand overA named owner on day one

1. Sit with the users

Watch how the work is done today, where the hours go and what a good outcome looks like. The useful details are rarely in a requirements document: the spreadsheet someone keeps on the side, the customer who always calls instead of emailing, the step everyone skips.

2. Connect the systems

Wire the model or product into the CRM, phones, email, databases and identity the business already runs. Decide which system owns each record, who is allowed to see it and what happens when an API is down.

Each connection needs an owner, a permission rule and a plan for when it fails.

3. Ship small and often

Put a scoped working version in front of users, review their feedback and agree on the next release. A narrow release that people use teaches more than a broad one that waits for sign-off. Teams that wait for the perfect launch rarely launch.

4. Measure it

Build the tests, review queues and monitoring that show whether the AI is right, and catch it when it is not. Real cases from the team make the best test set, because they include the messy requests a demo never sees.

5. Hand it over

Leave documentation and a team that can run it, or stay on to run it with them. Either way, someone named owns the system the day it goes live.

Skills a forward-deployed engineer needs

The role sits between engineering and the customer, and few engineers are strong at both. OpenAI’s posting-sf-san-francisco/) asks for five or more years of engineering or deployment experience that includes customer-facing work. Anthropic asks for four or more years in a technical, customer-facing role; Google Cloud asks for eight in cloud computing or a technical customer-facing role.

Years are the floor. In our experience, a great forward-deployed engineer is a one- or two-person team that understands every part of the business. Here’s what that takes:

1. A proven record of solving hard problems

The best ones build complex, difficult systems for themselves, just to see if they can. Nobody asked them to. That habit is what carries them through a client problem nobody has solved before.

2. They push AI to its limits, and know where the limits are

They use these systems every day and keep testing what they can and cannot do. That is the only way to know which jobs a model can own, which need a check and how to make the AI do exactly what you need.

3. Deep knowledge of code and of software from the user’s side

A firm grasp of code and systems is the baseline. Just as important is a huge reference library of software they have used, good and bad, so they can tell a clumsy screen from a great one before a user complains.

4. Systems engineers, not just software engineers

They understand people and user flows: who touches the system, in what order and what they need at each step. The software is one part of a system that includes the team using it.

5. Business sense: marketing, sales, upsells and money

They understand how the business makes money and where the software helps it make more. That includes marketing, sales, upsells and the numbers behind them, not just the feature list.

6. Search, AI search and distribution

A system nobody finds does not work. They understand search, AI search and the other ways customers arrive, and they plan for scale and distribution from the start.

7. Databases, infrastructure and the right tool for each client

A firm understanding of databases and deep knowledge of AWS, Cloudflare and Google Cloud. More importantly, they know which tools fit which client, and what is actually possible in the market today.

8. Models: frontier, open-source and local

They know the frontier models from Anthropic and the other labs, open-source models and models that run locally, and when each one is the right call for cost, privacy and accuracy.

9. Phones, texting and compliance

Many business workflows run through calls and texts. They know the telephony, the SMS rules and the compliance around both, along with the security and permission rules that protect customer data.

10. Scientific changes to a live product

They change a live system with feature flags and A/B tests, so every improvement is measured instead of guessed.

Forward-deployed engineer vs. consultant, solutions engineer and AI engineer

The titles overlap. These are common emphases, not fixed boundaries: consulting firms can write production code, and solutions engineers may own implementation. Ask who will build, release and operate the system.

Role Typical focus Responsibility to clarify
Forward-deployed engineer Software deployed with a customer team Scope, production acceptance and handover
Consultant Advice, implementation or both Whether delivery and operation are included
Solutions engineer Technical fit, demonstrations and solution design Ownership after the sale or proof of concept
AI engineer Models and AI features inside one product team Who owns integration and adoption in the business
In-house engineer The employer’s products and internal systems Roadmap capacity and long-term system ownership

The useful distinction is the agreed responsibility. A forward-deployed engagement should make implementation, user feedback and production acceptance explicit, along with who supports the system afterward.

Against a product software engineer, the difference is the direction of the work. A product engineer builds one capability for many customers. A forward-deployed engineer combines many capabilities for one customer, then reports what should become product.

Forward-deployed engineer salary

Pay depends on the employer, the city and the seniority. As one published example, OpenAI’s San Francisco FDE posting-sf-san-francisco/) lists a base salary of $185,000 to $300,000 plus equity. Salesforce posts a typical base range of $88,970 to $287,910 for its mid- and senior-level role, with different ranges in some cities. Anthropic’s London posting lists £225,000 to £255,000.

The same posting asks for three office days a week and travel of up to 50 percent. That travel is part of the price of the role, for the engineer and for the company hiring one.

Is forward-deployed engineering a good job?

For engineers who like seeing their work used, often yes. The feedback loop is short: you build something in the morning and watch the team use it that week. Cagan notes that people who have done this work well have gone on, out of proportion, to lead product teams and found startups.

The downside is real when the company treats the role as custom services with a better title. Without a platform to reuse, every customer becomes a fresh one-off build, and the travel adds up. Ask how field work turns into product before taking the job.

Where forward-deployed engineers help AI projects

An AI demo can work on sample data while leaving important production questions unanswered. Which system owns the record? Who can see it? What happens when a request is ambiguous or an API fails? Those questions shape the application around the model.

Closing that gap involves integration, model evaluation and decisions with the operating team. An engineer might investigate a database schema, review difficult cases with the sales team and turn the findings into a testable release. A team with those capabilities in-house may already have the capacity it needs.

In our experience, projects stall on the work around the model, not the model itself. Here’s where we see it happen:

1. Trust runs out before the work does

Doing this properly takes deep access to systems and data. When access arrives slowly, or a stakeholder gets cold feet halfway through, momentum dies. Set expectations with everyone at the start, including that something unforeseen can move a date.

2. The scope keeps growing

Every added feature adds complexity, and past a point the AI can no longer do the first job well. Separate wants from needs before the build starts, ship a real MVP and hold the line on everything else until it runs.

3. Nobody planned for production

A system with no users can move very fast. Real users bring staging databases, staging apps, staging QA and careful releases, and in our builds that work takes four to six times as long as the first version. Changes to core data carry the most risk, so the foundation has to be right from the start.

4. People give up too early

Engineers under-scope, the project runs into overtime and frustration sets in. Getting the product up is only the first step. The rest is diligence: every screen of every product, proper QA, checking that the data comes through, reading the code and making the AI do exactly what you need.

5. The pieces are left to someone internal

A capable employee asked to wire it together alone usually stalls on the parts nobody owns: access, integrations, QA and security. Those parts are the forward-deployed engineer’s job.

Signs your company needs a forward-deployed team

  • An AI pilot worked in a demo but never reached real users.
  • The work you want to automate lives across several systems, like a CRM, an inbox and a phone line.
  • Your engineers are fully booked on the core product.
  • Hiring a full AI team would take longer than the problem can wait.
  • You need additional engineering capacity with explicit delivery ownership.

FAQs

What does FDE stand for?

FDE stands for forward-deployed engineer. Palantir uses the longer forward deployed software engineer, or FDSE, and calls the people in the role Deltas.

Is a forward-deployed engineer a software engineer?

Yes. The role writes and reviews production code. What changes is where the engineer works and what they own: a customer’s result, not only a feature.

Do forward-deployed engineers travel?

Often. OpenAI’s San Francisco posting lists travel of up to 50 percent. Some engagements run mostly remote, with visits for discovery and launch.

Which companies hire forward-deployed engineers?

Palantir, OpenAI, Anthropic, Google Cloud and Salesforce all post forward-deployed roles, and AI startups increasingly do too.

How do you become a forward-deployed engineer?

Ship real systems to real users. The strongest candidates build hard systems for themselves, use AI at its limits every day and understand the business around the code: user flows, compliance and how a product makes money.

Can a company get forward-deployed engineers without hiring them?

Yes. dplyz places a senior forward-deployed team inside your company as an embedded team, a defined project or technical leadership. You skip the hiring cycle and start with engineers who have done this before.
