When to Use Graphs in RAG (2025) Explained for FDEs

FDE Interviews · 2026-10-26 · 3 min read

Summary

This breakdown covers GraphRAG-Bench (ICLR 2026 revision), a benchmark comparing graph-augmented retrieval against standard RAG across factual retrieval, complex reasoning, contextual summarization, and creative generation on narrative and domain-guideline corpora. No architecture wins universally: some graph methods improve specific reasoning tasks while losing on simpler facts or other corpora, and implementations vary widely in how much context they retrieve. For FDEs, the advice is to diagnose failures in a well-tuned baseline first, then test graph structure on the customer's actual query types, including evidence provenance and full operating cost in the comparison.

Key points

  • GraphRAG adds entity relationships, community summaries, and graph-based evidence gathering to retrieval.
  • GraphRAG-Bench evaluates four task types on two corpus types.
  • No single retrieval architecture wins across tasks and corpora.
  • Only add graphs when a tuned baseline repeatedly misses required relationships.
  • Compare against strong baselines with provenance and full token costs included.

The paper in one minute

GraphRAG adds structured relationships to retrieval. Instead of relying only on independently matched passages, it can follow entity connections, use community summaries or gather evidence through a graph. The additional structure may help a question that depends on relationships across documents, but it also creates extraction, indexing and maintenance work.

This paper introduces GraphRAG-Bench to compare those tradeoffs across factual retrieval, complex reasoning, contextual summarization and creative generation. It evaluates systems on narrative and domain-guideline corpora, inspecting graph properties and retrieved evidence as well as final answers.

The results do not identify one universally better retrieval architecture. Some graph methods improve particular reasoning tasks while losing ground on simpler facts or other corpora. Different implementations also retrieve very different amounts of context. A graph label alone says little about the actual system being compared.

This breakdown reviews the February 2026 revision, published at ICLR 2026. The official benchmark repository is the starting point for its resources. The practical lesson is to test graph structure against a strong baseline on the customer's query types, with evidence provenance and full operating cost included in the comparison.

Why it matters to an FDE

A question about one policy clause may need an accurate passage and its effective date. A question about dependencies across services may need several directed relationships and their supporting records. Those are different retrieval requirements, even if both arrive through the same chat interface.

GraphRAG is useful to investigate when the required relationships are repeatedly missed by a well-tuned baseline. It is less likely to help when the source document is absent, permissions are wrong or the question can already be answered from a single retrieved passage. Diagnose the failure before adding a graph-building pipeline.

Use RAG evaluation to separate evidence retrieval from answer generation. Then use an ontology or semantic layer only where the domain needs explicit entity types and relationship meaning. The graph should make a requirement easier to satisfy and verify, not merely make the architecture diagram more elaborate.
