How to Read FDE Salaries: A Comparison of Levels.fyi and Glassdoor Data

Original title: FDE 薪资怎么看：Levels.fyi 与 Glassdoor 数据对比

FDE Times · 2026-10-28 · 5 min read

Summary

The article explains why FDE salary figures conflict across sources: Levels.fyi reports user-submitted total compensation (base plus annualized equity and bonuses), Glassdoor reports base salary medians, and job ads list only base ranges. A Palantir US Forward Deployed Software Engineer posting lists $135,000–$200,000 base, below Levels.fyi's $220,000 US median—the gap being equity and bonuses. The author warns about small samples (33 UK reports, 11 India reports on Levels.fyi), double-counting in aggregated reports, and mixing countries or currencies. Advice: quote total compensation when recruiters ask, check sample sizes and dates, and use company job ads as the base-salary benchmark.

Key points

  • Levels.fyi measures total compensation; Glassdoor measures base salary only—different questions.
  • Palantir's US FDE ad caps base at $200,000, below Levels.fyi's $220,000 median.
  • Levels.fyi's UK median rests on 33 reports and India's on 11; a few outliers shift it.
  • At OpenAI and Anthropic, equity can roughly double cash compensation.
  • Answer recruiters with total comp, note sample sizes and dates, compare same-country sources.

Key takeaways

  • Levels.fyi tracks total compensation: base salary, annually-vested equity, and bonuses. According to Glassdoor's published methodology, its figure refers to the median base salary. The two use different metrics and cannot be compared directly.
  • On Levels.fyi, the UK FDE median is based on only 33 reports, and the India page has just 11; a few new reports are enough to change the numbers.
  • For base salary, go by the ranges in companies' own job postings; for total compensation, use Levels.fyi, and note the sample size and the date you checked.

In a job posting for a Forward Deployed Software Engineer targeting US government business, Palantir lists an estimated annual salary range of $135,000–$200,000. Meanwhile, on Levels.fyi's US page, the median salary for Forward Deployed Engineers (FDE) is $220,000, above the ceiling of Palantir's range.

Neither side is lying. They answer different questions, yet readers often blend them into one: "How much can an FDE earn?"

For those currently working as FDEs or preparing to move into the role, this confusion comes at a cost. If you quote the wrong number when a recruiter asks about salary expectations, you either undersell yourself or get filtered out because your ask exceeds a level the company never included in its base salary range. Before deciding which number to trust, first figure out what each number actually measures.

Each source measures something different

Levels.fyi is explicit: it reports total compensation, meaning base salary plus annually-vested equity value plus bonuses. The data comes from salary reports users submit to the site, not from company-published salaries.

Glassdoor measures a narrower scope. The methodology Glassdoor publishes for its Local Pay Reports mentions only the median base salary of full-time US employees, calculated nationally and by metro area. If the FDE figure you see on Glassdoor is calculated the same way, it excludes equity and bonuses.

A third perspective comes from job postings. Recruiting from Scratch analyzed 924 FDE job listings and explicitly states the data comes from real job postings, not surveys. The resulting median is $195,000, covering "all locations," but it does not specify which countries those locations are in.

This is the salary a company publishes when posting a job, not the final offer. Exponent's aced.io uses the same method, citing 135 US job listings to arrive at a median base salary of roughly $190,000, below Levels.fyi's median total compensation.

The gap is equity and bonuses

Read Palantir's posting carefully and you'll find a line many people skim past: the $135,000–$200,000 is only the estimated base salary; the role's total compensation may also include restricted stock units (RSUs) and a signing bonus.

Levels.fyi's $220,000 median exceeds that $200,000 ceiling. The most plausible explanation is that the difference lies in equity and bonuses, which is exactly the part Palantir does not include in its posted range.

Suppose an offer has a $170,000 base salary plus $200,000 in equity vesting evenly over four years. Under Levels.fyi's calculation, annual total compensation is $170,000 + $50,000 = $220,000. A site that only counts base salary would show $170,000: same person, same offer, a $50,000 gap between the two numbers.

At some companies, the gap is far larger. According to aced.io, at OpenAI (which grants PPUs) and Anthropic (where compensation is mostly RSUs), equity can roughly double the cash component. In those cases, the figure shown by a base-salary-only site may be less than half the offer's actual value.

getperspective.ai's 2026 FDE salary report, which aggregates 1,200 data points, reaches the same conclusion: base salary is the floor, not the ceiling.

How much can 33 reports tell you?

Sample size is the second trap. Levels.fyi's UK page shows an FDE median of £140,302, but it is based on only 33 salary reports.

The median of 33 values is the 17th one. Add just a few reports that are clearly high or low, and the 17th value changes hands, shifting the number on the page with it.

The India page has an even smaller sample: only 11 reports. With a sample this small, a handful of unusually high or low offers from a single company is enough to skew the entire median. So every time you cite a Levels.fyi figure, note the sample size and the date you checked.

Don't count the same source twice

The third trap is harder to spot: aggregate reports reuse sources you've already seen. getperspective.ai says its data is aggregated from five public sources between January and May 2026, including Glassdoor and Levels.fyi.

If that report's figures match Levels.fyi's, that is not independent corroboration, because part of the data comes from the same place. When collecting numbers, first trace where each one comes from, then decide whether it can count as an independent "vote."

Country is another source of distortion. getperspective.ai uses only US data, and says salary ranges in other countries are only 50–70% of US levels. Recruiting from Scratch's $195,000 median covers "all locations," with no way to know how many of those listings are outside the US.

Therefore, don't put an "all locations" figure next to a US-only figure and conclude the market is rising or falling. And don't convert pounds or rupees into dollars yourself to compare against the US page: instead, find sources in the same country and currency as the job market you're targeting.

So which number should you trust?

Trust numbers that can answer your question. If you want to know how much cash a company actually pays each month, the best benchmark is the base salary range in that company's own job postings, read together with the line explaining what falls outside that range. Use Glassdoor only to cross-check base salary.

If you need to compare two complete offers, or want to know how much negotiating room there is in equity, you can use Levels.fyi, but with three caveats: check the sample size, note the date you looked it up, and confirm the page you're viewing covers the target country.

Aggregated job posting data tells you the price the market is asking, not what people actually take home.

Three habits apply in any market. When reading a job description, separate the base salary range from what lies outside it.

When a recruiter asks about your salary expectations, answer in terms of total compensation and ask them to break it down: base salary, annual equity, bonus, and signing bonus. Once you receive an offer, compare it using sources from the same country and on the same basis, rather than against a median of unknown origin.

This is exactly what an FDE does on-site with clients every day: before trusting a number, ask how it was measured, what the sample was, and when it was measured. People accustomed to scrutinizing client data this way have no reason to take salary website numbers at face value.
