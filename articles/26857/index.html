From Your Role: FDE · OpenFDE

Original title: 从你的岗位看 FDE

前沿部署工程师社区 · 2026-10-16 · 3 min read

Summary

This article maps eight common roles onto a 4C framework for FDEs: Context, Code, Calibration, and Compounding. It argues most existing jobs cover only one or two dimensions, with calibration being a shared weakness. AI adoption has broken the relay-style division of labor, creating demand for people who can span all four. A transition table shows that weaknesses determine difficulty while existing strengths determine the destination role, such as Deployment Strategist or Applied AI Engineer. The author gives tailored advice for three starting groups and notes you can adopt FDE working methods without changing your title.

Key points

  • FDEs must combine Context, Code, Calibration, and Compounding.
  • Most current roles cover only one or two of the four dimensions.
  • AI deployment broke relay-style workflows, driving FDE demand.
  • Transition rule: weaknesses set difficulty, strengths set destination.
  • Adopting FDE methods adds value even without a title change.

Whether you currently write code, run client engagements, present solutions, or define requirements, FDE may be relevant to you—it's not a job title that appeared out of nowhere, but a reassembly of the "half a job" your role originally covered into a complete person.

Every role typically stands on only one or two faces of the 4C

In What is an FDE we said that a qualified FDE stands on four faces at once: Context—understanding the client's real business and constraints; Code—personally shipping solutions to production; Calibration—running evals with real metrics and failure modes, tuning the system until it's genuinely useful; Compounding—distilling field experience into reusable product capabilities.

The problem is that the vast majority of existing roles inherently cover only one or two of these faces:

  • Consultants, salespeople, sales engineers, delivery, and customer success spend all day with clients and are strong on Context, but rarely write production code themselves.
  • Software engineers and data scientists are strong on Code, yet remain separated by requirement documents year-round, never seeing where clients actually get stuck.
  • Product managers best understand "what should become a product" and have the sharpest instincts for Compounding, but are neither on the front lines nor hands-on with implementation.

In the past this division of labor worked fine: processes were clear, requirements were stable, and everyone guarded their own segment—passing the baton got things done. But AI deployment broke this relay chain—model capabilities change weekly, client problems are highly heterogeneous, and between demo and production lie the hard bones of data, permissions, evaluation, and adoption. The moment a need is discovered in the field, someone needs to be able to implement it and judge whether it should be productized. Relaying is too slow, so the market started demanding an entity that can connect all four faces—whether one person leading a fleet of agents, or a small team leading multiple agents. That's the FDE, and it's what we mean by "Forward Deployed-ification (big FDE)" that's happening now.

One principle for reading this table: your weakness determines the difficulty, your existing faces determine the destination. Learning a programming language is much faster than growing from scratch the intuition to "read a client's organizational politics"—so people close to code transition quickly, while those close to clients but who never touch engineering must either grind through code or take the more strategy-leaning Deployment Strategist route. Details on "how to fill the gaps" for each role are in Transition paths by role.

Three types of starting points, three directions for reinforcement

Consultants, salespeople, sales engineers, delivery/implementation PMs, customer success—your biggest asset is understanding clients, business, and buying logic; the Context face is slow for others to build. What you should focus on is Code: from being able to read code, to independently shipping a small system with permissions and evals to production. If pure engineering is too hard to fill, you can lean toward Deployment Strategist.

Traditional software engineers, data analysts / data scientists—you can get systems running, but what's missing is stepping out of the IDE. What you should focus on is Context: find a real business stakeholder and do a discovery to figure out "where the outcome is stuck," not "which feature is wanted"; and take responsibility for post-launch adoption, rather than calling it done once the code is merged.

Product managers—you are most sensitive to "what should be productized," and your judgment for Compounding is a scarce asset. But the FDE requires you to be on the front lines and implement things yourself, so you need to fill both Context and Code: see fewer secondhand requirements, sit next to clients more, and build up to the point where you can modify systems yourself—otherwise you'll stay stuck as a Technical PM.

Big FDE: adopting its way of working without changing roles

It needs to be made clear: moving toward FDE doesn't mean you must change your title. Even if you plan to stay in your current role, borrowing the FDE way of working—"owning the final outcome, daring to cross functional boundaries, turning field friction into compounding value"—already makes you more valuable than peers in the same role: salespeople who understand technical trade-offs, PMs whose requirements are closer to the front lines, engineers who care about business outcomes. We expand on this value separately in The value of FDE for each role.
