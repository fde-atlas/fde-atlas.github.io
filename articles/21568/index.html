The term "full-stack engineer" is widely used, but its definition remains vague and has taken on a life of its own. Some people call themselves full-stack if they "can do frontend and backend," while others say the term only applies if you also cover infrastructure and DevOps.

Let's first clarify the precise meaning of the term and its scope of responsibility.

A full-stack engineer is an engineer who can handle multiple stages of system development alone, from frontend to backend and even infrastructure. "Full stack" means having a certain level of skill across multiple technical fields and understanding the overall picture of development.

One thing to keep in mind is that full-stack engineer is not a specific job title. Even if a job posting says "Full-stack engineer wanted," the required skill range varies by company. For one company, knowing React and Node.js may be enough, while another may require experience building AWS infrastructure.

In other words, it is more accurate to think of "full-stack engineer" not as a job title, but as a term describing a skill set that covers a wide range of technical areas.

The scope of a full-stack engineer's responsibilities broadly spans the following three areas.

AreaMain responsibilitiesRepresentative technologies
FrontendImplementing the screens users interact with directlyHTML/CSS, JavaScript, React, Vue.js, Next.js
BackendServer-side processing, API design and implementation, building business logicJava, Python, Ruby, PHP, Go, Node.js
InfrastructureBuilding and operating application platformsAWS, GCP, Azure, Docker, Kubernetes, Terraform

In small projects and startups, it is not uncommon for a single engineer to handle all of these areas. On the other hand, in large projects, full-stack engineers often act as a bridge between frontend and backend, coordinating across teams.

Some people imagine that "full-stack engineer = a superman who can do everything," but reality is a bit different. It is not about being an expert in every field; the common pattern is having broad foundational knowledge while demonstrating deep expertise in specific areas.

For example, an engineer whose main focus is backend development but who can also handle frontend and infrastructure, or an engineer with frontend strengths who can also design APIs and work with databases. This combination of "a core area of expertise plus broad coverage" is what a real full-stack engineer looks like.

This also connects to the concept of the "T-shaped professional" discussed later, but the presence or absence of this "core expertise" greatly influences your market value.

The skills required of full-stack engineers are wide-ranging. Here, we explain the specific technology stacks and required skills across four areas: frontend, backend, databases, and infrastructure.

Frontend development is the area responsible for implementing the screen elements that users see directly. The fundamentals are markup with HTML/CSS and dynamic processing with JavaScript. In current web development, frameworks such as React, Vue.js, and Next.js have become the mainstream.

Required skills include understanding component design, state management (Redux, Vuex, Zustand, etc.), API integration, and responsive design implementation. Coding ability to accurately reproduce designs created by UI/UX designers is also essential.

It's not just about implementing designs as specified — whether you can implement them with performance and accessibility in mind changes how you are evaluated as an engineer.

Backend development is the area responsible for implementing business logic that runs on the server side and processing data. Major server-side languages include Java, Python, Ruby, PHP, Go, and Node.js (TypeScript). Each has associated frameworks such as Spring Boot, Django, Ruby on Rails, Laravel, Gin, and Express/NestJS.

What is expected of a full-stack engineer goes beyond simply being able to implement things using frameworks. It is important to be able to make design decisions with the entire system in mind, such as API design, authentication and authorization implementation, error handling, and logging design.

Precisely because they understand both frontend and backend, full-stack engineers can propose appropriate API designs and efficient data structures. This is their strength and the source of their value as a bridge between frontend and backend.

Databases are a critical foundation for storing and managing the data handled by applications. What is required of a full-stack engineer is the skill to select the right database—such as MySQL, PostgreSQL, or MongoDB—and to design tables and optimize queries.

Beyond basic CRUD operations and SQL writing as a given, knowledge from a performance perspective is also necessary, such as index design, decisions about normalization and denormalization, and resolving N+1 problems. In large-scale services, performance degradation due to growing data volumes tends to become an issue. It is also worth acquiring skills that prove useful in the operations phase, such as identifying and improving slow queries and introducing appropriate caching strategies.

The infrastructure area is responsible for building and operating the foundation on which applications run. In current development, the use of cloud services such as AWS, GCP, and Azure is the norm. Knowledge is required to understand the characteristics of each service—such as EC2, Lambda, S3, RDS, and CloudFront—and to combine them appropriately.

In addition, DevOps skills are growing in importance, such as container operations with Docker and Kubernetes, Infrastructure as Code (IaC) using Terraform, and building CI/CD pipelines with GitHub Actions or CircleCI. Basic Linux operations, foundational networking knowledge, and security concepts are also indispensable for covering the infrastructure area.

That is exactly why full-stack engineers who can take this on are highly valued in the field, and rate negotiations go more smoothly. People who can "write code and understand infrastructure" are rare and valuable.

Many people have heard voices saying "Don't become a full-stack engineer" or "They're not needed." It is true that some workplaces hold such views. However, the claim that "full-stack engineers are unnecessary" is a misunderstanding; in fact, demand is rising year by year.

Here, we explain why these negative voices arise and how to overcome them.

There are three main reasons for the negative evaluations of full-stack engineers.

① Expertise tends to be shallow

Because they learn a wide range of technologies, there is concern that the depth of each one may be insufficient. In situations requiring advanced expertise, they can give the impression of being "half-baked."

② It is difficult to keep up with the latest technologies in all fields

IT technology evolves quickly, and new technologies emerge one after another in both frontend and backend. Realistically, it is difficult to keep up with everything, and there is a risk of knowledge becoming outdated.

③ Their strengths are hard to see

People who say "I can do everything" tend to be met with "What is this person's greatest strength, exactly?" When compared with people who have a clear specialty, there are situations where they are less likely to be highly evaluated.

These criticisms have a degree of validity. In particular, if all your areas are merely "average," the risk of being undervalued in negotiations increases.

So how can you increase your market value as a full-stack engineer? The answer is evolving into a "T-shaped" professional.

A T-shaped professional is someone who has deep knowledge in one specialized area (the vertical axis) while also covering other areas broadly (the horizontal axis). For example, an engineer who has expertise at the level of being able to make architecture selections and design decisions in backend development, while also being able to handle frontend and infrastructure competently.

A professional who is "120 points in backend and 80 points in everything else" is clearly differentiated from someone who is "80 points across the board." Conversely, if everything is at the "I can implement it" level, you will be seen as a replaceable resource.

Have one specialized area as your anchor, and reach the level where you can "make design decisions" in it. This is the concrete strategy for graduating from being a jack-of-all-trades-master-of-none.

📌Pro Tip The vertical axis of the "T" can be anything—depth is what matters Whether frontend, backend, or infrastructure, if you reach the level of "being able to make design decisions" in one area, that becomes your vertical axis. What matters is not "which one you choose" but "how deep you go."

While there are voices saying "full-stack engineers aren't needed," demand from companies definitely exists. The four benefits companies expect from full-stack engineers are as follows.

① Faster development speed

Because one person can complete most of the development process alone, development proceeds faster than with a multi-person division of labor. Time spent on handoffs and coordination between roles decreases, leading to shorter lead times for the entire project.

② Reduced labor costs and resources

Instead of hiring separate frontend and backend engineers, entrusting everything to a single full-stack engineer may help reduce total costs.

③ Flexible Role Coverage

With the spread of DevOps and agile development, situations where a single engineer is involved from development through operations are increasing. A full-stack skill set matches the needs of such environments.

④ Able to Act with a Whole-System Optimization Perspective

Because you can see the entire system from a bird's-eye view, you can propose and implement optimal solutions while understanding how each process and technology connects. Being able to make decisions that avoid partial optimization—for example, adjusting frontend implementation based on backend constraints—is a major strength.

Demand is especially pronounced among startups and small and medium-sized enterprises (SMEs) looking to promote IT adoption. Startups developing services with small teams often lack the budget to assemble multiple specialized engineers. That is where full-stack engineers, who can handle a wide range of roles on their own, prove invaluable.

Even at SMEs where it is difficult to assemble specialized talent, someone who can oversee and lead the entire development effort is highly valued. Looking at freelance job market trends, there are abundant projects for full-stack engineers. Postings for web services and DX promotion projects stand out, and many offer remote work.

According to estimates by the Ministry of Economy, Trade and Industry, Japan is said to face a shortage of up to approximately 790,000 IT professionals by 2030. As demand for talent grows, full-stack engineers who can work across a broad range will attract ever more attention.

For freelance engineers, the biggest question is probably how much they can actually earn. In this section, we explain—in concrete data—how freelance income compares to full-time salaries, the conditions for high-rate projects, and the reality of how take-home pay differs depending on the contract chain.

Full-stack engineers' income varies greatly depending on employment type.

As a Full-Time Employee

The average annual income of a full-time full-stack engineer is roughly 6 to 7 million yen. For reference, the average annual income of a systems engineer (contract development) is about 5.57 million yen. Full-stack engineers tend to earn more than that, though not by a dramatic margin.

As a Freelancer

On the other hand, freelance full-stack engineers are said to earn around 9.6 to 15 million yen annually. Many projects offer monthly rates of 800,000 to 1 million yen, a high level even compared to other engineering roles.

Why does such a gap arise? One reason is that, for freelancers, skills and experience are directly reflected in rates. Companies want to secure immediately productive talent on a per-project basis, so they tend to offer high compensation in return.

Another reason is that freelancer pay does not include costs invisible in a full-time salary (the company's share of social insurance premiums, benefits, training costs, etc.). Freelancers bear these costs themselves, and in exchange, that amount is added on top of their compensation.

For a freelance full-stack engineer to land high-rate projects paying 800,000 to 1 million yen per month, several conditions must be met.

Years of Practical Experience

Many high-rate projects require "at least 3 years of practical experience." It is assumed that you have a certain amount of hands-on experience in both frontend and backend.

Experience in Design and Technology Selection

As shown in the skill proficiency table earlier, you are expected to be at a level where you can "make design decisions," not just "implement." Experience with architecture selection, API design, and infrastructure configuration design is highly valued.

A Composite Skill Set

If you can handle everything from frontend to backend and even cloud infrastructure, your range of project options widens. Engineers who can cover infrastructure in particular are rare and highly valued, giving them an advantage in rate negotiations.

Communication Skills

Beyond technical ability, smooth communication with clients and team members is also important. You are expected to handle requirements gathering, progress reporting, and issue sharing without friction.

When pursuing high income as a freelancer, an often-overlooked issue is the "contract chain" (shōryū). The contract chain refers to the sequence of intermediaries a project passes through from the end client to the engineer. The shallower the chain (the fewer intermediaries), the higher the engineer's take-home pay.

The table below shows how much take-home pay differs for engineers with the same skills depending on the contract chain.

A situation of "60万 yen a month for grueling full-stack work" versus "1 million yen a month with the same skills." This difference arises not from skill, but from the contract chain.

Projects that are direct with the end client or the prime contractor have smaller middleman margins, so a higher share goes back to the engineer. There are also more opportunities for direct communication with the client, with the added benefit of being involved deeply from the requirements stage.

Full-stack engineers, with skills spanning frontend, backend, and infrastructure, are invaluable professionals who can handle the entire development process. While some criticize them as "jack-of-all-trades, master of none" or "half-baked," by becoming a T-shaped professional with depth in one specialized area, they can become irreplaceable talent.

In the freelance market, demand for full-stack engineers is high, and there are plenty of high-rate projects paying 800,000 to 1,000,000 yen per month. Compared to full-time employment, an annual income exceeding 10 million yen is well within reach. However, it's not just skills that matter—your position in the contracting chain ("shoryu") is also important. Even with the same skills, your take-home pay differs significantly between being a third-tier subcontractor and working directly with the end client.

There's no need to worry about being a "jack of all trades, master of none." Your broad skill set is worth more than 1,000,000 yen per month—as long as you get the contracting chain right.
