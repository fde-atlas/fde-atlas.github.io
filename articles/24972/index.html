How to Measure FDE Cost-Effectiveness | ROI Evaluation Criteria

Original title: FDE費用対効果の測り方｜ROI判断基準

日本Fde協会 · 2026-09-01 · 6 min read

Summary

This article from the Japan FDE Association explains how client-side decision makers should evaluate the cost-effectiveness of hiring Forward Deployed Engineers (FDEs). It argues ROI should be judged over several quarters to years, not by initial tool-launch results alone. Benefits are split into replacing existing work hours and creating new business opportunities. The cost side must include often-missed items like internal staff time, operational tuning, training, and compliance work. Hidden long-term value—documented designs as reusable assets and in-housing of controls and audit work—should be counted as future avoided costs. The proposed approval framework starts with an inventory of current work hours and costs, presents quantified benefits with stated assumptions, and avoids citing industry-average reduction rates without sourcing.

Key points

  • Judge FDE ROI over quarters to years, not initial deployment results.
  • Split benefits into replacing existing work and creating new opportunities.
  • Include hidden costs: internal time, operations, training, compliance.
  • Count documented assets and in-housed controls as future avoided costs.
  • Base numbers on your own workload inventory, not industry averages.

The reason decision-makers on the client side struggle with investment decisions is that it is unclear which metrics to use to measure FDE cost-effectiveness. As the Japan FDE Association, we have organized these evaluation criteria from the standpoint of putting both technology and business into practice in the field. By reading this article, you will be able to build your own evaluation framework for internal approval: identifying which metrics capture quantitative and qualitative benefits, comparing them against costs, and preparing the case for internal sign-off. Here, FDE refers to Forward Deployed Engineer (an engineer who embeds themselves in the customer's field), not the abbreviation for disk encryption. Let's think through this together.

Key Concepts to Grasp Before Measuring FDE Cost-Effectiveness

A typical mistake in evaluating cost-effectiveness is judging ROI based solely on the initial effects of tool implementation. The value of FDE accumulates through the process of embedding AI and workflows into operations, establishing them in daily use, and continuously improving them. The starting point is a perspective that views investment recovery not on a single-month basis but over a timeframe of several quarters to years.

Divide Benefits into "Replacement" and "New Value Creation"

There are broadly two types of benefits. One is the effect of replacing the hours and costs of existing operations; the other is the effect of new value creation, where work that was previously impossible can now be done. The former is easy to measure in terms of time saved, while the latter is measured in terms of sales opportunities and speed of decision-making. In an approval process, presenting both separately makes it easier for evaluators to be convinced.

Speaking with Numbers Speeds Up Decisions

We value the principle of backing up proposals with ROI, costs, and work hours. At one manufacturing client's site, we first identified the manual work involved in shipping and receiving and quantified how many hours of work it generated per month. Discussions about benefits go smoothly when they start with this inventory of the current state.

Accurately Tallying the Cost Side

The costs that form the denominator of cost-effectiveness are not just initial expenses. In addition to FDE operating costs and outsourcing fees, you should include the time internal members spend on interviews and reviews, tool licenses, and improvement work hours during the operational phase. Underestimating these often leads to the mistaken conclusion that the investment cannot be recovered later.

Removing Short-Term Assumptions

The assumption that benefits will follow automatically if you build something good with technology is dangerous. Maekawa himself, when he first moved from finance to the EC business, had technology but lacked marketing and customer understanding, and experienced almost no sales for the first six months. Based on his experience of growth coming only after studying the market and the field, he believes that measuring the actual conditions in the field comes first when evaluating cost-effectiveness.

"Hard-to-See Benefits" to Include in the Evaluation

Typical explanations of FDE tend to stop at the value of embedding into the field and solving problems. However, what matters for investment decisions on the client side is the value of the assets that remain afterward. Whether or not you include this in the evaluation changes how ROI appears.

Preventing Knowledge Silos: Documentation Becomes an Asset

If business improvement does not end with person-dependent adjustments, and procedures and designs are documented and left in a transferable form, the benefits persist even when the person in charge changes. According to a story we heard from the CEO of a SaaS company, design documents left by an FDE who had become more knowledgeable about the operations than the company's own employees became the foundation for subsequent in-house improvements. This residual value can be counted as cost reductions in subsequent fiscal years.

Bringing Governance and Audit Compliance In-House to Reduce External Dependence

If the implementation is designed from the start with permission management, logs, and audit trails in mind, you can reduce the costs of later relying on external parties for governance compliance. Based on our experience at startups, outsourcing this at the stage when growth makes governance requirements stricter can cost many times the original estimate. The benefit of building it in at implementation time can be evaluated as future avoided costs.

How to Build Evaluation Criteria for Internal Approval

Now we organize the elements discussed so far into a form that will pass internal approval. The key is to base the case on quantitative benefits that can be reliably measured, and to add hard-to-see benefits as supporting material. Avoid overpromising and clearly state your assumptions; this is how to write a proposal that earns trust.

Order of Construction

First, inventory the work hours and costs of current operations. Next, estimate replacement benefits and new value creation benefits separately, and tally the total costs that form the denominator without omissions. Then, present the future avoided costs from documentation assets and in-housed governance compliance in a separate category. Give the payback period a range, and note that results will change if assumptions change; this keeps your understanding aligned with the evaluators.

Numbers Come with Assumptions

Avoid asserting reduction rates or payback periods as industry averages. Clearly state that the figures are estimates based on your own company's inventory, and cite sources when referencing external data. We, too, do not promise specific results. The basis for judgment is, ultimately, numbers built up from your own company's field. For an overview of implementation decisions, see The Complete Guide to Corporate FDE Utilization, and for role division, see Role Division Between FDEs and In-House Engineers.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q. Can FDE cost-effectiveness be judged immediately after implementation? Judging based only on short-term tool implementation effects tends to underestimate the value. Since benefits accumulate through operational adoption and continuous improvement, we recommend viewing them over a timeframe of several quarters to years.

Q. What should be done first when measuring benefits? Inventorying the work hours and costs of current operations. Once monthly work hours and incurred costs are quantified, estimating replacement benefits and new value creation benefits separately makes the discussion productive.

Q. How should figures such as reduction rates be handled in an approval process? Do not assert them as industry averages; clearly state the assumption that they are estimates based on your own company's inventory. Cite sources when referencing external data, and give the payback period a range to prevent misalignment in understanding.
