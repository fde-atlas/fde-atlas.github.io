FDE vs Professional Services: The Real Difference

FDE Times · 2026-10-09 · 7 min read

Summary

This article argues the real difference between FDE and professional services lies in where custom code ends up, not job titles. If on-site work is fed back into the core product, it is FDE work; if it stays with the customer and is billed per project, it is services. Drawing on a16z, Tandem, Thomas Otter, Valletta Software, Shivanath and PostHog, it frames customisation as product discovery, warns that unowned feedback loops lead to dozens of unmaintainable forks, and offers practical advice for engineers evaluating FDE roles: check reporting lines, ask who generalises code, and inspect the git log.

Key points

  • The test: does customer-site code return to the core product?
  • a16z argues most differentiation comes from deployment, not technology.
  • Without a designated owner, lessons-learned loses to delivery deadlines.
  • Unmanaged forks turn a product business into a labour business.
  • Candidates should ask who merges field patterns and check git history.

In brief

  • Short answer: if the work done at a customer goes back into the core product, it is FDE work; if it stays with the customer and is billed per project, it is professional services.
  • Customisation is normal: a16z argues that most of a product's differentiation comes from how it is deployed. The real danger is separate code branches piling up.
  • The feedback loop breaks when writing up lessons competes for time with delivery, so ask who is responsible for turning custom code into shared code.

The short answer to the FDE vs professional services debate lies in where the code goes, not in the job title. If what you write at a customer is documented and then fed back into the core product, you are doing FDE work. If it stays with the customer and is billed per project, it is professional services, whatever the business card says.

The question matters because FDE is a hot role. Semafor notes that the concept was popularised by Palantir: an engineer embedded inside a single customer. Semafor also reports that people with the right skill set are being courted by AI startups.

Yet the “embedded at the customer” model is exactly what makes people suspicious: if you have to send someone on site, is the product unfinished? For a developer weighing a move into FDE work, the answer decides whether the next three years teach you to build products or just to run projects under a more fashionable title.

Is a product that needs someone to install it a broken product?

a16z, in its analysis of accepting lower margins in exchange for a lasting competitive advantage, opens with a memorable image: enterprises buying AI are like your grandmother being given an iPhone. They want to use it, but they need you to set it up. According to a16z, that is why services-heavy deployment models are so common.

a16z goes a step further. It argues that most of a product’s differentiation comes from how the same underlying technology is deployed. In other words, customisation is not waste; it is where the product creates value.

Tandem, writing about how FDE teams work with product and engineering, puts it more bluntly: customisation is not a services cost to be cut, but a mechanism for product discovery. Read this way, “unfinished” is nothing to be ashamed of. The right question is whether, after each trip into the field, the product moves closer to “finished”.

The downside: forty code branches called a “platform”

The sceptics do not deny the value of working on site. Thomas Otter draws the line at who pays: if FDEs are billed to the customer, they are working for the project, not the product. Building for exactly one customer, in Otter’s view, is project work.

Otter cites Marty Cagan to show the long-term cost: thousands of large bespoke solutions, each of which must be maintained and improved forever. Every fork is a debt with no maturity date.

Valletta Software describes the outcome when nobody is responsible for generalising: the vendor ends up maintaining forty custom forks and calling the pile a platform. The detail is worth remembering because it shows that nobody slides into being a services company on purpose. They slide because nobody was given the job of preventing it.

FDE vs professional services: the test at step four

Valletta describes FDE work as a sequence of steps and pinpoints where it differs from consulting: at step four, the FDE is expected to feed patterns back into the core product. A consultant is done once the work is delivered. For an FDE, delivery is where the hardest part begins.

Shivanath, in a Substack essay in July 2026, warns that building for one customer easily leads to over-fitting. In his view, it is the discipline of feedback that turns sending engineers to sit with customers into a product business rather than a labour business.

PostHog’s public FDE handbook describes exactly the desired result: patterns that recur across deployments become reusable artifacts, skills and product improvements. That is the compounding return of the feedback loop.

Picture a startup selling an invoice-reading agent to businesses, with its first three customers. At the first customer, the FDE writes a converter for the customer’s ERP format, notes which parts are specific, then extracts the generic file-reading part into a connector in the product. By the third customer, if it uses the same kind of ERP, integration may come down to configuration.

A services team called FDE takes a different route: three customers, three code branches, three people who are the only ones to understand each branch. Customers are still happy and invoices still go out, but the tenth customer takes nearly as much effort as the first. Multiply that scenario over a few years and you have exactly the forty forks Valletta describes.

Why does the feedback loop break so often?

If the test is that simple, why do so many teams still slide? Tandem offers the most practical explanation: writing up lessons from the field is real work, it competes for time with delivery, and delivery always wins.

Customers have go-live deadlines. Nobody phones to chase the core product. So if step four is only a vague expectation, with no owner and no dedicated time, it gets pushed to next week, and then the week after.

a16z’s financial argument also explains why companies accept this risk. It argues that the only thing companies should optimise for right now is growing total gross profit as fast as possible, even if margins fall because of services.

That calculation only holds if today’s services become tomorrow’s product; otherwise the company is simply buying growth with headcount.

Three questions career-changers often ask

Is an FDE just a consultant who can code? No, if the company really does step four. The work at the customer is the same; the difference is whether the code returns to the core product.

Can I do professional services first and then move into FDE work? Experience working on site is a good foundation. What you need to add is the habit of spotting what recurs across customers and turning it into something shared, because that is what defines the FDE role.

How do I know a company is hiring for a real FDE role? Ask who is responsible for generalising, and whether they have dedicated time for it. Because delivery always wins, a feedback loop with no owner usually exists only on slides.

Read the job description like an org chart

In the job description, look for two lines. The first is who the role reports to: product, engineering or services. The second is whether it mentions contributing back to the core product, writing connectors or shared modules. A job description full of nothing but “deployment”, “customer support” and “ensure go-live” is usually describing a services role.

In the interview, ask specifically: what part of the FDEs’ recent work at customers has become a shared feature, and who merged it? If the interviewer cannot give a single concrete example, that is a signal worth noting.

On your CV, do not just write “deployed for customer X”. Tell it in the order of step four: what you built specifically for the customer, what you noticed recurring, and how you turned it into something other teams reuse. Told that way, it shows recruiters you understand the difference between a product and a project.

The debate among investors will eventually be settled by growth figures. For an engineer, a signal arrives much sooner: open the git log and check whether the code you wrote at a customer last month now lives in the product or is still sitting on that customer’s own branch.
