Today's Judgment

After an enterprise purchases an AI agent platform, who is responsible for adapting it to existing systems, handling exceptions, and running ongoing operations? Two public materials from November 10 offer answers at different levels: IBM and Microsoft have incorporated FDE support into their industry platform offering, while TCS has disclosed how a testing agent limits automated fixes. For Forward Deployed Engineers, reusable assets and project-specific engineering responsibility need to be designed together.

Key Signals

1. IBM and Microsoft launch Imagine Enterprise, with FDE support available for qualifying projects

Their joint announcement on November 10 introduces Imagine Enterprise, which combines AI for industry processes, enterprise data, and governance capabilities, and provides both companies' forward deployed engineering support for qualifying projects. Customers can engage IBM Consulting for deployment and operations, or choose co-creation or self-management.

What it means for FDEs: Platform and on-site delivery can complement each other. Even after reusing existing assets, there is still work to handle customer environment adaptation, business acceptance, and operational handover; the service offering should clearly specify what the vendor and the customer are each responsible for.

Evidence boundaries: This is a product and services announcement, not independent customer acceptance. The "over six thousand objects" mentioned in the announcement includes agents and assets combined, not customer counts or successful deployment counts; SEI's related statements still involve exploration and cannot be assumed to have delivered confirmed benefits. FDE support is subject to project eligibility conditions, and the announcement provides no general pricing commitment.

2. TCS testing agent: code errors allow limited fixes, environment errors follow a separate path

A co-authored article by TCS and AWS on November 10 describes a test orchestration solution that chains requirements, generation, execution, and reporting together, with data and context isolated by tenant. The process first classifies failure causes and only initiates limited fixes when the failure is a code error and the retry policy allows it; environment failures such as connectivity or authentication issues are not handed to the code-fixing agent. The final output retains code, attempt counts, status, and logs.

What it means for FDEs: Automated fixes should not use "task failure" as the sole trigger. Only by first determining the failure category and then defining the allowed scope of changes, retry limits, and handover conditions can you reduce the risk of turning environment problems into code problems.

Evidence boundaries: The labor-saving figures in the article come from a controlled internal comparison across roughly a hundred scenarios, and cannot be treated as independent acceptance by a named bank customer, a general success rate, or a guarantee of net benefits. This issue focuses on the publicly disclosed engineering mechanism and does not replicate the deployment.

Takeaways for FDE Practitioners

  • Differentiate delivery plans among platform capabilities, on-site adaptation, and ongoing operations, and assign a clear owner to each part.
  • Classify errors into code, environment, permissions, and business rule issues, and set up separate handling paths for each.
  • Limit automated fixes to a defined scope of changes and retry counts; escalate to humans when classification cannot be done reliably.
  • For acceptance, retain inputs, versions, execution records, and business sign-off; do not substitute asset counts for outcomes.
