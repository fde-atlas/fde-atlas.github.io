A Practical Handbook for Forward Deploying AI Agents in Chinese B2B Enterprises

Original title: 中国 B 端企业 AI Agent 前线部署实战手册

FDECareer.work · 2026-10-14 · 35 min read

Summary

This FDECareer industry handbook uses a synthetic after-sales ticket scenario to show how FDEs can turn AI Agent deployments at Chinese B2B enterprises into verifiable engineering deliverables. Its core argument: start with one narrow, trustworthy business chain with clear endpoints and exclusions; separate business authorization from model judgment, carrying tenant context through every step; evaluate both outcomes and process (correct object, valid evidence, authorized execution, recovery); count human review and maintenance as real costs; and treat handover as complete only when the customer can independently update knowledge, disable connectors, and revoke roles. It includes an offline lab package and six decision templates, with sources verified as of October 14, 2026.

Key points

  • Deliver one narrow, acceptance-testable business chain before expanding scope.
  • Keep business authorization separate from model judgment; verify tenant context at every step.
  • Evaluate object correctness, evidence validity, execution, recovery, and business impact separately.
  • Total AI cost includes human review, knowledge upkeep, and failure handling.
  • Handover requires customers to independently run update, disable, and revocation drills.

The difficulties in deploying enterprise AI agents often appear after the model can already give reasonable suggestions: whether the basis for the suggestion is still valid, whether the operator has permission, which specific action was approved, whether a write actually happened after an API timeout, and whether the customer's team can take over maintenance. This handbook follows an after-sales ticket workflow to discuss how an FDE can turn these questions into deliverable, verifiable engineering work.

Key Findings: Deliver One Trustworthy Business Workflow First

First, a project should be scoped around an acceptable business outcome. A window that can answer questions and a system that can complete ticket handling within limited permissions carry entirely different delivery responsibilities. First determine which step the customer wants to shorten and which type of rework to reduce, then decide how many steps the model should participate in. Problems that can be solved with queries, rules, or fixed workflows do not need to be escalated into open-ended agents.

Second, business authorization and model judgment must be separated. The model may propose "add a handling suggestion to this ticket," but it cannot gain the power to modify tickets, send emails, or issue refunds through its own text. Tenant, user, object scope, and action limits should be passed and checked by application code, and at execution time the system must confirm that the approval has not expired and the business object has not changed.

Third, production evals need to check both outcomes and process. A suggestion that looks reasonable does not mean the right customer was checked, valid knowledge was used, the correct tool was called, or nothing was executed twice. Business effects, data basis, authorization decisions, tool receipts, and recovery paths should all leave auditable evidence. Looking only at the response text misses the most dangerous failures.

Fourth, the total cost of AI includes human work. Support staff verification, experts maintaining knowledge, engineers handling failures, and administrators reviewing authorizations are all system costs. Driving down model call costs while shifting rework and maintenance onto other teams does not mean the project is profitable. The FDE must make costs, benefits, and responsibilities appear in the same acceptance record.

Research Method and Scope of Applicability

This article targets enterprise software delivery; it is not a statistical survey of FDE roles in China, nor does it claim to cover all industries. The data cutoff date is October 14, 2026, with the most recent 90-day observation window running from July 17 to October 14. Sources include institutional surveys, domestic policies, official engineering documentation, and public technical retrospectives from enterprises; citations and limitations are listed item by item at the end of the article.

Evidence is used according to its purpose. Institutional surveys help identify management issues but cannot replace business measurements for a specific customer; platform documentation describes achievable capabilities but cannot prove that a given deployment is already reliable; enterprise technical retrospectives provide real engineering experience but cannot be directly extrapolated to conclude that Chinese after-sales systems will achieve the same results. Specific thresholds, approval divisions, and system structures are engineering recommendations this article proposes for the example scenario.

The running case is a synthetic project: an equipment service company wants its support staff to handle warranty inquiries faster. The tickets, users, documents, approvals, and results are all illustrative and do not come from a real customer. The offline package uses fixed model outputs, the Python standard library, and SQLite to validate seven categories of engineering scenarios. It is not connected to a real model and provides no evidence of model accuracy, production concurrency, or customer benefits.

"Chinese B2B" in this article mainly manifests as delivery conditions: the customer already has business systems, multiple departments jointly decide permissions and launch, data quality and maintenance responsibilities are inconsistent, and the deployment environment and vendor choices need to be confirmed separately. These conditions cannot be used to infer that all Chinese enterprises must deploy on-premises, nor can a single policy document summarize the compliance obligations of all applications.

Current Observations: Turning Industry Changes into Field Questions

From Individual Productivity to Enterprise Benefits, There Is Still a Process in Between

McKinsey's global survey released in August 2026 discusses individual productivity improvements separately from enterprise-level benefits. Its survey period was May 4 to June 8, covering 1,719 respondents across 97 countries; these self-reported results do not represent proportions for Chinese enterprises. This article borrows that distinction: support staff writing faster is a local result; only when tickets are ultimately closed faster without increased rework does it approach the outcome the customer wants. Source: McKinsey

A common bias in the field: teams measure the time to generate suggestions but ignore the time managers spend re-approving, support staff spend switching systems, and customers spend calling again. If the speed of producing suggestions improves but nobody processes the approval queue, overall delivery remains stuck where it was. The FDE's job is to identify this bottleneck and write it into the project scope, not to keep tuning the model to be faster.

Therefore, the pilot report should map both the old process and the new process. Mark who made judgments originally and who confirms now, which steps disappear and which are added, and whether waiting time has merely shifted. Business owners should acknowledge the responsibilities in the new process, not just endorse a demo. The boundary of benefit calculations must include the added review and operations work.

Deloitte's 2026 State of Enterprise AI report focuses on scaling, business reinvention, talent, and governance readiness. The planning insight here: technical availability and organizational readiness are two different conditions. Source: Deloitte

One department being willing to manually watch every output does not prove that ten departments can operate that way. After expansion, knowledge owners, approvers, ticket statuses, working hours, and exception rules will all differ. Before copying tenant configurations, clarify which behaviors are product capabilities and which are just temporary stopgaps by the pilot team. The latter, without budget and an owner, cannot count as a foundation for scaling.

The "AI Replacing Employees" Discussion Should Be Grounded in Tasks and Responsibilities

For delivery projects, asking first which tasks can be transferred is usually more useful than first debating how many positions will disappear. Support staff may spend less time searching and organizing but more time verifying suggestions, handling exceptions, and feeding back knowledge. New tasks require time, permissions, and training. Leaving an old job description in place and expecting employees to adapt to all changes on their own will separate actual usage from the designed process.

During the project, observe whether operators actually use the results, whether they privately bypass the process, and whether they repeatedly correct the same kind of error. Do not interpret low usage directly as "employees resisting AI." It may also be that there are too many approval steps, results do not flow into business systems, errors carry extra accountability, or the tool is slower than the original way of working. These problems need to be solved jointly by product and business owners.

Domestic Model-Data Coordination Needs Specific Owners

The 2026 "Model-Data Resonance" initiative proposes coordinated development around industry scenarios, models, and high-quality datasets. This is a policy direction, not a ready-made procurement list, nor proof of realized benefits. For this example, the most concrete action is to designate maintainers for warranty knowledge, ticket fields, and business APIs respectively, and establish update and review processes. Source: National Data Administration

In the end, this background only changes the priority of project questions: first solve whether data is usable, whether responsibilities can be assigned, and whether tasks can be closed-loop, then compare models and frameworks. They cannot replace field interviews, let alone a real, authorized integration validation against the customer's systems.

Chapter 1: Scoping the Project—First Define a Scope That Can Be Rejected

Engineering illustration using a synthetic after-sales scenario. Reading focus: suggestion, approval, and business-effect confirmation are different nodes; completion is recorded only after receipt confirmation.

Write Out the Start and End Points of the Scenario

"Improving customer service efficiency" is not yet a deliverable scope. The starting point of the example project is: a customer service agent logs in and opens a warranty ticket belonging to their own tenant. The endpoint is: the system generates a well-grounded internal handling recommendation, which after approval by a designated person is written into internal notes, with an execution receipt retained. Closing tickets, sending replies to customers, refunds, and modifying customer master data are all out of scope for the first round.

This definition lets both technical and business sides point out what counts as done. Without a written receipt, it can only be called "recommendation generated"; without human approval, it can only be called "pending"; if the request times out and the business-side status is unknown, it cannot be called "failed and directly retryable." Every endpoint has a clear record location, not relying on a verbal "it's handled" in a conversation.

Also specify which tickets are covered. For example, only ordinary warranty inquiries with complete documentation are supported, excluding disputed claims, special contract terms, unverifiable identity, and tickets with missing device information. Exclusions are not about hiding problems, but shifting responsibility from model capability back to controllable processes. The business owner should know who receives the excluded tasks.

Adopting a fixed workflow in the first round often makes it easier to demonstrate engineering control of the pipeline. If the model is only responsible for extracting questions and drafting recommendations, there is no need to let it choose refund, email-sending, or deletion tools. Only when real samples show that the query path requires dynamic selection, and the team can judge the consequences of the model picking a wrong step, should the scope of autonomous decision-making be expanded. A technical upgrade should respond to a recordable failure reason.

The baseline should include waiting and rework

With the customer's authorization, collect a set of representative tickets and record the current time spent on finding materials, confirming information, forming recommendations, approval, writing back, and final handling. Look at manual operation time and queue waiting time separately; do not merge them into a single average. Prioritize recording tasks of the same type and similar complexity, and keep cases with missing materials and unsupported scenarios.

Before-and-after pilot comparisons need the same measurement criteria. Peak versus off-peak hours, simple inquiries versus dispute handling, experienced agents versus newcomers—these differences may exceed the change brought by the tool. Set up a parallel control group when conditions allow; when conditions are insufficient, state that only exploratory observation is possible. Do not write the difference between two before-and-after averages directly as causal gains from the model.

In the benefits table, record saved operation time, added verification time, error rework time, and engineering maintenance time. Salary cost is only one way to estimate time value, and does not automatically mean headcount can be reduced. If the enterprise does not convert the freed time into more throughput or better service, the financial benefit still needs separate confirmation.

Put pause conditions into the project charter

If there is no business owner, no data usage authorization, or no owner of the write-back interface, production development should be paused. You can still demonstrate the pipeline with synthetic data, but it cannot be labeled as a customer pilot. If the business side cannot accept human approval, the scope of actions allowed for automatic execution and the loss boundary must be made explicit; do not let the requirement of "a few fewer steps" implicitly expand permissions.

The recommended project charter includes: task start and end points, supported objects, excluded scenarios, business baseline, target criteria, human fallback, dependent systems, cost boundaries, and exit conditions. Assign a sign-off-capable owner to each item. At the end of the kickoff meeting, the FDE should walk away with these decisions, not a requirements list containing only feature names.

Chapter 2: Field Research—Drawing the Process in Front of the People Who Actually Do the Work

Replace abstract requirements meetings with a ticket replay

Have the actual operator choose an authorized, de-identifiable historical ticket and replay it step by step starting from opening the page. Record why they went to a certain system, how they judged document credibility, at which step they asked colleagues, which information needed copying, and when they decided to escalate. Do not only record the happy path; how the operator handles missing materials and contradictory rules usually determines whether the system can land safely.

Organizing records by task steps is clearer than listing system features first. For each step, write down inputs, outputs, decision basis, action executor, and failure destination. For tacit knowledge that "everyone knows," confirm whether it can be written as rules or samples. If the knowledge depends on a particular expert's judgment, the first round can explicitly retain expert confirmation rather than hard-coding incomplete knowledge into prompts.

Give the replay diagram to the business owner, the operator, and the technical team for separate verification. The owner may assume an operation already has an API; the technical team may assume customer service never needs cross-department approval; customer service may have been using a shared spreadsheet never included in the inventory. Only when discrepancies are revealed do the subsequent architecture discussions have accurate input.

The FDE is responsible for connecting this information, discovering conflicts, implementing the pipeline, and organizing validation. The FDE can help define metrics but cannot replace the business owner in approving the loss boundary; can integrate knowledge sources but cannot replace the knowledge owner in judging whether warranty policies are valid. When responsibilities are vague, technical implementation may proceed first, and disputes will surface in concentrated form after launch.

Small teams may allow one person to hold multiple responsibilities, but roles should still be recorded separately. Especially when a developer also serves as the approver, formal operation should confirm whether separation of duties is required. The example uses operator proposes, reviewer approves; this is an engineering design, not a mandatory requirement for all organizational structures.

In interviews, ask "what happens after an error"

Ask customer service: when a recommendation uses outdated rules, who can judge and correct it? Ask the interface owner: does HTTP 200 mean the note has been durably persisted? Ask the administrator: after a user leaves or changes roles, how quickly are permissions revoked? Ask the business owner: will an erroneous internal note be treated as fact by the next agent? These questions are closer to actual risk than "which model do we need."

Also confirm the tool usage environment. Can customer service only access tickets on the intranet? Does the browser allow third-party scripts? Can clients be installed on terminals? Do external model requests go through a proxy? Do logs contain customers' personal information? Write the restrictions into the deployment conditions table to avoid discovering only after the prototype succeeds that the customer cannot use the chosen solution at all.

Maintenance arrangements must be determined before the pilot

Knowledge updates should not wait for someone to notice after a wrong answer. Define update sources, trigger mechanisms, reviewers, and effective processes. After new rules are published, there must be operational entry points and records for who syncs the index, who checks whether pending approval tasks reference old rules, and who confirms whether existing recommendations need to be withdrawn. Relying on the FDE manually editing files can support a brief demo, but cannot serve as formal handover.

Register contacts and response boundaries for each external dependency. If the interface maintainer is unavailable, can the system temporarily fall back to read-only mode? If the model service is down, can customer service continue using the original ticket system? One condition for project success is that the customer can maintain basic business continuity when these dependencies are absent.

Chapter 3: Data and Permissions—First Prove You Are Querying the Right Object

Engineering schematic. The model receiving context does not mean it gains business write permissions; execution control, source of truth, and task recovery all require verifying tenant, object, and action.

For every field,追问 its source and purpose

A ticket workflow typically involves ticket text, customer identifiers, device numbers, warranty records, knowledge documents, and operator identities. First register the purpose of each field, then decide which ones are sent to the model. A device number may be used to look up business status and does not need to appear verbatim in the model context; if customer contact information is irrelevant to generating internal recommendations, it should not be automatically passed out with the entire ticket.

A data inventory should at least record the source system, object owner, tenant fields, classification labels, usage authorization, update frequency, storage location, deletion method, and responsible person. Do not interpret "IT can read the database" as the project being free to use its contents at will. Technical access capability and authorization for a specific purpose must be confirmed separately.

Synthetic data can validate control flows, but it cannot replace field quality checks on real data. Before a real pilot, separately check for missing values, duplicate identifiers, version conflicts, abnormal encodings, and historical migration records. Assign data quality issues to the data owner; do not expect the model to fill in device identity or warranty facts through language alone.

Tenant context passed along the entire chain

After the user completes identity authentication in the business system, the application obtains trusted tenant and user context. These flow into ticket queries, knowledge retrieval, caches, task status, tool calls, and logs. A "please query another company" instruction in the model input must not change this context, and tenant identifiers in tool parameters must not override the identity already confirmed server-side.

Microsoft's multi-tenant agent guidance explicitly implements tenant boundaries as deterministic controls. The minimum design in this article is: every resource query carries a trusted tenant scope and is re-checked at the tool gateway; caches and task status also carry the same scope. A single check before retrieval is not enough. Source: Microsoft

An effective counterexample is two tenants both having a ticket numbered T1. The system must return the current tenant's T1 and cannot use the ticket number alone as a global key. Going further, two tenants may use the same knowledge title, so indexes, citations, attachment downloads, and debug panels must also maintain boundaries. Tests should cover these duplicate identifiers, not just pick completely different data.

Permissions must be checked twice: for reading and for execution

Being able to read a ticket does not mean being able to modify it later. Read basis, execution action, and approval authority may belong to different permissions. During the waiting period, the user may change roles, the reviewer may be de-authorized, and the ticket may be archived. Before execution, the current identity, object scope, and approval status should be re-checked; do not simply reuse the results from when the task was created.

A least-privilege matrix can be built along "user role × business object × action". In the example, operators can read tickets in their own tenant, add internal notes, and request human takeover; reviewers can approve; the system executor only implements approved internal notes. Sending external replies and issuing refunds are not opened at all. The more specific the permission design, the easier it is to prove effectiveness with failure samples.

Knowledge is not permanently trustworthy once uploaded

For each knowledge chunk, save the document identifier, version, validity period, owner, and access scope. Retrieval results should carry not only text but also a link to a traceable version. When support staff see a citation, they should know which rule document it is based on; pending-approval actions save the cited version, and before execution, confirm the rule has not been revoked. When no usable basis exists, outputting "escalate to human" better serves the delivery goal than assembling an answer that merely looks complete.

Knowledge updates, revocations, and deletions are handled separately. An update may invalidate old recommendations; revocation requires that specific users can no longer see the content; deletion requires that indexes, caches, task snapshots, and related storage are handled according to agreement. Deleting the original file alone does not automatically prove derived data is gone. The project should agree on the deletion coverage and verification method, avoiding unverified promises of "real-time sync".

For scanned documents or tables, first check whether parsing preserves headings, column relationships, and terms and conditions. A bad split may separate "exception does not apply" from the main text, so retrieval returns only half a rule. The eval set should include such structural distortions so responsibility for data processing and model generation can be located separately.

Chapter 4: Architecture and System Integration — Let the Systems Beside the Model Carry the Controls

A reference architecture separated by responsibility

  • Support interface: display tickets, evidence, and action differences
  • Application entry: authenticate identity, fix tenant and user scope
  • Task orchestration: read-only queries, recommendation generation, checkpoints
  • Knowledge and business queries: versioned retrieval, scoped objects
  • Tool gateway: permission, parameter, action, and budget checks
  • Approval and execution: bind payloads, re-check status, save receipts
  • Operations chain: event logging, human takeover, reconciliation, and rollback

This diagram expresses responsibilities; it does not require deploying seven microservices. A small team can implement these modules within one application, as long as the boundaries are readable, testable, and independently locatable. The model service generates candidate content, the business service confirms resources and execution results, and the state store records progress. Wrapping all of them into a single conversation loop makes error sources and recovery actions hard to distinguish.

Model context must be minimized. What is sent is the question, authorized necessary fields, and valid materials — not the entire database, all historical tickets, or long-term logs. Set separate limits for retrieval, conversation history, and tool outputs. If materials are insufficient, request an additional authorized query or human confirmation, rather than endlessly expanding the context in pursuit of "smarter" behavior.

Tool contracts must be verifiable by the business side

A "universal API call" tool may let the model arbitrarily combine paths, objects, and write operations. A safer first-round interface is a small set of explicit tools, such as reading tickets in the current tenant, querying warranty status, retrieving valid rules, and proposing internal notes. Separate write execution out, requiring the server to verify approval and object version.

Tool results should retain structured status; do not let the model infer completion from a piece of natural language that "looks successful". HTTP success also needs to be mapped to a business receipt; if the interface says the task has been accepted, the final status must be queried. For legacy systems that cannot provide receipts, state that the result can only be marked as pending verification, and arrange manual reconciliation.

MCP is a connection method, not a business permission system

MCP can unify tool access, but the enterprise still needs to define who can access what, how tenants are passed, and which actions require approval. This article references the 2025-06-18 version of the authorization specification as an HTTP transport layer example and does not claim it is the latest version. A protocol token being accepted does not mean the user has the right to modify a given ticket. Source: MCP versioning specification

For each connector, register the authentication method, token lifecycle, target resources, error semantics, and version. Credentials stay on the server or in customer-approved key management; they must not enter prompts, frontend configuration, browser debug output, or public examples. After a connector changes, the authorization scope must be re-reviewed, not just confirming that tools can be listed.

Domestic platform capabilities should be verified separately

Alibaba Cloud's Agent Identity and Model Studio integration documentation provides concrete implementations of identity access. It helps understand how platform-level identity is integrated, but business object authorization remains the application's responsibility, and the documentation cannot be treated as a customer production case. Source: Alibaba Cloud

When selecting a domestic platform, verify model invocation, tool access, networking, logging, knowledge storage, deployment location, and version maintenance one by one. Do not assume that tenant, permission, and regional boundaries are automatically aligned just because the same brand provides multiple components. Use a controlled request to check which services are actually traversed, which fields are logged, and how recovery works after dependencies are disconnected.

Use public post-mortems to calibrate architectural applicability

Anthropic's public post-mortem on its research product discusses coordination, evals, and reliability in multi-agent systems. It shows that open-ended research and fixed ticket workflows have different design conditions; this article only borrows the lessons on task fit and engineering controls, and does not replicate its performance numbers. Source: public post-mortem

There is no need to copy a multi-agent research architecture for the first round of after-sales ticket handling. If the task sequence is clear, having multiple models discuss with each other may increase cost and state branches. Only consider multi-agent when you need to independently explore multiple lines of evidence and can merge conflicts and track each authorization and task state. Architectural complexity should have clear benefits and a validation method.

Chapter 5: Prototype to Production—Handle "Unknown Outcome" First

Engineering sequence diagram. First query the business-side receipt by task key, then decide whether to confirm, perform a controlled retry, or hand off to a human; the diagram assumes the business side supports idempotency and reliable receipts.

State must be saved to a recoverable location

Prototypes often execute queries, generation, and writes sequentially in memory, leaving only a chat transcript after the process exits. A production system needs to save the task identifier, current state, completed steps, referenced versions, proposed payload, approval basis, and result receipts. On recovery, read the persisted state rather than letting the model re-guess where things left off from conversation history.

  • created: verify identity and support scope
  • reading: read the authorized ticket and applicable rules
  • waiting_approval: save the proposal and wait for the designated person
  • approved: the exact payload is approved, but no business effect has occurred yet
  • done: the business-side receipt matches the local record
  • handed_off: a human has taken over, automated execution has stopped

Errors do not necessarily need a single unified failed state. Missing data may wait to be supplemented, insufficient permissions should be rejected, an API timeout may leave the outcome unknown, and human takeover represents a transfer of control. Different states should lead to different recovery entry points. Converting all errors into "please retry" will re-execute operations that have already produced effects.

LangGraph's persistence documentation provides implementation references for checkpoints and thread state, but checkpoints alone cannot guarantee that external systems will not write duplicates. Whether you use a framework or implement it yourself, business side effects and receipts must be managed separately. Source: persistence documentation

Approvals must be bound to the object and payload

The approval interface should display at least the ticket identifier, referenced materials, the action to be performed, the specific content, the scope of impact, and expiration conditions. The approval record is linked to an exact summary and version of the proposed payload; if the content changes after approval, the original approval can no longer be used. The example only allows adding internal notes, and does not allow quietly converting the "proposed content" under review into an external reply.

The business object may change while waiting. Before execution, confirm that the ticket version still matches, the knowledge has not been updated or revoked, and the operator and approver still hold current permissions. If any critical condition changes, return to the state of regeneration or re-approval. This process adds steps, but it prevents the approver from endorsing an old proposal while a new action is actually executed.

Rejecting an approval must also have defined behavior. After recording the reason, return to revision or human handling; do not automatically issue another approval request with nearly identical content to bypass review. When reviewers do not act for a long time, the business owner decides on escalation and timeout policies; the system must not default to approval after the wait ends.

Timeouts have three completely different meanings

Timeout locationKnown stateCorrect handling

The business side should ideally accept idempotency keys, such as a task identifier plus an action identifier within a tenant scope, and return the same result for the same key. If the business side does not support this, the execution layer needs reconciliation and compensation plans. Merely marking "sent" locally is not enough, because the service may exit between writing and recording. Do not extrapolate the guarantees of a local database transaction to exactly-once across systems.

The offline package deliberately simulates "submitted, response lost." When the task is executed again, it returns the saved receipt, and the business effect is applied only once. This result only proves atomicity within a single SQLite transaction in this example. After connecting to a real CRM or ERP, integration testing is needed for network outages, service restarts, and business-side receipt queries.

Stopping and takeover should be business features

After human takeover, the system stops new automated actions while preserving completed steps and pending reasons. The interface should tell the support agent what is half-done, which proposals have been approved, and which effects have occurred. Do not merely output "handed off to human" in the conversation while leaving background tasks running. The takeover operation and the executor need to share persistent state.

Set maximum steps, total duration, budget, and tool scope for tasks. When approaching limits, save state and hand back to a human; do not automatically expand the tool list or budget. Stopping is one of the expected ways to finish; it helps the team identify tasks unsuitable for the current automation scope and prevents the system from endlessly attempting in pursuit of completion rates.

Retries require error classification

Authentication failures, permission denials, missing objects, parameter errors, and business conflicts should first have their root causes corrected, not be resolved by random retries. Network interruptions, rate limiting, or temporary service failures can be retried within agreed counts and backoff, but write actions still require confirming side effects first. Each connector maintains its own error mapping, avoiding treating errors from different services uniformly as model problems.

Retry logs record the reason, count, last state, and the next responsible person. If the budget is exhausted, the task moves to pending human handling. The operations page can display "API temporarily unavailable"; there is no need to expose underlying library stack traces or server-side key-related errors to support agents. Debugging information and business explanations are provided separately to people with the appropriate permissions.

Deployment selection cannot rely only on whether the model runs. You must also confirm whether the identity system can be integrated, where data is parsed, where knowledge is stored, where logs go, whether model inputs are saved, what administrators can see, and how deletion covers derived results. Confirming each item with diagrams and tables is easier to accept than conclusions like "end-to-end security."

Owned environments also have boundary issues: shared model services, centralized logging, remote maintenance channels, and backups may cross the scope the customer expects. Public cloud may also meet certain project conditions through explicit configuration. Confirmation should be made jointly by the owners of the customer's data, IT, and applicable rules; do not treat deployment labels as conclusions.

First do minimal connectivity verification for network and identity

Make one minimal request each for the model, knowledge, business API, and logs. Record the origin, routing, credential source, response structure, timeouts, and quotas, without printing credential values. Test behavior when access is forbidden, credentials expire, and the network is interrupted. A smoke test with only the success path cannot prove that the customer's network can support the full task.

For customer single sign-on and organizational identity, confirm how user status changes are synchronized, whether permissions are re-verified in real time, and what identity backend tasks use. Background operations cannot permanently borrow a developer's account. Departures, authorization revocations, and service account rotation should be operations in the deployment checklist, not management issues patched up after launch.

Model availability also includes terms of service, current regions, quotas, model versions, and API capabilities. These change, so projects should record verification dates and selection decisions, and re-verify before formal launch. The manual does not list a vendor ranking claimed to hold forever, nor treat a one-time successful call as a long-term availability commitment.

Confirm external services and internal assistance separately

The intelligent agent implementation opinion from China's Cyberspace Administration proposes that security, reliability, and trustworthiness run through development and deployment. This article uses it as background for risk and responsibility checks, and does not derive from that document that all internal assistants must be filed or must be privatized. Actual projects should confirm obligations according to the service audience, functionality, data, and applicable rules. Source: China Cyberspace Administration

An internal note-taking assistant, a customer-facing auto-responder, and a system capable of executing financial actions have different responsibilities and loss boundaries. If it later expands from internal assistance to direct customer interaction, content moderation, identity verification, authorization, complaints, human takeover, and information disclosure must be re-confirmed. You cannot expand the original pilot acceptance scope just by flipping a switch.

Prepare business degradation for unavailable dependencies

When the model is unavailable, customer service should be able to return to the original ticketing system and keep working. When knowledge is unavailable, the system can stay in a pending-materials state and must not generate rules without basis. When the write API is unavailable, save pending suggestions and show that no effect has been produced yet; when receipts are unclear, hand off to personnel with authority to reconcile. Degradation must preserve the true state; do not beautify "service temporarily failed" as completed.

Check whether the deployment process can be rebuilt: dependency versions, configuration sources, database schemas, recovery steps, and build artifacts are all recorded. When migrating environments, first run control drills with synthetic data, then run customer-authorized integration samples. If the project runs only on some engineer's local machine configuration, it is not yet ready for handover.

Chapter 7: Evaluation and Acceptance — Making Failures Traceable to a Specific Layer

Evaluate answers, actions, and business outcomes separately

A ticket suggestion may be textually correct but use the wrong device record; or the basis may be correct, yet a timeout causes duplicate write-backs. Evaluation needs to simultaneously save user input, authorization scope, retrieval basis, model suggestions, tool traces, approval records, and final business state. Sensitive fields are masked per project conventions, and evaluation data also needs access and retention boundaries.

DimensionSample JudgmentCommon Troubleshooting Direction
Correct objectCurrent tenant matches the ticketIdentity, query conditions, cache
Valid basisCitations correspond to valid versionsIngestion, indexing, permissions, updates
Reliable suggestionSuggestion is faithful to the basis and stays in scopeContext, generation, business rules
Correct executionApproved action matches actual effectTool contracts, approvals, business APIs
Controllable recoveryNo duplicate effects when outcome is unknownState, idempotency, reconciliation
Business improvementEnd-to-end time and rework meet targetsProcess, adoption rate, queue ownership

Do not merge these dimensions into one composite score. The impact of an authorization failure differs from that of minor wording issues, and average scores easily mask launch blockers. At minimum, treat permissions, object mismatches, unapproved executions, and duplicate writes as independent outcomes; analyze the cause and fix of any known violation samples, rather than just raising the overall pass rate.

Evaluation sets need normal, boundary, and rejection samples

Select normal tasks from authorized representative tickets, and also add insufficient materials, contradictory rules, outdated knowledge, unauthorized objects, unsupported requests, and business concurrency changes. Write expected behavior for each sample: correct completion, request more materials, rejection, or human takeover. Expectations cannot be just one ideal answer; they must also include allowed tools, effects that must not occur, and evidence that must be retained.

Sample authors and reviewers should be able to explain the basis for answers. If even business experts disagree, mark it as needing clarification first; do not force one answer as the standard. Keep difficult samples — they help discover that scope needs narrowing, or that rules need unification by the business side. Deleting hard cases disconnects evaluation from real traffic.

Separate exploratory samples from the release regression set. Exploratory samples can be reviewed repeatedly during development; the regression set is used to compare candidate versions; newly discovered real failures are added to future benchmarks after masking and confirmation. Record sample source, date, version, and applicable scenarios, to prevent using outdated standards that no longer apply after model or retrieval adjustments.

Agree on metric denominators before writing thresholds

"Completion rate" needs to specify whether the denominator is all entered tickets, tickets within support scope, or approved tickets. If unsupported tasks are simply removed from the denominator, the number improves while masking insufficient business coverage. Report coverage, supported-task completion, human takeover rate, and reasons together, to avoid mixing rejections with failures.

Citation accuracy needs to clarify whether it is judged per citation or per task; latency should separate user waiting, model processing, and human approval; cost should distinguish per attempt and per completed task. Thresholds are determined by business baselines, risk, and resources; this article does not provide a pretend-universal "industry passing line." Target values in tables remain fillable.

Even if a test set shows no unauthorized actions, you cannot write that the system will never act without authorization. Record sample coverage and control implementations, and keep checking new connectors, role changes, and configuration updates. Production thresholds are built on combinations of controls and evidence, not a permanent guarantee from one zero-error result.

Chapter 8: Gradual Rollout and Operations Handover — Delivery Only Counts When Customers Can Handle Things Independently

Engineering advice. Offline controls, real APIs, model evaluation, and business gradual rollout provide different kinds of evidence; entering the next stage requires confirming scope, stop conditions, and responsibilities.

Expand scope in stages by risk

You can start with offline replay, then show only suggestions in real workflows, then open up approved internal notes, and only finally discuss higher-impact actions. Each stage specifies users, support scope, sample criteria, stop conditions, and the responsible person. Entering the next stage requires new evidence; you cannot carry over all conclusions just because the previous stage's demo went smoothly.

Shadow mode also requires authorization. Even without producing business effects, the system is still reading and processing real data. Shadow results must not be mixed into official customer service records, and pilot personnel should know which content comes from testing. If shadow results are manually copied into business systems, record this behavior to avoid misjudging that the system automatically completed the task.

During the gray release period, watch anomaly categories, not just call counts. Frequent rewrites of suggestions by users, excessively long pending-approval times, concentrated interface conflicts, and background actions persisting after takeover should all trigger analysis. Assign each problem category to someone who can change it; do not default everything into the model tuning queue.

Operations events should map directly to handling actions

Alerts should describe impact and actionable next steps: which task types are blocked, which tenants may be affected, whether there are writes with unknown outcomes, and which action chain to pause first. A bare "model error rate has risen" is not enough. The system should be able to disable a specific connector or write action while preserving read-only and manual work paths.

Quality feedback also needs to be localized. Collect error types, object identifiers, referenced versions, and necessary snippets; do not require support staff to submit full chat logs. After an issue is handled, record the fix version, regression samples, and review results. Treating "the prompt has been updated" as grounds for closing a case will miss knowledge, permission, and interface root causes.

Rollback does not end with deleting the new code

First distinguish code, model configuration, prompts, knowledge indexes, business data, and tasks currently in execution. Rolling back the application version does not automatically undo written notes, nor does it guarantee the old version can read new task states. Release records should state compatibility boundaries clearly; when necessary, freeze unfinished tasks and reconcile before resuming.

A practical rollback drill can start with pending tasks awaiting execution after approval: a release candidate fails, writes are paused, confirm tasks remain saved in the state store, restore a compatible version, verify payloads and knowledge versions, then decide whether to continue or re-approve. The result should include steps and receipts, not just "the service is back up."

When business impact needs compensation, record the original impact, compensation authorization, and compensation receipt; do not erase history without a trace. Some actions are irreversible, and whether they are enabled should be decided before release. Restricting the first round to internal notes is meant to keep the impact scope within a position that can be explained and verified.

Handover should rehearse at least three things

Have the customer's maintainer independently update a knowledge source and confirm the new version takes effect and old pending tasks are not executed incorrectly; have an operations person pause a connector and confirm users get the correct status and can switch to a human; have a business administrator revoke a role and confirm pending executions are rejected. The FDE observes and records; they cannot perform everything themselves and then claim the customer has taken over.

The handover package includes architecture, environment configuration documentation, responsible contacts, tool contracts, permission matrices, knowledge update procedures, eval sets, release records, rollback steps, budgets, and known limitations. Secrets are transferred via a method the customer approves, not written into manuals or public attachments. Any step that still exists only on the FDE's own machine must be explicitly resolved before handover.

Project closure should also be checked against the business baseline. If generation quality passes but actual usage is low, process obstacles should continue to be identified; if operational time is saved but after-sales disputes increase, the business cannot be declared a success. Writing out unmet goals, applicable scope, and follow-up improvements clearly is more valuable than treating a single launch as the conclusion.

FAQ

Should the FDE be responsible for all of the customer's AI problems?

They should be responsible for discovery, implementation, verification, and collaboration within the scope of this delivery, but business loss boundaries, data validity, permission management, and long-term operations must have their own responsible owners. The FDE cannot substitute their own technical judgment for these authorizations and responsibilities.

If all offline drills pass, can we go straight to launch?

No. It only validates control logic with synthetic examples; it has not yet proven real identities, business interfaces, model outputs, customer networks, or production operating conditions. Post-integration integration, real model evals, and business acceptance still need to be completed independently.

Does every task require human approval?

It depends on the impact scope and customer authorization. Low-impact, reversible, and well-controlled actions can be separately evaluated for automatic execution; when first opening outbound, financial, or irreversible actions, the acceptance results from internal notes cannot be reused. This document defaults to writing internal notes after approval in the first round.

How do we avoid having no one maintain the system after the pilot ends?

Before the pilot, identify owners for knowledge, interfaces, identity, and operations, and have them rehearse independently at handover; if there is no maintenance capability, narrow the scope or postpone expansion. Delivering only source code and one training session does not equal sustained maintenance capability.

Conclusion and update record

Enterprise AI agent deployment can start from a very narrow chain, but that chain must have accurate objects, valid grounds, execution authorization, business receipts, and human recovery. The FDE should turn these conditions into deliverables the customer can inspect and maintain, then expand scope based on actual business results.
