How to Draw a Current-State Process Flowchart: Mapping Out Processes Before AI Automation

Original title: 现状流程图怎么画：AI 自动化前的流程梳理

FDE Times · 2026-10-08 · 6 min read

Summary

This skills guide explains how to map as-is business processes before automating them with AI. Drawing on a logistics project where consultancy Edana found four undocumented email exchanges and shared files used to manually fix data, it argues that employees adapt around constraints and create workarounds, so real processes drift from documented ones—automating without discovery just digitizes the mess. The proposed six-step method: gather evidence (forms, emails, logs) first; run open-ended walk-through interviews per role; list all steps roughly, including hidden email-based ones; add exceptions, rework loops and waits; build swimlane diagrams and validate them with frontline staff in workshops; then tag friction points. The key deliverable is a friction-point list, which can change requirements—clients may need to eliminate duplicate data entry before deploying an order-reading agent. The article also lists common mistakes and how to present this skill on an FDE resume.

Key points

  • Real processes drift from documentation because employees create workarounds; automate only after discovery.
  • Six steps: evidence, interviews, step list, exceptions, validated swimlanes, friction points.
  • Every email-based manual task must appear in the model; exceptions are where agents fail most.
  • The most valuable deliverable is a friction-point list, not a polished diagram.
  • On resumes, state how many manual steps you found and removed before automation.

Key Takeaways

  • Employees adapt to constraints and invent their own workarounds, so the real process diverges from the official one; automating without mapping first just moves the chaos into digital systems.
  • The method has six steps: gather evidence, run "walk me through" interviews, roughly list every step, add the exceptions, arrange swimlanes and validate them in a workshop, and finally flag the friction points.
  • The real deliverable is a list of friction points to eliminate first, not a pretty diagram.

In a logistics project, the consulting firm Edana was mapping a client's data integration process when it discovered four email threads that never appeared in any documentation. There were also some shared files: employees would open these first to manually correct the data before importing it into the system.

If the engineering team had automated based on the official process diagram, these steps would have vanished from the new process, and dirty data would have flowed straight into the system.

At every client, you should assume this will happen. Edana has observed that employees adapt to all kinds of constraints: working around tools, or inventing shortcuts to get things done faster.

As a result, the real process gradually drifts away from the documented one. In Edana's view, digitizing without seeing these gaps just turns offline chaos into online chaos.

This guide explains, in practical working order, how to map the as-is process. No specialized software is needed: a text editor, a spreadsheet, and permission to sit next to frontline employees is enough.

What will you end up with?

The output has three parts: a complete list of steps (including manual ones), a swimlane diagram divided by role, and a labeled list of friction points. Of the three, the friction point list is the most valuable.

Edana writes that process mapping exposes friction points and, before automation, proposes ways to simplify or eliminate them.

To make things concrete, the examples below use a hypothetical scenario: a distributor receives orders from resellers, finance checks credit, and the warehouse ships. The client wants an AI agent that automatically reads orders and generates shipping documents.

Step 1: Gather evidence before talking to people

Visual Paradigm's guide to as-is modeling recommends first collecting every piece of evidence you can get: forms, emails, system logs, policy manuals. In this hypothetical scenario, you could request an order template, a few emails between sales and finance, a log exported from the finance software, and the existing process documentation.

Check for this step: for every department involved, you hold at least one real record. If a department has only documents and no traces of actual work, flag it, because the real process usually diverges most in exactly those places.

Step 2: Interview with "walk me through"

Don't ask "Do you follow the process?" That question only gets you the answer the person thinks you want to hear. Visual Paradigm recommends open-ended questions, such as: "Tell me what happens after a customer places an order."

When the person says "then I enter it into the system," keep probing: enter it where? What happens when information is incomplete? Do you need to ask anyone? Every "and then..." may be a step. Interview each role separately, because for the same order, sales and finance often tell two different versions.

Step 3: List all the steps, rough is fine

MockFlow recommends listing all the steps first rather than chasing a perfect process diagram right away. The template below is a simple text table made for demonstration and follows no modeling standard. The first three rows:

# | 角色 | 动作                       | 备注
1 | 销售 | 通过电话/聊天接单          | 文档中没有
2 | 销售 | 把订单重新录入 Excel       | 第 1 次录入
3 | 销售 | 发邮件请财务查信用         | 隐藏步骤

The last three rows:

4 | 财务 | 核查信用，邮件回复         | 财务软件
5 | 销售 | 把订单录入销售系统         | 第 2 次录入
6 | 仓库 | 按发货单发货               | 仓库软件

Visual Paradigm is explicit: any work done manually via email must appear in the model. Row 3 is exactly the kind of step people tend to omit, because "it isn't part of the process." In fact, it is the process.

Check for this step: every handoff between two people has its own row in the table.

Step 4: Add exceptions, rework, and waiting

The happy path is only part of the story. MockFlow reminds you to record rework loops, exceptions, waiting states, and bottlenecks. Go back through each row and ask: what happens when this step goes wrong?

In the hypothetical scenario, you might discover: when a reseller exceeds their credit limit, finance forwards the email to the department head for approval, and the order sits idle until a reply arrives. The AI agent you plan to build will run into exactly this situation. If that branch isn't in the process diagram, the agent won't know what to do.

Step 5: Arrange the swimlanes, then validate

When a process spans multiple roles, departments, or systems, MockFlow recommends a swimlane diagram. ManageEngine describes a workflow as a series of stages, connected by intermediate actions that move the work from one stage to the next. With that framing, arranging swimlanes becomes much easier.

Text sketch (simplified; the numbers in parentheses correspond to the table in Step 3):

[销售]        (1) 接单 → (2) 录入 Excel → (3) 邮件查信用
                                              ↓
[财务]                        (4) 核查信用 → 额度内？ ── 是 ──┐
                                              └ 否            │
                                                 ↓            │
[部门负责人]                           审批（等待）──────────┤
                                                              ↓
[销售]        (5) 录入销售系统 ←──────────────────────────────┘
                     ↓
[仓库]        (6) 发货

Whether or not approval is needed, both branches return to sales, get entered into the sales system, and then flow to the warehouse. The swimlane diagram makes it instantly clear how many times an order bounces between lanes. Every lane change can slow the work down, lose information, or require re-entry.

But a diagram you drew yourself is still just a hypothesis. Visual Paradigm recommends bringing the as-is sketch to a workshop with the people who actually execute the process. Print the diagram large, or project it on a screen, and let them mark it up directly with a pen.

The most effective question in this session is "What's wrong here?", not "Is this right?" Check for this step: for every role on the swimlane diagram, at least one person has confirmed their own lane.

Step 6: Flag the friction points before deciding where AI goes

Edana warns that modernizing an undiagnosed process carries all the workarounds along with it: duplicate data entry, multi-layer approvals, detours. So, before writing the first line of agent code, flag every friction point:

第 2 + 5 步   [DUP-ENTRY]     订单在两个系统里各录一次
第 3 步       [HIDDEN-EMAIL]  通过邮件查信用，任何系统里都没有记录
审批路径      [WAIT]          订单搁置，直到部门负责人回复

This list will change the requirements themselves. The client may not need an order-reading agent at all; they may first need to eliminate the Excel re-entry step, and then have the agent handle what remains.

Common Mistakes

The most common mistake is drawing the diagram from the documentation and calling it the as-is process. The second is interviewing only managers: they describe how the process is supposed to run, while frontline employees know how it actually runs. The third is dropping exceptions because they "rarely happen."

Yet exceptions are precisely where agents break most often in production.

Where does this skill fit in FDE work?

These six steps borrow from business analysts' requirements elicitation skills: interviewing, modeling, and scenario analysis. Microsoft's Requirements Gathering in Business Analysis course on Coursera covers exactly this, and includes a project on writing requirements for a manual process.

If you've never done this kind of work, it's a good place to start practicing.

When reading Forward Deployed Engineer (FDE) job descriptions, watch for passages that mention working directly with client operations teams, or understanding and redesigning business processes. When you see such requirements, put your process mapping experience near the top of your resume, rather than writing a generic "business analysis."

Be specific about how many manual steps you found, and which ones you eliminated before automating.

However capable an AI agent is, it will only execute the process you give it. Give it the paper process, and it will fail at the first email that never made it into the documentation.
