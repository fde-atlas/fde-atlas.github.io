Building an AI Governance Framework | Committees, Responsible Parties, and How to Run Operations

Original title: AIガバナンスの体制づくり｜委員会・責任者・運用の回し方

FDE Consulting · 2026-10-21 · 9 min read

Summary

This article explains how companies can build an AI governance structure once usage rules exist, drawing on Japan's AI Business Guidelines version 1.2 (published March 31, 2026) by the Ministry of Internal Affairs and Communications and METI, plus its annexes and a draft utilization handbook. The author maps governance into five roles—accountable executive, promotion team, consultation desk, committee, and audit—and argues existing committees and channels should be reused rather than creating new AI-specific bodies. It includes a risk-based triage table for when self-checks, consultations, or committee review are needed, a monthly-to-annual operating cadence, and a simplified setup for companies with a few dozen staff. Case examples from NEC, Panasonic Group, IBM, and Ubie share a common pattern: separating promotion from risk review while linking the two.

Key points

  • Based on Japan's AI Business Guidelines v1.2 (March 31, 2026) and its annexes.
  • Five roles: accountable executive, promotion team, consultation desk, committee, audit.
  • Reuse existing committees instead of creating AI-specific bodies.
  • Risk-based triage decides self-check, consultation, or committee review.
  • NEC, Panasonic, IBM, Ubie all separate promotion from risk review while linking them.

The next stumbling block for companies that have created rules for AI use is the organizational structure. Even though they have policies, no one knows who to consult. No one knows who decides whether a new AI tool can be introduced. The contact point for when an incident occurs has not been determined. A document containing rules alone cannot answer these questions.

This article organizes the structure of AI governance as a division of roles: "who decides, who is consulted, and who reports to whom." It is based on the AI Guidelines for Business Version 1.2 (published March 31, 2026) by the Ministry of Internal Affairs and Communications and the Ministry of Economy, Trade and Industry, the examples of corporate structures in its appendix, and the practical guide (draft) released at the same time. The concepts of AI governance itself and the frameworks in Japan and overseas are summarized in What Is AI Governance? 5 Things Companies Should Decide, and the overall picture of the guidelines and a checklist in What Are the AI Guidelines for Business.

What the Guidelines Say About the Structure of AI Governance

The AI Guidelines for Business do not prescribe a single form of organizational structure. What they describe is what the structure should accomplish. Extracting the passages related to structure yields the following four.

What is describedSummary of the guideline textSource
Appoint a responsible personAs an initiative under the common principle of "accountability," each entity should designate a person responsible for fulfilling accountabilityMain text, Part 2
Management leadsFor AI governance to be an effective initiative, management's responsibility is significant, and exercising leadership is important. Building AI governance should be seen not merely as a cost but as an upfront investmentMain text, Part 2E
Create a mechanism for reporting to managementExamples of methods include establishing an internal organization accountable to the board of directors (such as an AI ethics committee), reporting on the status of initiatives at board meetings, and circulating materials that organize benefits and risksAppendix 2
Build the foundation for preparationFour elements: a mechanism for understanding AI usage, a risk assessment structure, an incident reporting structure, and rule development and operationPractical Guide (Draft), Chapter 3

Regarding both the risk assessment structure and the incident reporting structure, the Practical Guide (Draft) states that if an existing structure is in place, there is no need to create a new one just for AI use. In my view, this sentence is the most practically useful. Before creating a new committee just for AI, you should first consider whether existing committees or contact points can handle it.

Five Roles That Make Up the Structure

Translating the guideline text into internal roles yields, in my view, the following five. In companies with few people, one person may hold multiple roles.

RoleResponsibilitiesExample of where to place it
Responsible personJudges the benefits and risks of AI from a management perspective and bears final responsibility. Reports to the management meeting or board of directorsAn executive in a position to give opinions to the business divisions that use AI
Promotion team (secretariat)Runs the usage ledger, rule revisions, training, and committee operationsInformation systems department, DX promotion department
Consultation contact pointAnswers questions from the field such as "Can we use this AI for this purpose?" Applies the criteria and escalates serious cases to the committeeCan be combined with the promotion team, with legal and security staff backing it up
CommitteeDeliberates on matters that cannot be decided by the consultation contact point alone. Decisions to halt use are also made hereAdd agenda items to an existing risk/compliance committee or information security committee
AuditVerifies, from a position independent of operations, whether the structure is functioning as decidedInternal audit department

The audit role must be separated from those running the operations. The guideline appendix requires confirmation by persons with expertise who are independent of the design and operation of the AI management system. How to conduct audits is summarized in What Is AI Auditing.

The dilemma when appointing a responsible person is whether to choose the head of the information systems department or the head of a business division. In my view, if the responsible person cannot voice opinions to the business divisions that generate results using AI, the committee's conclusions will be ignored in the field. That is why I recommend appointing an executive as the responsible person and delegating practical work to the promotion team.

Examples of Structures in the Guideline Appendix

The appendix to the AI Guidelines for Business Version 1.2 includes actual corporate structures. Since the companies differ in size and industry, they serve as material for finding a form close to your own.

CompanyStructure described in the appendix
NECHas built a structure that incorporates AI governance into its internal control framework, under the supervision of the board of directors and following the instructions of the Risk and Compliance Committee, centered on the CDO (Chief Digital Officer), who is the person responsible for executing AI governance. Legal, human rights, personal information/privacy, quality control, security, and other staff departments collaborate with business divisions
Panasonic GroupEstablished an AI Ethics Committee within the former Panasonic in 2019, and reorganized it in 2022 into the group-wide "Panasonic Group AI Ethics Committee." It selects at least one AI ethics officer from every group operating company and promotes initiatives together with the legal, intellectual property, information systems/security, and quality departments
IBMPromoted by the "Responsible Technology Board," renamed from the AI Ethics Board in 2025. Members are senior executives from each department, and each member designates focal points responsible for their departments and regions
UbieHas an internal "Generative AI Promotion Team" and a "Risk and Compliance Committee." It uses a flat rather than hierarchical organization to encourage information sharing between the two bodies. Risk assessment is conducted by specialists in each field, independent of the development and usage departments, and any concerns are deliberated by the committee

What the four companies have in common, in my view, is that they separate the side promoting AI from the side deliberating on risks, and then connect the two. NEC connects them through collaboration between business and staff departments, Panasonic through officers at each operating company, IBM through focal points, and Ubie through a flat committee structure. There is no need to imitate large companies' examples as-is, but the framework of "separating promotion and deliberation, and connecting them" works for small companies too.

Decide the Criteria for Escalation to the Committee in Advance

In my view, the most common snag after building a structure is that all consultations pile up at the committee, and deliberation cannot keep up. Regarding the risk assessment structure, the Practical Guide (Draft) states that instead of routing all use cases to the consultation contact point, criteria should be established to distinguish cases requiring consultation from those that can be handled by self-checks (a risk-based approach).

An example of criteria is shown in the table below. This is an example I created; the lines will shift depending on industry and the data handled.

CategoryExampleWho decides
Self-check is sufficientDrafting text with a company-contracted generative AI, using only publicly available informationThe user themselves. They check the items on the rules checklist
Ask the consultation deskIntroducing a new AI tool in a department and having it read internal non-public materialsThe consultation desk. They answer by applying the criteria
Deliberate at the committeeHandling customers' personal information, sending AI output to customers as-is, or having an AI agent operate internal systemsThe committee. Escalate to the responsible executive if necessary

To draw the lines between categories, the company must know which AI is running in which department using which data. The Utilization Guide (draft) also places a mechanism for grasping usage status as the first preparation item, listing as a management item which departments are using which AI for what purposes and uses. The inventory procedure is described in Inventorying Shadow AI.

How to Run Operations

Any structure becomes outdated from the day it is created. The AI Business Guidelines call for "agile governance," continuously and rapidly cycling through environment/risk analysis, goal setting, system design, operation, and evaluation. Translating this into an internal schedule, in my view it works as follows.

FrequencyWhat to doWho
Each time a consultation comes inAnswer by applying the criteria and record the response in the ledgerConsultation desk
Once a monthReview the ledger and check newly introduced and discontinued AI. If the same consultations keep coming, add to the rulesPromotion team
Once a quarterAt the committee, review the cases deliberated and incidents that occurred, and redraw the criteria linesCommittee
At least once a yearReport to the board of directors or management meeting on AI benefits and risks and the status of the structure's operationResponsible executive
When external changes occurReview the rules in response to revisions of laws and guidelines and changes in the terms of the AI services in usePromotion team

Decide the incident reporting destination at the start of operations. As an incident reporting structure, the Utilization Guide (draft) lists establishing a desk where incidents can be reported promptly, and considering in advance the flow of explanation and reporting both internally and externally, not just evaluation, analysis, and emergency measures, and rehearsing it. In my view, making the desk the same as the existing information security incident desk avoids confusion in the field.

As an example of external change, the AI Business Guidelines themselves have been revised repeatedly, from version 1.0 published on April 19, 2024 to version 1.2 on March 31, 2026. I have summarized the developments of the AI Act (AI Promotion Act), enacted in 2025, in What Is the AI Promotion Act.

Structure for Companies with Few People

The AI Policy decided by the AI Strategy Headquarters on December 19, 2025 requires responding according to the entity's size and position and the risks posed by AI, and states that using the governance mechanisms already applied to existing IT systems is also useful. For companies with few people, assigning roles to existing meetings and staff rather than creating a new organization is more sustainable.

In my view, a company of a few dozen people can start with the following setup.

  • The responsible executive should be the president or the officer in charge of the business using AI
  • The promotion team and consultation desk should be handled by the information systems staff. Decide in advance the legal consultation contact (including outside counsel)
  • Do not create a committee; add "AI consultations and ledger" to the agenda of the monthly management meeting
  • Make the incident desk the same as the information security incident desk
  • Start writing the rules with just two points: which AI may be used and what information must not be entered

I have included a template in How to Write AI Usage Regulations. In my view, the better a company's structure runs, the faster the question of "may we use it?" gets decided when trying a new AI, making it easier to move from PoC to production.
