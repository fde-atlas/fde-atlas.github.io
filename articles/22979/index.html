Summary

This Japanese guide by Shohei Tokumitsu, CEO of DeFactory, compares freelance and full-time employment for engineers across seven dimensions: income, social security, work style, creditworthiness, and career. It estimates freelancers need roughly 1.5x a salaried salary to maintain the same lifestyle, since they bear full insurance costs and lack paid leave. Citing surveys, it notes about 37% of freelance engineers have considered returning to full-time work and 10.8% actually have. The article offers pre-independence preparation advice (savings, skills, networks) and practical tips for freelancers re-entering employment, arguing neither path is inherently better and choices should flex with life stages.

Key points

  • Freelance engineers average ¥6–7 million annually vs about ¥5.57 million for salaried system engineers.
  • A 1.5x income multiple is the break-even point for freelancers to match a salaried lifestyle.
  • About 37% of freelance engineers considered returning to full-time roles; 10.8% did.
  • Before going independent, prepare 6–12 months of savings, 3+ years experience, and referrals.
  • Returning freelancers should cite positive motives like management goals, not income instability.

Freelance engineer or full-time employee—which work style suits you best? Many engineers struggle with this question.

According to the latest survey, about 37% of freelance engineers have considered switching to full-time employment, and 10.8% have actually returned to full-time work. On the other hand, the number of people moving from full-time employment to freelancing continues to grow.

In this article, we thoroughly compare the two across 7 aspects—income, social security, career, and more—and provide practical information for both directions: "full-time to freelance" and "freelance to full-time."

This is not a matter of whether freelancing or full-time employment is "better" or "worse." What matters is determining which one suits you.

High-rate, direct-contract project information

Full-time employees sign an employment contract with a company and work as employees. They are protected under the Labor Standards Act against unfair dismissal and unilateral changes to working conditions.

Freelancers, on the other hand, sign a service agreement for each project as sole proprietors. With no employment relationship, they are not bound by company rules, but they also cannot receive protection under the Labor Standards Act.

However, with the "Freelance New Law" enacted in November 2024, certain protections are being put in place, such as prohibiting delayed payment of compensation and requiring the delivery of written contracts.

This difference forms the foundation for income, security, and work style alike.

Full-time employees receive a fixed salary every month, making it easy to forecast annual income including bonuses. Even if the economy worsens, their salary won't drop immediately.

Freelancers' income fluctuates significantly depending on project availability and rates. If projects dry up, they face the risk of having zero income starting the following month.

However, in terms of annual income, freelance engineers tend to earn more. According to a survey by the Freelancers Association, 77% of engineers and technical development freelancers reported an annual income of 4 million yen or more, with average annual income estimated at around 6 to 7 million yen.

Meanwhile, according to the Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare's "2024 Basic Survey on Wage Structure," the average annual income of full-time systems engineers is approximately 5.57 million yen.

Choose "stability" or "the potential for high income." This choice is the first fork in the road.

Source: Freelancers Association, "Freelance White Paper 2023"

For full-time employees, the company pays half of the health insurance and employees' pension premiums. Paid leave, parental leave, retirement benefits, and commuting allowances are also provided.

Freelancers enroll in National Health Insurance and the National Pension themselves, bearing the full cost of premiums. There is no concept of paid leave, and no income protection if illness or injury prevents them from working.

Regarding workers' compensation insurance, freelancers became eligible for special enrollment from September 2021, but they cannot join employment insurance.

This difference directly affects take-home pay. Even with the same gross annual income of 8 million yen, take-home pay differs considerably between full-time employees and freelancers. We'll explain this in detail later.

For full-time employees, working hours, location, and job duties are determined by company rules. Many companies prohibit side jobs, limiting freedom in how you work.

Freelancers can choose the projects they join, as well as when and where they work. According to surveys, 74.3% of freelance engineers prefer fully remote work, and this freedom is a major attraction of freelancing.

However, "freedom" comes with "responsibility." Schedule management, quality control, and winning projects—all must be managed by yourself.

For those who can self-manage, it's heaven; for those who can't, it's hell. It's no exaggeration to put it that way.

Source: Mynavi Career Research Lab, "The Full Picture of Freelance IT Engineers"

While there are voices saying "don't become a freelancer" or "you'll regret it," many people are satisfied after making the switch to freelancing.

Here, we organize the real advantages and disadvantages of freelancing as a work style. You should also see the differences between those who succeed and those who struggle.

The greatest appeal of being a freelance engineer is that, depending on your skills, you can earn significantly more than a full-time employee.

When a company outsources engineers, the SI company or staffing agency takes a margin. Even on a project with a monthly rate of 500,000 yen, it is not uncommon for the engineer to actually receive only about half of that.

As a freelancer, you can cut out most of this margin. Even through an agent, fees are only around 5-15%, so with the same skills, your take-home pay can be 1.5 to 2 times higher.

A meritocratic environment where your income directly reflects your results is a great motivator for ambitious engineers.

However, "high income" is a possibility, not a guarantee. You need to keep making the effort to maintain your market value.

Freelancing allows you to "work when you want, where you want, on what you want."

Since you can choose your own projects and technical fields, you can either focus on areas that interest you or gain broad experience. If you don't like a client, you simply don't renew the contract.

Many people report that interpersonal stress is reduced, commuting time disappears, and their personal happiness improves.

Being able to work around your children's school pickups, make time for hobbies, and design your own work-life balance is a benefit that is hard to obtain as a full-time employee.

The biggest disadvantage of freelancing is income instability.

If projects dry up, your income drops to zero from the following month. There is no paid leave if you can't work due to illness or injury. During the COVID-19 pandemic, many freelancers experienced project cancellations or reductions.

Also, due to the image of "freelancer = unstable," your social credibility tends to be viewed as low. It is not uncommon to be at a disadvantage when applying for a mortgage or credit card.

If you have upcoming life events such as marriage, childbirth, or buying a home, you need to be especially careful. To prepare for these risks, consider saving at least six months' worth of living expenses or purchasing private income compensation insurance.

As a freelancer, you must handle everything yourself, not just engineering work, but also sales, contract negotiations, invoicing, accounting, and tax filings.

Many people feel frustrated that they "can't focus on technology" because these tasks, which they never had to think about as employees, take up their time.

Furthermore, competition is intensifying as the number of freelancers grows, so sales efforts to win projects are essential. This is an era where you cannot survive on the naive assumption that "good work will bring projects to me."

To reduce these burdens, using a freelance agent is effective. Some services not only introduce projects but also handle contract negotiations and invoicing on your behalf.

From a freelancer's perspective, full-time employment may seem "restrictive" or "low-paying." However, full-time employment has its own unique value.

For freelance readers, here is a summary of the pros and cons of full-time employment to use as a reference when considering the option of "returning to full-time work."

The biggest advantage of being a full-time employee is that a stable monthly income is guaranteed.

Even if the economy worsens, your base salary is protected, and if your company has a bonus system, it is easier to forecast your annual income. Even with unexpected expenses, the peace of mind of being able to plan "I'll cover it with next month's paycheck" is significant.

In addition, there is a robust safety net for when you cannot work, including company-paid social insurance premiums, paid leave, and sickness and injury benefits. Sickness and injury benefits pay about two-thirds of your salary for up to 1.5 years, so your life is less likely to fall apart even if you need long-term medical treatment.

For people with families or mortgages, this stability is a value that cannot be replaced by anything else.

Full-time employees have career paths through promotion, and the road to becoming a manager or CTO is open.

There are environments where the company supports skill development, such as training programs and certification support. Having the company cover the cost of self-investment is a major benefit.

Through team development, you can hone collaboration and leadership skills, and gain experience participating in large-scale projects. Experience managing projects with dozens to hundreds of people is hard to obtain as a freelancer.

Freelancing tends to be about "selling the skills you currently have," while full-time employment provides an environment for "developing your future skills."

The disadvantage of being a full-time employee is the low degree of freedom in how you work.

Working hours, location, and job duties must follow company rules. Transfers to departments you didn't want or assignments to projects you don't want to do are also possible.

Also, no matter how much you achieve, your income does not rise quickly. Getting a few percent raise in the annual review is considered good, and at companies with seniority-based elements, you may receive evaluations that don't match your abilities.

The more skilled the engineer, the more likely they are to feel dissatisfied, thinking "my market value should be higher than this."

<From an Agent's Perspective> Your experience as a full-time employee becomes a major asset when you become a freelancer later. Engineers who have experienced team development and management and acquired not only technical skills but also a "business perspective" tend to win high-rate projects in the freelance market. At DeFactory as well, we highly value engineers who can handle upstream processes and those with CTO or VPoE experience. Gaining "experience that can only be obtained within an organization" during your full-time employment expands your future career options.

If you jump into freelancing based purely on the feeling of "I want to be a freelancer," there is a high chance you will regret it.

Here, we explain in detail what you should check and prepare before going independent. Please use this as a reference so you don't end up thinking "I should have prepared back then..."

When you become a freelancer, even if your annual income figure goes up, your actual life can become harder. The cause is not understanding your "break-even point."

Full-time employees have half of their social insurance premiums covered by the company, but freelancers bear the full amount themselves. There is also no paid leave, so if you take time off, your income decreases.

Generally, it is said that to maintain the same standard of living as when you were a full-time employee, you need to earn about 1.5 times your previous annual income. A salaried worker earning 5 million yen a year needs to earn over 7.5 million yen as a freelancer to reach an equivalent standard of living.

The take-home pay gap between a freelancer earning ¥8 million and a full-time employee earning ¥5 million is about ¥2.15 million. This is the basis for the saying that "1.5x the salary is equivalent." If you only earn ¥6 million as a freelancer, your standard of living may actually worsen.

Before going independent, you should prepare three things: "funds," "skills," and "connections."

Finances: Ideally, you should have savings that allow you to live for six months to a year even if you can't land projects. Right after going independent, it can take time to acquire projects, and if you rush into unfavorable deals out of desperation, you'll fall into a downward spiral.

Skills: Objectively assess your market value and polish your portfolio. If you can't concretely explain "what you can do," you won't win projects. Three or more years of professional experience is considered a guideline for going independent.

Connections: Expand your network of acquaintances and former colleagues who might introduce you to projects. The most common way freelancers win projects is through "personal introductions." The connections you built as a company employee will become your greatest asset after going independent.

Since going freelance lowers your social credibility, it's wise to complete the following procedures while you're still a company employee.

  • Mortgage screening: Screening becomes stricter for freelancers
  • Credit card applications: Proving income becomes difficult
  • Health checkups and dental treatment: While you can still use company health insurance
  • Major purchases: While loans are easier to obtain

After leaving your job, you'll need to complete various procedures, such as filing a notification of business commencement (within one month of starting the business), submitting a blue tax return approval application (within two months of starting the business), and switching to National Health Insurance and the National Pension. Knowing these in advance will save you from scrambling after going independent.

"When to go independent" is an extremely important decision.

Many people suggest using three or more years of professional experience as a guideline, but you should also consider the following points.

  • Whether you have experience with projects through side work
  • Whether you have a clear estimate of your post-independence income
  • Whether you're likely to receive contract work from your former employer
  • Whether your family understands and supports the decision

A recommended option is to "start with side work and see how it goes." Since around 2018, more companies have allowed side jobs, letting you avoid the risk of jumping straight into independence.

By not impulsively quitting your job and instead going independent with thorough preparation and resolve, you can greatly reduce the risk of regret.

"Returning" from freelancing to full-time employment is by no means a negative choice.

According to recent surveys, about 37% of freelance engineers have considered switching to full-time employment, and companies are also open to hiring people with freelance experience. With proper preparation, "returning" is entirely possible.

There are four main reasons why more people are returning from freelancing to full-time employment.

1. Seeking income stability: Life events such as marriage, childbirth, or buying a home create a need for stable income.

2. Richer benefits and social security: As people age, they become more aware of health risks and their future pension amounts.

3. Redesigning their career: Joining large-scale projects or taking on new technology areas is often easier to achieve within an organization.

4. Wanting team development and management experience: Positions such as PM, VPoE, and CTO require experience within an organization.

According to surveys, about 60% of people who moved from freelancing to full-time employment have held management roles. For those aiming to shift their career from "specialist" to "manager," returning to full-time employment is a rational choice.

When hiring people with freelance experience, companies' biggest concern is: "Will they just go back to freelancing soon after we hire them?"

To dispel this concern, it's important to clearly explain "why you want to return to full-time employment" and demonstrate your intent to work long-term.

Examples of persuasive reasons:

  • "I want to hone my management skills through team development"
  • "I want to be involved in large-scale projects from the upstream stages"
  • "I want to be involved long-term in your company's XX business"

Answers to avoid:

  • "Because my income was unstable"
  • "Because winning projects was exhausting"
  • "Because filing taxes was a hassle"

Negative reasons give the impression that "you'll quit again the moment things get tough." Concretely convey "goals you can only achieve as a full-time employee."

Your freelance experience can become a major selling point if communicated properly.

Show concrete achievements:

  • What projects you worked on and what you were responsible for
  • What technologies you used and what you accomplished with them
  • What value you delivered to clients

Highlight strengths unique to freelancers:

  • Experience working with multiple clients
  • Experience with contract negotiations and creating estimates
  • Self-management and schedule management skills

On your resume and work history, clearly state your freelance period as "worked as a sole proprietor" and describe your project achievements in detail.

<From an Agent's Perspective> The most valuable in the market are "returnee engineers with a business perspective." Precisely because they've experienced freelancing, they understand the value of an organization, and they can write code with a management mindset. Strengths like "cost awareness," "strong responsibility for deadlines and quality," and "ability to handle client negotiations" are unique to those with freelance experience. At DeFactory, we highly value engineers with this kind of business perspective. "Returning" is not negative at all—rather, it's a positive career history that demonstrates depth of experience.

If you're too attached to your high freelance income, you may struggle in your job search.

Freelance and full-time salaries cannot be simply compared. When you factor in the employer's share of social insurance premiums, paid leave, and benefits, it's not uncommon for nominal pay 20-30% lower to be effectively equivalent.

Tips for salary negotiation:

  • Focus not on "how much you earned as a freelancer" but on "what you can do for this company"
  • Negotiate within an appropriate salary range based on market rates
  • Make a comprehensive judgment including conditions beyond salary (remote work, discretionary labor system, etc.)

By using a recruitment agent, you can have them handle salary negotiations on your behalf. Even condition negotiations that are hard to bring up yourself can proceed smoothly through a third party.

Whether freelancing or full-time employment is the "right answer" differs from person to person.

What matters is clarifying what you want to prioritize.

  • Want to maximize your income → Freelancing is a good fit
  • Want a stable life → Full-time employment is a good fit
  • Want freedom in how you work → Freelancing is a good fit
  • Want to grow within an organization → Full-time employment is a good fit

Also, there is no need to be bound by the work style you choose once. It is important to flexibly review your career according to your life stage and market conditions.

IT engineers are in high demand, and it is a profession blessed with opportunities to thrive both as full-time employees and freelancers. Please use this article as a reference to make career choices you won't regret.
