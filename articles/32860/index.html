Forward deployed engineer roles that need a security clearance

Kernavique · 2026-11-20 · 17 min read

Summary

Kernavique reviewed job ads on November 20, 2026 from Palantir, Scale AI, Vannevar Labs, Dataminr and Anduril for forward deployed engineer roles involving US security clearances. Across eleven ads, requirements ranged from active Top Secret or TS/SCI clearances to mere eligibility, with salaries roughly $113,000–$292,560 depending on employer and location. No ad promised employer sponsorship. Under 32 CFR 117.10, the government decides eligibility and only the employer can submit the request, so individuals cannot apply alone. Processing times run from about 200 days upward, and the article offers practical advice for both uncleared applicants and hiring companies.

Key points

  • Eleven ads across five employers required or accepted clearance eligibility.
  • No ad stated the employer would sponsor a clearance.
  • Under 32 CFR 117.10, only employers submit clearance requests; individuals cannot apply alone.
  • Initial Top Secret clearances average 206–243 days and times are trending longer.
  • Roles demand on-site work or heavy travel, up to 80% in one ad.

Some forward deployed engineer roles on US government programs need a security clearance. In ads read on November 20, 2026, Palantir, Scale AI, Vannevar Labs, Dataminr and Anduril asked for one, or for eligibility to obtain one. No ad said the employer would sponsor it. Under 32 CFR 117.10 the government decides eligibility and the employer submits the request. The regulation gives an individual no route to apply alone.

Neighboring roles ask for more. Some government-facing roles sit beside this one under other titles. Anthropic’s Applied AI Engineer, Public Sector ad in Washington, DC (first published October 6, 2026) asks for “Active TS/SCI clearance with Polygraph” and lists “Annual Salary” of $280,000 to $320,000. It names forward deployed engineer among the backgrounds it accepts. Anthropic’s Applied AI Architect, Public Sector ad uses the same clearance wording. Scale AI’s Field Engineer, Public Sector ad says “An active TS/SCI clearance is required.”

The work is on-site or on the road. All five Palantir ads are on-site. Tactical Edge states travel of “~25-75%”. Scale AI says “Expect 25% travel with up to 50% travel as necessary, and/or relocation to Washington DC or St. Louis.” Anduril’s Connected Warfare role is embedded at customer locations at least 50% of the time, and its Air Defense ad describes travel of up to 80% of the year. Dataminr’s remote role gives two travel figures in one ad: “approximately 25–75% of the time” and “50-75% to secure customer sites”.

What the ads say about sponsorship

No ad we read says the employer will sponsor a clearance. That is the plain finding, and it rests on our read of each page on November 20, 2026.

The ads fall into two groups.

An existing clearance is required. Palantir’s two Intel ads, Vannevar Labs and Anduril’s Connected Warfare ad ask for a clearance the engineer already holds. So do the Anthropic and Scale AI Field Engineer ads above.

Eligibility or willingness is accepted. These wordings leave the door open to an engineer without a clearance:

  • Palantir, US Government: “willingness to obtain/upgrade to or above Top Secret”.
  • Palantir, Tactical Edge: “willingness to upgrade to Top Secret”.
  • Scale AI, Public Sector: “or eligibility to obtain one”.
  • Dataminr: “or eligibility and willingness to obtain one”.
  • Anduril, Air Defense and Strategic Defense: eligibility for a clearance (our paraphrase).

Accepting eligibility is not the same as promising to sponsor. The ads stop short of that. The regulation in the next section says the employer is the one who submits the request, so it is reasonable to ask the employer directly how it handles this for a new hire.

One more ad needs a label. A copy of an OpenAI ad titled Forward Deployed Engineer, Gov on Thrive Capital’s job board lists “Active TS/SCI clearance or equivalent” under preferred qualifications. It is a mirror marked as posted more than six months ago, the role may be closed, and we could not read OpenAI’s own page. We do not rely on it.

Who decides, and how a clearance is obtained

The rule for contractor employees is 32 CFR 117.10. We read the Code of Federal Regulations edition dated July 1, 2025, on govinfo.gov, and did not check for later amendments. What follows is what that text says. It is a summary, not legal advice.

  • The government decides. “The CSA is responsible for determining an employee’s eligibility for access to classified information.” CSA means cognizant security agency. Access “must be clearly consistent with U.S. national security interests as determined by the CSA.”
  • The employer starts it. The contractor must first determine that classified access is essential to tasks under a classified contract.
  • Only for the employer’s own people. Contractors may not request eligibility for people who are not their employees or consultants, or for a subcontractor’s employees.
  • No stockpiling. The contractor “will limit requests for determinations of eligibility for access to classified information to the minimum number of employees and consultants necessary for operational efficiency”. Requests may not be used to build a pool of cleared staff.
  • A new hire can be processed before the start date. If access is needed from the first day, the contractor may submit the request early, given a written commitment of employment and the person’s written acceptance. The commitment must say employment begins within 45 days of eligibility being granted.
  • Citizenship. Applicants claiming US citizenship must provide evidence: “All documentation must be the original or certified copies of the original documents.” Contractors “must make every effort to ensure that non-U.S. citizens are not employed in duties” that may require classified access. The CSA may grant a Limited Access Authorization “in those rare circumstances”.
  • Temporary eligibility. “the CSA may grant temporary (previously called interim) eligibility for access to classified information”. It may be granted only if there is no evidence of adverse information, generally cannot exceed one year and cannot be granted to non-US citizens. Withdrawal of temporary eligibility is not a denial or revocation and cannot be appealed.
  • An earlier investigation can carry over. Where a current eligibility determination rests on a prior investigation of sufficient scope, “The prior investigation will be used without further investigation or adjudication” unless significant derogatory information has surfaced since. We read this paragraph, and the point that a Limited Access Authorization is not valid for Top Secret, on Cornell’s non-official copy of the same section.

Can an individual apply alone? The regulation gives no route for it: the contractor determines the need and submits the request. IC Careers, the US Intelligence Community careers site, says the same of agency hiring: “You can only start the process after you receive a conditional offer.”

Levels. The regulation names three levels of classified access: TOP SECRET, SECRET and CONFIDENTIAL. The Department of Energy uses its own terms. Its personnel security FAQ (updated March 5, 2026) says “Generally speaking, there are two types of access authorizations, the L and the Q” and “Under the reciprocity guidelines, a Q is the equivalent of a Top Secret.”

Polygraph. IC Careers says “Some agencies require you to take a polygraph test.” The Department of Energy lists a polygraph among the extra requirements some programs add.

In practice: a clearance is tied to a job that needs it. The route in runs through an employer with a classified contract and an offer, not through an application of your own.

How long it takes

Three sources in our reading give figures. They measure different things and should not be compared with each other.

Source Published What it measures Figure
GAO testimony GAO-26-108838 (official) February 24, 2026 The fastest 90% of initial Top Secret clearances, as of the second quarter of fiscal year 2025 Average of 206 days, against a 114-day goal
IC Careers (official) No date shown The whole process for intelligence agency hiring, not contractors 9 to 12 months on average
ClearanceJobs, reporting a DCSA briefing (secondary, not official) September 2, 2026 Industry initial Top Secret, end to end 243 days
ClearanceJobs, reporting a DCSA briefing (secondary, not official) September 2, 2026 Industry initial Secret, end to end 197 days

GAO. In testimony of February 24, 2026, GAO said “agencies took an average of 206 days” to complete the fastest 90% of initial Top Secret clearances, which was “92 days beyond the 114-day goal”. It added: “Average times for initial Top Secret clearances have trended longer from fiscal year 2022 to fiscal year 2025.” An earlier GAO report (GAO-26-107100, December 11, 2025) explains the measure: the goals apply to the fastest 90% of cases, so the slowest 10% are left out before averaging. The real spread is wider than the average suggests.

IC Careers. “The entire process takes, on average, 9-12 months and having a security clearance already may not speed up the process.” This describes hiring by intelligence agencies. The steps it lists are a conditional offer, the SF-86 form, a possible polygraph, a possible psychological or medical evaluation, a credit check and National Agency Check, a background investigation and adjudication.

ClearanceJobs. This is a secondary source, not an official one. In an article of September 2, 2026, Lindy Kyzer reported figures presented by DCSA’s Tom Giancoli at a NISPPAC meeting. For an industry initial Top Secret, “the current end-to-end timeline is 243 days”: “20 days for initiation, 97 days for the investigation and 126 days for adjudication”. For an industry initial Secret, “the total is 197 days”. The article says DCSA now reports overall averages, not the fastest 90%, so these figures are not comparable with GAO’s 206 days. We did not open the underlying DCSA reports.

If you do not hold a clearance

Start with the wording. An ad that asks for an “active” clearance is closed to you on its own terms. An ad that accepts eligibility or willingness is not. In our sample those were Palantir’s US Government and Tactical Edge ads, Scale AI’s Public Sector ad, Dataminr, and Anduril’s Air Defense and Strategic Defense ads.

Then keep four points from the official sources in mind.

  • You cannot begin the process alone. It starts with an employer and an offer.
  • Citizenship comes first. The regulation asks for evidence of US citizenship and treats access for non-US citizens as a rare exception.
  • Plan in months. The published averages run from about 200 days upward, and GAO says times for initial Top Secret clearances have trended longer.
  • Ask about the waiting period. The regulation allows temporary eligibility and lets an employer submit a request before the start date. Whether an employer uses either is its own decision, so ask.

Applying without a clearance is not unusual in itself. On the question of applying for a job without one, the Department of Energy’s FAQ says “Yes, you can definitely apply for the job.” That page covers the Department’s own access authorizations.

If you already hold a clearance, say so plainly, with the level and the agency that granted it. The regulation lets a prior investigation of sufficient scope be used again. IC Careers warns that for agency hiring an existing clearance “may not speed up the process”.

The clearance sits on top of the job, not in place of it. The engineering bar is the same one described on our forward deployed engineer role page, and the forward deployed engineer interview guide covers how employers test for it. Engineers can send Kernavique their details, including any clearance held.

If you are hiring cleared engineers

The same regulation shapes what a company can do.

  • Need comes first. A request rests on your determination that access is essential to tasks under a classified contract.
  • You can request eligibility only for your own employees and consultants, and only for the minimum number needed. You cannot build a pool in advance.
  • You can start before day one. With a written commitment of employment and the person’s written acceptance, the request may go in before the start date.
  • Time is the constraint. Set program plans against the published figures above, not against a hoped-for date.

Your ad decides who applies. Three things are worth stating clearly, because the ads we read vary on each:

  • Whether an existing clearance is required, or eligibility is enough. Seven of the eleven ads in our table accept eligibility or willingness.
  • The level, in the program’s own terms.
  • Travel and on-site time, with a figure.

None of the ads we read says the employer will sponsor. If you will, saying so sets your ad apart from every one in our sample.

Companies hiring for government programs can ask Kernavique for help.

In practice

For an engineer: read the clearance line of the ad before anything else, sort ads into “must already hold” and “eligibility accepted”, and ask each employer how it handles the request and the wait. For a company: confirm the contract need, write the requirement in plain words, publish pay with its basis and plan the start date around the published processing times. For both: the government makes the decision, and nobody in the hiring process can promise its outcome or its timing.

What we left out

  • Definitions of “active”, “current” and “expired” clearance. No official page we could open defines them. The regulation speaks of “eligibility for access”. We also do not define the shorthand the ads use, such as TS/SCI or “Full Scope Poly”.
  • DCSA’s own statements and processing figures. Its pages could not be opened and its quarterly reports were not read. The 243-day and 197-day figures come through ClearanceJobs.
  • State Department and ODNI guidance. Those pages could not be read as text.
  • Any amendment to 32 CFR 117.10 after July 1, 2025. We read the annual edition only.
  • Cleared ads we saw only in search results, including others from Vannevar Labs, Anduril and Databricks, and ads from federal contractors. We did not open them.
  • OpenAI’s government roles on OpenAI’s own site. The pages could not be read; the one mirror copy is labeled above.
  • Ads where a clearance is optional or where a different vetting applies. They are not cleared roles in the sense used here.
  • Whether cleared roles pay more. Nothing we read measures it.

Questions

  • Can I apply for a security clearance myself?
  • The official sources we read give an individual no route. Under 32 CFR 117.10 the contractor decides that access is needed and submits the request, and only for its own employees, consultants or people holding a written offer. IC Careers, the US Intelligence Community careers site, says “You can only start the process after you receive a conditional offer.” This is a summary of those pages, not legal advice.
  • Which employers ask for a clearance for forward deployed engineer roles?
  • In ads read on November 20, 2026, Palantir, Scale AI, Vannevar Labs, Dataminr and Anduril each advertised a role titled forward deployed engineer or forward deployed software engineer with clearance wording. Some require an existing clearance. Others accept eligibility or willingness to obtain one. It is a sample of ads we opened, not a full list of the market.
  • Will an employer sponsor my clearance?
  • No ad we read says so. Several accept “willingness to obtain” a clearance or “eligibility to obtain one”, which leaves the door open to an engineer without one. The regulation says the employer, not the individual, submits the request. Whether a given employer will do that for a given hire is a question to put to that employer.
  • How long does a security clearance take?
  • GAO testified on February 24, 2026, that agencies took an average of 206 days to complete the fastest 90% of initial Top Secret clearances, as of the second quarter of fiscal year 2025, against a 114-day goal. IC Careers says its process takes 9 to 12 months on average for intelligence agency hiring. ClearanceJobs, a secondary source, reported higher figures from a DCSA briefing on September 2, 2026.
  • Do cleared forward deployed engineer roles pay more?
  • Our sources cannot answer that. No ad we read states extra pay for holding a clearance, and the published ranges vary by employer, location and pay basis. Palantir’s government ads show $135,000 to $200,000 a year, Vannevar Labs a base range of $160,000 to $240,000 and Anduril’s Connected Warfare ad $114,000 to $192,000, base only (all read November 20, 2026).
  • Do I need to be a US citizen?
  • The regulation points that way, with a narrow exception. 32 CFR 117.10 says applicants claiming US citizenship must provide evidence, and that contractors must make every effort to ensure non-US citizens are not employed in duties that may require classified access. It allows a Limited Access Authorization in rare circumstances. Temporary eligibility cannot be granted to non-US citizens.
