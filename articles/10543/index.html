FDE Is Trending: The People Handling the Last Mile of AI Deployment—Half Are New Roles, Half Are Old On-Site Engineers

Open a job-hunting app and search for a term you might not have heard of three months ago: FDE, Forward Deployed Engineer.

Over the past few months, OpenAI, Anthropic, and Google Cloud have all been pushing engineers toward customer sites. In China, roles like FDE, B2B FDE, and FDE team lead have started appearing too. Along with some eye-popping numbers: million-yuan salaries, surging job postings, and OpenAI putting $4 billion into a deployment company.

The problem is, after all the buzz, you still don't know whether this is actually a role worth switching to. Is it a new profession of the AI era, or is it just "pre-sales + implementation + account manager + technical support + on-site outsourcing" repackaged with an English name?

My take: the FDE hype is real, but under the same name, two completely different things are mixed together. Half is productized forward deployed engineering; half is the old on-site model with a new skin.

So this article won't sell you "million-yuan salary" anxiety. First, let's set the numbers straight, then look at where the real value of this job lies.

First, let's correct the numbers most easily distorted

First, OpenAI is not spending $4 billion of its own money to support a team of FDEs.

The verifiable facts are: on May 11, 2026, OpenAI announced the creation of the OpenAI Deployment Company. It will acquire the applied AI consulting and engineering firm Tomoro, bringing in roughly 150 FDEs and deployment experts. The new company is majority-owned by OpenAI, with an initial investment of over $4 billion, led by TPG, with participation from Advent, Bain Capital, Brookfield and others, as well as systems integrators and consultancies like Capgemini and McKinsey.

This distinction matters. What it shows is not "OpenAI being rich and capricious," but that capital, consultancies, and systems integrators are all betting on one thing: the bottleneck for model companies in the next stage is not the models themselves, but getting models into real enterprises.

Second, the job growth is real, but don't mix up the metrics.

The publicly verifiable data is already dramatic enough: Business Insider, citing Indeed data, reported that FDE job postings grew about 729% year-over-year in April 2026; the WSJ also reported that FDE job postings in 2025 grew more than 10x compared to 2024.

But the commonly circulated "LinkedIn 42x / AI engineer 13x" figures — I couldn't find a public original report for those. It's not that these numbers can't exist, but they can't be cited as hard evidence in an article. The defensible statement is: this role has gone from niche to explicit demand, but the exact multiples depend on the platform, baseline, and time window.

Third, that "built a $20 million product in 6 weeks" story can't be traced to a source.

This quote is often attributed to former Palantir engineer Nabeel Qureshi. But I went through his 2024 piece "Reflections on Palantir" and didn't find it. What the original text does support is: his first big project was Airbus, where he moved to Toulouse, France for a year, working four days a week near the A350 final assembly line, extremely close to the business on the ground.

That's already enough to convey the intensity of the FDE role — no need to add an unsourced, rousing number.

Why now? Because models finally have to make money

Over the past few years, the industry has focused most of its attention on "making models stronger." But once model capability reached a certain stage, the questions customers actually care about changed.

Not "what's your model's benchmark score," but:

  • Can it integrate with my permission system?
  • Can it read my historical data?
  • Can it pass compliance and audits?
  • Can it actually save me money, grow revenue, and improve efficiency?

This is the deployment gap. Models can do everything in demos, but once they enter a real enterprise environment, they run into hundreds of Excel files, scanned PDFs, cross-department permissions, legacy systems, compliance processes, and bosses who can't articulate their requirements.

In a public interview, OpenAI FDE lead Colin Jarvis made a point very plainly: after ChatGPT launched, customers were excited, but struggled to actually extract value from the models. So the only reliably effective approach is to embed engineers at the customer site, understand the workflows, and build the system into production together with the customer.

The emergence of the FDE is essentially the industry admitting one thing: whether an AI project ultimately lives or dies often depends not on the model, but on whether someone pushes the model into real workflows.

What does an FDE actually do? Writing code is only part of it

Palantir is the birthplace of this role. Nabeel wrote that Palantir FDEs often spend three or four days a week at customer sites — flying there first, then figuring out on the ground what problem actually needs solving.

It sounds like an engineer, but it's not purely writing code. It's more like a three-in-one role:

Engineer: connecting models, data, tools, permissions, and business systems.

Product manager: translating requirements the customer can't articulate into a deliverable system.

On-the-ground business person: pushing things through the departmental walls, process walls, and compliance walls of the customer's organization.

The hard part isn't building a demo — it's getting the data, figuring out permissions, persuading the business side, and getting the system into daily workflows. That's where the FDE's value lies: not selling the model, but getting the model actually used.

A real FDE and a senior on-site outsourcer are separated by just a thin sheet of paper

To judge whether an FDE role is worth taking, don't look at the title first, and don't look at the salary first. Ask one question first:

Will what you build at the customer site eventually become the company's product, or will it stay with just that one customer?

Real FDE

  • Paid by results or subscription; once the customer succeeds, the company can reuse it.
  • Customer pain points flow back into product capabilities, platform primitives, and standard solutions.
  • What you personally accumulate is transferable judgment: how to break down problems, how to integrate systems, how to make trade-offs.

Re-skinned on-site outsourcing

  • Paid by person-day; one more day on site, one more day billed.
  • Results stay with a single customer; once you leave, nobody understands the system.
  • What you personally accumulate is customer-specific knowledge that gets discounted the moment you leave that context.

This is also the biggest risk in China. In the US, enterprise software and subscription models are more mature, making it easier to amortize FDE costs into recurring revenue; in China, many projects are naturally settled by person-day, on-site presence, or customization, so you can easily get pushed into being a "senior implementer."

This isn't a problem with people — it's a problem with the business soil.

Klarna is the best reminder: one case, two sides to see

Many people treat Klarna as the success template for AI customer service. And it was indeed high-profile: in February 2024, Klarna announced that its OpenAI-powered AI assistant handled 2.3 million conversations in its first month, doing the equivalent work of about 700 full-time agents, covering roughly two-thirds of customer service chats, with an estimated $40 million in profit improvement.

But there's another side to the story. In 2025, the Klarna CEO emphasized again that nothing is more valuable than people in the AI world, and admitted that an excessive cost focus leads to service quality problems, beginning to reinvest in human customer service.

Klarna is neither a simple "AI success" nor an "AI failure." What it shows is: AI can replace part of the process, but service quality, complex problems, and human trust will still pull the system back to reality.

This is exactly where the FDE should exist: not crudely removing people from the process, but redesigning the division of labor among people, systems, and models.

How to view domestic salaries? Don't just stare at "million-yuan annual salary"

A circulating domestic article contains a set of salaries: ByteDance Doubao FDE, Ant Digital B2B FDE, and Zhipu FDE lead, with monthly pay ranging from tens of thousands to 80,000. But I haven't verified official JDs or original public media pages yet. They can serve as clues, not as hard proof.

From public searches on BOSS Zhipin, you can see two anchors for AI FDE roles, at Neolix Huitong and Ant Group respectively:

Senior IC: 35-55K × 13 months, Hangzhou, 5-10 years, bachelor's degree.

Team lead P9: 80-110K × 15 months, Beijing / Shanghai / Hangzhou, 10+ years, requiring an AI technology or product background in finance, healthcare, or energy industries.

This is more informative than the four words "million-yuan annual salary." What they're looking for is not fresh graduates, not pure algorithm engineers, nor people who can only write code, but people who have genuinely navigated AI deployment in traditional industries and understand both technology and business.

If you happen to come from finance, insurance, healthcare, energy, or manufacturing, and have worked on AI projects, this FDE wave is closer to you than you think.

My judgment: where FDE will go

First, FDE is a deployment-gap window role. When the product is not yet mature, customer scenarios are highly messy, and model capabilities are just good enough to create value, it becomes expensive. Once the product matures and the toolchain is complete, it will fall back from a core role to a transitional one, but it won't disappear.

Second, the real winners will treat FDE as a product radar. The customer site is not a service revenue pool, but a product discovery system. Whoever can force customer pain points to crystallize into products will get lighter over time. Conversely, treating FDE as an outsourced profit center will make you heavier over time.

Third, AI coding and agents will eat part of the plumbing work. Automatically connecting data, automatically generating integration code, automatically running validation—these will raise the leverage of a single FDE. So headcount may not expand indefinitely, but top FDEs may become scarcer.

Fourth, China will more easily slide into high-end on-site outsourcing. The reason is not that the engineers are inadequate, but that pricing models, trust structures, client maturity, and the SaaS soil are different. You can copy the Palantir name, but the reality will likely still be business trips, on-site work, and hand-holding.

Finally, three sentences for those who want to switch to FDE

First, don't rewrite your resume because of one "million-yuan annual salary" article. Behind the high salary are high travel demands, high communication burnout, high uncertainty, and the risk of being locked in by a single client.

Second, to judge whether an FDE role is real or fake, ask just one question: Will what you build go into the product, or will it only stay with that one client?

Third, what's truly scarce is not the FDE title, but the capabilities it trains: digging out real problems from vague requirements, building systems under real constraints, breaking through organizational silos, and turning chaos into reusable solutions.

Titles will cool down; muscles won't. Whether "FDE" is a buzzword doesn't matter—what matters is whether you have the ability to push AI from demo to reality.

One thing to do today

Find a real business scenario you've recently encountered that claims to be "transformed by AI." Your own company works, or a client site works. Then answer just one question:

Is this stuck because the model isn't strong enough, or because the data can't be accessed, the process isn't sorted out, the permissions aren't connected, or no one can articulate what's needed?

If the answer is the latter, then what you've just seen is exactly the problem FDEs truly solve.
