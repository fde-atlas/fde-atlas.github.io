What Is an Architecture Decision Record (ADR): How to Write One and Templates

Original title: 架构决策记录 ADR 是什么：写法与模板

FDE Times · 2026-11-11 · 6 min read

Summary

This guide explains how forward deployed engineers can use Architecture Decision Records (ADRs) to handle clients who change direction mid-project. Each ADR should cover one decision in one to two pages, recording context, the decision itself, and consequences—including negatives and considered alternatives. Accepted ADRs are never edited; when circumstances change, a new ADR supersedes the old one, turning disputes into a single question: has the context changed? Storing ADRs in a docs/adr/ folder alongside code makes them valuable handover material for client operations teams.

Key points

  • One ADR records one decision, one to two pages, always with rationale.
  • Context must list considered options; consequences must include downsides.
  • Accepted ADRs are immutable—write a new ADR and mark the old as superseded.
  • Keep ADRs in the code repo's docs/adr/ directory as handover material.
  • Common failures: overly long documents, missing alternatives, pros-only lists, editing old records.

An ADR can't stop the client from changing direction, but it can narrow the debate down to a single question: has the context changed?

Key Takeaways

  • Each ADR should cover one decision only, be one to two pages long, and must record the reasoning, not just the outcome.
  • The context section should list the alternatives considered; the consequences section should record negative and neutral impacts as well as benefits.
  • Accepted ADRs are never modified: when the decision changes, write a new ADR, mark the old one as "superseded," and keep it in the log.

Imagine the project is in its third month. A newly arrived director from the client's side joins a meeting and asks: "Why is the data synced overnight? It should be real-time." Everyone in the room looks at you.

The person who signed off on that design has since moved to another role, that old email is buried somewhere in an inbox, and you only vaguely remember there was a very good reason at the time.

Microsoft Learn describes exactly this scenario: decisions that were made but not documented are easily forgotten, leading either to repeated debates or to later changes that inadvertently violate the original intent. Teams either go along with the crowd because no one can push back, or cling to a decision whose rationale no one can recall.

Forward Deployed Engineers (FDEs) work on-site at clients, where both personnel and requirements can change mid-project, so this scenario is far from rare. The easiest tool for handling it is the Architecture Decision Record (ADR), which takes only a page or two.

An ADR Records the Reasoning, Because the Reasoning Is What Gets Forgotten First

In his 2011 article on documenting architecture decisions, Michael Nygard pointed out that over a project's entire lifecycle, one of the hardest things to trace is the motivation behind each decision. He set a clear constraint: the whole document should be kept to one or two pages. Martin Fowler is even stricter, recommending that ADRs stay short, usually just one page.

The definition on adr.github.io is: each ADR records one architecture decision and its rationale, and all of a project's ADRs together form its decision log. AWS Prescriptive Guidance puts the purpose bluntly: to avoid discussing the same architecture topic over and over again.

Three Essential Sections, Two Often-Overlooked Details

According to AWS, every ADR must at least include the context of the decision, the decision itself, and its consequences for the project. The context should also list the alternatives the team considered. Add a status and a change history, and you have a framework that can handle a client changing their mind.

The difference between a usable ADR and a box-ticking one lies in two details. The first is the consequences section: write down the drawbacks, not just the benefits. An ADR full of advantages reads like sales material and will convince no one when someone raises an objection.

The second is how the decision sentence is written. Write it assertively, such as "The team adopts..." rather than "should adopt...". "We should use batch processing" is an invitation to debate; "The team adopts batch processing" is a commitment.

A Sample ADR for the Scenario Above

Suppose you are deploying a policy-retrieval AI agent for an insurance company. The client's IT department only allows read access to a read replica of the database between midnight and 5 a.m., because the core systems are under heavy load during the day. You chose overnight syncing. The ADR would look roughly like this:

# ADR-0007：通过夜间批处理将保单同步到搜索索引

## 状态
已接受（经客户 IT 代表确认）

## 上下文
- Agent 需要客户核心系统中的保单数据。
- 客户 IT 只允许在 00:00 至 05:00 之间读取只读副本。
- 主要用户是核保员，他们处理的是前一天的保单。
- 考虑过的方案：
  1. 每晚从只读副本批量同步。
  2. 通过变更数据捕获实现实时同步：客户 IT 暂不允许。
  3. 每次查询都调用核心系统 API：高峰时段有负载风险。
- 如果 IT 启用 CDC，或核保员需要当天的保单，需重新审视本决策。

## 决策
团队采用凌晨 1 点运行的批处理任务，读取只读副本并更新索引。

## 后果
- 好：工作时间内不给核心系统增加负载。
- 坏：白天新建的保单要到第二天早上才能被检索到。
- 坏：如果某天夜里任务失败，数据会再晚一天；需要配置告警。
- 中性：如果 IT 启用 CDC，必须编写新的 ADR。

Note the last line of the context and the last line of the consequences. They state the conditions under which the decision should be revisited. It is precisely these two lines that turn an ADR from a memo into a negotiation tool.

When the Client Changes Their Mind, Don't Modify the Old ADR

Back to that meeting in month three. You don't have to defend the old design, nor concede immediately. Open ADR-0007 and ask: does IT now allow change data capture (CDC)? Or, do the underwriters now need same-day policy processing?

If the answer is no, the debate can end quickly, because the original rationale still holds. If the answer is yes, the context has genuinely changed, and there is a reason to change direction. At this point Fowler's rule is strict: an accepted ADR is never reopened or modified; it must be superseded by a new ADR.

Concretely: write an ADR-0012 for the real-time solution, recording the new context and change history. Change ADR-0007's status to "Superseded by ADR-0012."

Nygard wrote that when a decision is overturned, the old record should be kept, simply marked as superseded. Microsoft Learn recommends linking the two records to each other, while AWS Prescriptive Guidance emphasizes keeping the old record in the decision log.

Six months later, someone newly joined reads this chain of records and can understand what steps the project went through, and why.

ADRs Belong in the Handover Materials

A clean approach is to keep the ADRs in the same repository as the code they describe, in a

docs/adr/

directory, with files named by number. For an FDE, the biggest benefit appears on handover day: the decision log is delivered along with the code, alongside the README and the runbook, so the client's operations team doesn't have to dig through a wiki they may not even have permission to access.

Treat this directory as part of the handover materials, on par with the installation documentation. The runbook answers "How does this thing run?"; the ADR answers the question the person taking over will ask when they want to make a change: "Why was it done this way?"

At the handover meeting, spend a few minutes walking through all the accepted ADRs and the trigger conditions for revisiting each decision.

Write an ADR as soon as each decision is made, while the reasoning is still fresh in everyone's mind; don't pile them up to write all at once in the final week. The AWS Architecture Blog recommends that each ADR focus on a single decision. If the word "and" appears in the title, split it into two files.

Mistakes That Turn ADRs into Wasted Paper

The most common mistake is writing too much. Few people will read a five-page ADR, so it's unlikely to be useful in any meeting. The second is omitting the alternatives considered: when the new director proposes the real-time solution, you need to show that this option was already evaluated and why it was rejected.

The third is listing only the benefits, which gives skeptics reason to question the entire document. The fourth is modifying the old ADR directly when the decision changes, erasing the very history the ADR was meant to preserve.

The last is having only the engineering team know the ADRs exist. The "accepted" status only carries weight after the people with decision-making authority on the client's side have read and confirmed them.

A Skill Worth Putting on Your Resume

In FDE job descriptions, technical documentation is real, hands-on work. Tungsten Automation's Forward Deployed Engineer job posting explicitly requires writing customer-specific technical documentation and best practices to reduce knowledge loss. ADRs are a concrete way to do exactly that.

So when reading job postings, look for items about documentation or knowledge loss, and mention this habit there. Putting a few ADRs with client information removed in your portfolio, or telling a story in an interview about how an ADR defused a proposed architecture change, is more persuasive than any adjective on a resume.

Clients will always change their minds; that's their right. Your job is to make the cost of each change of mind just one new page, not three weeks of debate starting from scratch.
