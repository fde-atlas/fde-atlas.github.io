AI Strategy Consultant vs Forward Deployed Engineers

MarsLab · 2026-11-01 · 7 min read

Summary

This MarsLab article contrasts AI strategy consultants with forward deployed engineering teams. Consultants deliver roadmaps, ranked use cases and costed business cases; embedded engineering teams deliver a working production system built inside the client's operation. The deciding factor is whether your bottleneck is choosing what to build or building it. The piece covers when each option is wasted money, cost and billing structures (citing US FAR time-and-materials rules), output ownership (citing Copyright Office guidance), and questions to ask before signing. MarsLab's own model runs strategy to production in eight weeks, illustrated by a Sanjeevani Group case where sugar recovery rose toward 9.0-9.5%.

Key points

  • Buy advice when you can't name the problem; buy delivery when you can.
  • A strategy engagement ends with a document, a deployment with real users.
  • Roadmaps without budget or an owner are the common waste case.
  • Get code, cloud accounts, credentials and model ownership in writing.
  • MarsLab promises production in eight weeks or says no gain exists.

Choose an AI strategy consultant when you cannot yet say which problem to solve, and a forward deployed engineering team when you can. A consultant delivers a roadmap and recommendations. A forward deployed engineering team delivers a working system in production, built inside your operation. The deciding question is whether your bottleneck is choosing what to build or building it.

What does an AI strategy consultant deliver?

A document and a decision: a roadmap, a ranked list of use cases, and a recommendation on what to build, buy or skip. On the last day you hold a plan, not a running system.

Good strategy work maps your workflows, estimates where AI could move a business metric, tests whether your data and systems can support it, and sorts the options by value and effort. The output is usually a report and a presentation, and sometimes a costed business case that a board can approve. Someone else, inside or outside the firm, then has to build it. MarsLab's own AI discovery and ROI roadmap is an example of the genre.

What does a forward deployed engineering team deliver?

A system in production, and the engineers who keep it running until your own people can. They sit inside your operation, connect your data and older systems, and build against the constraint that is actually in the way.

Our guide to forward deployed engineers covers the role itself, so this article covers only how it compares with advice. The practical difference is the artifact. A strategy engagement ends with a document. A deployment ends with real users on real data and the old process retired. How MarsLab runs it is described on the forward deployed engineers page.

When is a strategy engagement the right first step?

When the open question is what to build, not how. If you cannot name the process you want to change, the number that will prove it worked and the person who signs it off, building produces an expensive guess.

  • Several teams want AI for different problems and nobody has ranked them.
  • You do not know whether your data is good enough, and finding out is a piece of work in itself.
  • A board, a lender or a regulator needs a written and costed case before money is released.
  • You will need several vendors and want an independent view to compare their bids.

Independence is the strongest argument for pure advisory work. A firm that does not build has no reason to size a roadmap to its own delivery capacity, and you can hand its output to any team.

When is a strategy engagement wasted money?

When you already know the problem, or when nobody is committed to build what the roadmap recommends. A roadmap that sits in a drawer is the most common way this money is lost, and it is avoidable.

  • The problem is already named and measured, so the report would restate what your operators said in week one.
  • No budget or owner exists for the build, so the roadmap has no next step.
  • The recommendations are generic: use cases that could apply to any company in your sector.

Before signing, ask what decision the document will let you make, and by when. If there is no answer, wait.

When is a forward deployed team the wrong choice?

When you cannot yet say what success looks like, when you will not give the team access to the systems and data, or when nothing built will be reused. In each case an embedded team spends its time on a target that has not been defined.

Two further cases matter. If your own engineers have spare capacity and only lack direction, advice plus your own team is cheaper than an embedded one. If the work is routine upkeep of a stable system, an embedded team built for change is the wrong tool. A traditional support contract fits that job better.

MarsLab's own rule is to say so when there is no real gain to be had in eight weeks, because a pilot that never ships is worse than no pilot. We describe why in week eight is a production date.

What is the cost and time shape of each?

A strategy engagement is short, front-loaded and bounded: a set number of weeks, a fixed deliverable, and a cost that stops when the document is handed over. A deployment runs longer, costs more in total and includes the cost of the thing running.

  • Strategy: the effort goes into interviews, workshops and analysis, so the cost is mostly senior people's time. It ends on a date. The risk is that the value is zero until someone builds.
  • Deployment: the effort goes into integration, data work and adoption, so the cost follows engineers working on your problem for weeks or months. The risk is that scope grows and value arrives late.
  • Strategy then build: you pay twice, and the second firm has to learn your operation again. Ask whether the roadmap is written so another team can estimate and execute it.

Billing models differ as much as prices. Open-ended build work is often charged by the hour, because its extent is hard to estimate in advance. US federal procurement rules reflect this: FAR 16.601 says a time-and-materials contract "may be used only when it is not possible at the time of placing the contract to estimate accurately the extent or duration of the work", and that it "provides no positive profit incentive to the contractor for cost control or labor efficiency." Those are government rules, but the incentive applies to any buyer.

Where you can, tie the fee to a defined scope and a production date. MarsLab's standard engagement runs from strategy to production in eight weeks. We give no prices here, because the right comparison is the cost of a working result and not a day rate.

Who owns the output of each?

The contract decides, not the type of firm. From a strategy engagement you usually hold a document, so the questions are who may reuse it and share it with other vendors. From a deployment the questions are code, cloud accounts, credentials and models.

Ownership does not pass to the buyer automatically in every case. The US Copyright Office's Circular 30 says a work made for hire can arise "when a certain type of work is created as a result of an express written agreement between the creator and a party specially ordering or commissioning it." The lesson is to write ownership down. Rules differ by country, so have a lawyer check the wording.

For a deployment, ask for each of these in writing:

  • The source code sits in a repository in your account.
  • The cloud accounts and credentials are in your name.
  • You hold the models or fine-tunes, or you know exactly which third-party API you depend on.
  • You can export your data in full without a support ticket.
  • The system is documented well enough for another team to run it.

On MarsLab engagements the client owns the system, the cloud account and the models. The reasoning is in if you can't export it, you don't own it.

Can one firm do both, and does it matter?

Yes, and it cuts both ways. One team removes a handover and keeps the plan honest about what can be built. An independent adviser has no stake in what you decide to build.

MarsLab is one firm that does both. Its AI strategy work is done by the team that builds, so the recommendation has to survive being implemented by the people who made it. That suits a buyer who wants one accountable party. A buyer who wants advice that does not depend on who builds should hire an adviser who will not bid for the build, and should insist the roadmap is written so any competent team can execute it.

What should you ask either one before signing?

Ask what you will hold at the end, who owns it, and what they will tell you if AI does not help. A firm that cannot answer plainly is selling hours.

  • What exactly will I hold on the last day, and in what format?
  • What decision or measurable result is this meant to produce, and who signs it off?
  • Which named clients can I speak to, and what changed for them?
  • Who owns everything you produce, in writing?
  • What will you say if there is no real gain to be had?

Ask a consultant:

  • Are you paid for anything you recommend, directly or through partners?
  • Will the roadmap include effort estimates that another team could use?
  • How many of your past roadmaps were built, and by whom?

Ask a forward deployed team:

  • What is the production date, and what counts as production?
  • Who runs the system in the week after you leave?
  • How much access do you need, and how is it limited?

How do you decide in practice?

Match the first purchase to the question you cannot yet answer. If it is what to build, buy advice. If it is how to get something built and running, buy delivery.

  • You can state the problem and the metric: start with a delivery team, after a short scoping session.
  • You cannot: buy strategy first, capped in time and tied to one decision.
  • You have capable in-house engineers: buy strategy or a short review, then build it yourselves.
  • You need independent sign-off: use a separate adviser, then a separate builder.

Talk to us when you can name the problem and want it in production in eight weeks, or when you are unsure and want an engineer to tell you which of the two you need.
