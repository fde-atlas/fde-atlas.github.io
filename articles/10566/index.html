Bosses Make Wishes to AI, Employees Tell AI to Get Lost | Deep Kr

Original title: 老板向AI许愿，员工让AI滚蛋｜深氪

36氪 · 2026-11-02 · 15 min read

Summary

This 36Kr feature examines the reality of forward deployed engineers (FDEs) in China, a role pioneered by Palantir and popularized by OpenAI and Anthropic. Bosses treat FDEs as wish-granters, asking to replace entire IT departments or monitor employees with agents, while frontline staff resist AI projects out of fear of layoffs. Deployments stall on messy legacy data, vague workflows, and runaway token costs. Big tech firms use FDE teams as bets to unlock cloud revenue, but one engineer can serve only one or two clients, and headcount reduction remains the main yardstick of AI's value.

Key points

  • Bosses ask FDEs for unrealistic wishes like replacing whole IT departments.
  • Employees resist AI projects, fearing their knowledge will lead to their replacement.
  • Messy, unstandardized data forces companies into costly data cleanup before AI works.
  • Big tech firms deploy FDEs to win cloud deals, but each engineer serves only 1-2 clients.
  • Headcount reduction remains the clearest measure of whether AI deployment succeeded.

The Boss's "Wishing Well"

"Do you know how Musk manages his employees?" When Hu Xinlan heard this probing question, she sensed trouble.

The boss across from her came from a heavy industry enterprise with over ten thousand employees. On social media, the boss had seen news about Musk using agents to replace human workers, and couldn't help but have the same idea. He said bluntly to Hu Xinlan: "Can you help me lay off the entire IT department? I'll just communicate with agents every day."

Among the many bosses Hu Xinlan has dealt with, wishes about "layoffs" are widespread. They come from different industries such as finance, manufacturing, and the internet, with company sizes ranging from a few hundred to over ten thousand employees. Some tentatively float the idea: "Don't you think our company is somewhat overstaffed?" Others throw out the ultimate question: "What's the maximum number of people we can lay off?" The one imitating Musk was the most radical she had encountered.

There are also bosses who, without explicitly mentioning layoffs, hope AI can monitor employees' every move. "Can you install an agent for me that records what each employee does every day, what they produce, and how far along each project is? Ideally, it should also monitor and call on these agents at any time, and calculate each person's input-output ratio based on complete logs."

Hu Xinlan knows full well that bosses making such requests simply haven't put layoffs on the table yet. They want to first collect enough workforce efficiency data before "laying people off as precisely as possible," to avoid "cutting into a major artery."

Although Hu Xinlan deals with "layoff" demands every day, she doesn't work in human resources. Her job is one of the most popular roles in the AI world today—FDE, or Forward Deployed Engineer.

Right now, AI is developing at a staggering pace: models iterate rapidly, applications launch one after another, and startup funding shows no sign of slowing. But this speed and heat have not been successfully transferred to the enterprises hoping to use AI to boost efficiency and transform production. Accenture surveyed 163 Chinese companies across seven industries, and the results showed that only 9% of enterprises achieved improvements in productivity, revenue, and profit from AI applications.

The FDE role grew precisely out of this "gap between technological development and technological implementation." It was first proposed by the Silicon Valley company Palantir, later popularized by OpenAI and Anthropic, and in 2026 rode the wind from Silicon Valley to China. This job is like a bridge between AI and enterprises: FDEs are stationed at client companies, deeply understand their business needs, and then implement AI capabilities such as large models and agents in a targeted way, narrowing the gap between AI and real work scenarios.

But in China, much of FDE work is awkward—many of the demands companies throw out are as unrealistic as "making wishes."

Once, Hu Xinlan was presenting her company's AI products to clients from different industries at a meeting, when a boss suddenly took over the conversation and passionately described the great significance of AI for humanity: "It's not just about work—when will our bodies and health data be handed over to AI? Let silicon-based life forms replicate and benefit us carbon-based life forms. You should figure out a way to build this."

When he finished, no one in the room said a word. After struggling to manage her facial expression, Hu Xinlan took control of the situation: "That would require careful long-term planning." She then hastily ended the meeting, preventing the demand from leaping from improving work efficiency to transforming human life.

Julie, an FDE entrepreneur, has faced similar troubles. Not long ago, an e-commerce client sent her a promotional video for a product called "AI CMO": just by entering the company's website, the product could handle the entire marketing workflow from copywriting to operations to ad placement, all seamlessly. The video's soundtrack was rousing, and the boss's attitude was even more fired up than the film: "If this can be achieved, I'm willing to pay a high price for it!"

Julie's heart tightened. "I never thought I'd now be the one expected to deliver on the promises AI companies make." She knew full well that just showing AI the information on a company's website could never achieve the effect in the promotional video. "It can automatically post ads, but it can't guarantee users receive valuable information."

This is the awkwardness of another kind of mismatch: business bosses don't know what they truly need, nor do they know what AI can achieve. "To our clients, we're just a wishing well," Julie said.

In fact, behind these "wishes" lies the deep anxiety of many business bosses: inefficiencies exist to varying degrees across many of their company's production processes, and more importantly, they are terrified of being left behind by the times for missing the AI train.

It's not just business bosses—on the other side of AI, the AI companies with the technology and products have their own anxieties.

For example, in March this year, Tencent launched WorkBuddy, and ByteDance and Alibaba quickly followed. ByteDance launched Doubao Work, and Alibaba launched Qwen Office, both targeting the enterprise market. The three giants have turned the battlefield toward the AI office entry point, but in reality, their office agents don't differ much in functionality and lack technical moats. How to get customers to choose them has become one of the giants' biggest headaches.

So the giants began setting up FDE teams intensively, hoping this service model would push their own AI into enterprises. FDE has also become a new card for the giants to relieve their commercialization anxiety.

A sales manager at Alibaba Cloud told 36Kr that facing WorkBuddy's first-mover advantage, his approach is to first offer enterprises free usage quotas, then assign more people to do FDE work. "Right now it's about who's willing to bet bigger. Quite a few technical and pre-sales staff at the company have transferred to FDE roles, and strategically important products like Bailian (Alibaba Cloud's MaaS platform) even have dedicated FDE teams."

WorkBuddy has also recently built its own FDE team. A pre-sales manager at Tencent was originally reluctant to have his team do FDE work, but out of fear that clients would be poached by competitors, he also chose this approach to "hold on" to clients.

The giants want to use FDEs to fight for market share, while small and medium-sized enterprises want FDEs to turn AI into real efficiency. Both anxious sides are betting on FDEs at the same time.

The challenges FDEs face are not unique to the AI era—they are merely a replay of the SaaS era from 20 years ago. The tricky part is that as technology can take over more work, the gap between technology and implementation also grows wider.

It seems the problems of the past are reappearing in more complex forms.

The Tug-of-War Under the Shadow of Layoffs

When faced with clients' wishes, Hu Xinlan never refuses—instead, she uses "magic to defeat magic."

For the client who wanted to imitate Musk, Hu Xinlan first nodded earnestly, then reasoned along his logic: "Understood, but this would require reading tens of thousands of AI execution logs every day. Do you think you could free up the energy for that?" The boss hesitated for a moment, and Hu Xinlan kept pushing: "Also, once employees use other tools, you'd lose access to the context."

If reasoning wasn't enough to calm the client down, Hu Xinlan would pull out her trump card: "Implementing this could cost as much as ten thousand US dollars per day in token fees." After this sentence, the client instantly calmed down. Hu Xinlan knew it was time to discuss a realistic solution.

The company Hu Xinlan works for is not large, and while working as an FDE she also handles sales. To win orders, she doesn't have much room to argue—instead, she needs to help clients understand in an acceptable way that "it's not impossible, it's just not right for you at the moment."

Lin Ling is an FDE at a leading tech giant. Over the past six months, she has noticed clients' timeline requirements for AI projects getting shorter and shorter. Project delivery periods once measured in years no longer exist. Clients often push a four-month delivery period down to three. And as project deadlines approach, clients come up with more and more new ideas they want implemented. She doesn't accommodate all of them; instead, she digs out the original emails and contracts and checks item by item what was agreed upon, "like a couple pulling up chat logs during an argument."

Compared to small startups, big companies indeed have more leverage with clients: clients are usually already locked into their cloud services, and migration costs are high, so they won't easily cancel the partnership. Whenever clients make her furious, Lin Ling reminds herself: we're an old married couple—what are we going to do, get divorced?

And even after aligning expectations with the boss, FDEs run into new difficulties: employees find it hard to empathize with the boss's urgency about AI.

Pan Kelin is a second-generation owner of an auto parts factory. Troubled by the factory's inefficiency, he tried using AI to transform it. At first, whenever he built an AI tool, he would send the plan to a WeChat group, then gather a dozen or so department heads for a meeting to solicit feedback. But the meetings usually swung between two extremes: "either no problems at all, or nothing but problems."

Sometimes employees would nod along: "No problem, we'll do whatever the boss says." But when it came to actual implementation, problems popped up everywhere: some rushed to explain how it would increase their workload and difficulty, some said resources were insufficient and cross-department cooperation was hard, and some even brought up difficulties at home—in short, "it's simply impossible to move forward."

Before becoming a second-generation factory owner, Pan Kelin had never held a job and "didn't understand the workplace." After a few meetings, he gradually came to see the difference in perspective between bosses and employees: bosses care about the company's efficiency and revenue, while employees care about whether their workload will increase, whether they will have to take on more responsibility, and more directly, whether they will be laid off because of AI.

Before becoming an FDE, Hu Xinlan never anticipated that "what's harder than building AI tools is the game of human nature between bosses and employees."

When asking clients' technical staff for cooperation, Hu Xinlan often hit a wall. The first time she visited the R&D department, she asked what needs they had for AI to boost efficiency. The researchers all stared at their computer screens, and only the clatter of keyboards answered her. After two minutes, an employee at the far edge replied: "I've got a lot of work lately, no time to migrate tools."

Later, at a requirements meeting, an R&D engineer asked pointedly: "Is your product as good as Claude Code? If we switch, how do you guarantee our work progress?" Hu Xinlan quickly realized that "being busy" was more of an excuse, and the main reason was likely the engineers' "tool purism"—they had their own ideas in mind about which tools worked well.

There were also those who simply refused to engage. Once, Hu Xinlan offered a free Agent seat to the IT employee sitting at the next desk. The employee, in a calm tone, replied: "No need, Doubao is fine."

If an AI efficiency initiative touches employees' livelihoods, it walks straight into a dead end.

Julie once served a small bank whose boss had a clear demand: he wanted AI to optimize the loan approval process.

To do this, Julie first needed to understand the business process from frontline staff, build a running pilot, and then use the results to secure budget from the business managers. In loan approval, different banks have different risk appetites and calculation standards, and Julie realized this was more critical than the generic process—and it depended heavily on human experience.

But the loan officers Julie approached for their insights seemed unwilling to cooperate: the more urgently and specifically Julie asked, the more hesitant and vague the officers' responses became. Until one of them shot back: "What are you actually doing this for?" Only then did Julie realize that what she wanted to learn was the officers' most important knowledge barrier in their work. The moment she tried to have AI optimize that work, the officers instantly felt the fear of being laid off.

Later, meetings between Julie and the company's employees began to be postponed with all kinds of excuses. When the first version of the Agent made errors in a trial run, Julie sent it to a loan officer for correction, but never received a reply.

The boss wants to embrace AI but doesn't understand the business; frontline employees understand the business but don't want to embrace AI; middle managers mostly show support, but only on the condition that results are obvious and they bear no risk themselves.

Caught between these different roles, Julie found that a boss's simple statement of "supporting AI" is far from enough. The biggest contradiction here is: bosses who want to use AI to replace people precisely need those replaceable employees to first teach the AI "how to work."

Paying Down the "Debt"

On the very first day after FDE entrepreneur Edison deployed an Agent for a client, he received nearly 300 error messages.

His client was a garment manufacturing company. In the past, the boss needed at least two hours a day just to review production information. All of the factory's production data was compiled in one giant Excel spreadsheet: orders for over a hundred garment styles arranged in rows, production status for different stages—fabric, zippers, hangtags, and so on—spread across columns, jointly updated and maintained by dozens of production line supervisors.

The factory had an ERP system, but it only recorded the most basic static data: product type, customer, amount, delivery time. The data involved in actual production, such as the production information above, was not in the system. This data tends to be fragmented and changeable, scattered across local Excel files, chat logs, and people's heads in different departments.

Edison originally planned to deploy an Agent on this Excel spreadsheet to help the boss identify production risks. But because the supervisors could never keep the spreadsheet up to date in time, the Agent kept sounding alarms. When employees were busy and didn't update immediately, the AI judged the order as delayed; when a character was missing a space, the AI flagged a production anomaly... The workarounds humans could understand were all treated as problems by the AI.

For AI to enter a company's core production and work processes, one key is having data that can be called upon at any time. But for many companies, this is a long-buried old debt.

When Pan Kelin first started transforming his factory with AI, he also found data gaps everywhere. For example, ten workshops had ten sets of daily reports, each written differently. With inconsistent recording methods, cross-workshop information aggregation was inefficient, and subsequent steps like production scheduling slowed down accordingly. Once AI got involved, let alone improving efficiency, it could barely even read this information accurately.

Thus, the AI efficiency gains companies originally envisioned got stuck, turning into "paying down debt"—falling back to the stage of "data cleaning and governance."

For Pan Kelin, that meant reworking the daily report standards from the source. He laid the ten versions side by side and compared them item by item, asking workshop supervisors on one hand and discussing with AI on the other, spending two weeks re-establishing the recording standards for workshop daily reports. And this work was only the beginning: "Who knows how long it will take to sort out all the scattered data from the past."

What surprises many FDEs is that before considering AI adoption, a large number of companies never even thought "non-standardized data" was a problem.

An FDE at Alibaba Cloud told 36Kr that during periods of rapid growth, most company managers' attention revolves around business growth. In the past, when a department failed to report information or the system lacked a feature, such flaws didn't stop the company from making money, so over time they were shelved.

But now that they are pinning their hopes on AI to bring new efficiency and growth, these "minor issues" that never affected revenue have all become hurdles to clear first.

"This Is Not Doubao"

At the first month-end reconciliation, Hu Xinlan's client was startled by the Token bill. A company of one to two hundred people was consuming hundreds of thousands of yuan in Tokens per month.

After checking the backend data, Hu Xinlan quickly found the cause: although employees were using the Agent, their habits were still stuck in the "chatting with Doubao" stage. For example, for every single task, they would open a new conversation, then upload large files and restate company background and business rules. The same information was read by the model over and over, and Tokens were rapidly consumed in repeated contexts.

The correct approach is to write routinely repeated work into Skills, put long-term unchanging materials into a knowledge base, and have the Agent read only the information relevant to the current task each time it works.

Hu Xinlan repeatedly emphasized to the client company's employees, "This is not Doubao," but with little effect. She later realized that behind the disorganized conversations was the fact that no one had ever systematically mapped out their own workflows.

Every Tuesday through Friday, Hu Xinlan stayed at the client's company. The large conference room where she worked was like a temporary "AI clinic." Employees from different departments streamed in wave after wave, bringing work problems and asking Hu Xinlan what Skills could solve them. Seeing more than twenty people a day was the norm; sometimes, even near the end of the day, people were still waiting outside the door.

The problem is that most of the people who came to Hu Xinlan couldn't articulate exactly which part of their work they hoped AI would help with.

For example, a data analyst once came to Hu Xinlan hoping to build a "data analysis Skill." That term covered the entire workflow of fetching data, cleaning, statistics, analysis, and report writing. To figure out where exactly the work was getting stuck, Hu Xinlan could only have him "play back" his day: where the data came from, what to do first after getting it, which numbers required judgment, and how it was finally delivered...

After several rounds of follow-up questions, it turned out the employee simply wanted to stop having to output reports in a fixed format after finishing data analysis. She let out a long breath and said, "Tell them: this is called a Skill for filling data into reports."

Hu Xinlan found that the premises, steps, and verification standards in work had, through employees' daily repetition, become a kind of behavioral habit—before doing each step, they were not clearly aware of which step they were taking. But an AI agent does not accept "vagueness"; it cannot complete work based on vague descriptions.

This problem also inspired Julie to change her startup approach. She believes that at this stage, an enterprise's first need is not to build AI agents directly, but to first identify "which work is worth improving with AI."

She and her team developed a rather primitive experimental product: software that runs on employees' computers, takes a screenshot every few seconds, and analyzes the most time-consuming tasks and repetitive workflows, helping bosses discover AI efficiency opportunities that were never clearly articulated.

What is even more troublesome than breaking down workflows is "distilling" the key skills in employees' work to AI agents.

Alibaba Lingyang once did an AI customer service project for a leading electric vehicle brand. The client wanted the AI to first understand users' questions, answer directly what it could, and escalate to human agents what it could not. But the team found that customer service staff face a large number of random situations every day—whether to escalate, to whom, and how to follow up. These judgments were hidden in the agents' experience, with no ready-made material for an AI agent to learn from immediately.

Later, Alibaba Lingyang sent staff on-site, put on customer service headsets, and sat next to frontline employees for nearly two months, observing how they handled different user requests—a product demo could be built in two or three days, but to make it truly useful, the team spent months conducting nearly 100 research sessions.

The Account That Cannot Be Calculated

A while ago, Jiang Han, an FDE at a major tech company, received a request to develop an AI assistant for an app. It seemed simple, but in reality every feature had to connect to an internal system, and each system had different API permissions and security requirements. In the end, he and his team built more than twenty business AI agents, and behind each one were countless interviews, coordination, and waiting.

In the past, a B2B sales engineer could serve 5-8 clients simultaneously, but FDE-style delivery requires long-term immersion in an enterprise—understanding processes, coordinating departments, and connecting systems. It is already difficult for each person to serve 1-2 clients at the same time. AI has indeed gotten closer to clients' businesses, but human efficiency has dropped significantly.

And doing all this is also driven by the commercialization pressure of AI competition among major tech companies—only when AI is truly "useful" to enterprises can it drive revenue for MaaS and even more cloud services.

Now, in Jiang Han's eyes, FDE is like a bet. Before a project begins, he first calculates how many cloud, GPU, and other product orders behind the client can be leveraged. Only when he encounters a client worth "betting on" will he send people to do FDE delivery for free or at a low price.

Once, Jiang Han worked with a chain enterprise. Through spending day and night with the client, they decided to later migrate their cloud services to Jiang Han's company as well and continue purchasing other B2B products. This meant a ten-million-level order would be unlocked, and the upfront FDE service would immediately pay for itself.

But in another example, Jiang Han put great effort into helping a client deploy AI agents, only for the client's employees to use them superficially for simple office tasks. Over six months, there was only about 500,000 in token consumption—revenue that could not even cover the labor costs of Jiang Han's team.

As an entrepreneur, Edison even more frequently doubts whether his work is worth it.

Because the client company had a low level of digitalization, Edison first had the client switch to Feishu, consolidating data that had been scattered across employees' WeChat and Excel files into Feishu. Later, he successively helped the client build multidimensional tables, create frontend pages with Miaoda, and more—all of which ultimately generated revenue that flowed to ByteDance.

This process made Edison feel like free outsourced labor for Feishu.

FDEs have also gradually realized that the ideal "AI efficiency improvement" is in fact a long process of "AI evangelism," requiring constant education of clients about AI and promotion of new ways of working to gradually drive change. These are all "hidden costs" of the job.

Later, some people changed their initial strategy of recommending AI to employees with PowerPoint presentations, switching instead to "attraction." For example, casually mentioning to a client's employees that their own work was already "90% AI-powered," then introducing AI agents once the other party got curious. To encourage employees to adapt to AI, Pan Kelin also tried a different approach: he told employees that AI agents could tutor their children and help with homework, and participation increased noticeably.

How the value of AI implementation should be measured remains, for now, an account that cannot be calculated.

An FDE at a major tech company told 36Kr that his client happily reported that after purchasing his company's AI coding tools, the development team shrank from 140 people to 100. "When clients pay for AI, they either want to see more output or lower costs—if you can't create more revenue with the same people, then complete the same work with less money."

FDEs are often called the "last mile" of AI implementation, but how far along that mile we have traveled, and how much actual benefit completing it can bring to enterprises, remain unknown.

For now, "the number of layoffs" still seems to be the most intuitive yardstick companies use to measure whether AI is effective.
