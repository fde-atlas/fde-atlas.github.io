How to prepare for the customer simulation round in an FDE interview

Original title: FDE 面试客户模拟环节怎么准备

FDE Times · 2026-10-22 · 6 min read

Summary

This guide from FDE Times lays out a 60-minute practice drill for the customer-simulation round of FDE interviews: a friend plays an evasive stakeholder using a role card with hidden constraints, while the candidate practices questioning, restating constraints, proposing a minimal valuable solution, and handling mid-call scope changes. It draws on advice from FDE Academy, PostHog, Scaler, Educative, and Cognition's case-study format.

Key points

  • Strong candidates spend the first ten minutes only asking questions, since clients rarely state real constraints upfront.
  • A role card with hidden constraints (data stays on-prem, non-technical staff, a 4-week board demo) drives the drill.
  • Restate constraints aloud, then propose a proof of concept rather than a full architecture.
  • When scope changes, accept but state the timeline impact; doubled effort means renegotiating, per PostHog's handbook.
  • Close with a three-line scope summary and have your partner score you on structure, constraints, scope, refusal, and wrap-up.

In the customer simulation, top-performing Forward Deployed Engineer (FDE) candidates spend the first ten minutes only asking questions. The reason is that this round tests just one thing: can you find the real problem before you start building? And customers rarely state their real constraints in the first conversation.

Interviewers will also deliberately raise the difficulty. In Cognition's case study round, someone plays a non-technical or difficult customer to see how you handle a barrage of follow-up questions.

The good news is that this skill can be practiced. All you need is a friend willing to play a dodgy stakeholder who won't give clear requirements. Below is a complete practice routine you can do tonight.

What does the 60-minute practice include?

The whole session breaks down into 10 minutes of prep, a 45-minute call, and 5 minutes of scoring. These durations are set for practice purposes, not taken from any company's interview process.

You'll need a friend to play the customer, a countdown timer, a blank text file for notes, and, if possible, a recording app. The player reads the role card from Step 1, which you must not see.

Step 1: Write the role card and hide the constraints in it

The whole exercise hinges on the role card. The example below is fictional; you can swap in an industry you know well:

角色：某物流公司运营负责人（虚构）
开场白：“我们想把 AI 用在客服工单上。”

隐藏约束（只有被问到对的问题时才透露）：
- 客户数据不能离开内部基础设施
- 客服团队共 6 人，没人会写代码
- 4 周后要给董事会做演示
- 目前大家把紧急工单手动复制到一个共享 Excel 文件里

范围变更（扮演者主动提出）：
- 第 20 分钟：“其实老板希望它能自己回复客户。”
- 第 35 分钟：“能不能加上英语和日语？”

These two scope changes are deliberately planted. In a real interview, the interviewer may play a difficult stakeholder who keeps shifting goals, and this role card recreates exactly that pressure.

Check: The player understands that each constraint is only revealed when directly asked about, never volunteered.

Step 2: Draft your questions during prep and ask them in the first ten minutes

During the 10 minutes of prep, write your questions into the text file in groups. The two most valuable ones come from practitioners. FDE Academy recommends asking what would count as a "win" for the customer this month. Educative notes that workarounds often reveal the real pain point most clearly.

用户：      谁每天用它？现在是怎么做的？
成果：      这个月要发生什么，你才会觉得算赢？
变通做法：  现有系统出问题时，大家怎么应付？
数据：      工单存在哪里？能不能拿到外部？
人员：      部署团队撤走后，谁来运营？
期限：      有没有不能改动的固定日期？

Once the call starts, spend the first ten minutes only asking questions. Don't read them out in order like a questionnaire. Let the customer's answers lead to the next question; glance at the file only to confirm which groups you haven't covered yet.

Check: By the end of minute 10, how many of the four hidden constraints have you uncovered? If fewer than two, this session has already told you what to practice.

Step 3: State the constraints back

Uncovering the constraints is only half the job. Scaler recommends stating them back even if the interviewer doesn't bring them up, because evaluators can't read your mind.

One sentence is enough: "Before proposing anything, let me repeat back three conditions: the data stays on internal infrastructure, the operators can't write code, and there's a demo in 4 weeks. If any of these is wrong, please correct me." This sentence shows you've grasped the shape of the problem, and it gives the customer a chance to correct you before you build the wrong thing.

Check: Did you restate the constraints before saying the word "architecture"?

Step 4: Propose the smallest version that still creates value

According to techinterview, strong candidates propose the smallest version that still creates value, not the most complete system they can imagine. Scaler puts the same point differently: offer a proof of concept, not a full architecture.

For the role card above, the minimal version could be a system that classifies incoming tickets and drafts replies, which employees review before sending, all running on internal infrastructure. It replaces the manually copied Excel file, satisfies all three constraints, and still supports a real demo in 4 weeks.

Check: Can you describe the solution in one sentence and name one number to measure on demo day?

Step 5: When the customer keeps changing requirements

Around minute 20, the "customer" will ask the system to reply on its own, and many people just nod here. FDE Academy argues that "yes, but this is how it affects the timeline" is stronger than an unconditional nod. New FDEs must learn to scope requirements and compare them against what has already been promised.

Applied to the example: "Automated replies are doable, but they require an extra round of quality evals before going to real customers. If we do it now, there will be nothing working for the demo in 4 weeks. So I suggest the demo use the human-reviewed version, with automated replies in phase two."

PostHog's FDE handbook draws the line even more clearly: doubled workload means a new quote, not a silent delay. In practice you don't need to mention pricing, but hold on to the principle: when requirements grow, renegotiation must be explicit.

Saying no doesn't mean rigidly sticking to the original plan. If the change at minute 35 is reasonable, for example because most tickets are in English anyway, then adjust the plan and explain why.

Check: After each change, did you compare the new requirement against the agreed commitments and state explicitly what trade-offs are needed?

Step 6: Lock in the scope with a brief summary

Around minute 45, end the call with the agreed scope. PostHog has described how its FDE team works: write the scope as a brief summary, send it to the customer for confirmation, then commit. In practice, read out the following three lines:

已商定：    分类 + 回复草稿建议，员工审核后发送
约束：      数据留在内部基础设施；操作人员不写代码；4 周后演示
后续阶段：  自动回复客户；更多语言（如果第一版没有包含）

Check: Did the player approve these three lines without any changes?

Step 7: Five minutes of scoring

Have the player score you; don't score yourself. If you recorded the session, listen back afterward.

CriterionCheck question
StructureAt which minute did you first propose a solution?
ConstraintsHow many of the four constraints did you uncover, and did you state them back?
ScopeCan the solution be explained in one sentence?
PushbackFor each requirement change, did you explain the impact, or just nod?
Wrap-upDid the player confirm the final summary?

The three most common mistakes

First, treating the ten minutes of questions as a formality and then proposing the solution you had already planned. If the customer's answers didn't change anything in your solution, you skipped exactly what this round tests: finding the real problem before you start building.

Second, rejecting with vague reasons like "that's not a good idea." A rejection is only persuasive when tied to the timeline or a constraint the customer just stated. The third mistake is the opposite: sticking to your first version to the end, when the interviewer's relentless follow-ups are precisely meant to see how you handle the next question.

What does this skill look like with real customers?

This round isn't just an exam. The scoping advice in Step 5 comes from FDE Academy, written for newly hired FDEs; locking in scope with a summary is how PostHog's FDE team works every day. After you join, these are exactly the skills you'll use.

Imagine a real project kickoff: vague requirements, constraints that only surface when asked about, a workaround hidden in a file nobody mentions, and goals that shift midway. It's identical to the practice, except the stakes are real.

So don't just list technologies on your resume. Write one line about a scope decision: what you cut, because of which constraint, and what you delivered on time as a result. When the interviewer asks about a time you had to say "no," this line is a ready-made story.

Run this exercise three times with three different role cards, and scoping through questions will become instinct. In the real interview, the scope change at minute 20 won't catch you off guard.
