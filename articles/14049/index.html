Deployment strategist vs forward deployed engineer

FDE Interview Prep · 2026-09-25 · 17 min read

Summary

Drawing on job postings from Palantir, Databricks, ElevenLabs, Scale and others, this article contrasts the Deployment Strategist with the Forward Deployed Engineer. Both sit onsite with customers, but the strategist owns the why and what of the problem while the FDE owns the how. Coding expectations and travel requirements vary: Palantir's strategist postings require 25–75% travel versus up to 25% for FDSEs, and a September 2026 census counted 75 engineering and 35 strategist postings at Palantir. The article warns that at companies like Scale, the same title describes quota-carrying sales roles, and offers a checklist for telling delivery roles from sales roles before applying.

Key points

  • Strategists own the why and what; FDEs own the how of customer deployments.
  • Palantir labels strategists Echo and FDSEs Delta, as separate hiring lines.
  • Palantir strategists travel 25–75%; FDSEs up to 25%.
  • Strategists write less code than FDEs, but still explore data and prototype.
  • At Scale, the strategist title means a quota-carrying sales role, not engineering.

The short answer

A deployment strategist is the FDE’s problem-side twin: same customer, same deployment, but the strategist owns the why and the what, and the FDE owns the how. Different seat, different question:

  • The strategist asks: what problem is worth solving, for whom, and how will we know it worked?
  • The FDE asks: how do we get the data in, build the thing, and keep it running?

Before you apply, run the posting through this test. It is our method, not something employers publish.

Is this strategist posting a delivery role or a sales role?

  • Does it mention quota, capture, pipeline or statements of work? That leans sales.
  • Does it name FDEs or engineers as the partners who build? That leans delivery.
  • Does it describe onsite work with the customer’s analysts or users? That leans delivery.
  • What does it want you to do with code: build prototypes, read pipelines, or nothing?
  • Which department is it filed under: engineering, deployment, product or sales?

How much code a strategist writes

Less than an FDE, but in these three postings, not none:

  • Palantir. The New York posting lists programming, scripting or statistical-package experience under “What We Require”, while the US Government posting calls the same experience “a plus”. Source 2Palantir Technologies - Deployment StrategistPublisherPalantir Technologies (Lever)Source typecompany job postingSource 4Palantir Technologies - Deployment Strategist - US GovernmentPublisherPalantir Technologies (Lever)Source typecompany job posting
  • Databricks. Candidates are proficient in at least one language such as Python, Java or SQL, and can explore data and work in notebooks. Source 3Sr. Deployment Strategist, FDE - Financial ServicesPublisherDatabricks (careers site / Greenhouse)Source typecompany job posting
  • ElevenLabs. The posting asks for basic Python proficiency and experience working with customers in a technical capacity. Source 11Deployment Strategist - North America @ ElevenLabsPublisherElevenLabs (Ashby job board)Source typecompany job posting

Our reading of those three: a strategist writes code to explore data and prototype, and an FDE writes the code that stays running. Our bar for a strategist candidate: answer a customer’s question from a table without help. For example, “which accounts stopped opening the report we built?”:

-- SQLite date syntax
SELECT a.account_id,
       COUNT(v.viewed_at) AS views,
       MAX(v.viewed_at)   AS last_view
FROM accounts a
LEFT JOIN report_views v
  ON v.account_id = a.account_id
GROUP BY a.account_id
HAVING MAX(v.viewed_at) IS NULL
    OR MAX(v.viewed_at) < DATE('now', '-30 days')
ORDER BY last_view;

Start from accounts, not views: an account that never opened the report has no rows to group, and it’s the one the customer most needs to hear about. Then say what you’d ask next: is that cutoff the right window, and did those accounts get the report at all? That habit of checking what the data leaves out is the kind of data exploration these postings describe. An FDE candidate needs that and much more: the pipeline that fills

report_views

, and what happens when it breaks. Try

building a feature from fitness-app event data

to see where your own line sits.

Which one to apply for

Choose by what you want to be measured on. This is our framing, not a rule any employer publishes.

Lean strategist if:

  • the work you are proudest of is a decision you changed, a scope you cut or a customer you won over;
  • you bring domain depth (clinical, defense, finance, operations) that engineers on the account lack;
  • you would rather write the query that proves the point than own the service that runs it.

Lean FDE if:

  • the work you are proudest of is a system you built and kept running;
  • you want to be the one on the hook when it breaks;
  • you would rather debug the pipeline than run the steering meeting.

A quick test: describe your last good project in two sentences. If you can’t get to the code without being asked, look at strategist roles. If you can’t get to the customer without being asked, look at FDE roles.

Say your reason in one line, because you will be asked. For a strategist role: “I want to own what gets built for the customer and be accountable for whether it changed how they work.” For an FDE role: “I want to be the one who builds it and is on the hook when it breaks.” Our free lesson FDE, software engineer, solutions architect, sales engineer: telling them apart walks through the neighboring roles the same way.

One prompt, two openings

Take a made-up decomposition prompt: “A regional hospital network says its emergency department is overwhelmed. Where do you start?”

A strong strategist opening: “Who feels this most: the charge nurse deciding who is seen next, or the operations director deciding next month’s staffing? Those are different products. Let’s pick the charge nurse, agree on what ‘better’ means, say the time from arrival to first assessment, and decide what a first version must show to count as working.”

A strong FDE opening: “Where do arrival and triage records live today, and how fresh are they? I’d get one feed of arrivals into a table, build the simplest queue view the charge nurse could use, and check it against what they see on the floor before I add any prediction.”

Each opening needs the other’s half before the round ends. The strategist still has to name the data. The FDE still has to name the user. Mistakes that cost strategist and FDE candidates

  • Pitching a tool before scoping. In the hospital prompt, “I’d build a triage-prediction model” in the first minute skips the user. Fix: spend the first minutes on who the user is and what decision they make.
  • Strategists avoiding the data. “The engineers will handle that” reads as someone who can’t work with them. Fix: name the tables and one query you would run, and have a customer-facing story that shows you did it before.
  • FDEs skipping the user. A pipeline with no named user is a guess. Fix: ask one “who uses this?” question before drawing anything.
  • Giving in to scope creep. Sierra’s post on its Agent Strategists lists pushing back on scope creep as part of keeping deployments on track. Source 16Agent Strategist: Your PhD in applied AI (Andrew Kim)PublisherSierraSource typecompany blog Fix: practice handling a customer PM who adds a dashboard every week.
