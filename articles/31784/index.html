Boundaries, Risks, and Exit

Original title: 边界、风险与退出

pafa.github.io · 2026-09-25 · 13 min read

Summary

Using the UK NS&I transformation delays and the Homes for Ukraine migration away from Palantir as cases, the article argues that reducing dependence on one vendor does not automatically reduce complexity; coordination and operational responsibility often shift back to the customer. Dependence spans data, code, knowledge, and organizational capacity. The author proposes treating exit as upfront design: rehearsing handovers of unfinished work, itemizing licensing rights, agreeing stop conditions, and verifying exit through three kinds of evidence—operational, rights, and access revocation.

Key points

  • NS&I's multi-vendor transformation missed its deadline; a committee cited poor understanding of system dependencies.
  • Vendor lock-in spans data, code, knowledge, and organization—not just data exportability.
  • Homes for Ukraine's in-house replacement took about a year and returned maintenance burdens to the customer.
  • Exit drills must handle unfinished work item by item to avoid duplicate execution or misread states.
  • A completed exit requires operational, rights, and access-revocation evidence together.

Can replacing one vendor with several give customers more freedom?

The UK's National Savings and Investments (NS&I) issues savings and investment products to the public. Its experience is a reminder that the answer depends on whether the customer has the capacity to absorb the new arrangement. It launched a transformation in 2020, hoping to move from a long-term outsourcing relationship to multiple smaller contracts. The original plan was to migrate most services by March 2024; a report published by the UK Public Accounts Committee on February 13, 2026 noted that by that original deadline, the new services had not yet been modernized and brought online. The core of the Committee's criticism included: insufficient understanding of system dependencies, lack of an integration plan, inadequate delivery capacity, and signing contracts too early. 1

This is not a project confirmed to have used FDEs, nor can it be used to assert that a multi-vendor model is worse. It is a useful mirror: reducing dependence on one company does not automatically reduce the complexity of the work. The coordination responsibility once concentrated in one place likely fell back on the customer.

Chapter 9 divides responsibilities between the two parties, Chapter 12 calculates the economics, and Chapter 19 examines product reuse. This chapter follows a different line: the engagement has already begun—how do you preserve the options to stop, switch teams, and switch systems, without dropping the work in progress between the two?

What options to preserve even after deciding to engage

Routine integration over stable interfaces does not necessarily require an FDE, and customers cannot hand business decision-making authority to a project contact who lacks the corresponding authority. These initial judgments are covered in Customer Selection and Division of Responsibilities. The question unique to this chapter arises after the engagement begins: when the materials are brought back, can their meaning, unfinished commitments, and rights of continued use be brought back too?

The NS&I comparison places the cost between contracts. Reducing dependence on one vendor increases the integration work borne by the customer. The same is true with FDEs: external engineers can bring capabilities, but many key interpretations may remain only within the engagement relationship. Exit arrangements need to turn those interpretations into artifacts that a successor can verify.

Dependence is not a switch

When discussing vendor lock-in, people tend to ask only whether data can be exported. Exportability certainly matters, but a system's dependencies are often scattered across several places.

One place is data: whether historical records are complete, whether identifiers can be matched, and whether relationships between different tables are preserved. Another is code: which code the customer can continue to use, which requires ongoing licenses, and which services become uncallable once stopped. Still another is knowledge: why a rule was set up back then, how exceptions should be handled, and who actually knows the true state of the existing system.

The last place is organizational. Even if all files are obtained, if the customer has no one to troubleshoot, accept changes, and maintain permissions, operations still depend on the external team. This dependence is not necessarily unreasonable. Many enterprises choose to purchase ongoing services in the first place. The key is that both parties know what they are buying, and the fees and exit arrangements match it.

Eliminating all dependencies is not necessarily worthwhile. If the customer develops every capability in-house, it takes on additional staffing and maintenance costs; maintaining multiple complex implementations just to be able to swap models at any time may also exceed the benefits the project brings. A more realistic goal is to identify which dependencies are acceptable, which dependencies would leave critical business without options, and to preserve alternative paths for the latter category.

For example, an enterprise can accept that replacing a certain analytics interface takes time, but cannot accept that a brief vendor outage causes all customer applications already received to be lost. These two requirements should not be merged into one vague statement that "the system must be migratable." The former concerns migration efficiency; the latter concerns business continuity, and they require different construction work.

Product exit can also happen on a small feature. Microsoft stated that Excel's COPILOT preview function will no longer be available from September 14, 2026: cached values already computed can remain in the workbook, but recalculating a cell will return

an error; the Copilot pane continues to provide the related capability.

A spreadsheet that displays results today may not be able to regenerate them tomorrow. Exit drills should therefore re-run key steps, not merely open old artifacts and confirm they look complete.

The interface name may be preserved while the underlying model has already changed. In its September 10, 2026 announcement, DeepSeek stated that V4-Flash and V4-Flash-Vision-Exp had been retired, with the old API names temporarily redirected to V4.1-Flash. 3 This proves the vendor publicly disclosed the routing change; it does not mean this book independently tested the actual interface. Maintainers therefore need to confirm the actual routing and re-run the original critical tasks, and cannot treat "the old name still works" as "it is still the original model."

Open formats can reduce some data dependencies, but they cannot substitute for a full handover. On September 15, Palantir announced that Iceberg is generally available in standard Foundry enterprise environments; its external clients still verify permissions through the catalog and obtain storage credentials that are scope- and time-limited. 4 Data being readable by other engines is a different milestone from the original permissions, business actions, applications, and operational responsibilities having been migrated. When choosing an open format, you can require the taking-over team to read samples with its own identity, re-run critical tasks, and record what has not yet been migrated.

A real replacement: first take on the migration, then take over operations

The UK's Homes for Ukraine housing resettlement program needed to share data between central government and local authorities. To meet urgent needs, the government initially adopted Palantir's system, free for the first six months; the BBC later cited UK National Audit Office materials stating that the next two twelve-month contracts were worth £4.5 million and £5.5 million respectively. 5 These two figures are contract values, not FDE labor fees, nor the full cost borne by the customer.

The customer department's later retrospective recorded the other half of the work. In the summer of 2024, the team began building a replacement system in-house; the team needed to migrate three years of operational data, determine day-one essential features with business staff and local users, migrate in phases with repeated data quality checks, and arrange demonstrations, guidance, and user support. The department said the new system was delivered in September 2025; the retrospective also said it had exited the original vendor contract, but did not state the exact date of exit. 6 Whoever maintains it afterward, it is not free: building in-house gave the customer more control, but also brought ongoing development, support, and operational responsibilities back in-house.

This is not a project confirmed to have been carried out by FDEs, but a customer platform migration that can be compared with forward delivery. The phased migration and user support show that completing a replacement requires caring for work in progress; completing the replacement later also does not mean the transition was costless. The customer reported savings in operating costs, but did not disclose a fully recomputable baseline and migration costs, so net benefits cannot be calculated from this. What it demonstrates is not that a particular procurement form is necessarily better, but that exit options require time, capability, data verification, and someone to take over the day-to-day work.

An exit drill that can actually be executed

Below is a teaching drill, not corresponding to an implemented policy at any company. Suppose an after-sales team uses an externally co-built ticketing system, with six months remaining on the contract. The customer wants to confirm it has preserved the option to switch vendors.

The first step is not to shut down the entire system, but to pick one complete and ordinary business path: a customer submits an application, an employee verifies the materials, a supervisor approves, the system executes, and finally the result is recorded. Have the taking-over team explain what data, code, and permissions each step requires.

The second step is to take away a small batch of authorized test records. Beyond the body text, this should include status changes, attachment relationships, rule versions, and operation timestamps. The taking-over team attempts to restore their meaning in the new environment. If all you get is a table marked "completed" without knowing who completed it or under what conditions, a successful export has not achieved an understandable migration.

The third step is the easiest to fail: handling work that is not yet finished. To this end, the teaching team freezes new writes to the old system at the switchover point T and exports the statuses; the new system does not auto-execute for now, and first verifies three tickets one by one. The decisions below are limited to this isolated drill and do not imply that all old approvals can carry over across systems.

Ticket status at TWhat the old team hands overNew team's next action and stop condition
T01: Materials complete, not yet approvedInputs, task version, status missing approvalSubmit to business owner for approval; a successful import does not add any approval
T02: Version 5 approved, not yet executedVersion 5 content, approver/scope, action number A02, and confirmation that the old queue has stoppedProceed only after the business owner confirms the approval still applies to the new execution environment; if the task or scope changes, re-approval is required
T03: Action A03 sent, receipt not confirmedOriginal action number, external service, and last send recordFirst check the business result by A03; in this case, an executed record E03 is found, so complete the mapping, mark it as done, and do not execute again; if not found, escalate for verification

There is no single unified "pending" state here. T01 lacks business permission, T02 lacks conditional execution handover, and T03 lacks result confirmation. If all of them are compressed into "incomplete" at export, the new team might repeat T03 or mistakenly treat T01 as approved. If the old queue might resume sending, T02 also cannot be handed over as "it is now the new system's turn"; first confirm that the old write path has stopped.

The fourth step then schedules a normal change, such as a supervisor replacement, to check whether the new team can modify the owner's configuration while preserving the historical identity of the original approver. Recovery drills must return to matching data and program versions; do not let an old program read a new state it cannot interpret.

Only the fifth step decides to expand the migration. The teaching team approves T01 into manual review, continues T02 based on the verified approval, and only completes the receipt for T03; no migration completion is declared for tasks beyond the list. Intermediate tasks and permission object package retains export records, handover actions, stop conditions, and a blank checklist for readers to make decisions item by item.

After the migration ends, old access must also be revoked. First, the client's business and records management owner confirms which materials need to be retained and who will keep them; then the system owner inventories accounts, access keys, and shared links belonging to the departing team, revoking or replacing them in the agreed handover order. Rehearse the revocation in a test environment and check whether the new team's operations still secretly depend on old accounts; only then schedule the formal cutover. Copies of materials held by the departing party are retained, returned, or disposed of according to applicable retention requirements and the contract, with a completion record left behind. There is no deletion deadline that applies to all projects; what must be made explicit is who decides, who executes, and who checks. The new team can operate, the old team no longer holds unauthorized access, and required records remain traceable—these are three things that each need separate confirmation.

The UK government's Digital, Data and Technology procurement playbook requires early consideration of contract exit, covering knowledge transfer, ongoing support, handover of assets and data, and the transition between old and new arrangements. This is public procurement guidance, not a uniform legal requirement for all projects; its inspiration for this chapter is to treat exit as upfront design rather than last-month-of-contract contingency work. 7

On exit day, which rights remain

For how exploration and production are phased and how pricing and changes are agreed, see Contracts and pricing. Here we keep asking: after the contract ends, which specific objects is the successor entitled to use?

Intellectual property likewise cannot be reduced to a single sentence "deliverables belong to the client." Data provided by the client, code newly written for the project, the vendor's existing platform, generic components, and third-party licenses may each be subject to different arrangements. The UK government's AI procurement guidance lists intellectual property, support and training, and risk allocation among the considerations. 8 In a specific engagement, the person responsible for the contract should spell out these objects clearly, rather than expecting engineers to interpret a blanket clause at the end of delivery.

Only by separating the objects can we answer what can still be done on the first day after the contract ends. What follows is still a teaching arrangement assuming both parties have verified each item; it does not mean the law automatically grants either party these rights.

Licensed or depended-upon objectArrangement confirmed in this exampleExit check
Client tickets, attachments, and status historyClient may export and retain for agreed purposesCounts, associations, file digests, and T01–T03 status are recoverable
Adapter code and configuration newly written for this projectClient receives a license for continued internal use and modificationSuccessor actually reads, installs, and interprets the configuration using an independent account
Vendor's existing runtime platformCallable only during the service term; platform code not obtainedThe new environment must choose a separate runtime service; exported configuration cannot be treated as independently runnable software
Third-party component LIn this example, not yet confirmed whether it can transfer to the new maintainerDistribution not approved for now; the contract owner clarifies the license or replaces the implementation before deciding on cutover
Old accounts, keys, and shared linksAccess authorization transfers with responsibilities, not inherited by file copyingRevoke old access once the new identity can run; check whether operations still secretly depend on old accounts

A single sentence "who owns the code" cannot cover this table. Even with the adapter code, the client may still depend on a subscription platform; the vendor permitting internal use does not necessarily decide how third-party components may be distributed. Engineers are responsible for listing all dependencies and actual objects, the contract owner verifies the scope of rights, and the successor validates that they can be used. While component L remains unverified, the overall cutover in this example has not passed; three clearly explained tickets must not cause another class of blocker to be ignored.

Stop conditions cannot be invented only after failure

Once an engagement begins, people easily treat time already invested as a reason to keep investing. Engineers say "just a few more fixes," clients say "we've already trained our staff," and owners are reluctant to make the earlier investment look wasted. So the project keeps extending without re-examining the conditions for continuing.

A better approach is, while calm discussion is still possible, to agree in advance on which changes will trigger re-evaluation. For example: key data remains unavailable for a long time, the business owner leaves with no successor, expected workload changes substantially, or the system needs to cross a decision boundary that was never authorized. These situations need not automatically lead to cancellation, but they should make "continue" once again a choice that requires justification.

For generative AI, risk management material from the US National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) proposes defining deactivation criteria, notifying the organization's risk owner when the corresponding conditions are met, and arranging alternative processes. It is risk management guidance, not the law of every country, and it does not prescribe a uniform acceptable error rate for readers. 9 For project owners, what is useful is translating these measures into their own business actions. The narrowing of automation scope below is this book's applied extrapolation.

For example, when the system cannot reliably verify materials provided by an applicant, it can shift to helping staff organize them instead of automatically executing subsequent actions. If a tool repeatedly produces unexplainable results, that part can be deactivated while other validated functions are retained. Stopping does not necessarily mean shutting down all the software; it may also mean narrowing the scope it can decide independently.

But the alternative process needs real capacity. If the system originally handled a large volume of tasks per day and "escalate to a human when there's a problem" only assigns one employee, then human takeover is merely a fallback on paper. The owner should know how much work can be handled after degradation, what gets processed first, how long the remaining people will wait, and should arrange an acceptable service model in advance.

Completing the exit requires looking at three kinds of evidence at once

Operational evidence shows whether tasks were lost, redone, or exceeded the original approval; rights evidence shows whether the new team can continue using the relevant objects; revocation evidence shows the old team no longer holds access beyond what was agreed. Only when all three hold independently can the scope of this exit be evaluated. A test export file with no lost rows cannot substitute for the other two.

The project owner in this example therefore makes a decision that is not elegant but executable: maintain the arrangement of a stopped old queue with controlled processing by the new system, handle T01–T03 first, pause the overall cutover, and wait until component L's licensing and the alternative runtime service are verified. If extended transition costs exceed the value the client is willing to bear, the scope can be reduced to a smaller path, or ongoing support renegotiated; conditions cannot be made to hold automatically by continuing to burn engineer time.

A good ending for an engagement can therefore take several forms: the client can already operate independently; stable work transitions into a clearly defined maintenance service; the jointly built product already covers the main needs; or both parties end in time when the conditions do not hold. Continued dependence can be a clear choice, and independent operation also requires ongoing maintenance. No form is inherently correct by name alone.

Returning to the start of this chapter, the number of vendors is only the surface. What the client truly needs to retain is the ability to understand the system, judge its results, and organize alternatives. When these abilities exist, the FDE can be a partner in change; when they are all absent, no amount of contracts or however diligent the engineers can shoulder the business itself on the client's behalf.
