How to Prioritize Multi-Customer Incidents: Severity, Urgency, and SLA

Original title: 多客户故障优先级怎么排：严重程度、紧急程度与 SLA

FDE Times · 2026-11-07 · 5 min read

Summary

This guide teaches forward deployed engineers how to triage when several customers report issues at once. It argues that severity (damage caused), urgency (time pressure), and SLA commitments (contractual response and resolution times) are three separate questions, and priority should be derived from all three rather than from who calls loudest or first. A worked example ranks a retail client's P1 agent failure first, a logistics client's deadline-driven report bug second (solved with a manual CSV export as a workaround), and a strategically important banking pilot's minor latency issue third. The article stresses sending first responses within minutes, setting explicit next-update times, escalating big-client issues only with a documented rationale, and documenting the triage order in a shared channel. It closes with advice on presenting this skill in interviews and resumes using verifiable numbers.

Key points

  • Severity, urgency, and SLA are separate questions; priority combines them.
  • Send first responses before starting any fix; they are fully in your control.
  • Tight deadlines with low impact often need only a workaround, not a root-cause fix.
  • Escalating a big client's minor issue is fine only with a written rationale.
  • In interviews and resumes, describe real triage cases with provable numbers.

By separating severity, urgency, and signed SLAs, a Forward Deployed Engineer (FDE) can sort out the morning's work in fifteen minutes and explain the reasoning.

Key Takeaways

  • Severity measures damage; priority determines when to act. They are not the same thing — don't conflate them.
  • A first response takes only a few minutes, yet it keeps your SLA commitments, so send one to each customer before you start fixing anything.
  • Problems with tight deadlines but low impact can often be handled with a workaround; the root-cause fix can wait.

Picture this scenario: it's 8:40 in the morning, and your phone has rung three times in ten minutes. Three customers, three anxious voices, each convinced their issue is the most important.

Most engineers act on instinct at this point: whoever shouts loudest gets helped first, or whoever called first gets helped first. Either way, you may unknowingly violate commitments you've already signed.

Suppose you're responsible for three deployments, each with its own contract, business hours, and level of importance. The skill you need is to sequence the work within fifteen minutes and be able to explain why you ordered it that way.

Three different questions, not one

The fundamental mistake is cramming everything into a single word: "urgent." In fact, every call contains three separate questions, each with its own answer.

The first is severity: how much damage the incident causes to users and the business. At the highest level, PagerDuty's example is SEV-1: a critical incident affecting a large number of customers, usually requiring a public announcement and someone coordinating directly with senior management.

The second is urgency, i.e., how time-sensitive the problem is. A small bug can still be extremely urgent if the customer has to deliver a report at 2 p.m.

The third is the response time you've committed to. According to Plain, a support service level agreement (SLA) is a measurable commitment to the customer specifying how quickly you respond and how quickly you resolve. The answer to this question doesn't depend on anyone's feelings — it's written in the contract.

Priority is not a fourth question; it's the outcome of the first three. It determines when you start working on something and how fast you react. ITIL summarizes it as Priority = f(Impact, Urgency).

One morning, three calls

Back to the earlier scenario. Customer A is a logistics company: the report export feature has failed for one analyst, and the report is due to the board at 2 p.m. Low impact, high urgency.

Customer B is a retail chain: the agent that answers inventory questions is giving wrong numbers to employees at all stores. Broad impact, and suppose the contract promises a first response within 30 minutes for P1 incidents.

Plain notes that the first-response target for P1 incidents is typically 15 to 60 minutes, so this hypothetical number falls within the common range.

Customer C is a bank running a pilot, and also your most strategically important customer. They report that model latency on an internal dashboard has risen slightly; there's no deadline. Low impact, low urgency, but a big name.

The sensible order is: B first, then A, and C scheduled in. But the order of fixes is not the order of contact — and this is exactly where many people lose points.

Respond first, fix later

Plain splits the SLA into two clocks: first response and resolution. Resolution time is harder to control because some bugs require the engineering team, so Plain recommends setting separate targets for each priority level. First response, by contrast, is entirely in your own hands.

So at 8:50, before you've opened a single log, you send three messages. To B: "Received, classified as P1, next update at 9:20." To A: "Understood, your deadline is 2 p.m., and you'll have a solution before 11." To C: "Received, we'll assess this afternoon and get back to you before end of day."

Writing these three messages takes only a few minutes. In return: B receives a first response within the committed window, A knows you understand their deadline, and C, though not yet being worked on, knows their issue has been heard.

For A, a tight deadline doesn't mean the root cause must be fixed immediately. Sending over a manually exported CSV before 11 a.m. satisfies their real need — the report for the meeting. The underlying bug can be fixed tomorrow.

Can big customers cut in line?

incident.io acknowledges that a low-severity incident affecting a major customer may be worth raising in priority. That's perfectly reasonable — you can absolutely move Customer C from P3 to P2. But the escalation needs a reason you can write down, such as the pilot's review meeting approaching, rather than fear.

If you let Customer C jump ahead of Customer B, thousands of retail employees are still getting wrong inventory numbers while you tweak an internal dashboard. When someone asks about it afterward, you'll have no justification to defend the decision.

Try it yourself: five steps for the first fifteen minutes

Step one: record each call in one sentence: who is affected, how many people, and what the symptoms are. Step two: score severity based on the number of affected users and the blocked business process, not the caller's tone.

Step three: ask directly: "When do you need this by, at the latest, and why?" That "why" often reveals a workaround. Step four: check each customer's SLA and send the first response, starting with the customer with the shortest committed window.

Step five: post the priority order and the reasoning to the team's shared channel. When a manager or account manager asks why Customer C has to wait, the answer is already there.

Common pitfalls

The most common pitfall is treating severity and priority as the same thing, resulting in a small bug with a tight deadline being forgotten until 1:55 p.m. The second pitfall is promising a resolution time on the phone, when the only thing you truly control is response time.

The third pitfall is going silent during the fix. Customers can't see you reading logs — they only see no messages. Set a time for the next update and deliver on it, even if there's no new progress.

Turning this skill into evidence

When reading Forward Deployed Engineer (FDE) job descriptions, watch for phrases like on-call, SLA, escalation, and handling multiple customers simultaneously. They signal that the role demands exactly this skill. In interviews, walk through a real triage experience covering the three dimensions of impact, deadlines, and commitments, then explain what choices you made and why.

On a resume, one specific sentence carries more weight than "works well under pressure." For example: "Triaged incidents for four customers simultaneously with zero first-response SLA breaches in one quarter." Only write numbers you can prove.

The morning with three calls will come again. With the matrix and message templates ready, the first fifteen minutes won't be so frantic.
