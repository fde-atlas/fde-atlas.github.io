How to Reuse After FDE Project Delivery: ADRs, Runbooks, and Template Repositories

Original title: FDE 项目交付后如何复用：ADR、Runbook 与模板仓库

FDE Times · 2026-10-09 · 6 min read

Summary

This guide explains how forward deployed engineers can turn finished customer projects into reusable assets instead of one-off customizations. Using a fictional invoice-processing agent as an example, it proposes a minimal toolkit buildable in about three hours: ADRs capturing decisions and their rationale (especially when to revisit them), Runbooks that separate actions from explanations following Diátaxis, a playbook mapped to deployment phases that flags customizations as product candidates, and a GitHub template repository rather than a fork to avoid leaking client history. It also lists three common mistakes and suggests showing this reuse skill concretely in FDE job applications.

Key points

  • Delivered projects should become product inputs, not one-off custom work.
  • Minimal toolkit: ADRs, Runbooks, explanations, a playbook, and a template repo.
  • The most valuable ADR section states when to re-evaluate a decision.
  • Write Runbooks with the people who will actually operate the system.
  • Use GitHub template repos, not forks, to keep one clean commit and no client data.

Spend about three hours writing five documents and building a template repository, and the team's experience won't be lost when a Forward Deployed Engineer leaves.

Key Takeaways

  • Every delivered project is raw material for the product. If you don't write it down, that work remains a one-off customization that's hard to scale.
  • The minimal toolkit includes: ADRs for recording decisions, a Runbook for operations, a phased playbook, and a template repository.
  • A repository created from a template has only one commit, leaving the old customer's history in place; a fork carries the entire history along with it.

The last week of a deployment is often a scramble: demos, acceptance sign-off, handover, then off to the next customer. Three months later, a second customer shows up with the same problems, and the team starts from a blank page again.

The FDE handbook on fde.academy divides a deployment into six phases. The final phase is the feedback loop: work customized for each customer is gradually standardized into reusable features. An article on the same site describing the Palantir model puts it even more directly: every solution built by a Forward Deployed Engineer (FDE) feeds back into subsequent product development.

That article also points out the downside. If this loop isn't managed, the work slides into pure customization and becomes hard to scale. So whether you can properly package completed projects actually determines whether you're contributing to the product or doing one-off customization customer after customer.

What can you accomplish in one evening?

When you're done, you'll have a documentation directory and a template repository on GitHub. The following example runs through the whole article: suppose you've just delivered an AI agent for a logistics company that reads PDF invoices and writes the data into the customer's ERP. The example is fictional, but the structure below applies to any type of project.

You need four things: a GitHub account, the old project's repository, meeting notes or chat logs from the deployment, and roughly three hours of uninterrupted time. The target structure looks like this (simplified):

fde-invoice-agent-template/
├── README.md            # 这个仓库解决什么问题，如何运行
├── docs/
│   ├── adr/             # 架构决策，每个决策一个文件
│   ├── runbooks/        # 操作指南：故障处理、日常运维
│   ├── explanation/     # 系统为什么这样工作
│   └── playbook.md      # 面向新客户的部署阶段
├── config/
│   └── customer.example.yaml
└── src/

The five documents to write are the README, the ADR, the Runbook, the explanation of how it works, and the playbook. The steps below cover each one, and finally everything gets packaged into the template repository.

Step 1: Record decisions while memory is fresh

The definition on adr.github.io is brief: an Architecture Decision Record (ADR) captures an architecture decision and the reasoning behind it. Open the meeting notes and find the places where the team argued—such as which OCR to use, batch versus real-time processing, or whether the AI agent should correct data on its own or wait for human approval.

# ADR-003：Agent 不直接写入 ERP，而是等待人工审批

## 背景
发票格式不统一；客户的 ERP 不支持回滚。

## 决策
Agent 写入暂存表；财务审批后，数据才推送到 ERP。

## 影响
+ 错误数据不会进入账簿。
- 增加一个人工步骤；需要统计审批耗时。

## 何时重新评估
当新客户的 ERP 提供撤销 API 时。

"When to re-evaluate" is the most valuable part. It tells future readers: this decision depended on the old customer's specific conditions, and a new customer may need a different approach. Check: hand the ADR to a colleague who wasn't involved in the project. If they still ask "why not write directly into the ERP?" after reading it, the document is missing context.

Step 2: Separate the Runbook from the explanation

Diátaxis defines how-to guides as: instructions that lead the reader through solving a problem or achieving a result. The Runbook should be written in that spirit: every step is an action, with no explanatory paragraphs in between. The "why" belongs in the

docs/explanation/

。

# Runbook：发票队列卡住

目标：队列恢复运行，且不丢失任何发票。

1. 打开队列看板，记下待处理发票的数量。
2. 查看最近 15 分钟的 worker 日志，查找 OCR 超时错误。
3. 如果存在超时：将 worker 切换为逐个文件处理模式。
4. 确认待处理发票的数量在下降。
5. 如果 30 分钟后仍未下降：通知客户的值班联系人（见通讯录）。

了解原因：docs/explanation/ocr-timeouts.md

The numbers in the example are for demonstration only; replace them with the real thresholds in your system. Check: skim the Runbook quickly. If any step starts with "Note, the system will...", move that sentence into the explanation document.

Step 3: Write it with the people who will actually use the Runbook

Google's SRE book, in its chapter on incident management, recommends preparing in advance: establish and document incident response procedures early, and agree on them with the people who will take part in incident response. In the invoice AI agent example, if the customer operates the system themselves after handover, those people are the customer's IT team, not yours.

So don't write the Runbook alone and then email it over. Schedule a 45-minute session where the operators walk through it step by step while you sit beside them and quietly observe. Every step where they have to ask a question should be rewritten.

Step 4: Organize the playbook by phase

playbook.md

Not a copy of the ADRs, but a map: for each deployment phase, write down what to ask the customer, which ADRs to read, and which Runbooks to prepare in advance. You can use the six phases of the fde.academy handbook as the framework, as long as the final phase is always the feedback loop back to the product.

## 阶段：数据集成
- 询问客户：ERP 是否支持撤销？（→ ADR-003）
- 准备：runbooks/queue-stuck.md
- 为上一个客户做的定制：针对自定义发票模板的解析器
  → 可纳入产品的候选（已通知核心团队）

The last line is where the Palantir-style loop actually happens: field solutions are reviewed by the core team and become product capabilities. Every time you write down a "candidate for inclusion in the product," you pull the project one step out of the pure-customization trap.

Step 5: Build a template repository, don't fork

GitHub's documentation states that anyone with access to a template repository can use it to create a new repository. A repository created this way starts from a single commit. A fork is different: it carries the full history.

For an FDE, this distinction matters. The commit history of an old project may contain customer names, internal endpoints, or a sample CSV file that someone accidentally committed and later deleted.

The cleanest approach is: create a new repository, copy in only the cleaned-up code, replace all customer-specific values with

customer.example.yaml

, then enable the template setting in the repository settings.

Check: create a test repository from the template, open the commit history, and confirm there is only one commit. Then search the entire codebase for the old customer's name, domain, and words like "prod"—the results should be empty.

Three mistakes that make documentation useless

The first mistake is treating the Runbook as a textbook. The SRE book, in its chapter on training on-call engineers, lists training based only on processes, checklists, and manuals as an anti-pattern.

The Runbook helps people execute the right steps;

explanation/

the documentation helps them understand the reasons. When an incident doesn't match any written step, only that understanding can save the day.

The second mistake is forking the old customer's repository to save time, dragging the entire history along. The third mistake is recording only "what was done" and skipping "why." Without an ADR, the next customer inherits decisions that only made sense in the old customer's context.

Where do interviews test this skill?

When reading FDE job descriptions, you may encounter the feedback loop above in different wording. Look for passages that mention bringing field experience back to the product team, or building assets reusable across customers: that's a sign the company needs exactly this skill.

On your resume, don't just write "deployed system X for customer Y"—be more specific, for example: "packaged the project into a template repository, ADRs, and a Runbook; the next customer reused it directly with no rewrite from scratch." If possible, attach a link to a cleaned-up public template repository. In the interview, pick one ADR and talk through the debate behind it.

On your first day with a new customer, the first thing you should open is the previous project's

playbook.md

, not the IDE. A delivered project only becomes a team asset when the next person can reuse it.
