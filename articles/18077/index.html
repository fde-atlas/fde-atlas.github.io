FDE Guide to Production Incident Response and Blameless Postmortems

Original title: FDE 生产事故响应与无责复盘指南

FDE Times · 2026-10-08 · 6 min read

Summary

This guide teaches forward deployed engineers how to coordinate production incident response at customer sites and run blameless postmortems. Drawing on Google's SRE books, PagerDuty role definitions, and Cloudflare's November 18, 2025 outage postmortem, it argues that early incident declaration, a single incident commander, restricting system changes to one operations team, and pre-defined postmortem triggers determine how much customer trust survives an incident. It closes with common mistakes and resume advice for aspiring FDEs.

Key points

  • Declare incidents early; late disclosure turns bugs into cover-ups.
  • One incident commander is the single source of accurate information.
  • Only the operations team may change systems during an incident.
  • Control damage first, diagnose root cause later.
  • Blameless postmortems fix systems and processes, not people.

Declaring the incident early, appointing a single commander, and writing an honest postmortem determine how much trust the customer has left afterward.

Key Takeaways

  • Declare the incident early; don't wait hours while the problem festers.
  • Only one team is allowed to change the system; the incident commander is the single source of accurate information.
  • Without a formal process for learning from incidents, such as blameless postmortems triggered by predefined criteria, the same incidents may recur.

Imagine this scenario: at 9 p.m., the shared chat channel with your customer is suddenly flooded with messages. The data sync job you deployed last week is overwriting order data. Three people on the customer side are asking questions simultaneously, and one of their engineers has already logged into the server, ready to "try restarting it." You're tailing logs while answering a phone call.

At this moment, no amount of good code can save the situation. What really determines the outcome is whether you can coordinate: who may touch the system, who communicates with the customer, and who documents what happened.

For Forward Deployed Engineers (FDEs), incidents rarely happen "at home." You are caught between two organizations, two engineering teams, and two sets of processes. How you handle the first hour, and what kind of postmortem you write afterward, determine how much the customer will continue to trust you.

Why declare incidents early?

Google's SRE book recommends declaring incidents early, rather than waiting hours until the problem snowballs. An engineer's instinct is often the opposite: quietly fix it first, report afterward, and avoid making a fuss.

On-site at a customer, the cost of reporting late is even higher. If the customer discovers the problem before you do, they will handle it their own way, and it becomes "the vendor hid the bug." Declaring early and downgrading later is far cheaper than declaring late.

Declaring an incident requires no ceremony. A clear message in the shared channel is enough:

[INCIDENT] 订单同步任务自 20:50 起一直在覆盖记录。
影响：今天的部分订单状态可能有误。
指挥官：Minh（FDE）。所有系统改动都须经 Minh 确认。
下次更新：21:30，有进展会提前通报。

The third line matters most, because it makes everyone immediately stop trying to fix things on their own.

Four hats, even with only two people

PagerDuty defines the Incident Commander as the single source of accurate information about the incident. Google adds a hard rule: during incident response, only the operations team may change the system, while the commander maintains the big picture.

Two other roles are often overlooked. PagerDuty defines the Scribe as the person who records the timeline during the incident, and the customer liaison as the person who communicates directly with the customer. Google's book has a similar role, the Communications Lead, who speaks on behalf of the entire response team.

For small incidents, PagerDuty allows one person to hold multiple roles. In the reality of customer sites, the FDE is often both commander and liaison. What you must avoid is leaving any role unstaffed.

With the customer's engineers, this is the hardest thing to say. You have no authority to give them orders, but you can make a direct proposal: "Could you take on operations? Before each command is executed, let's confirm it in this channel first." They will usually breathe a sigh of relief, because someone is finally at the helm.

The first hour, as a timeline

Continuing the hypothetical example above, the timeline recorded by the scribe would look roughly like this. Note that each line records an event, not a judgment.

20:50  同步任务按计划运行。
21:04  客户报告订单状态有误。
21:07  Minh 宣布事故，分配角色。
21:12  运维通过配置开关临时停用同步任务。
21:20  确认：没有新的覆盖写入。
21:30  向客户更新：扩散已控制，正在评估受影响数据的范围。

22:15  原因查明：最近一次部署修改了键映射。

This order is deliberate: contain the damage first, investigate the cause second. At 21:12, nobody yet knew why the job went wrong, but shutting it down was enough to stop the bad data from being written.

If the incident drags into the next day, Google stresses that command must be explicitly handed over at the end of the workday. A single sentence—"Lan is commander from 8 a.m., timeline at this link"—prevents two people from both thinking they're in charge, or nobody being in charge at all.

The postmortem: fix systems, not people

Google defines a postmortem as a written document covering the incident itself, its impact, and the actions taken to mitigate or resolve it. The reason for writing one is simple: as the SRE book puts it, without a formal process for learning from incidents, they may repeat indefinitely.

When is one required? Google recommends setting trigger criteria in advance, such as any form of data loss. The overwritten orders in the example meet this criterion exactly, so nobody needs to argue about whether to write one.

The spirit of the postmortem is blamelessness. The SRE book points out that people cannot be "fixed," but systems and processes can be repaired to better support people. "Minh deployed the wrong mapping" teaches nothing. "The deployment pipeline didn't validate key mappings against real data" points to the work that needs to be done.

Cloudflare's public postmortem for the November 18, 2025 incident is a model worth studying. It opens with a direct apology from management: on behalf of the entire team, they apologize for the damage caused to the internet that day. The trigger is stated just as plainly: a permissions change in a database system.

The remediation list is equally concrete, including adding more global kill switches for individual features.

The kill switch detail is worth remembering for FDEs. In this article's example, the 21:12 step was fast only because the job already had an off switch. If it hadn't, that would be exactly the first item on the postmortem's action list.

The postmortem sent to the customer can be just one page: summary and apology, impact (how many records, how long), timeline, trigger, resolution, and preventive action items—each with an owner and a deadline.

A small exercise to build blameless thinking: rewrite the line from the opening scenario, "the customer's engineer logged into the server and restarted it," as a system-level flaw.

Hint: think about what made that action both possible and reasonable at the time? For example, the lack of a channel for agreeing on commands, or nobody being assigned to operations. Your answer is a preventive action item.

What if the customer has never written a postmortem?

This is common. Google's SRE Workbook acknowledges that introducing postmortems into an organization is both a technical and a cultural change. Its advice is to start with a very basic process, then gradually adapt it to the organization.

For an FDE, starting small means writing the first one yourself and inviting the customer to read and comment on it, rather than proposing an entire new process right away. Once the customer sees a document that blames no one yet produces concrete work, they'll ask you next time whether you're writing a postmortem.

Common Mistakes

The most common mistake is having several people fix things at once. You're rolling back while the customer's engineer restarts the service, and in the end nobody knows which change actually worked. The second is having no scribe: when it's time to write the postmortem, the whole team has to piece the timeline together from memory and chat logs.

The third mistake is in communication: promising a fix time before the cause is even identified. Only promise the time of the next update, then keep that promise. The last mistake is filing the postmortem away once it's written without assigning any action items to owners, so the old incident repeats itself exactly—just as the SRE book warns.

If you're targeting an FDE role, read carefully the job descriptions that mention on-call, incident response, or direct exposure to customer infrastructure. Don't just write "handled production incidents" on your resume; state clearly that you served as incident commander, how quickly you contained the damage, and which postmortem and preventive action items you implemented.

What employers need to see is that you can coordinate, not just read logs.

Every system you deploy will eventually have an incident. What customers remember afterward is who took the wheel at 9 PM, and which document kept the same failure from happening twice.
