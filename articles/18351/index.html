Differences Between FDEs and Traditional Engineers [Visual Comparison]

Original title: FDEと従来エンジニアの違い【図解で比較】

株式会社Sai · 2026-07-25 · 7 min read

Summary

This article by Japan's Sai explains how Forward Deployed Engineers differ from conventional engineers, arguing the distinction lies in scope of responsibility, not workplace. FDEs own the full cycle from problem discovery through implementation, adoption, and verified outcomes, while traditional contracted work is judged by delivery. It contrasts FDEs with SES (client-site dispatch), noting contracts are typically quasi-mandate (jun-i-nin) based on hours rather than guaranteed results, and cites OpenAI, Palantir, and Anthropic's DXC training plans as evidence of the role's rise. Sai promotes its own monthly FDE service for Japanese SMEs.

Key points

  • FDEs are defined by end-to-end responsibility, not by working on-site.
  • Success is measured by outcomes and adoption, not delivery.
  • Sitting with customers directly cuts rework and speeds development.
  • Unlike SES, FDEs decide what to build themselves, starting from proposals.
  • Contracts are usually quasi-mandate based on workload, not guaranteed results.

Key Takeaways

  • The difference between an FDE and a traditional engineer is not where they work, but the scope of their responsibility. They take end-to-end ownership, from identifying problems through implementation, operational adoption, and verifying results.
  • While the yardstick for contracted development is "was it delivered," the yardstick for an FDE is "did it produce results." They stay on-site even after release and keep improving until the solution takes hold.
  • FDEs sit at the same desk as the customer and talk directly, without going through intermediaries. This closeness directly translates into less rework and faster development.
  • Beyond deep expertise, FDEs need cross-boundary skills: the ability to structure problems, the implementation ability to build things end to end, and the ability to make change stick.
  • The difference from SES comes down to who decides what to build. FDEs do it themselves, starting from the proposal. Seeing things through to results is about how they work; contracts are mainly quasi-delegatory agreements based on hours worked, which do not guarantee completed results.

"So an FDE is basically an on-site SE, right?" — As the term Forward Deployed Engineer has spread, I've been asked this question more and more. To answer up front: the difference is not where you work, but the scope you are responsible for. An FDE (Forward Deployed Engineer) is an engineer who embeds at the customer's site and takes first-person ownership of everything from discovering problems to designing, implementing, and operationally embedding the solution.

The same content is explained in a roughly 2-minute video, so if you'd like to grasp the big picture first, please watch it.

What Is an FDE: The Scope of Responsibility, Illustrated

Three elements define an FDE: entering the customer's site, identifying the problems to solve on their own, and staying involved until the solution is used and produces results. It is not a term for a specific contract type.

As the diagram above shows, the essence of the difference is "which parts of the project you are involved in."

  • Engineers at contracted development firms and SIers: receive finalized requirements and handle design, development, and delivery. Identifying problems and whether results are achieved after delivery are, in principle, outside their scope of responsibility
  • In-house SEs: understand their own company's operations deeply, but development is often outsourced to vendors, so opportunities to exercise their "ability to build" tend to be limited
  • FDE: embeds at the customer's site and takes end-to-end ownership of problem discovery → solution design → implementation → operational adoption → verifying results

Comparing Across Four Dimensions — Starting Point, Yardstick, Distance from the Customer, and Skills

Dimension 1: The Starting Point — Does It Begin with a "Spec Sheet" or with a "Problem"?

Traditional contracted development starts from a requirements document prepared by the customer. But in reality, customers themselves can rarely articulate their true problem. It is not uncommon for a request like "we want a sales management system" to hide the real problem: "know-how is disappearing as veteran employees retire."

FDEs don't wait for a spec sheet. They enter the field, observe operations, look at the data, and start their work from the question of "what actually needs to be solved."

Dimension 2: The Yardstick — Not "Was It Delivered" but "Did It Produce Results"

Success in contracted development means "delivering something that matches the spec, on time, with assured quality." That is itself an important form of professionalism, but the contract can count as a success even if the deliverable is never used in the field.

The yardstick for an FDE is results. Is the system actually being used on-site? Has workload time decreased? Has it contributed to revenue? That is why FDEs stay on-site after release and keep improving until the solution takes hold.

This yardstick also shows up in how job postings are written. OpenAI's job posting for FDEs at its Tokyo location explicitly states that success is measured by adoption into production and business impact. What is evaluated is not completion of delivery, but a state of actual use (OpenAI official job posting, Forward Deployed Engineer - Tokyo, verified September 9, 2026).

Dimension 3: Distance from the Customer — "Through an Intermediary" or "At the Same Desk"

In SIer projects, multiple "intermediaries" — sales, PMs, IT departments — sit between the engineer and the field, and the telephone-game effect easily causes misalignment in understanding.

FDEs sit at the same desk as the customer. They talk directly with the people on the ground, show screens on the spot, and reflect feedback by the next day. This closeness directly translates into less rework and faster development.

Dimension 4: Skill Set — Adding "Cross-Boundary Ability" to "Deep Expertise"

Traditional engineers are expected to have deep expertise in their assigned domain. On top of that, FDEs are expected to have the following "cross-boundary skills."

  • The ability to draw out problems in the language of business and management, and structure them (the consultant's domain)
  • The implementation ability to build something working within limited time (the full-stack domain)
  • The ability to engage the people on the ground and make change stick (the change management domain)

This does not mean one person must do everything perfectly. What matters is keeping "building" as your anchor while being able to step into adjacent domains.

Why FDEs Are in Demand Now

Behind the rapid spread of this role is the arrival of generative AI.

AI has dramatically lowered the cost of writing code itself. As a result, the center of value has shifted from "writing" to "determining what should be built and turning it into results in the field." US-based Palantir positions this role as one it pioneered, describing it as a way of working where talented engineers are assigned directly to customers to tackle their most pressing problems head-on (Palantir official job posting, Forward Deployed Software Engineer, verified September 9, 2026). AI companies like OpenAI and Anthropic have the same role because this way of working is the most reliable way to deliver AI — a powerful but hard-to-master technology — to customers' operations.

In an announcement on June 11, 2026, Anthropic described FDEs as engineers embedded directly inside customer organizations. The same announcement also outlined a plan for major IT services firm DXC to train tens of thousands of Claude-certified FDEs (Anthropic official announcement, June 11, 2026).

For global trends and the structural background behind the boom, see Why Are FDEs Increasing? for a detailed explanation.

How Are FDEs Different from SES (On-Site Dispatch)?

Let's return to the question raised at the beginning. Japan has a work arrangement called "client-embedded work (SES)," but it is similar to yet distinct from the FDE role. The difference lies in who decides what to build. With SES, the common pattern is to take on tasks already decided by the client, whereas an FDE proposes what should be built and does so themselves. Equating the two based solely on the shared trait of being on-site leads to a misunderstanding of their essence.

Pursuing outcomes is about how you work; contracts are a separate matter

The "seeing things through to outcomes" described in this article concerns how one works in the field, and does not mean contractually guaranteeing outcomes. A contract for work (ukeoi) is a contract promising the completion of work, while a quasi-mandate (jun'inin) is a contract entrusting the handling of affairs (Civil Code Articles 632 and 656, verified November 5, 2026). FDE-style support is mainly based on quasi-mandate contracts, which determine the amount of work hours (Three types of contracts). What is written in the contract is the workload and the scope of duties; pursuing outcomes is about how you operate within that framework.

SES arrangements are also sometimes governed by quasi-mandate contracts, so the contract's name alone cannot draw the line. What matters is who is giving work instructions to the person doing the work. Having someone work under another's direction and supervision constitutes worker dispatch (Worker Dispatch Act, Article 2, Item 1, verified November 5, 2026), and whether an arrangement is dispatch or contracting for work is judged by the actual situation, not the contract format (Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare, Notice No. 37 Q&A collection, verified November 5, 2026). For individual contracts, please consult legal counsel or specialists.
