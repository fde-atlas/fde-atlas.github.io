How FDEs Point Out Process Issues to Customers

Original title: FDE 如何向客户指出流程问题

FDE Times · 2026-11-05 · 5 min read

Summary

This guide addresses a common FDE dilemma: deployment data reveals that a broken process step was designed by the very operations lead who must now help fix it. The author argues that automating a flawed process only accelerates errors, and proposes a communication method drawn from Crucial Conversations' STATE approach: lead with verifiable facts, offer your interpretation as a falsifiable hypothesis, invite rebuttal, and pair every finding with a small, immediately testable change. A worked example—an invoice-matching agent where 248 of 310 manually handled invoices stemmed from late PO creation—comes with a five-sentence script. The piece also covers preparation (recheck your methodology, gather physical evidence, build trust through small wins) and common failure modes, plus how to turn such stories into interview evidence.

Key points

  • Automating a broken process makes errors occur faster, not fewer.
  • State facts first, then offer interpretation as a hypothesis open to challenge.
  • Attach a small pilot with a measurable before/after comparison to each finding.
  • Avoid opening with conclusions, speaking before earning trust, or blindsiding the owner in front of superiors.
  • For interviews, structure the story as data, verification, persuasion, and measurable change.

Key Points at a Glance

  • The step the customer asks you to automate is sometimes exactly the step that's broken; automate it without fixing it, and errors will only arrive faster.
  • Lead with facts, present your interpretation as a hypothesis, then invite the process owner to challenge it.
  • Every finding should come with a small change that can be piloted immediately, not just a chart.

Three weeks into a deployment, you run a query on the logs and get a result nobody wants to hear: most of the exceptions the customer asked you to automate originate from an approval step designed by the head of operations. At Monday morning's progress meeting, that person is sitting across from you.

There are two ways to get this wrong. Say it clumsily, and you lose the most important ally inside the customer's organization; say nothing, and you automate a flawed process, with errors happening faster than before.

The skill in between sounds "soft" compared to writing code. But it often determines whether a deployment brings real change or leaves behind a dashboard nobody opens.

The customer asked you to treat the symptom, but you can see the cause

When describing situations Forward Deployed Engineers (FDEs) commonly face, the GSDC Council makes a familiar point: the solution the customer asks for isn't necessarily aimed at the real problem. They ask you to automate a certain step, but what needs fixing is sometimes the step before it.

Why do FDEs often spot this before the customer does? When discussing how Kepler approaches forward deployed engineering, Vinoo Ganesh argued that evidence from observing how people work is fundamentally different from evidence from asking them whether they like a feature.

An analysis of the Palantir model shows that FDEs there spend most of their working week—30 to 40 hours—sitting alongside analysts and operators, often inside secure facilities. Sitting that close, it becomes obvious where the process on paper and the process in reality diverge.

This means your findings come from real work, not hearsay, but they still need verification. The difficulty is that the person who wrote down the process on paper still believes in it.

Facts first, interpretation second

The STATE method from the book Crucial Conversations offers a clear sequence: every sensitive conversation should start with observable facts; then state your interpretation, but frame it as a guess that could be wrong, not an indisputable truth.

Next, humbly ask how the other person sees it, and make clear that you genuinely want to hear their view, even if it's completely different from yours.

This sequence works because facts are hard to argue with, while anyone can argue with a conclusion. But the premise is that your facts actually hold up. In a guide on delivering bad news, Quirk's emphasizes that any bad news must be backed by solid data, and that data must in turn rest on sound methodology.

That Quirk's article also makes an easily overlooked point: bad news is rarely all bad—many customers simply need to adjust their strategy and lean on their strengths. So when presenting bad facts, always also state what's working.

One meeting, broken down line by line

Imagine a distribution company asks you to build an agent to reconcile invoices against purchase orders (POs). Suppose that four weeks later, the logs show 1,200 invoices in total, of which 310 entered the manual processing queue.

You trace them one by one and find that for 248 of those 310, the PO was created only after the goods had already arrived at the warehouse. So the agent had nothing to match against.

The "receive goods first, create PO later" step was introduced by the head of operations to speed up delivery. Below is a five-sentence script you can use directly.

State the facts: "Over the past four weeks, 310 invoices required manual processing. For 248 of them, the PO was created after the goods were received. I've brought ten specific invoices for you to look at."

State what's working: "That said, the other 890 invoices matched automatically, which shows the reconciliation itself is effective."

State your hypothesis: "Late PO creation may be the main reason the number of manually processed invoices won't come down. But I don't yet understand why this step was designed this way."

Ask and invite them to push back: "What's your read on these numbers? If I've misunderstood something—for example, if some urgent orders have to be handled this way—I'd really like to know."

Propose a small pilot: "If you think it makes sense, we could pick a group of suppliers and pilot this for two weeks: create a provisional PO before goods arrive. At the end of the two weeks, we'd compare that group's manual invoice count against the past four weeks."

Notice that not a single sentence in the script says "your process is wrong." The listener either reaches that conclusion themselves, or points out a legitimate exception the logs didn't capture. Either outcome works in your favor.

Before the meeting: assume you'll be challenged, then check again

Preparation starts with methodology, not wording. Re-verify how you computed your statistics: the time window, the source of timestamps, which records were excluded. The process owner will attack the weakest link.

Then pick a few cases and go see them in person, as Ganesh says: observing the work yields evidence a query can't. Ten real invoices are more persuasive than one chart.

Then come prepared with a plan of action. When discussing the "dirty secret" of forward deployed engineering, Natalie Meurer argued that a useful operational application must help people act on data, not just look at it.

In the example above, that's the last sentence of the script: a two-week, narrowly scoped pilot that ends with a number you can compare.

How the conversation goes wrong

The most common mistake is opening with the conclusion. If your first sentence is a verdict, the other person will spend the entire meeting rebutting you instead of looking at the numbers with you.

Another trap is speaking up before you've earned trust. Ganesh describes how FDEs earn trust through small, successful fixes one by one, which then opens the door to deeper problems. If you point out flaws in someone's process in your first week, you're spending capital you haven't earned yet.

Also, don't spring this finding on them in front of their superiors. Talk to the process owner privately first, give them a chance to push back, and report upward together with them.

Finally, don't walk into the meeting with only a dashboard and no proposal. Those meetings tend to end in nods, and nothing changes.

When job hunting, turn this skill into evidence

When preparing for FDE interviews, be ready to answer questions like: "Tell me about a time you had to tell a customer something they didn't want to hear." A good answer has four parts: what data you found, how you verified it, whom you persuaded, and what measurable change followed.

When reading job descriptions, look for language about working with customer stakeholders and understanding their operational processes. This story belongs exactly in that spot on your resume—told with numbers, not adjectives.

This week's exercise: pick the most worrying number from a project you're working on, and write a five-sentence script using the template above. Read it to a colleague and ask: which sentence sounds like an accusation?

The process owner doesn't need you to prove them wrong. What they need to see is that fixing the process with you is less risky than continuing to defend it.
