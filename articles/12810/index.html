Why I Do "Small-Business FDE"? I'm the One Between the Boss and AI, Making Things Actually Work

Original title: 我为什么做“小生意 FDE”？我在老板和 AI 之间，负责把事跑通

53AI · 2026-07-31 · 7 min read

Summary

The author, with 22 years of government-enterprise business experience, runs a one-person operation supported by six AI agent employees. He found that agents execute tasks but cannot decide which business problem matters most, what clients really want, or where human sign-off is required. He therefore defines his role as a "small business FDE": entering a real business, identifying the most painful repetitive work, translating it into agent-executable tasks, and setting division of labor, pause points, and acceptance criteria. He stresses delivering workflows clients can rerun and verify independently, then distilling reusable templates into standardized AI employee products. He also lists screening questions for choosing a first task and prices a ¥9.9 knowledge base and a ¥699 diagnostic service, noting market validation is still ongoing.

Key points

  • AI agents execute but cannot judge which business problem to solve first.
  • His FDE role: find high-frequency pain, translate it into agent tasks with checkpoints.
  • Success means clients can independently rerun and accept the workflow, not watch a demo.
  • Real projects feed reusable templates that become standardized AI employee products.
  • Offerings include a ¥9.9 knowledge base and ¥699 diagnostic service; validation is ongoing.

Full text

I have 22 years of experience serving government and enterprise clients, including Fortune 500 companies.

Now, I've also assigned myself 6 AI employees for execution.

In theory, a one-person business should be able to run smoothly by now.

But once things actually started running, I discovered something very real:

They can execute tasks.

But they don't automatically know:

Which business problem should be solved right now?

What does a client's offhand remark actually mean they want?

Which steps can be automated, and which ones require a human decision?

To what degree is something truly ready for delivery?

If no one takes ownership of these questions, the faster AI works, the more rework there may be.

This is also the pitfall most small business owners fall into when using AI.

They've installed plenty of tools.

They've built a few agents too.

But they're still busy every day.

Because what's really missing isn't more tools.

It's someone who can stand between the business and AI and make things actually work end to end.

That's how I now position myself:

I'm not standing on the outside recommending tools to business owners.

I step into a specific business, first find the most painful, highest-frequency repetitive work, then translate it into tasks an agent can execute.

Then I define the division of labor, set pause points, check outputs, until the workflow is usable, can pass acceptance, and can be maintained by the owner themselves.

So for me, FDE is not a new title.

It's my position within a real business.

FDE is usually translated as Forward Deployed Engineer.

OpenAI's description of this role covers problem discovery, technical scoping, system design, building, and production launch. Palantir has also always emphasized that engineers should stay close to real business problems and bring field feedback back to product and engineering.

What I'm borrowing isn't the job title, but this way of working:

Large companies have complex systems, data platforms, and engineering teams.

The scenarios small businesses face aren't as big, but the pain is no less:

  • Content updates keep getting interrupted.
  • Questions clients asked before can't be found.
  • People who promised to follow up often forget.
  • Reports, materials, and schedules get reorganized over and over.
  • Despite installing plenty of AI tools, they're still working late to finish things themselves.

These owners don't need another lecture on "why AI matters."

They need someone to sit with them and ask the questions clearly:

Which task is actually the most time-consuming?

Can it be broken down into fixed steps?

Which part can AI take over?

Where must a human make the call?

What counts as "good enough"?

That's my first job when I step into a business.

Many owners open with:

"I want to make my company AI-powered."

That sounds ambitious, but you can't actually start work from it.

So I keep asking:

  • In the past 7 days, which task was repeated at least 3 times?
  • Who kicked it off each time?
  • What materials need to be gathered before starting?
  • Where does it most easily get stuck?
  • What final deliverable counts as done?
  • When something goes wrong, who notices and takes over?

The owner says, "I want to go AI."

My job is to translate that into:

> Every Monday, pull 5 high-frequency questions from the user question list, generate topic ideas based on the latest product materials, and after my confirmation, move into writing.

Or:

> After a client inquiry, organize the questions, product interest, and next steps; no automatic quotes, no commitments on my behalf—just generate a follow-up task card.

Only at this point does the matter go from a wish to a task that can be built.

So I don't start by asking clients which model they want to use.

I first help them find:

This role is more like a business translator.

On one side, understanding what's really frustrating the owner; on the other, translating it into materials, steps, boundaries, deliverables, and acceptance criteria.

If this step isn't done well, no matter how many tools are installed afterward, it just means running the chaos faster.

Only after the task is clearly defined do the agents come in.

What I do at this point isn't handing the client a very long prompt.

I need to decide:

  • Which agent gathers the materials?
  • Which agent generates the first draft?
  • Which agent checks facts and boundaries?
  • Which step must pause for human confirmation?
  • Where are the results saved?
  • How does the next run pick up right where it left off?

In my own workspace, I now have 6 AI employees for execution, with A Ren handling business gatekeeping—7 collaborative seats in total.

They handle tasks like development, content, visuals, information gathering, and operations.

But I won't simply describe this as "I have a lot of agents."

Having many agents doesn't mean the business runs.

Without division of labor, handoffs, and acceptance checks, a group of agents will just create chaos together.

My role is the chief dispatcher.

I'm responsible for setting goals, assigning tasks, setting pause points, resolving conflicts, reviewing results, and deciding the next step.

So what I deliver is not a bot that answers questions.

It's a workflow that keeps running:

AI services are prone to a particular illusion.

The demo goes smoothly.

Once the deliverer leaves, the client can't use it anymore.

They don't know where to edit the prompt, where to put the materials, or which step to check when the output is wrong.

In the end, the client has merely swapped dependence on a human contractor for dependence on an AI contractor.

That's not the outcome I want to deliver.

I care more about three things:

It's not about watching my demo.

It's whether the client can put in their own materials, restart the process, and still get an acceptable result.

AI finishing an article doesn't mean the article is ready to publish.

It still needs checks on positioning, facts, products, tone, platform risks, and calls to action.

Without acceptance criteria, AI is just mass-producing things that "look about right."

The client needs to know:

  • What this AI employee is responsible for.
  • What it is not responsible for.
  • Where the materials go.
  • Which parts can be modified.
  • How to pause when something fails.
  • Which situations must be handed back to a human.

I will not take on pricing, funding, client commitments, or final responsibility for the owner.

But I must keep these human decision points in the workflow.

My goal isn't to make clients permanently dependent on me.

Quite the opposite.

If every new client means building a completely different system from scratch, I'm still just selling my own time.

More projects, more exhaustion.

That's no different in essence from traditional outsourcing.

So I also need to do a fourth thing:

Find reusable parts from real deliveries.

My criteria are clear:

Next, I'll first lock in one specific content task and find three paid samples of the same task.

These haven't happened yet, so they can't be written up as existing client cases.

What I'll compare is:

  • Whether the materials the three clients need are similar.
  • Whether the execution steps are mostly the same.
  • Whether the acceptance criteria can be shared.
  • Which errors keep recurring.
  • Which parts must retain human judgment.
  • Whether the delivery can be independently replicated afterward.

Only if these parts can be reused will I turn it into a standardized AI employee.

Task templates, knowledge bases, workflow modules, acceptance checklists, and maintenance documentation all need to be kept.

For me, an FDE project is not just a source of income.

It is also a product development effort.

On-site work helps me discover real needs.

A standardized product completes a reusable delivery.

Only then can ongoing maintenance, knowledge bases, subscriptions, and licensing have a chance to create long-term value.

This is also the company I want to build now:

I have 22 years of experience in government-enterprise services and commercial projects.

My background covers business development for telecom operator projects, digital marketing at ByteDance, consulting for leading brands in the new economy, government industrial investment promotion, and services for tech startups.

I have served over 30 Fortune 500 clients and have been deeply involved in frontline projects across different organizational settings, including private companies, state-owned enterprises, listed companies, and government agencies.

My strength is not just understanding business communication; I am also familiar with the real process of government-enterprise projects, from need assessment and resource coordination to solution design and implementation.

This allows me, when working on AI projects, to judge what is a real need and what can actually run, from the perspectives of the market, industry, government, and enterprise clients. I mention this not to impress anyone with my résumé.

What these experiences have truly left me with are three capabilities:

First, understanding what different people actually want to solve.

Second, breaking a complex task into steps that can be advanced.

Third, in collaboration involving many people and many stages, assigning outcomes and responsibilities to specific positions.

I am not a programmer by training.

This actually makes me more wary of one thing:

Do not start by overwhelming clients with technical jargon.

Ask about the business first.

Look at the actual work site first.

Find that repetitive task that drains people every single day.

Technology selection matters, but it should come after the problem.

I am now building my own personal brand and using my own business as a testing ground.

For content production, research, visual creation, operations, and back-office organization, whatever can be handed to an AI agent, I hand over first.

I keep judgment, deal-making, and decision-making for myself.

The parts that can run, be replicated, and be verified, I then use to help others.

So "small business FDE" is not a new label I put on myself.

It is the position I have chosen at the intersection of my past business experience, my current AI agent practice, and my future product direction.

To make the boundaries clear, I want to state four things explicitly.

I am not an AI tools teacher who only teaches prompts.

I am not a systems salesperson who leaves after the software is sold.

I am not a senior contractor who runs the boss's business long-term on their behalf.

I also will not promise that installing an AI agent will necessarily grow your followers, close deals, or increase revenue.

What I am truly responsible for is:

As for whether this service has a real market, one article cannot prove it.

It depends on whether real clients are willing to pay, whether the delivery actually works, and whether a second client can reuse it.

This evidence is still being validated.

I will not write assumptions down as results in advance.

First, write down the things that have happened at least three times in the past 7 days.

Then ask yourself five questions:

  • Does it happen frequently?
  • Can the required materials be gathered together?
  • Can the main steps be repeated?
  • Can the outcome be verified after completion?
  • Can a human take over when something goes wrong?

If most of the answers are "yes," it could become your first AI employee.

If you just want to run through it yourself first, you can start with the ¥9.9 knowledge base "My First AI Employee."

If you cannot even figure out "which thing to fix first," I currently offer a ¥699 first-batch pilot service.

At this stage, it handles only one specific content task and delivers one structured diagnostic report and 3 custom templates.

It is not a full workflow deployment, and it does not include automated publishing, unlimited revisions, or guaranteed results.

Only after the task is clearly defined and has gone through a real trial run, acceptance, and independent replication will we decide whether to evaluate a single-business closed-loop workflow.

I do not want clients to pay for the new term "FDE."

I want them to pay because a specific task no longer requires them to personally keep an eye on it over and over.

Finally, let me answer the opening question once more:

I am a business needs translator.

I am the AI agent's chief coordinator and workflow builder.

I am the person responsible for delivery acceptance.

I am also the product developer who distills on-site experience into a standardized AI employee.

I stand between the boss and AI.

I do not make the final decision for the boss.

But I am responsible for making the part that should be handed to AI actually run.

Even a one-person business deserves a team that can get work done.
