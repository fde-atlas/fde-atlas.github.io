How are reasoning tokens billed, and how does an FDE adjust reasoning effort?

Original title: 推理 token 怎么计费，FDE 如何调推理强度

FDE Times · 2026-10-08 · 6 min read

Summary

This article explains how reasoning tokens are billed: OpenAI's are invisible yet consume context, while Anthropic charges thinking tokens as output tokens, meaning customers pay for work they never see. The author argues reasoning effort is a per-workload knob to be measured with evals, not a quality fix, and lays out a five-level ladder from plain prompts to manual budgets. FDEs should bring clients a table of quality, latency, and token costs from their own data rather than benchmark rankings.

Key points

  • Reasoning tokens are billed and consume context even though users never see them.
  • Chain-of-thought only emerges in large models, and plausible reasoning chains can still be wrong.
  • Both vendors treat effort and thinking budgets as diminishing-return tuning knobs, not quality rescues.
  • A five-level ladder matches reasoning configurations to cost, latency, and SLA needs.
  • FDEs should decide with client-data evals and logged usage numbers, not intuition.

Reasoning is a knob that must be measured per workload; what convinces customers is evals on their own data, not benchmark leaderboards.

Key takeaways

  • Reasoning can help models solve complex problems better, but a plausible-sounding chain of reasoning can still be wrong.
  • Reasoning tokens are not returned via the API, yet they still consume context. On Claude they are billed as output tokens, so log them separately.
  • Both OpenAI and Anthropic treat effort/budget as a tuning knob with diminishing returns, not a way to rescue quality.

There is a detail in OpenAI's API documentation that is easy to skim past: reasoning tokens cannot be seen through the API, yet they still take up space in the context window. Anthropic states outright that thinking tokens are billed as output tokens. In other words, customers are paying for work they will never read.

So "should we turn on reasoning?" is rarely a purely technical question. It touches the three things customers care about most: the bill, latency, and how much the answer can be trusted. On-site, the person expected to answer that question is usually you.

This article offers a clear conclusion: reasoning is a knob that must be measured separately for each workload, not an upgrade switch you flip once and apply everywhere. The answer most likely to convince customers does not come from benchmark leaderboards, but from eval results run on the customer's own data.

The benefits are real, but with caveats

The 2022 chain-of-thought (CoT) paper showed that generating intermediate reasoning steps significantly improves a large model's ability to solve complex problems.

IBM's explanation of the mechanism is simple: break the problem into smaller logical steps, and the user can also see the path the model took to reach its answer.

The original paper also contains a premise few people mention: this ability only emerges naturally in models large enough. So if a customer insists on running small models locally for security reasons, don't assume that adding reasoning steps will rescue quality.

The second caveat is more alarming. IBM warns that models may produce reasoning chains that sound plausible but are actually wrong. When customers see a long, coherent argument, they tend to place more trust in the answer than it deserves. Without an eval set to check against, the transparency CoT provides can turn into false confidence.

The bill hides in the part nobody reads

OpenAI describes reasoning models as adding a third category of tokens beyond input and output. IBM puts it more bluntly: generating and processing large numbers of reasoning steps requires more compute and time, and the cost of enterprise adoption rises accordingly.

Take Claude as an example, because Anthropic explicitly states that thinking tokens are billed as output tokens. Imagine a request with numbers chosen to make the math easy: 500 input tokens and a 200-token answer. If the model thinks for 3,000 tokens before answering, the billed output is 3,200 tokens, not 200, even though the user only sees those 200.

Context gets eaten away the same way. If an agent needs to fit long contracts, conversation history, and tool results into the same context window, every thousand tokens of thinking means a thousand fewer tokens of documents. Anthropic returns a number in the response indicating how much of the billed output tokens was internal reasoning.

For any project that uses reasoning, the first thing to do is log this field from day one.

A knob, not a life raft

Both vendors provide such a knob; they just call it different names. OpenAI has the reasoning effort parameter, and its documentation recommends treating it as a tuning knob rather than the primary means of restoring quality. Low effort is faster and uses fewer tokens, suited to latency-sensitive scenarios.

Anthropic sets a thinking budget in tokens, with nearly the same framing, just more detail on diminishing returns: a higher budget means more thorough reasoning, but the gains taper off depending on the task, at the cost of higher latency.

Anthropic's specific advice: for simple tasks, start near the floor of 1,024 tokens and increase gradually; for complex tasks, start at 16k or above.

For a Forward Deployed Engineer (FDE), "not the primary means of restoring quality" is an operational instruction. When a customer complains about poor answer quality, the sensible troubleshooting order is: Is the context sufficient? Is the prompt clear? Do the evals reflect the customer's real needs? Only after checking all of these should you turn the effort knob.

Let the model decide, or keep control yourself?

Newer Claude models replace the fixed budget with adaptive thinking: the model decides for each request whether to think and how much; at low effort, it can even skip thinking entirely for simple inputs. This sounds like a cure-all for cost concerns, but Anthropic keeps one important exception.

According to its documentation, manual mode remains useful when a workload needs predictable latency or precise control over thinking costs. On-site, both situations come up more often than you'd expect: a system with a response-time service level agreement (SLA), or a finance department that wants to know its monthly cost in advance.

Arranged from cheapest to most expensive, these options give the FDE a five-rung ladder to climb, with the last rung being the choice between adaptive thinking and a manual budget. At rungs 3 and 4, "effort" is OpenAI's parameter, while the 1,024 and 16k token budgets belong to Anthropic:

A morning at the customer's site

Imagine a logistics company with two workloads. The first is a chatbot that tells customers where their orders are. The second is a reconciliation tool that checks carrier invoices line by line against contracts full of surcharge clauses, run in batch every night.

The chatbot is a lookup task, with users waiting right in front of the screen. Start at the bottom rung of the ladder, and stay there if quality is good enough. If more reasoning is needed, low effort or a small manual budget keeps latency within what the SLA allows.

The reconciliation tool is the opposite. It is the kind of multi-step problem where CoT research shows intermediate reasoning helps, and it runs overnight, so latency matters less. You can start with a larger budget, but you should still run evals at several budget levels to find the point where quality starts to plateau.

Diminishing returns mean there is always a threshold beyond which the customer is just paying more money.

For both workloads, what you bring into the customer meeting is a three-column table: eval quality, latency, and inference tokens for each configuration. Customers don't need to understand chain-of-thought; they need to see which tier is worth the money, and why.

One more risk you should flag to customers upfront: a reasoning chain that sounds plausible can still be wrong. If the customer plans to show the reasoning process to reconciliation staff, make clear that it is only a suggestion that needs verification, not evidence.

What developers should practice starting this week

What you should practice here is not memorizing API parameters—the parameter names have already changed from fixed budgets to adaptive, and may change again. What really matters is the instinct for measurement: build a small eval set, run the same task across multiple effort tiers, read the usage data, and then give a recommendation backed by numbers.

When reading job descriptions for FDE or AI engineer roles, watch for phrases like "latency budget", "cost optimization", "evals", or "production LLM". They signal that the company needs someone who understands trade-offs, not just someone who can call APIs.

On your resume, a sentence about how you compared multiple reasoning configurations in evals and selected the one that met the customer's SLA is more persuasive than "familiar with GPT and Claude".

If you don't have a real project yet, take a familiar problem, such as bank statement reconciliation or contract clause extraction, run it through each level of the ladder above, and write up the results in a short article.

When job hunting, attach the quality, latency, and token table you measured yourself to your portfolio, because it shows you make decisions based on data rather than intuition.

Models will get better and better at thinking, and will increasingly make decisions on their own. But whether customers should pay for that thinking is still decided by the person standing between the model and the bill—and that position belongs to the FDE.
