Colin Jarvis OpenAI: why the model is 20% of the gap

FDE Times · 2026-10-20 · 3 min read

Summary

Colin Jarvis, who leads Global Forward Deployed Engineering at OpenAI, estimates that model capability accounts for only about 20% or less of the enterprise AI deployment gap. The rest lies inside the customer's organization: scattered data, access restrictions, undefined evaluation standards, and unclear ownership after launch. Drawing on deployments at Morgan Stanley, Klarna, Coca-Cola and others, he argues trust must come before ROI, since engineers need customer trust to access sensitive data and change workflows. OpenAI's FDE team, reportedly just 39 engineers as of November 2025 with a target of 52, aims for customer self-sufficiency and reusable playbooks rather than services revenue. The article also advises FDE job candidates to describe customer-side bottlenecks they cleared, not just applications they built.

Key points

  • Model capability is only about 20% of the deployment gap, per Jarvis.
  • The remaining gap is organizational: data, access, evals, and ownership.
  • Trust is a precondition before engineers can touch sensitive data or workflows.
  • The FDE goal is customer self-sufficiency and reusable playbooks, not services revenue.
  • OpenAI's FDE team had 39 engineers in November 2025, targeting 52.

In brief

  • Colin Jarvis, who runs OpenAI's global FDE function, estimates the model accounts for about 20% or less of the deployment gap
  • The rest is organizational and sits with the customer: data, access, evaluation and team structure
  • The FDE goal is customer self-sufficiency and reusable playbooks, not services revenue

Model capability accounts for “about 20% or less” of the deployment gap in enterprise AI, by Colin Jarvis’s estimate. Jarvis leads Global Forward Deployed Engineering at OpenAI. In his view most of what remains lies inside the customer’s organization rather than at OpenAI: data, access, evaluation and team structure.

The claim carries weight because Jarvis has done the work himself. His speaker biography at HumanX says he was responsible for OpenAI’s technical deployments at Morgan Stanley, Klarna, Coca-Cola and others. At HumanX Amsterdam he gave a session titled “Turning the Promise of Frontier AI into Reality”.

For anyone working as an FDE, or hoping to become one, the estimate doubles as a job description: the hard part of the role is not calling an API.

The model is no longer the bottleneck

Take a hypothetical bank that wants an agent to answer questions for its financial advisers. The demo, built on sample documents, works well. Once the system goes into real use, the difficult questions surface one by one.

The real documents are scattered across three systems. Advisers at one branch are not allowed to see client files from another.

Nobody has defined what a “correct” answer looks like. And no team has agreed to own the system after launch.

A stronger model solves none of these problems. They make up the large remainder Jarvis describes, and they explain why OpenAI has to place engineers inside companies instead of simply selling access.

Trust comes before ROI

In an Altimeter Capital podcast episode released in November 2025, Jarvis was introduced as OpenAI’s Head of Forward Deployed Engineering. He talked about the work with Morgan Stanley and with a European semiconductor company.

The episode summary stresses that concrete ROI requires FDEs to stay close to customers, understand their specific needs and build trust.

The order matters. An engineer gets to touch sensitive data, obtain access or change a team’s workflow only once the customer trusts them. Stakeholder skills are therefore a precondition for the technical work to start at all, rather than an optional extra filed under “soft skills”.

Success is when the customer no longer needs you

Jarvis is explicit about the goal: “We want to help customers build the capability to do the next use cases themselves.” Another summary of the podcast says the FDE team aims for reusable product playbooks, not services revenue.

The size of the team points the same way. According to that same summary, published around November 2025, OpenAI’s FDE team had just 39 engineers at the time, with a target of 52 by the end of the year.

A team that small cannot stay with each customer indefinitely. Every deployment has to leave behind a capability the customer can run on its own, and a lesson the team carries to the next customer.

What this means for your CV

When applying for an FDE role, “built a RAG application” is too thin. Describe which customer-side bottleneck you cleared: which data sources you consolidated, how you designed access controls, how many test cases the evaluation suite had, and which team runs the system now that you have left.

Return to the bank example. Before: “Built a RAG chatbot for a bank.” After: “Consolidated documents from three systems, set up branch-level access controls, defined a correct-answer evaluation suite with financial advisers, then handed the system over to an internal team to run.”

When reading job descriptions, look for words such as “enablement”, “playbook” or “handover”. They signal a company that understands the job the way Jarvis does.

The self-sufficiency goal Jarvis sets also suggests a simple test for your own work: a deployment is truly finished when the customer’s team can deliver the next use case without calling the FDE back.
