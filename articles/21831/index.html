State of FDE 2026: The Survey Results

FDE Hub · 2026-10-27 · 8 min read

Summary

The State of FDE 2026 survey closed with only 24 responses, far below the author's 100-response target, which he attributes to weak distribution, FDEs' workload, and the gap between how much is written about the role and how few practitioners engage. The sample skews toward North America and AI startups, with experienced engineers who moved into the role recently. Key findings: reporting lines split across five functions; practitioners see FDE work as a product function, but companies rarely structure it that way and code rarely flows back to the core product; the role divides cleanly along whether people write production code; and while 15 of 24 see the role growing more critical, most expect to leave it within two years. The author plans a larger, referral-driven survey next year.

Key points

  • Only 24 responses came in; personal asks outperformed broadcast emails and posts.
  • Reporting lines split five ways: engineering, CEO, delivery, sales, product.
  • 17 write production code, but only 4 say it merges back into the core product.
  • The 80% discovery group doesn't code; the 80% building group does—two jobs, one title.
  • 15 see the role as more critical, yet only 4 expect to stay in it two years.

When I opened the State of FDE 2026 survey in September, I had a number in mind. A hundred responses was the minimum I needed to say anything useful about compensation or team structure. 150 to 200 was the stretch goal. The survey closed with 24.

I want to write about what those 24 people said, because they took the time and what they told me is genuinely interesting. But I should start with the number, because I think it says something too.

What happened

Part of it is on me. The questionnaire itself came together quickly and I’m happy with it: the questions, the anonymity setup, the segment definitions. Where I underinvested was distribution, and specifically in asking people directly.

The pattern was pretty clear once it was running. The launch email to subscribers brought in a handful of responses. A LinkedIn post brought in a few more. Then one evening I sent personal messages to people I know, and that single evening produced about as many responses as the email had. People don’t fill in surveys because they see them. They fill them in because someone they know asked them to. I knew that in theory and still planned around broadcasts.

Part of it is the audience. FDEs are busy in a very specific way. They sit between the customer and the product, they’re usually the only person who can unblock whichever project is on fire that week, and a ten minute survey about the job is never going to beat the job. Several people told me they’d get to it. I believe all of them. That’s exactly how you end up with 24.

And part of it, I think, is something I wrote about in September. There’s more being written about FDEs than ever, and most of it comes from outside the role. It turns out that a market producing a new article about FDEs every day still can’t easily produce a hundred of them to describe their own week. I’m wary of leaning on that too much, because it’s also a convenient excuse for my distribution plan. But it’s there.

What 24 people can tell you

Before the survey opened, I’d written down the thresholds I’d report at. I’m sticking to them. That means no medians, no percentages and no compensation figures. With 24 people a percentage is misleading: twelve becomes “half of respondents” and suddenly sounds like a market. I did collect salary bands and I’m not going to publish them. Seventeen people paid in dollars is enough to describe a room, not enough to tell anyone what to ask for.

What 24 detailed answers can do is roughly what 24 interviews can do. They show you the shape of something. They surface agreement in places you didn’t expect it. And they tell you what a bigger survey should be asking.

So, who answered. Twenty of the 24 are in North America, fifteen of them in the US. Sixteen work at AI product startups or scale-ups. Fifteen have been doing FDE-type work for under two years, although six of them have more than fifteen years of professional experience, so this isn’t a junior crowd. It’s experienced people who moved into the role recently. Twenty said their team had grown in the last year. Nobody said it had shrunk.

Three things stood out to me, and then there’s what people wrote in the open text box at the end.

Nobody agrees where the team sits

I asked who the FDE function reports to. Six said engineering. Five said the CEO or founder directly. Five said services or delivery. Four said sales. Three said product.

Five different answers, and the biggest group is a quarter of the room. I expected some spread, but not this flat. It suggests companies haven’t decided what kind of thing an FDE team is: an engineering team that happens to face customers, a delivery organisation, a sales resource, or a product function. Each of those gives the same job a different boss, a different definition of done and a different idea of what the work is for.

Here’s what makes it interesting. In the open text question at the end, four people independently described the role as a product function. One wrote that FDEs have the most context on real-world usage and the gaps, because their goal is to go live rather than to look good in a demo. Another described the job as getting things back from customers that improve the product, while unblocking deals for them. A third said FDEs should be treated like product engineers and kept off aggressive quotas.

Three of the 24 report into product.

The code tells the same story. Seventeen respondents write production code as part of customer engagements. I asked where it ends up. Four said it gets merged back into the core product. Nine said it stays in customer-specific solutions and integrations. The rest said both.

The original version of this role, the Palantir one, works on a loop: one engineer, one customer, and what you learn flows back into the product. Four of the seventeen people writing code described that loop. Most of the others are doing bespoke work that stays bespoke. One person put it plainly: solutions are more custom than most would like to admit.

If there’s one conclusion in this data, it’s this. The people doing the work think of it as a product function. Most companies haven’t set it up as one, and the code mostly doesn’t travel back. The role as practitioners describe it and the role as companies have built it aren’t the same thing yet.

The week splits along the coding line

I asked how the working week divides between discovery and context-gathering on one side and building on the other. On its own, the spread is unremarkable. Some people are mostly discovery, some mostly building, most are somewhere in the middle.

Then I crossed it with whether the person writes production code. All four people who said their week is 80% discovery don’t write production code. All four who said 80% building do. The middle is mixed. The ends aren’t.

With 24 people that’s a pattern, not a proof. But it’s the cleanest thing in the data, and it lines up with something John Gleeson and I wrote about earlier this year: the role is splitting into a context-gathering, relationship-holding half and a deeply technical half. The people at the two ends of that question aren’t doing a lighter or heavier version of the same job. They’re doing two different jobs with the same title.

More critical, and not staying

Fifteen of the 24 think the role is becoming more critical as models get better. One thinks it’s becoming less critical.

At the same time, only four expect to be doing the same kind of work in two years. Seven expect to be leading an FDE team. Five expect to be founding something. Four expect to be in a product or engineering role. Three said they honestly have no idea.

I find that combination worth sitting with. The role is seen as more important by the people in it, and most of them plan to be somewhere else fairly soon, mostly upward or outward. One reading is that this is a stage rather than a destination, a bit like a residency, and that’s fine. Another is that the job as currently set up wears people out. Seven of the 24 said the systems they build stay with them after handover, and twelve spend more than a tenth of their week keeping delivered systems running, so there’s some support for the second reading. I can’t tell which one it is from 24 people. It’s the question I most want the next edition to answer.

What people wrote

Thirteen people answered the open question about what the market gets wrong about this role. The answers grouped themselves more than I expected.

The biggest group was about the title. One person wrote that pre-sales and solutions engineers are being labelled as FDEs for no good reason. Another said people treat it as a generic role that fits engineering, product, sales or customer success, and that they’d seen CSM FDEs. One felt treated as a consultant. One said the market sees it as a glorified engineer. Same complaint, four different angles.

The group I didn’t expect was about hiring. Two people, separately, argued that the industry over-indexes on software engineering credentials. One said you don’t need a computer science degree or years as a software engineer, you need to be able to think ahead, build, and know what good looks like. Another put the ideal mix at 60% business and people skills and 40% technical, against an industry currently hiring for 80% technical.

And the longest answer, from someone at a lab, argued that FDEs end up in a “too much work for one person” situation precisely when companies treat building processes and feedback loops as a waste of time. You only get to a repeatable playbook if you invest in that work.

Put together, the people doing the job seem to think it’s harder to describe and easier to learn than the job descriptions suggest.

What I’d take from this

If you run an FDE team, I’d look at two things. First, where does your FDEs’ code actually end up? If the answer is always “with the customer”, you have a services team with a product team’s title and cost structure, and it’s worth deciding whether that’s intentional. Second, who owns the things they shipped six months ago? If the answer is “they do”, you’re spending your discovery budget on maintenance.

If you’re hiring, the people in this sample are telling you the bar is in the wrong place. Not lower, but somewhere else.

If you’re an FDE, your peers see the role as a product function and expect to be in a different seat within two years. Both are useful to know the next time scope comes up.

Next time

The first State of FDE report, back in January, analysed two thousand public profiles. This one asked 24 people directly. Neither is the report I actually want, which needs both: a few hundred practitioners answering honestly, enough of them to put a number next to a region or a company type and mean it.

The questionnaire, the anonymity setup and the definitions all stay. What changes is how I ask. Next year it starts with one person asking another, and everyone who took part this time gets asked first.

And to the 24 of you who answered: thank you. It was more useful than the number suggests.
