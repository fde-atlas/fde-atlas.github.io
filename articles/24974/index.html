Practical Steps for Automating Business Processes with AI Agents and the Role of FDEs

Original title: AIエージェントで業務自動化を進める実務手順とFDEの役割

FDE総研 · 2026-09-11 · 9 min read

Summary

This Japanese article contrasts RPA with AI agents for business automation: RPA follows explicit steps and fails visibly, while agents receive goals and can fail silently with wrong outputs. The author argues most projects stall at PoC due to vague business definitions and unclear accountability, not model accuracy. It lays out a five-phase rollout (inventory, scoping, eval preparation, staged permissions, handover), details the first 30 days focused on observing workflows and building eval data, and describes the FDE's role in surfacing exceptions on-site, designing evals, permissions, and logging, and transferring operations. Success should be measured by processing time and human intervention rate two to three months after launch.

Key points

  • AI agents fail silently—logs look normal while answers are wrong—so monitoring must track output quality.
  • Choose first use cases with many exceptions, recoverable failures, and human final review.
  • A five-phase plan spans roughly 11–22 weeks, from inventory to operational handover.
  • If staff disagree on correct answers, agents will too; codify judgment criteria first.
  • FDEs must span evals, logging, permissions, integration, and handover to avoid gaps between specialists.

How does business automation with AI agents differ from RPA?

The biggest difference is that RPA reproduces fixed steps, while AI agents receive a goal and assemble the steps themselves.

This difference changes the difficulty of implementation itself. RPA is a tool premised on humans writing out "how to operate" completely; if fully written, it runs stably. AI agents are tools where you hand over "what you want achieved" and delegate the decisions along the way. How far to delegate becomes the core of the design, and organizations that cannot settle this will see projects stall regardless of model performance.

The role of FDE (Forward Deployed Engineer) is attracting attention because this work of "deciding the scope of delegation in the field" has a nature distinct from both traditional contracted development and in-house SE work. Palantir popularized the title, and now domestic SaaS companies and AI startups are beginning to establish similar roles.

How do RPA and AI agents break down differently?

RPA records screen operations while AI agents delegate judgment, so their failure modes and operational costs are fundamentally different.

RPA failures are clear and reproducible. If a button position on a screen changes, it stops, and the stoppage is immediately apparent. AI agents, on the other hand, fail by returning wrong answers without stopping. Processing completes, logs look normal, and someone notices weeks later. Whether you can design monitoring premised on this "silent failure" is the dividing line for operations.

From what I see in the field, teams with long RPA experience tend to retain the sense that "it works = it's done" and often forget to include evaluation and logging effort in initial estimates. It's easier later if you nail this down explicitly before starting.

What kinds of work are suited to AI agents?

Work with many exceptions and judgment calls, but where failures are recoverable, is the first candidate for AI agent adoption.

Suitable areas include first-tier inquiry triage, extracting conditions from internal documents, drafting quotes and applications, and detecting data defects. What they share is that "room for human final review remains." Conversely, if you choose processes with costly reversals as your first project—such as finalizing amounts, auto-sending to external parties, or committing inventory or reservations—fear of accidents will lead to tightened permissions, and it tends to end up with humans confirming everything anyway.

What are the steps for advancing business automation with AI agents?

The practical approach is to proceed in order: inventorying operations, limiting scope, preparing evaluation data, granting permissions gradually, and transferring to operations.

PhaseRough durationMain deliverablesCondition to proceed
1. Inventory2–4 weeksBusiness flow diagrams, exception listException patterns enumerated
2. Scope limiting1–2 weeksDefinition document of target operationsWhat will not be done is documented
3. Evaluation preparation2–4 weeksEvaluation dataset, pass criteriaCorrect answers can be judged manually
4. Gradual rollout4–8 weeksSuggestions only → execution with approvalHuman intervention rate falls below target
5. Operations handover2–4 weeksOperations manual, monitoring setupField staff alone can notice anomalies

Durations are only general guidelines and depend heavily on the complexity of the target operations and the number of departments involved. For work spanning departments, it's safer to assume consensus-building takes more time than the technology.

What should be done in the first 30 days?

The shortcut for the first 30 days is to spend time not on automation but on articulating the target operations and collecting evaluation data.

Specifically, proceed in this order:

  • Sit with the person responsible for the target operations and observe 5–10 actual cases, operation by operation
  • Identify "things not written in the manual but actually done"
  • Extract several dozen representative and exceptional cases from past processing histories
  • Have the person in charge judge "what the correct answer is" for each case
  • Document the criteria for items where judgments diverged, as decision standards

Step 5 is the essence. Items where even the staff disagree will inevitably diverge for the AI agent too. Delegating judgments humans haven't agreed on to a system tends to lead to endless debates about accuracy.

What should be solidified before production rollout?

Before production, decide how to roll back failures, logging, and where approvals sit, premised on keeping accountability with humans.

The minimum set is four items: (1) audit logs of all agent actions, (2) procedures to undo mistaken operations, (3) thresholds requiring human approval beyond certain conditions, and (4) a kill switch. For (3) especially, defining it in business terms—amounts, counts, target customers—lets the field adjust it themselves. Thresholds only technicians can touch are always abandoned after operations handover.

Where does AI agent business automation fail?

In most cases, it stalls not due to accuracy issues but due to ambiguous business definitions and the absence of an operational framework, preventing progress beyond PoC.

Why do projects stall at the PoC stage?

PoCs have loose success criteria, while production demands exception handling and clear accountability, so the same results can be judged in opposite ways.

At the PoC stage, "roughly correct" earns applause. In production, the first question is "who takes responsibility when something goes wrong?" A PoC that isn't designed to answer this question cannot move to production, even if its accuracy is sufficient. Conversely, if you validate approval flows and audit logs from the PoC stage onward, decision-making becomes remarkably fast. I believe the value of FDEs getting involved on the business side early on lies precisely in being able to incorporate these "production questions" into the design from the very beginning.

What happens if permissions and data are designed incorrectly?

Granting permissions too broadly makes the blast radius of incidents unpredictable, while granting them too narrowly increases human rework and erases the benefits.

A common mistake is handing an existing system's admin account directly to the agent. It works, but no one can explain what could happen, and it gets rejected in the security review. The recommended approach is to issue a dedicated account for the agent and grant only the specific operations it needs. The list of granted operations then doubles as risk documentation, making audit compliance easier as well.

On the data side, teams often forget to check whether personal or HR information is mixed into the reference scope. If you let the agent reference "documents needed for the work" wholesale, unexpected information ends up in its answers.

What skills does an FDE need for AI agent business automation?

Three core FDE skills: the ability to structure business processes, design data and permissions, and hand off operations.

What capabilities are needed on the business side?

The most valuable ability is being embedded in the field to uncover exceptions and translate decision criteria into documentation and evaluation data.

The business described in requirements documents always differs from the actual work. The FDE's job is to capture that gap in the field, not at a desk. Checks that staff perform unconsciously, operations that change only during busy seasons, exceptions that apply only to specific customers. This kind of information never surfaces in questionnaires; it comes out as casual conversation while you observe the work side by side. The difference in skill lies in whether you can convert the information gathered there directly into evaluation data entries.

What capabilities are needed on the technical side?

FDEs need evaluation design, logging design, permission design, and the implementation skills to roll back when things break.

Skill areaWhat it enablesWhat happens if it's missing
Evaluation designDefining business correctness in a verifiable formAccuracy discussions devolve into clashing opinions
Logging and observabilityMaking decision rationale and I/O traceableUnable to explain the cause of errors

I understand that Palantir-style FDEs are valued because they can cover all five of these areas single-handedly and complete the work in the field. When work is divided among specialists, problems inevitably fall into the gaps between each person's responsibilities, and that is where the most time is consumed.

How do you measure the results of AI agent business automation?

Use processing time and human intervention rate as primary metrics, running quality and incident counts alongside as guardrail metrics.

What metrics should you set?

Looking at "how many times humans touched the work" rather than the automation rate more accurately reflects whether the workload has truly lightened.

MetricDefinitionType
Processing time per itemTime from receipt to completionPrimary
Human intervention rateNumber of items corrected or approved by humans ÷ total itemsPrimary
Output quality scorePass rate against evaluation dataGuardrail
Critical incident countNumber of processes requiring reversalGuardrail
Target business coverage ratePercentage of business processes included in automation scopeSupplementary

If you chase only the automation rate, you drift toward automating only easy cases to inflate the numbers. Viewing human intervention rate alongside processing time makes visible the state of "automated, but verification takes too long."

When should you measure the effects?

Measuring effects 2-3 months after deployment, once exceptions have made a full cycle, gives numbers closer to reality than measuring immediately after launch.

Monthly, quarterly, and annual processes never appear in the first few weeks after go-live. Only by covering a period that includes fiscal closing or peak season can you see the true human intervention rate. If you use the strong early numbers as-is in internal reports, you will likely end up scrambling to explain the later decline. Practically speaking, it's safer to clearly state the measurement period and unverified exceptions in initial reports.

Speaking from my experience as an FDE, the projects where results truly take hold are not the ones with good numbers, but the ones where field staff start adjusting thresholds themselves. I consider the point at which operations pass into the hands of the field to be the true completion point of a business automation project.
