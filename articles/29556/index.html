FDE Technical Debt: Who Maintains the Custom Code After Handoff?

Original title: FDE 技术债：定制代码交接后谁来维护

FDE Times · 2026-10-20 · 6 min read

Summary

This article examines who maintains custom code after a forward deployed engineer (FDE) leaves. It argues that when no one is assigned to generalize custom work back into the product, technical debt silently accumulates and usually lands on the customer, citing a Solenis handover where a non-technical person ran the system alone. Drawing on Thomas Otter, Marty Cagan, Chris Degnan, Antoine Buteau, Valletta Software, and the PostHog handbook, it identifies five points where debt enters a project and offers practical steps: documenting scope changes, modular design, and contractual handover obligations. It closes with three questions candidates should ask in FDE interviews to distinguish product companies from disguised consultancies.

Key points

  • Building for one client only is a project, not a product.
  • Unrenegotiated scope creep is how technical debt quietly accumulates.
  • Without a generalization owner, customers inherit maintenance burdens.
  • Debt enters at five gates, from client selection to handover.
  • Candidates should ask who owns generalization and post-launch maintenance.

After handover, custom code keeps running as usual. Whether it flows back into the product tells you whether you joined a product company or a consulting company in disguise.

Key Points at a Glance

  • Thomas Otter believes that building for just one customer is doing a project, not a product; if no one is responsible for standardization, the FDE team becomes a consulting firm.
  • According to FDE Academy's analysis, technical debt accumulates when scope creep happens without renegotiation or written documentation; Valletta Software warns that custom branches can eventually make new deployments impossible.
  • When no one is designated to own generalization work, the debt usually falls on the customer: in the Solenis case, after handover, a non-technical person ran the system alone.

As Valletta Software describes, the handover day in the Solenis case ended with a perfectly ordinary step: revoking remote access. From that day on, a person with no technical background ran the system alone.

There was nothing dramatic about it, and that is exactly why it deserves a close look. Every line of custom code written by the Forward Deployed Engineer (FDE) during the engagement kept running after that day; the only thing that left was the person who understood it.

If you are an FDE, or want to become one, who pays off this debt is not just a matter for management.

It tells you whether you are joining a product company or a consulting company in disguise; it also tells you whether you will be measured by what you bring back to the product or by how many hours you spend at the customer.

The simple answer is: when no one is explicitly assigned, the customer usually pays the bill.

Project or Product: Where Is the Line?

Thomas Otter draws the line bluntly. In his view, if you focus on one customer and build only for that customer, you are doing a project, not a product. He adds that an FDE who bills the customer is working on a project.

Otter cites Marty Cagan to explain what happens when this approach scales. Cagan warns that a company made up entirely of FDEs will quickly accumulate thousands of large custom solutions. In Cagan's view, there must be a platform-side product role to distill what FDEs learn.

David Petersen of Angular Ventures, whom Otter also cites, puts it more sharply: if you don't push hard for standardization, you are probably telling yourself a nice story while quietly building a consulting company.

Chris Degnan, former Chief Revenue Officer (CRO) of Snowflake, called FDEs "packaged professional services people" on the 20VC podcast in May 2026. Let's Data Science interprets this as concern that technical debt and maintenance burdens will fall on the customer.

These views share one thing in common: no one opposes FDEs writing code for specific customers; what they oppose is custom code having no path back into the product.

Debt Isn't Manufactured; It's Quietly Absorbed

FDE Academy pinpoints the mechanism: technical debt typically appears when scope creep is quietly absorbed, with neither renegotiation nor written documentation. No one sets out to create debt; it comes from every "I'll just do it for you while I'm here."

Take a hypothetical example. In week three, the customer asks for a data sync to a legacy accounting system. The FDE finishes it in two evenings, and the customer is happy. But the work is out of scope, undocumented, and no one on the product side knows it exists.

Six months after handover, the accounting system is upgraded and the sync breaks. The person responsible for operations is like the system owner in the Solenis case: non-technical, with no access to the old team and no documentation to consult. Taken individually, every decision the FDE made was reasonable; taken together, they add up to an unsigned debt.

Now scale it up. Suppose an FDE team serves 10 customers, each with three such small branches. That's 30 pieces of code the product team has never read. Valletta warns that such custom branches may at some point make deployments for new customers impossible.

Who Owns Generalization?

Valletta sums up the problem in one sentence: no one is responsible for generalization. The article argues that the core promise of the FDE model is to push the generalizable parts back into the product, and the model only holds up if a specific person is accountable for that.

Antoine Buteau puts it another way: forward deployed companies don't avoid services; they "digest" services into reusable assets. Companies that fail to do this gradually slide into becoming consulting firms.

PostHog's FDE handbook follows the same line of thinking, describing how patterns that recur across multiple projects become reusable artifacts, skills, and product improvements, with FDEs accountable for the results.

Notably, even PostHog's handbook doesn't say who maintains the custom code after a project ends. Many organizations are clear about what should go back into the product but vague about what stays behind. What stays behind is debt.

In the end there are only three possibilities: the product team takes over the parts that can be generalized; the customer takes over the rest under a clear handover agreement; or no one takes over, in which case the customer usually bears it anyway, just without being told.

Five Gates Where Debt Enters

Debt isn't created at a single moment. Over the course of a project, it slips in through five gates, and at each gate there is something the FDE can do immediately.

What to Do at the Customer Site Tomorrow

Start with the cheapest gate: documentation. Every time the customer raises a new requirement, write one line: what the requirement is, whether it was renegotiated, and which trade-off was accepted. That single line turns invisible debt into a decision someone owns.

Next is the design gate. Before writing the sync feature from the example above, ask yourself: which part is specific to this customer, and which part would the next customer need too? The latter should go into a separate module with a clean interface, so the product team can extract it without digging through the customer's systems.

Finally, handover. Use the system owner from the Solenis case as a test: could a non-technical person with no access to the old team run this system alone? If your documentation only makes sense to the person who wrote it, the handover isn't finished.

What This Debt Means for Your Career

When interviewing for an FDE role, ask three questions directly: Who is responsible for generalizing custom work back into the product? Is there a platform-side product role to distill what FDEs learn? How is custom maintenance tracked after launch? If the answers are vague, you may be heading into a consulting company.

When reading job descriptions, watch for phrases like "feed back into the product" or "reusable artifacts," the way PostHog describes its own work. Job descriptions that only talk about customer satisfaction and time on-site are hiring people to do projects, not to build products.

On your resume, don't just list what you did for clients. Spell out which custom work you turned into reusable modules, which product features came out of projects you worked on, and which handover documents enabled clients to run the system on their own. That's what proves you were building product, not repackaged services.

All custom code will eventually be looked after by someone. The only difference is whether that person is chosen from the start, or only discovers they've become the owner on the day their access is revoked.
