Summary

A Tencent Research Institute report examines the Forward Deployed Engineer (FDE) model in the era of large language model adoption. It argues the bottleneck has shifted from model capability to organizational adoption, with over 60% of enterprise AI efforts stuck in pilot stages. FDE is framed not as a new job title or on-site outsourcing but as a frontline learning mechanism: Echo roles define the right business problems while Delta roles deliver working systems, and field experience is distilled into reusable skills, connectors, templates, and ontology assets. Using Palantir's Ontology and AIP as the archetype (82% gross margin, 70% Bootcamp conversion), the report analyzes cost-structure changes AI enables, hiring data showing FDE job postings up over 700% year-over-year on Indeed, China-specific market constraints, and a platform-partner-customer collaboration path to scale.

Key points

  • Over 60% of enterprise AI projects remain stuck at pilot stage; the gap is organizational, not model capability.
  • FDE is a frontline learning mechanism—its test is whether projects leave reusable assets, not just delivered systems.
  • Echo defines what to build; Delta builds it; both roles distill field knowledge back into products.
  • Palantir validates the model: ~82% gross margin, 70% Bootcamp conversion, falling marginal delivery costs.
  • In China, scaling requires skills, connectors, industry templates, and platform-partner-customer collaboration.

C O R E F I N D I N G S

First, the core contradiction of AI implementation is shifting from "whether models can do it" to "how organizations use it." Model capabilities are leaping forward quarter by quarter, yet more than 60% of enterprise AI initiatives remain stuck at the pilot stage. The bottleneck is not the demo, but how to integrate with systems, embed into workflows, meet compliance requirements, and produce measurable business results.

Second, the essence of FDE is not a new job title, but a paradigm reshaping of the delivery model. It recombines the capabilities of pre-sales, implementation, product, and customer success into a single frontline squad, allowing delivery and learning to happen simultaneously. The driving force is that AI has simultaneously lowered three cost thresholds—knowledge distillation, custom development, and the supply of hybrid talent—making the heavy-delivery model commercially viable on a broader scale.

Third, the essential difference between FDE and traditional on-site staffing lies in whether the project leaves behind reusable assets. Leaving the customer with only a system is outsourcing; taking back experience that cannot be reused is project-based work; converting field experience into skills, templates, test sets, or product capabilities is FDE; and only when such accumulation significantly reduces the cost of the next similar delivery does it become scalable FDE.

Fourth, AI makes engineering execution cheaper and judgment scarcer. AI coding and agent tools have lowered the cost of prototype building and system adaptation, but the ability to identify high-value scenarios, understand industries, and drive organizational adoption has become more valuable. Frontline roles are evolving from "people who write code" to "people who direct AI to accomplish business goals."

Fifth, the sustainable path is accumulation first, reuse first, and gradual customer self-service. Building everything independently from scratch is extremely costly; a more realistic path is to first accumulate high-frequency skills and system connectors, then gradually form industry templates and knowledge bases, so that every frontline engagement leaves behind reusable assets.

Sixth, three-way collaboration among platforms, partners, and customers is the consensus forming across the industry. Platforms accumulate capabilities and benchmarks, partners scale replication and provide localized services, and self-service capabilities gradually form during implementation. This collaboration gives leading customers deep service while allowing mid-market customers to access mature capabilities at lower cost.

Seventh, the future belongs to those who understand business better, understand organizations better, and are better at directing AI. Low-end implementation will be continuously squeezed, while scenario definition, customer trust, organizational drive, and industry knowledge accumulation will continue to appreciate. Customers just starting out need not worry about a "standard answer"—FDE was born for differentiated scenarios in the first place; for teams already on the journey, what deserves more attention is how to truly turn frontline experience into their own capabilities.

Over the past year, leading AI deployment practices both in China and abroad have repeatedly pointed to a common conclusion: customers do not lack large model demos; what they lack are people who can truly put AI into business processes. Enterprises have rapidly shifted from "buying models, connecting APIs, and making demos" to "building agents, integrating systems, changing processes, passing compliance checks, and hitting metrics." In this process, traditional sales engineers cannot clearly articulate model boundaries, implementation teams cannot keep up with business changes, and product teams are too far from the customer site. Frontline roles that can simultaneously understand business, models, and engineering delivery are being revalued.

This is the context behind the frequent discussion of FDE in the domestic industry. FDE stands for Forward Deployed Engineer, usually translated as "Forward Deployed Engineer," referring to a hybrid role that goes deep into the customer's business site, converts technical capabilities into runnable business outcomes, and feeds frontline experience back into product and platform capabilities. This concept is easily misunderstood at face value as another term for custom development, or as an upgraded version of on-site engineer services, but the information behind it goes far beyond that. The reason FDE is attracting attention at this stage is not that enterprises have suddenly become more willing to buy on-site services, but that AI has simultaneously lowered three cost barriers—industry knowledge distillation, custom development, and the supply of hybrid talent—making the heavy-delivery model that only a few companies could afford in the past commercially viable on a much broader scale.

1.1 From Software as a Service to Model as a Service: A Paradigm Shift in Delivery Models

The core logic of the traditional Software as a Service (SaaS) model is product standardization and scaling. After finding Product-Market Fit (PMF), a single unified product serves as many customers as possible, with marginal cost approaching zero. This logic suits relatively standardized scenarios such as Customer Relationship Management (CRM), Human Resources (HR), finance, and collaborative office work, but it struggles to cover the large volume of non-standard, cross-system, process-heavy, and organizationally constrained work in the real enterprise world.

The problem in the AI agent era is that what enterprises want is not a model, nor a general-purpose API, but a process that can get things done. Getting an agent to actually run often involves enterprise data integration, permission systems, process adaptation, compliance auditing, exception handling, and human fallback. After purchasing a large model, customers quickly discover that demos are easy but productionization is hard; building a Q&A chatbot is easy, but embedding it into real approval, customer service, marketing, content production, manufacturing quality inspection, or educational administration processes is the real challenge.

This changes the delivery logic of enterprise software. In the past, standardized SaaS avoided deep customization as much as possible, while traditional industry software, though customizable, involved long cycles, thin margins, and poor reusability. AI agents sit between the two: they need to fit the customer's business closely, but AI tools have also lowered the costs of prototyping, process orchestration, and system adaptation. Customization scenarios that were once "not worthwhile" are becoming economically viable again with AI assistance.

More importantly, the business value of AI agents does not end with one-time delivery. Once a scenario is truly up and running, it generates continuous model calls, tool calls, and business process executions, and platform revenue shifts from "selling seats" to "sustained usage." This means FDE investment should not be viewed merely as an upfront delivery cost. By helping customers identify high-value scenarios, complete the first runnable closed loop, and drive sustained adoption by business departments, it is essentially amplifying customer lifetime value (LTV).

1.2 Why AI Projects Must Start with a Runnable Demo

In traditional IT projects, solution documents, prototypes, and reference cases are often enough to build customer confidence. AI projects are different: model performance, boundary conditions, and business benefits all involve uncertainty. Customers can hardly judge from a PPT alone whether a system can run stably under their own data, processes, and organizational constraints. Therefore, AI projects inherently require moving earlier into the runnable demo stage, validating feasibility with real problems, real data, or near-real business processes.

The purpose of a demo is not to replace the production system, but to expose risks early: whether the model understands business language, whether the data is usable, whether interfaces can be connected, whether users are willing to use it, and whether there is room for improvement in business metrics. A healthy FDE model does not treat the demo as the end, but as the starting point for production validation and experience accumulation.

1.3 From 80% to 99% Is Where FDE Fights

Enterprise AI deployment follows an "80/95/99" rule: an agent covering 80% of use cases can be built quickly, reaching 95% is already difficult, and reaching 99% often requires FDEs, dedicated platforms, and tens of thousands of real conversation data points. This rule explains why many customers are excited at the demo stage and disappointed at the production stage. A demo only needs to cover the most common paths, while a production system must face the long tail, exceptions, compliance, and accountability boundaries.

From 0 to 80%, a general-purpose large model plus simple prompts can cover it, and an experienced engineer can even build a "pretty good" prototype in a single day. This is why many people mistakenly believe AI deployment is easy. The real difficulty is going from 80% to 95%: the model makes errors in specific scenarios, industry terminology is misunderstood, edge cases cannot be handled, and this requires extensive prompt optimization, rule supplementation, data annotation, and test set construction.

1.4 The Scissors Gap Between AI Capability and Adoption Speed

The hardest part is going from 95% to 99%. In enterprise scenarios, even a 1% error rate can cause serious consequences. A single wrong customer service answer can become a brand incident; errors in finance, healthcare, or government scenarios can trigger compliance risks; the more systems are integrated, the higher the complexity of permissions, auditing, rollback, and human takeover. AI testing also differs from traditional software testing: traditional testing emphasizes deterministic inputs and outputs, while AI testing must deal with probabilistic systems and long-tail distributions, often encountering situations where "fixing A breaks B."

Therefore, FDEs do not do the work from 0 to 80%, but the work from 80% to 99%. Customers are willing to pay for FDEs not because they cannot see a demo, but because there is a long gap between demo and production.

Why is the ceiling 99% rather than 100%? LLMs are inherently probabilistic systems with open output spaces, so unforeseeable edge cases will always exist; the marginal cost of pushing from 99% to 99.9% grows exponentially while marginal returns diminish; and in high-risk scenarios, that 1% should not be covered by AI but should be safeguarded through human review and rollback mechanisms. 99% is not a technical compromise, but the optimal solution of engineering economics: AI covers the scalable 99%, while humans guard the 1% that cannot be automated.

This rule also reveals the long-term nature of AI deployment. Enterprises adopting AI must pass through three stages—individual productivity, organizational transformation, and business restructuring—each taking years, and each requiring FDE involvement in different ways. In the individual productivity stage, the FDE's work resembles frontline operational bootcamps, getting business teams to actually start using the tools rather than dispersing after watching a demo. In the organizational transformation stage, FDEs need to provide senior leadership with cross-industry organizational change experience—how to adjust performance evaluation systems, how to break down "human API"-style intermediary layers—work that is more like management consulting and strategic advocacy. The business restructuring stage is the familiar system building, distilling tacit industry knowledge into ontologies and bringing large models into the core of the business. The three stages progress in sequence, with no shortcuts.

A notable trend is that as AI deployment deepens, almost all customer-facing roles in software companies are converging toward FDE. Sales engineers need to build prototypes that run through customer scenarios, architects need to understand the boundaries and probabilistic nature of large models, and customer success teams need to continuously drive the journey from pilot to production. AI capabilities are advancing far faster than enterprise adoption, creating a deployment gap between the two. The opportunity for FDEs lies in closing this gap: translating what models can actually do into business processes that customer organizations can adopt, operate, and measure.

Statistics from multiple institutions show that over 70% of enterprises have already experimented with generative AI in at least one function, yet more than 60% remain stuck at the pilot or experimentation stage. The bottleneck is not model capability—the "80/95/99" pattern described in the previous section makes this clear. What is truly stuck is the last 20% of productionization engineering. Meanwhile, model capabilities continue to iterate rapidly, and every upgrade widens the distance between "what technology can do" and "what organizations can actually use."

This means the product bottleneck is shifting from "tools" to "people." As the barrier to building applications with natural language falls, customers' biggest problem becomes "not knowing what to have AI do." The core value of the FDE changes accordingly: not simply helping customers build systems, but helping them think through what to do, why to do it, to what extent, and how to continuously generate business results afterward.

1.5 2025–2026: Explosive Signals in the Hiring Market

Clear signals have already emerged in overseas markets. FDE job postings on the Indeed platform grew from 643 in April 2025 to 5,330 in April 2026, an annual growth rate exceeding 700%; a16z's industry observations put the increase between 800% and 1000%. Bob McGrew, former Palantir executive and former OpenAI Chief Research Officer, mentioned in a public talk that more than a hundred YC-backed startups are already hiring FDEs—a number that was nearly zero three years ago. Even more noteworthy is the business impact: data cited by Bob McGrew shows that AI companies adopting the FDE model grew annual recurring revenue (ARR) three times faster than peers on average in their first 18 months, while maintaining customer success teams only one-third the size. This shows that FDE is not about piling on headcount, but about exchanging high-density frontline investment for more efficient customer success and faster revenue growth.

In terms of organizational structure, FDE is taking shape along three paths. The first is the internal team model, whose advantage is the shortest product feedback loop, but whose scaling is constrained by talent development cycles. The second is the ecosystem amplification model, which expands coverage through certified partners and methodology transfer, but makes delivery quality control harder. The third is the independent entity model, combining model companies with capital and consulting channels, which can scale quickly, but whose biggest risk is a broken feedback loop between on-site customer problems and the parent company's R&D roadmap.

OpenAI and Anthropic announced the formation of deployment companies on the same day, sending a strong signal. In May 2026, OpenAI announced the creation of the OpenAI Deployment Company and its intent to acquire Tomoro—an applied AI consulting and engineering firm—which, upon completion, would bring in roughly 150 experienced FDEs and deployment experts. On the same day, Anthropic also announced the formation of a dedicated deployment team, using a similar mechanism to push Claude into the business processes of industry customers. Both companies realized almost simultaneously: no matter how strong model capabilities are, they will not walk into customer sites on their own. OpenAI officially emphasized that the FDE's job is not simply integrating models, but working with business leaders, operations staff, and frontline teams to identify high-value AI scenarios, restructure organizational infrastructure and key workflows around AI, and turn the gains into sustainably operating systems.

1.6 Characteristics of the Domestic Market

The Chinese market has its own uniqueness and cannot simply copy Silicon Valley experience. China is not without precursors to the FDE: cloud vendors' Solutions Architects and delivery engineers, as well as AI pre-sales, AI application engineers, and industry deployment engineers at AI startups, have all already taken on partially similar functions. The difference is that these roles have historically revolved more around project delivery, resource coordination, and private deployment, rarely forming a closed loop of "on-site experience feeding back into products and platforms."

In the US, many large enterprises have accumulated years of established processes, data, role divisions, and system governance; systems such as Enterprise Resource Planning (ERP), CRM, and Information Technology Service Management (ITSM) are relatively mature, and data has a strong standardization foundation. AI there is more like installing an intelligent brain onto a precision machine. In China, by contrast, many enterprises operate in a "conversation-driven" manner: requirements are often raised in unstructured ways, collaboration relies on instant messaging, decision chains depend heavily on key individuals, and processes are often written not into systems but into tacit understanding and personal relationships.

On one hand, this difference increases the difficulty of deploying FDEs; on the other, it creates an opportunity to skip stages. In the past, software required filling out forms, drawing process diagrams, and configuring rules first; AI's entry point is natural language—and natural language is precisely the collaboration method such organizations know best. Many customers may not be able to map out their processes clearly before implementing a system, and are instead better suited to "building the road while driving it," with an FDE accompanying them along the way.

The core challenge for FDE in China remains economics. High-value Silicon Valley customers can support annual contracts in the six to seven figure dollar range, while many domestic projects remain in the range of 100,000 to 1,000,000 RMB. If FDE relies entirely on high-cost expert investment, the math does not work; if it is handed entirely to low-cost outsourcing, it loses product feedback and experience accumulation. Therefore, FDE in China must solve two problems: first, use AI and platform capabilities to reduce delivery costs; second, use Skills, connectors, industry templates, and partner ecosystems to increase reuse rates. Whoever solves this problem first may gain a structural advantage in China's enterprise services market.

All delivery-heavy ToB models face the same fundamental contradiction: the deeper you go on-site, the more you depend on individual experience; the larger the scale, the thinner the margins. Traditional system integration solves problems by piling on people, and in the end it becomes a labor business. FDE is singled out for discussion because it attempts to complete learning while delivering—converting the industry knowledge, system interfaces, and process rules exposed in a single customer project into reusable platform capabilities. If this transformation holds, heavy delivery can achieve economies of scale; if it does not, FDE is ultimately just outsourcing under a different name.

2.1 Misleading Name and the Real Difference

The name FDE is easily misleading. "Forward" is easily understood as people being stationed long-term at customer sites, and "deployed" as installing, connecting, and debugging systems. Put together, domestic ToB teams naturally map it back onto the old framework of software projects over the past decade: pre-sales presents solutions, delivery takes over systems, engineers modify processes per requirements, and teams move on after project acceptance. As a result, FDE is easily misunderstood as "higher-end on-site outsourcing" or "AI-savvy implementation engineers."

This understanding is not entirely wrong—FDEs do need to be close to customers, and they do need to deploy things into production environments. But it only describes the shell of the FDE. The "forward" in FDE is not about physical location, but about being on the frontline of the customer's real business problems; "deployed" is not about installing systems, but about deploying models, data, processes, permissions, organizational accountability, and business metrics into sustainably operating results.

The real difference lies not in whether one is on-site, but in whether a learning loop forms after the project ends: first, redefining the customer's problem—translating what the customer says they need into a business problem truly worth solving. Second, delivering verifiable results—not just functional usability, but proving answer rates,

Conversion rates, processing time, or human efficiency genuinely improve. Third, bring field experience back—whether the processes, interfaces, testing methods, and compliance requirements that recur across the industry can be consolidated into reusable platform capabilities. Without this third step, the FDE role degenerates into high-end outsourcing.

In practice, the FDE role often corresponds to a composite position of "solutions, implementation, and product feedback," but its emphasis varies across business models: mature AI companies care more about product deployment and accumulating industry knowledge, startups may use customer sites to explore product-market fit, and model companies place greater weight on token consumption and scenario feedback. A practical criterion is this: whatever customers can be trained to do themselves should not depend long-term on FDEs; what truly requires FDEs are scenarios where three kinds of complexity intertwine and which can ultimately be consolidated into product.

2.2 Echo and Delta: Organizing Cross-Boundary Capabilities

The FDE model is difficult because it compresses capabilities that were once scattered across multiple roles into a small team. This paper breaks them into two types of capabilities for analysis: Echo is responsible for "what should be done," and Delta is responsible for "how to build it."

Echo's core is understanding the customer's business—where the money comes from, where the process gets stuck, and at which point AI can unlock value. You cannot simply listen to what the customer says they want; you must also judge whether what they describe is actually the problem itself. Often, the customer asks for a feature when what they really need is to restructure a process; the customer wants to buy a tool when what they truly lack is an organizational mechanism for putting it to use. Echo essentially handles the input side of frontline learning: bringing field industry knowledge and business rules back to the organization.

Delta's core is rapidly building prototypes, integrating systems, and tuning models, iterating continuously in real environments. AI coding has lowered Delta's technical barrier, but it has not eliminated the need for engineering judgment—rough prototypes are acceptable, but there must be a quality baseline; rapid iteration is acceptable, but security, permissions, and auditability cannot be ignored. In domestic organizations, Echo and Delta are often scattered across different teams. Industry architects understand the customer but may not be able to deliver; delivery engineers can implement but may not be able to define problems; product teams understand the platform but are too far from the customer's site. The value of the FDE model lies precisely in reconnecting these capabilities through organizational mechanisms. In practice, Echo and Delta are not necessarily two positions but two roles. In early-stage teams, one person may hold both capabilities, but as project scale grows, a natural division usually emerges—those whose discoveries contribute more to feedback loops gradually take on the Echo role, while those focused on customer delivery take on the Delta role, and the two should be evaluated and incentivized differently.

2.3 Bidirectional Distillation: How Delivery Becomes Learning

If Echo and Delta explain what capabilities FDEs need, bidirectional distillation explains how those capabilities become organizational assets.

The essence of FDE can be summarized as bidirectional distillation. The first layer is distillation toward the customer: converting the business experience, process rules, and tacit knowledge scattered in the minds of customer employees into knowledge bases, rule systems, workflows, SOPs, and application capabilities that AI can invoke. The customer thereby gains more stable and more replicable business execution capability.

The second layer is distillation toward the vendor itself: consolidating the industry knowledge, system interfaces, testing methods, scenario templates, and failure cases gained from KA projects into product capabilities, industry templates, internal tools, and platform components. The vendor thereby improves delivery efficiency and service depth for similar scenarios.

The shared vehicle for both layers of distillation is the ontology layer. The theory behind ontologies is not complicated—entities, relationships, and attributes; in essence, it is a structured business knowledge graph. Within a customer's systems, the same business object may have different field names, different codes, and different process representations. If AI faces these messy systems directly, it struggles to reliably understand business meaning. What the ontology layer must do is translate these system differences into a unified business language: this is a customer, this is an order, this is an approval, this is a risk event; what relationships exist between them, what actions they can trigger, and under what conditions human intervention is required. An ontology summarized from one customer can potentially be replicated to another customer in the same industry—not by using customer data to train models, but by consolidating industry knowledge into reusable abstractions. These concepts belong to no single enterprise; they belong to a shared business domain.

Only when the two layers of distillation are stacked together can the old model—heavy, expensive, and heavily dependent on elite teams—potentially become a path adoptable by more industries.

2.4 Delayed Returns and Economies of Scale: Why the Underlying Logic Holds

The most fundamental logic behind the ontology consolidation model lies in the combination of delayed returns and economies of scale.

Delayed returns mean that the investment in consolidating the ontology is not recovered within the current project. The first project, or even the first few projects, will cost more than pure project-based delivery—because beyond delivering the customer's system, you must additionally complete knowledge abstraction, structuring, and validation. This is hard for many organizations to accept: on the first project, you are not even sure whether there will be a second one, and the effort spent on consolidation may go to waste.

Economies of scale mean that if the vertical domain has enough similar customers, the ontology's value will be released at scale. After completing one benchmark enterprise, dozens or even hundreds of similar enterprises can reuse the same ontology, significantly reducing delivery costs and shortening delivery cycles. In a typical vertical domain, after seven or eight customers, the ontology basically stabilizes, and subsequent updates shrink markedly.

Combined, the FDE model is essentially a business logic of investing first and recovering later. It does not suit all scenarios—if a domain has only one or two customers, the cost-effectiveness of ontology consolidation is low; if the domain is large enough and has enough customers, the upfront investment yields long-term compounding returns. This also explains why platform-type enterprises are better suited to undertake ontology building—they have the strategic patience for deep industry engagement, and enough customers to amortize the upfront costs.

Three prerequisites determine whether the touchstone can be passed: whether the customer is worth deep cultivation (real demand, business participation), whether the project can consolidate assets (processes have commonality, data is replicable), and whether the company has mechanisms for reuse (head-customer experience can be passed down to mid- and lower-tier customers). If none of the three is met, it is high-end outsourcing; only when all three are met can it become a distillation machine for industry knowledge.

Two core questions serve as supplementary judgment: whether the product boundary moves toward the product side each quarter, and whether on-site investment for mature customers continues to decline. Bob McGrew's core measurement principle, emphasized in public interviews, can serve as a supplement: measure two correct things—the value of delivered outcomes (even if not yet fully capturable), and product leverage (on-site investment per unit of value should decline). If the first customer requires 10 person-months and the tenth similar customer still requires 10 person-months, the model has not worked.

3.2 ONTOLOGY: FROM TECHNICAL ARCHITECTURE TO BUSINESS SEMANTICS

Bob McGrew gave a detailed account of the birth of the Ontology in public talks. When the team initially built databases for intelligence customers, the instinctive approach was to create one table for "people" and one table for "funds"—but they quickly found that every time they switched to a new customer or agency, the data structure had to be rebuilt, making it impossible to generalize.

The solution was to raise the level of abstraction: the underlying database keeps only four types of primitives—objects, properties, media, and links between objects. The specific semantics of each customer scenario are encoded by the Ontology: annotating a certain entity as "person," annotating a certain entity as "ship," annotating a set of relationships as "fund flows." Product managers and FDEs no longer think about "how to build a specific table structure for this customer," but rather "how to model within the Ontology framework so that functionality can be reused across customers." Bob McGrew recalled that Palantir did not hire product managers for a long time—thinking at this level of abstraction is extremely challenging for traditional product managers.

In plain terms, the ontology layer is a software semantic layer oriented toward business execution. Take industries such as finance, education, and media as examples: different customers' system fields may be completely different, but behind them there are always stable concepts: in finance scenarios, accounts, customers, transactions, credit, approvals, and risk events; in education scenarios, students, courses, assignments, lesson plans, evaluations, and academic affairs processes; in media scenarios, topics, materials, manuscripts, review, publishing, distribution, and operational data. What the ontology layer must do is abstract these stable concepts and describe their relationships and state changes—customer systems may call them "customer number," "user ID," or "subject code," and the ontology layer needs to know that they correspond to the same class of objects in business semantics; when a customer says "if review fails, return for revision," the ontology layer needs to know which roles, states, permissions, and notification actions are involved. Without this semantic abstraction, AI can only stay at Q&A and summarization; with this semantic abstraction, AI can possibly enter process execution.

4.1 AI Changes Three Cost Structures of the FDE Model

First, the cost of distilling industry knowledge has fallen. In the past, even when on-site customer teams identified real pain points, they could often only write requirement documents and queue up for the product team to evaluate, design, develop, and launch. Between field experience and product capability lay a long feedback chain. AI coding has changed this path—frontline FDEs can now write scripts, package tools, build prototypes, generate test cases, and organize documentation directly on site, moving forward much of the work that previously had to wait for back-office teams. Problems discovered on site can more quickly become verifiable product improvements or early Skill prototypes.

Second, the cost of custom development has fallen. Small-scale scenario-specific development, rapid prototyping, and customer-side process adaptation can now achieve value validation with smaller teams. What used to be "understand the business, write requirements, schedule development, test and launch" can now become "understand the business, abstract semantics, generate prototypes, validate on site."

Third, the cost of supplying hybrid talent has fallen. AI tools compensate for some engineering, documentation, and product skills, enabling more frontline personnel to work across business and technical boundaries. Lower engineering execution thresholds mean Delta-layer costs are compressed, but Echo-layer capabilities—judging scenario value, understanding industry logic, and driving organizational adoption—do not become easier to acquire as a result.

Bob McGrew made a key point in his YC interview: "Pain is signal." Rewrites, rough prototypes, and friction between field teams and product are features of learning, not bugs to be solved by process. If an FDE team experiences no pain during delivery, it means they are not exploring anything new, just repeating known solutions.

4.4 The Divide Between "Adding AI" and AI NATIVE

The opportunities AI brings are reflected not only in lower costs but also in a shift in customer perception. Most traditional enterprises are still at the "adding AI" stage: they keep their existing organizational structure and business processes and simply hope to use AI to save labor in isolated steps. Typical requests are adding an AI assistant to customer service, adding a template to report generation, or adding a Q&A entry point to a knowledge base. Such projects are easy to start but have a low ceiling, because the processes and organization have not truly changed.

A few AI native enterprises, by contrast, ask the reverse question: if AI capability already exists, how should this business have been designed in the first place? They are willing to adjust processes, roles, and organizational structures, treating AI as part of the business architecture rather than a bolt-on tool. Such customers are harder to serve but also more valuable. For an FDE, serving "adding AI" customers is more like local renovation, with limited willingness to pay and limited room for expansion; serving AI native customers is more like co-designing new processes—early exploration is more complex, but it is easier to produce benchmark cases and high deal sizes. The realistic strategy is often to first serve a small number of leading customers willing to change, establish benchmarks, and then leverage the industry's follow-the-leader mentality to expand to more traditional customers.

4.5 Product Evolution Path: From SKILL to Ontology

For China, a more realistic strategy is layered investment. Any industry can start with terminology standardization and scenario inventories, organizing customers' common questions, business objects, and high-frequency tasks; once a sufficient number of similar customers is reached, high-frequency Skills can be combined into industry templates; only when customer count, deal size, and reuse rate are all high enough is it worth systematically building a complete ontology layer. This path is slow, but it matches the realities of deal sizes and organizational capabilities in China.

It should be emphasized that not building a "complete ontology layer" does not mean no knowledge accumulation. Skills, industry templates, and lightweight semantic layers are themselves progressive forms of the ontology layer—they solve the same problem (avoiding starting from scratch on every delivery), differing only in depth of abstraction and coverage. The "vicious cycle of a missing ontology layer" discussed in Chapter 7 refers to the situation where even this progressive layer has not been established: the team wipes the slate clean after finishing one project and completely restarts on the next, with experience never accumulating. That is the most fatal scenario.

In this sense, the ontology layer is not a grand model designed a priori, but an organizational asset that grows out of real projects, one by one. First come Skills within projects, then cross-project templates, and only finally a systematic ontology layer. The order cannot be reversed.

It is worth adding that the value of Skills goes beyond current delivery. Observing the iteration of leading models over the past two years, many tasks that once required complex Skills can now be handled directly by new model versions—showing that models are continuously digesting expert-level operational experience. For platform providers, even if some Skills are eventually covered by native model capabilities, accumulating Skills today still has dual value: in the short term, directly serving customers; in the long term, providing industry corpora and training signals for model capability improvement. FDEs are not just helping customers solve immediate problems; they are also continuously injecting industry nourishment into the entire AI capability ecosystem.

5.1 The Pairing Logic of FDE and FDPM

Multiple cloud vendor solutions teams and agent platform practices point to the same problem: it is difficult for one person to simultaneously handle industry judgment, customer communication, solution design, rapid prototyping, and product feedback. A more mature team structure breaks the assumption that "one FDE does everything," splitting technical implementation and customer engagement into complementary roles. The FDE is closer to the owner of technical implementation and product improvement, with core work including AI testing, prompt development, rule system construction, product code modification, and system integration. The FDPM (Forward Deployed Product Manager) leans more toward customer communication, requirements alignment, and test case design, with core work of understanding the customer's business logic, translating requirements into executable technical solutions, and managing project progress and customer expectations.

The essence of this division of labor is separating "technical momentum" from "organizational momentum," then having the two collaborate around the same customer outcome.

5.2 The Real Working State of Frontline FDEs

FDEs' time allocation differs markedly from that of traditional software engineers. A large share of time goes to customer engagement, discovery interviews, on-site deployment, and design reviews; writing or reviewing code remains important but is no longer the sole core; internal coordination, product feedback, and technical research also take up a considerable proportion. An FDE is not "a pre-sales engineer who writes more code," nor "a developer who occasionally meets customers," but a hybrid role that lives long-term between the customer site and the product team.

Several misconceptions need correcting. First, FDE does not mean long-term on-site presence—what is bought is the outcome, not time: with clear deliverables and KPIs, FDEs can work at their own pace and go on-site when necessary to resolve high-density communication issues. Second, FDE does not mean all-around warrior—the core capabilities are judgment and drive; for anything they cannot do, they can find team members, tools, or external resources. Third, most FDEs do not build brand-new products from scratch; they deploy existing products and platforms into new customer environments, doing extensive adaptation.

The true evaluation criteria are: whether the customer's business outcomes improve, whether project experience accumulates, whether product capabilities thicken, and whether similar deliveries get faster.

A seemingly simple agent may simultaneously call speech recognition, content generation, safety review, intent recognition, sentiment analysis, knowledge retrieval, and business databases. Model selection, parameter tuning, version management, and exception fallback are themselves complex engineering.

The second step is collaborative building. Business experts and the FDE work together to turn scenarios into agent, workflow, or application prototypes, instead of technical staff shouldering it all alone. The third step is using tools to ensure quality: generating test sets from real chat logs, historical tickets, and business data, and running automated evals and A/B testing. The fourth step is validating value with business results: comparing the AI group against the manual group at every stage, using metrics such as reach rate, response rate, conversion rate, and per-capita efficiency to convince the customer to keep investing.

The core principle is selling outcomes, not software. Customers don't pay for "using AI"; they pay for real business improvement. The FDE's task is to translate AI capabilities into business results that customers can perceive, verify, and continuously scale.

7.1 The Vicious Cycle of Missing Ontology

Building the ontology layer is the necessary path to scaling FDE, yet it is also the thing most easily postponed. When there are many projects and tight staffing, teams prioritize delivering for customers, and asset accumulation always comes last; without accumulation, the next project starts from zero again; low efficiency makes people more exhausted, leaving even less time for accumulation. Once this cycle forms, FDE loses its scaling narrative.

The "missing ontology layer" discussed here does not refer to the complete heavy ontology discussed in Chapter 4 not yet being built—that requires the preconditions of customer scale and deal size. It refers to a more fundamental problem: the team has not even done the most basic Skill accumulation, industry templates, or terminology standardization, and every delivery starts completely from zero. This degree of deficiency is more fatal than ordinary delivery problems, because it directly affects the business model. Without this layer of accumulation, what management sees is that more people mean more revenue and fewer people mean less revenue; what investors see is non-scalability; what customers see is that a few particular engineers are very capable, not that the platform itself has value.

The broken feedback loop is the concrete manifestation of this cycle. Interviews found that after frontline Delta staff complete customer projects, there is no incentive to feed back the new business entities and new processes discovered on-site to the backend. Performance reviews only look at whether the project was delivered, not at contributions to the ontology; accumulated assets need to be revised, released, and trained for frontline use—far slower than just using AI coding to get things done on the customer site. If this loop remains broken for a long time, the model's long-term advantages cannot materialize.

Breaking this cycle requires management to explicitly treat the accumulation of Skills, templates, and ontology assets as output equally important as project delivery; it requires dedicated personnel responsible for asset curation and platformization, rather than everyone being pulled away by projects; it also requires using AI to lower the barrier to accumulation, converting work that once required writing large amounts of code into Prompts, process descriptions, test sets, and platform configurations.

7.2 Organizational Placement Determines FDE's Fate

Where FDE is placed within the organization directly affects what it becomes. Placed in the sales organization, the advantage is closeness to customers, fast response, and strong deal-closing motivation, but the risk is being treated as a deal-closing freebie—doing free demos in exchange for contracts—with weak motivation for accumulation. Placed in the delivery organization, the advantage is standardized project management and controllable delivery quality, but FDE can easily become ossified into a project-based team. Placed in the product organization is most favorable for knowledge accumulation and productization, but carries the risk of becoming distant from frontline customers.

A more reasonable approach is a matrix structure. Administratively, FDE should maintain strong connections with product and platform capabilities to ensure frontline experience flows back; operationally, it should collaborate with sales, customer success, and industry teams to stay close to customers; in terms of performance evaluation, it should be measured both on customer value and on knowledge output.

7.3 LAND Is Easy, EXPAND Is Hard

The commercial sustainability of the FDE model depends on customer renewals and expansion. Initial implementation is relatively easy; what is hard is subsequent expansion: after the first scenario is completed, the customer does not know what to do next; in the early days after an application goes live, novelty drives usage, but if it is not embedded into workflows within a few months, usage declines.

The key to improving Expand is planting expansion seeds during the Land phase. The first project must not only prove single-point capability; it must also demonstrate what else can be done along the same business chain; after go-live, usage data and business metrics must be used to prove value; FDE should expand from the IT department to business departments, bringing in the real users and value beneficiaries.

7.4 The Structural Challenge of Profit Margins

The FDE model's cost structure is inherently heavy. High-salary talent, travel, on-site communication, and project management all drive up costs. There are three paths to improving gross margin: first, productization—packaging the Skills, industry templates, and standard solutions accumulated by FDE into reusable products, developed once and sold many times; second, leverage—amplifying the productivity of a small number of high-end talent through Echo and Delta division of labor, AI tool amplification, and partner-system replication; third, value-based pricing—gradually shifting from per-person-day billing to charging based on business outcomes, usage, or customer gains.

However, value-based pricing faces structural obstacles in China: customers are accustomed to buyout models or acceptance by feature points, procurement processes require explicit deliverable lists and acceptance criteria, and "improved business outcomes" is difficult to write into contract terms. This means that improving the margins of domestic FDE cannot rely solely on value-based pricing; the more realistic path is productized reuse—lowering the cost of each delivery rather than raising the price of a single engagement.

There are two technically viable paths. Path One: the case-driven approach. Start not from theory but from the business flow of a specific industry—for example, the complete lifecycle of an order from creation to completion in an ERP scenario, and how the corresponding ontology is accumulated—using examples to help the team quickly grasp the essence of ontology modeling.

Path Two: reverse engineering. Extract ontology concepts in reverse from existing design documents, database schemas, or code. AI has clear value here—it can quickly draft entities and relationships from code. But extraction quality depends on two prerequisites: first, the code itself must be well designed, otherwise it's "garbage in, garbage out"; second, even with high-quality code, the reverse-extracted results still require costly human confirmation—the ontology is the ground truth, the single correct business language, and AI cannot replace the final confirmation.

Existing practice for reference: during a period when there was no concept of FDEs or ontologies, the WeChat Pay team already used "system use cases" to accumulate the rules, logic, and scenarios of complex business domains in a dedicated structured representation—this was essentially an early form of ontology accumulation. The difficulty is not technical; it lies in having deep understanding of the rules, risk controls, and scenarios of the business domain, and then converting that understanding into structured documents. Tools can lower the cost of expression, but the understanding itself cannot be avoided.

FUTURE OUTLOOK

Capability layer | Change trend | Human value

When agents can write code, connect tools, and orchestrate processes, FDEs face a sharp question: if AI can complete more and more deployment work on its own, do enterprises still need human FDEs? Looking at the task structure, what is more likely to happen is not extinction but differentiation—technical execution is continuously compressed, while problem definition, customer trust, industry knowledge accumulation, and ecosystem enablement become more important.

10.1 The Stronger Agents Get, the More FDEs Will Differentiate

There are three common judgments about the future of FDEs. The first holds that FDEs will disappear—once models are strong enough, customers can directly use natural language to have agents complete all tasks. This judgment underestimates the complexity of enterprise environments: models can understand text, but not necessarily organizational power dynamics, departmental interests, budget logic, compliance responsibilities, and real usage habits. The second holds that FDEs will exist for the long term—enterprise needs will never be standardized. This judgment captures the complexity but underestimates AI's compression of the technical execution layer.

What is more likely is the third: FDE differentiation. The technical execution layer is heavily compressed by AI, and the FDE's focus shifts from "forward deployment" to "frontline problem definer" and "organizational transformation consultant." Future FDEs may not write more code, but they must understand business better, understand organizations better, understand how to direct agent fleets to complete tasks, and be able to verify quality, manage risk, and drive customer adoption.

The conclusion is clear: FDEs will not disappear, but low-end implementation will be replaced by tools, and high-end Echo capabilities will become scarcer. Those who can define problems, build trust, and accumulate industry knowledge will not be replaced by agents; those who can only build systems to spec will be continuously squeezed by platforms and tools.

10.3 Implications for Enterprise Customers

Whether enterprise customers need an FDE depends on whether AI has already entered business processes. If only basic translation, summarization, or general Q&A is needed, standard SaaS will suffice; if there is already a strong internal technical team, building in-house is also an option. But if a company has bought an AI platform yet usage is low, if IT has built systems that business departments don't use, or if there are clear business scenarios but no idea where to start, an FDE has value.

When working with an FDE, the customer side must involve business owners, not just interface through the IT department. If AI implementation stays at the technical interface level, it will hardly enter daily business operations. In addition, budget for an adjustment period—a production-grade agent often takes weeks or even months to complete scenario calibration, data cleaning, permission configuration, user training, and organizational acceptance. The earlier customers open their data, processes, and business metrics to the FDE, the higher the success rate.

10.4 Recommendations for Cloud Vendors and Platform Providers

Platform providers have three possible positioning options: platform only, platform plus in-house FDEs, or platform plus partner ecosystem. The more sensible path is "0 to 1 in-house, 1 to N partners."

a16z's analysis further refines this rhythm: FDE embedded deployments should be treated as "scaffolding" rather than "the house"—time-limited deployments (setting a 90-day sprint-to-launch goal), headcount/ARR ratio caps (forcing productization), and quarterly recycling of custom code (converting custom solutions built on-site into reusable configurations and templates for the next quarter). If a team remains in a state where "the scaffolding is never taken down," it is effectively disguised on-site outsourcing.

The partner ecosystem also needs a POC risk-sharing mechanism. Early-stage validation of AI projects often involves uncertainty; if trial-and-error costs are borne unilaterally by partners over the long term, the ecosystem will tend to take on only low-risk projects and struggle to enter innovative scenarios. Platform providers can reduce the upfront trial-and-error costs of high-potential projects through industry templates, technical support, joint go-to-market funds, or staged guarantee mechanisms.

Platform-type organizations should also establish a unified resource dispatch mechanism. Once a customer requirement comes in, first determine whether it belongs to executive-level awareness, light business consulting, tool training, or project implementation, then dispatch it to the research institute, product teams, training systems, or ecosystem partners accordingly. A unified entry point, unified pricing, and unified feedback mechanisms are prerequisites for turning FDE capability from individual experience into organizational capability.

10.5 Implications for AI Startups

For AI startups, doing FDE work is essentially trading heavy delivery for industry data and insight. Upfront investment is large and payment collection is slow, but if they can get the ontology layer and reuse mechanisms working in a vertical industry, the moat is high. Startups should not attempt "general-purpose AI for all industries"; they are better suited to niche segments with complex business processes, high industry knowledge density, and insufficient coverage by leading platforms.

Compared with large platforms, startups' advantages lie not in headcount scale but in industry depth, feedback speed, and closeness to customers. They can go deep into customer sites, track process changes over the long term, and build industry know-how more granular than standard products. But they must guard against cash flow pressure—not every customer can become unlimited customization; reuse must be a focus from the very first customer.

10.6 Implications for Individual Practitioners

FDE suits people with above-average technical skills who are willing to face customers, enjoy learning new industries, and can tolerate uncertainty. It does not suit those who only want to write code in stability, avoid communication, or pursue purely technical depth.

Future career paths will become more diverse: at the junior level, learn the platform, learn communication, and follow projects; at the mid-level, run projects independently and build industry knowledge; at the senior level, Echo takes on defining problems, leading teams, and building benchmarks. Beyond that, paths may diverge into entrepreneurship, industry product leadership, AI business leadership, or independent industry consulting.

The most important thing at the current stage: learn the industry beyond technology. For every project completed, leave behind demonstrable cases and quantified results—how much time the customer saved, how much efficiency improved. People who can truly accumulate industry knowledge will have more long-term value than those who merely know how to use AI tools.

10.7 The Ultimate Form of the FDE

Models will keep getting stronger, but models still need to know "how this industry operates, how this customer makes decisions, how this process gets implemented, and how this outcome is measured." The people who continuously supplement this knowledge, validate it, and turn it into organizational assets are the ultimate form of the future FDE.
