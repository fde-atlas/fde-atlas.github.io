Alex Xu's System Design Interview Book Review: How to Use It for FDE Interviews

Original title: Alex Xu 系统设计面试书评：FDE 面试怎么用

FDE Times · 2026-10-08 · 4 min read

Summary

This review examines Alex Xu's System Design Interview from an FDE candidate's perspective. All 30 chapters cover product and infrastructure design but none cover ML or LLM systems. The book's real value is its four-step framework, which mirrors an FDE's first client working session. The author shows, via an insurance company's internal document Q&A design, that back-of-the-envelope estimation (under 1 QPS average, ~3.5 QPS peak) can redirect the whole design, and that AI questions require adding data, model, and serving layers with explicit accuracy-versus-latency tradeoffs.

Key points

  • All 30 chapters omit ML and LLM system design entirely.
  • The four-step framework works as a client discovery session, not a memorized script.
  • Estimation first: the insurance example peaks at only ~3.5 questions per second.
  • For AI questions, add data, model, and serving layers with explicit tradeoffs.
  • Suggested two-week reading order starts with the framework and estimation chapters.

Key Takeaways

  • The book has 30 chapters, from "Scale From Zero To Millions Of Users" to "Stock Exchange", and not a single one covers ML or LLM systems.
  • What's worth keeping is the four-step framework: clarify the scope; sketch an initial design and ask for feedback; dive deep selectively; and never claim your design is flawless when wrapping up.
  • For AI-heavy FDE interview questions, you need to add the data, model, and service layers, and explain how you'd trade off accuracy against latency.

The official ByteByteGo course page lists the 30 chapters of System Design Interview, from "Scale From Zero To Millions Of Users" to "Stock Exchange". These chapters teach you to design news feeds, chat systems, YouTube, and payment systems, but not one of the 30 chapters touches on ML or LLM systems.

Forward Deployed Engineer (FDE) candidates should know about this gap from the start, especially when AI questions come up in interviews. This book covers product and infrastructure problems; ML systems are not among them.

The book is still worth reading. For FDEs, however, its main value lies not in the YouTube or Stock Exchange designs, but in the short chapter on how to answer questions. That approach closely mirrors how FDEs run their first working session with a client.

What does the book teach, and who has praised it?

The chapters fall into two groups. The fundamentals cover back-of-the-envelope estimation, rate limiters, consistent hashing, and key-value stores. The second group consists of product-style design questions, such as news feeds, chat, YouTube, and payments.

Sandor Dargo independently reviewed the book on DevReads in February 2022. He wrote that the book offers a systematic approach to help readers prepare for and handle these kinds of questions. In his view, experienced engineers can also benefit, because the problems are presented from the interviewer's perspective.

Note that this is just one positive review, and it points out no shortcomings.

If you want to review the distributed systems concepts these chapters rely on, Swimm's overview is a good supplementary resource. It restates the CAP theorem: a distributed system can only provide two of the three guarantees at once.

The article also covers Sagas: a sequence of local transactions where each step has a corresponding compensating action. You'll encounter this pattern in the payments and digital wallet chapters. Swimm is a company blog promoting its own product, so the article is only useful for review.

The four-step framework is a client discovery session

Step 1 requires understanding the problem and determining the design scope. The book's advice is straightforward: slow down, think carefully, and clarify requirements and assumptions by asking questions. Step 2 is to sketch a first high-level design and ask for feedback, treating the interview as a collaboration.

Step 3 is to dive into the design while avoiding unnecessary detail. Step 4 is the wrap-up, where you should never say your design is perfect and has nothing to improve.

Now compare these four steps to real work. On-site with a client, an FDE asks questions before writing anything, shows sketches to stakeholders early to align on direction, digs deep only into the highest-risk parts, and ends by listing open items.

So the sensible way to prepare for an FDE interview is to treat this framework as a brief client discovery session, not a script to memorize.

One number can change the entire design

Consider this question: "Design an internal document Q&A assistant for an insurance company with 2,000 employees." Someone who has memorized all 30 chapters might jump straight to sharding and multi-level caching. Do Step 1 first, and use the estimation chapter.

Suppose each employee asks 10 questions per day. That's 20,000 questions over an 8-hour workday, i.e. 28,800 seconds: less than one per second. Even if the peak is five times the average, that's only about 3.5 questions per second.

At that load, scale is not the hard part. The difficulty shifts to answer accuracy and the wait time for the model.

That one-minute calculation shows the interviewer that you're solving the client's problem, not the problem you're used to solving. It's also a skill worth putting on your resume. A single line describing an experience of "estimating load first, then deciding not to build something" carries more weight than five lines listing your tech stack.

What the book is missing: the three layers of AI systems

To fill the AI gap, pair this book with the AI system design guide from the System Design Handbook. It likewise starts by clarifying the problem, but spells out the specific questions to ask: data types, latency, and scale. This part aligns with Alex Xu's Step 1.

The difference lies in the high-level design. The guide recommends splitting the architecture into data, model, and service layers, and explaining how you trade off scalability against cost, and accuracy against latency.

Returning to the insurance company question, draw these three layers. The data layer is the storage for policies, terms, and internal process documents. The questions to ask: what format are the documents in, how often are they updated, and which employees can view which documents.

The model layer finds relevant passages and drafts answers based on them. Step 3 should go deep here, because the harm from getting a claims term wrong far outweighs waiting a few extra seconds.

As for the service layer, the earlier calculation already gives the answer: about 3.5 questions per second at peak, no sharding or multi-level caching needed. Given the low load, a good answer would accept a slightly slower model in exchange for accuracy, then use Step 4 to explicitly flag which parts still need to be measured with the client's real data.

In what order should you read it?

If you only have two weeks, read the framework chapter first, then the estimation chapter. Next, read the rate limiter, consistent hashing, and key-value store chapters to build the fundamentals. Among the product chapters, chat and payments are closer to enterprise systems than YouTube.

Read the payments chapter together with the Saga material to understand why compensating actions are needed. Then add the data, model, and service layers, and re-solve any two problems in the book as AI versions, just like the insurance company example above.

After you close the book, three habits are worth keeping: ask before you draw, seek feedback early, and never claim your design is perfect. They'll help you pass the interview, and you should bring them to client sites from day one.
