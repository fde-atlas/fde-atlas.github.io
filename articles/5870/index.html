A subset of the tech talent supply crunch to watch out for

Washington Technology · 2026-09-01 · 2 min read

Summary

A Washington Technology article covers a study by executive search firm Christian & Timbers on the shortage of forward deployed engineers (FDEs), customer-facing engineers who embed in client environments to deploy software. Of roughly 17,000 FDEs in the U.S., only about 2,000 have consistently turned enterprise AI deployments into documented ROI; the study calls this group "Elite." Co-founder JC Christian says frontier AI labs depend on enterprise adoption, but the elite pool is too small to meet demand. His proposed solution is pairing junior FDEs with elite engineers on large-scale problems to build expertise. Aerospace and defense contractors face the same shortage, needing customer-facing, relationship-driven leaders to drive government adoption.

Key points

  • Only about 2,000 of 17,000 U.S. FDEs have delivered documented AI ROI.

Full text

Gettyimages.com / Lemon Tm

Forward-deployed engineers are the ones who consistently translate artificial intelligence deployments into documented results and not enough of them are senior enough, a new study says.

Amid the rush across all industries to fill out their technology staff, the lack of forward-deployed engineers is emerging as a talent crunch-within-the-crunch that companies are grappling with.

FDEs are customer-facing software engineers that embed directly inside a client’s environment to develop and deploy software within that enterprise, often times working alongside the client’s employees for a set duration. This is in essence a hybrid position with technical and business responsibilities that is crucial for taking artificial intelligence from the lab to workflows and producing measurable results.

Only 2,000 out of a total 17,000 FDEs currently working in the U.S. have turned enterprise AI into meaningful return-on-investment, according to a new study from the tech-focused executive search firm Christian & Timbers.

The study labels that group of 2,000 as “Elite,” meaning they have consistently translated AI deployments into documented results.

JC Christian, a co-founder of the firm, told Washington Technology that the main frontier AI labs are aware of situation thanks to their networks of companies acting as service providers to aid deployment efforts.

The health and wealth of frontier AI labs depends on how many enterprise organizations adopt their technology, but Christian the latter group needs to see value to continue spending at the quantities that they are.

A group of 2,000 people caps out quickly for any decently-sized services company, as Christian pointed out.

With that backdrop in mind, how does that small group become larger in order to meet the global demand for these AI tools and see meaningful ROI?

One main answer lies in recruiting junior forward-deployed engineers to work under those at the elite-level, according to Christian.

“You need to turn them into elite FDEs by working with them on problems at scale, that they have conviction that they can win on,” Christian said. “Once you have that, then you’re getting into a proliferation of knowledge, know-how, technology and business processes that will lead to positive results.”

As the study strongly suggests, defense and government contractors are facing the exact same quandary of having a limited pool of elite-level FDEs.

Aerospace-and-defense companies are looking for leaders on their teams to sitting in roles that are customer-facing but not strictly sales-focused, Christian said.

“Within A&D, I think it’s even more of an elite category, where these people might not be implementing a lot of these services, but it’s going to be very much relationship-driving to drive consumption of whatever service they’re ultimately offering, and to drive stronger relationships into the government bodies that they’re trying to ultimately have as buyers,” Christian said.
