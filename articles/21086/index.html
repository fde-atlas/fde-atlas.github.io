Differences Between FDE and Solution Engineer | Comparing with Sales Engineer, SA, and Client-Side PM

Summary

This Japanese article distinguishes FDEs from five adjacent roles—sales engineers, solutions engineers, solutions architects, customer engineers, and outsourced development PMs—using a single criterion: where the person's responsibility ends. Sales engineers stop at contract signing, solutions engineers at design and PoC, while FDEs stay responsible until their work runs in the customer's production environment. The author argues FDEs are engineers who also talk to customers, measured by whether customer operations actually change, not by deal size. The title originated at Palantir and spread to AI companies like OpenAI and Anthropic because AI products require customization around customer data. The article closes with three checks for reading job postings: whether coding is listed as a main duty, whether metrics reference bookings or adoption, and whether on-site implementation is stated.

Key points

  • Responsibility endpoint is the key axis separating these roles.
  • FDEs own outcomes through production deployment and adoption.
  • Success is measured by changed customer operations, not bookings.
  • The title came from Palantir and spread through AI companies.
  • Three job-posting checks: coding duties, metrics, on-site implementation.

When reading FDE job postings, you often run into questions like "How is this different from a solution engineer?" or "Isn't this just pre-sales in the end?" There are many similar job titles, and even the same title can mean different things at different companies, so judging by name alone leads to a mismatch after joining. This article compares FDE with five adjacent roles, using a single axis: where their responsibility ends.

The Bottom Line Up Front

  • The difference in one sentence is "where the responsibility ends." A sales engineer's responsibility ends at contract signing, a solution engineer's at design and PoC, and an FDE's extends to the point where the solution keeps running in the customer's production environment.
  • An FDE is not a sales-leaning technical role but an engineer who also works directly with customers. The dividing lines are the proportion of time spent writing code, and the fact that success is measured not by "deals closed" but by "whether the customer's business actually changed."
  • Since titles vary by company, when reading a job posting, judge it by three points: whether the main duties include writing code, whether the evaluation metrics are deal value or uptime/adoption, and whether implementation at the customer site is described as the primary job.

The Essence of the Difference: "Where the Responsibility Ends"

The clearest axis for distinguishing adjacent roles is neither the depth of expertise nor the amount of customer interaction, but at what point in the project the person's responsibility ends. A sales engineer's role is done once the contract is signed; a solution engineer's is done once the technical design and validation are complete. An FDE carries it further, staying responsible until the solution actually runs in the customer's environment, gets used, and produces results.

This difference directly affects how you spend your days. If your responsibility ends at the contract, most of your time goes to demos, proposal materials, and handling technical objections. If it extends to production deployment, your work centers on cleaning up the customer's messy data, writing connections to existing systems, and rebuilding things to fit the customer's operations. Neither is "better"—the day-to-day work is simply entirely different.

The fact that the term FDE originated at Palantir and later spread to AI companies like OpenAI and Anthropic is also explained by this scope of responsibility. AI products don't deliver value just by being deployed; they must be tailored to the customer's data and business processes. In other words, there was a need for a role dedicated to "someone who finishes building on-site after the sale."

Differences from Sales Engineers and Pre-sales

This pair is the most commonly confused combination, and the one most likely to cause dissatisfaction after joining. A sales engineer is part of the sales organization, working alongside account executives to remove technical obstacles and close deals. Their technical skill is used "to sell."

FDEs also talk directly with customers, but for a different purpose. An FDE talks with customers to dig out the requirements for what they are about to build, and to figure out why what they built isn't being used in the field. They don't talk in order to sell—they talk in order to build.

A practical way to tell them apart is to check the evaluation metrics. If the goals include deal value or pipeline numbers, the role is closer to a sales engineer; if they include the number of deployed use cases or customer usage volume, it's closer to an FDE. If you ask in an interview, "How are goals set for this role?" you'll usually get a fairly candid answer.

Differences from Solution Engineers and Solution Architects

Among adjacent roles, "solution engineer" is the title with the widest range of meanings. At some companies it effectively means pre-sales; at others it includes post-deployment implementation. In other words, the title alone tells you nothing, so you must always read through to the description of the actual duties.

A solution architect is responsible for designing how something should be built and advising on it, but generally not for seeing the build through to completion. A cloud vendor's SA is the classic example: they review the customer's configuration, recommend an architecture, and the implementation is done by the customer or another partner. An FDE is the role that does that "someone else implements it" part themselves.

This difference also shows up in the preparation required. An SA needs broad product knowledge and a deep repertoire of design patterns, while an FDE additionally needs the ability to handle messy data and to ship something working in a short timeframe. For details, see the article on skills.

Differences from PMs in Contracted Development and SEs at System Integrators

Looking only at the surface—going to the customer's site and building things—an FDE looks a lot like contracted development or SIer work. In fact, many people aiming to become FDEs in Japan come from this field. However, there are two decisive differences.

One is that the foundation of what you build is your own company's product. In contracted development you build from scratch every time, but an FDE builds only the customer-specific parts on top of their company's AI models or platform. For this reason, the job often includes feeding common patterns discovered at customer sites back into the product, and job postings frequently contain phrases like "identify repeatable patterns and bring them back to the product team."

The other is starting on the assumption that requirements are not yet settled. In contracted development, work begins only after the requirements specification and estimates are finalized, but with FDE projects, it is not uncommon to start from a state where even "what to build to achieve results" is unknown. What is valued is not the ability to stick to contracted requirements, but the ability to create the requirements themselves.

Three checkpoints to distinguish them from job postings

Since job titles are unreliable, you have no choice but to judge from the body of the job posting. In practice, looking at the following three points will let you distinguish them with considerable accuracy.

If a posting is vague on all three points, the company may not have clearly defined the role internally. This does not mean it is a bad company, but the role tends to change easily after joining, so it is safe to ask in the interview, "What would need to be accomplished within six months of joining for this to be considered a success?" You can check which companies are actually hiring FDEs in Japan via the job listings on FDE board.

  • Does the main duties section explicitly mention implementation or coding? If it only contains words like demos, proposals, and support, it leans toward pre-sales
  • What is written as evaluation criteria or goals? If words like orders or sales meetings appear, it is a sales organization; if words like operation, utilization, or adoption appear, it is a delivery organization
  • How is work at the customer site described? If it mentions "implementation in the customer's environment" or "on-site building," it is close to the reality of an FDE; if it only says "technical support" or "technical consultation," the involvement is relatively shallow

Frequently Asked Questions

  • Are FDE and Solution Engineer ultimately just different names for the same thing?
  • At some companies they may be effectively the same, but generally their scope of responsibility differs. A Solution Engineer is responsible up to technical design and verification, while an FDE is responsible up to keeping things running in the customer's production environment. Judge not by the job title, but by the duties and evaluation criteria in the job posting.
  • Is moving from Sales Engineer to FDE realistic?
  • Your ability to talk with customers will be directly useful, so you have the foundation. However, the selection process will assess your implementation skills, so you need to be able to explain code you have recently written and how it was used in production. How to prepare is covered in the interview article.
  • How is a career change from SE at an SIer to FDE viewed?
  • Experience in understanding a customer's business and translating it into requirements is highly valued. On the other hand, the deciding factors are whether you have experience creating requirements yourself from an unsettled state and whether you can show the substance of implementation you actually did hands-on. Be aware that if management tasks still make up a high proportion of your work, your implementation skills may be questioned during selection.
  • How should I decide which role suits me?
  • If you "enjoy talking with customers but ultimately want to solve problems with your own code," FDE is a fit; if "the appeal lies mainly in selling with technology as your weapon," Sales Engineer; and if "you want to contribute through the correctness of your design," Solution Architect is likely the closest. Reading the actual duties of job postings side by side will give you a feel for it. The job listings on FDE board compile FDE positions you can apply for in Japan.
