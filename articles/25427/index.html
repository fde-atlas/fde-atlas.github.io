Cisco FDE role: SSE skills and escalation evidence

FDE Times · 2026-10-24 · 6 min read

Summary

A Cisco job listing, posted under Duo Security for its Secure Services Edge business, shows that not every FDE role is about AI. It requires 7+ years in networking or security, 5+ years of identity and SSO integration, ownership of large accounts, follow-the-sun escalation duty, and up to 50% travel. The author argues the real deliverable is reproducible escalation evidence—logs, packet captures, redacted configs, repro steps—with verified facts separated from unverified hypotheses, and recurring faults converted into concrete roadmap requests with clear scope and test criteria. Preparation advice includes building a small lab with IPsec, TLS decryption and SAML, deliberately breaking things, and practicing escalation write-ups.

Key points

  • Cisco's SSE FDE role demands 7+ years in networking or security; it is not entry-level.
  • Required skills are protocols like DNS, IPsec, TLS and SAML, not programming languages.
  • The core output is reproducible escalations separating verified facts from unverified guesses.
  • Recurring faults across three or more accounts should become concrete roadmap requests.
  • Preparation: build a lab, break each layer deliberately, and practice writing escalations.

In brief

  • Cisco's Secure Services Edge FDE role wants 7+ years in networking or security and direct customer ownership; it is not an entry-level job
  • The core skills are DNS, routing, IPsec, TLS, SAML, web proxies and cloud networking, plus 5+ years of identity and SSO integration
  • The real output of the role is escalations with reproducible evidence and concrete roadmap requests, even when AI is used to read logs

Cisco wants a Forward Deployed Engineer who will travel up to 50% of the time, has spent seven-plus years in networking or security, and can hand engineering evidence it can reproduce. The listing, for the Secure Services Edge (SSE) business and posted under the Duo Security name, is a useful corrective to the idea that every FDE job is about AI.

Take a large customer that has just moved all of its company web traffic onto a cloud proxy. The next morning, staff at one branch are stuck in an endless login loop on an internal application. The customer’s IT team blames your product; your own engineers want evidence before they accept anything.

Standing between those two sides is the job Cisco describes. The listing asks the FDE to take full ownership of complex escalations, keep customer expectations realistic and deliver precise, reproducible technical evidence.

The FDE label is why this matters. Palantir popularized the role, and many people hear “FDE” and think of AI or data. Cisco’s listing shows a very different variant: heavy on networking, security and incident discipline.

If you are aiming for infrastructure FDE roles, reading this listing properly gives you a training plan for the next few years.

Three jobs inside one listing

The first is customer ownership. The listing says the FDE will lead the technical relationship with an assigned set of large or strategic SSE accounts. You have your own list of customers, and when something breaks, you are the first person they call.

The second is handling escalations under a follow-the-sun rotation across time zones. The third is spelled out in the listing itself: problems that customers hit repeatedly must be fed back into the product and engineering roadmap as concrete requests.

All three come with demanding conditions. The listing calls for up to 50% travel, within the US and internationally, to customer offices and engineering centers. Ask about the travel and on-call schedules in the first interview round.

The experience bar is stated plainly: 7+ years in networking or security, with direct customer ownership.

Networks first, code second

The technical list covers DNS, routing, IPsec tunnels, TLS, SAML, SWG/web proxies and cloud networking, plus 5+ years of identity and SSO integration (SAML/OIDC, SCIM) in enterprise environments.

In an SSE product, each of these is a place where things break. DNS and routing decide whether traffic passes through the service at all, IPsec connects branches to the cloud, TLS is where content inspection happens, and SAML handles identity.

Every requirement on that list is a protocol or a piece of infrastructure; none is a programming language. In a role like this, the most useful code tends to be small scripts that diagnose and reproduce faults, like this one:

import socket, ssl

DOMAINS = ["app.example.com", "login.example.com"]

for host in DOMAINS:
    try:
        ip = socket.gethostbyname(host)
        ctx = ssl.create_default_context()
        with socket.create_connection((host, 443), timeout=5) as s:
            with ctx.wrap_socket(s, server_hostname=host) as t:
                issuer = dict(x[0] for x in t.getpeercert()["issuer"])
        print(host, ip, "issuer:", issuer.get("organizationName"))
    except socket.gaierror as e:
        print(host, "DNS error:", e)
    except ssl.SSLError as e:
        print(host, "TLS error:", e)
    except OSError as e:
        print(host, "connection error:", e)

If the certificate issuer is the proxy’s CA instead of a public CA, you know the traffic is being decrypted for inspection. That detail alone is enough to split a login incident into two lines of investigation.

What does a clean escalation look like?

In practice, the listing’s demand for “precise, reproducible technical evidence” means logs, packet captures, redacted configuration and steps to reproduce the fault. Going back to the login loop above, a clean escalation might look like this:

Title: SAML loop at branch B after enabling TLS inspection
Scope: branch B only, some users affected; branch A normal
Started: immediately after a configuration change (time and change ticket attached)
Verified:
  - Client-side pcap: the IdP site's certificate is issued by the proxy's CA
  - Disabling inspection for the IdP domain in the lab -> fault gone (repro steps attached)
Unverified:
  - Hypothesis that the session cookie is stripped at the proxy (AI suggestion from logs, not confirmed by pcap)
Attachments: proxy logs, pcap, redacted configuration
Request: confirm expected behavior for the IdP domain; customer is using a temporary exception

The “Unverified” section is the one many people skip, and it matters most. The listing allows AI for log analysis but demands sound judgment, and no unverified conclusion should reach anyone, customer or engineering team.

From the third ticket to a roadmap request

Suppose a third account on your list hits exactly the same fault. At that point, fixing it customer by customer is no longer enough. The listing asks FDEs to turn recurring patterns like this into concrete requests, and a complaint differs from a concrete request in three ways:

Vague feedback

  • Customers often get login errors with the proxy
  • No numbers
  • No definition of done

Concrete request

  • 3 accounts hit a SAML loop when the IdP domain is inspected
  • Repro steps and related escalations attached
  • Proposal: automatically exclude declared IdP domains; done when the lab no longer loops

Product teams can schedule work that has a clear scope and a clear way to test it. PostHog’s handbook describes the same principle at another company: when a customer is blocked by something only the product team can fix, the FDE hands it to the owning team, and some teams prefer an async write-up while others want you at their standup.

So before sending anything, ask the receiving team which format it prefers, then define the scope and write the test yourself.

How to prepare

Start with a small lab: two cloud virtual machines joined by an IPsec tunnel, a self-hosted proxy with TLS decryption, and an application that signs in with SAML. Break each layer on purpose, capture packets, then write an escalation for the fault you just caused. That is how you practice the reproducible evidence the listing asks for.

Next comes the CV. The listing stresses “direct customer ownership”, so avoid a generic line such as “supported enterprise customers”. State how many accounts you owned, one escalation you led from start to finish, and one product request you pushed onto the roadmap.

This end-to-end ownership is not unique to Cisco. Nexus IT Group’s guide to the FDE profession says the work is not done when the architecture sounds right, only when the system runs in the customer’s environment.

If you have three or four years of experience, treat this listing as a map for the next few years, and choose work that puts your name in front of customers.

Mistakes that sink strong candidates

One mistake is seeing “FDE” and assuming it is an AI role. The skills in this listing revolve around things like TLS and SAML; AI appears only as a tool for reading logs, conditional on sound judgment.

Another is telling incident stories as hero stories, without showing how you separated what was verified from what was guesswork.

The third is smaller but just as damaging: sending raw configuration with the customer’s details still in it. For a security product, leaking customer data in the escalation itself destroys your credibility.

A 45-minute exercise

Run the script above against two domains you log into often, once on your home network and once with a VPN or corporate proxy switched on. If the certificate issuer changes, treat it as an “incident” and write an escalation using the template above: scope, evidence, repro steps and at least one line under “Unverified”.

Then give the write-up to a colleague. If they understand what happened without asking you a single question, you have written the kind of escalation this role needs.

In this role, your greatest asset is trust: engineers open your escalation and know at once that they will not have to redo the work from scratch.
