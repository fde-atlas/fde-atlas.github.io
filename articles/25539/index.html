FDEs: Hire for the Last Mile, Build for the Long Run

MMC · 2026-09-08 · 30 min read

Summary

MMC analyses 2,589 LinkedIn profiles covering 2,880 FDE stints and interviews hiring managers from ElevenLabs, Helsing, Sana and Anthropic to produce a founder's guide to building a Forward Deployed Engineering function. Median FDE tenure is estimated at 2.3 years, and 81% of leavers move into a different role type, so founders should hire with progression paths and bench strength in mind. The piece explains what distinguishes FDEs from consultants and solutions engineers, offers four tests for whether the model fits (unit economics, customisation needs, customer heterogeneity, company stage), and identifies three failure modes: no product thesis, non-compounding work, and customers who never become self-sufficient.

Key points

  • Median FDE stint is about 2.3 years; 81% change role type after.
  • AI has shifted the bottleneck to deployment, driving 800%+ job listing growth.
  • Four tests for needing FDEs: economics, customisation, customer variance, stage.
  • Failure modes: no product thesis, work that doesn't compound, dependent customers.
  • Hiring should screen for agency, grit, product judgement and commercial fluency.

If you’re considering building a Forward Deployed Engineering (FDE) function within your company, plan around one uncomfortable fact: the people you hire may be unusually valuable (and unlikely to stay in the role for long).

We traced the careers of 2,589 people across 2,880 FDE stints. Using an analysis that accounts for both people who have left and those still in-role, we estimate a median FDE stint of 2.3 years. And when FDEs move on, they tend to move meaningfully: among the complete career journeys we could trace, 81% transitioned into a different kind of role from the one they held before FDE.

For founders, these statistics have significant implications for organisational design. If the typical FDE may spend only a couple of years in-role, you need to hire with future progression in mind, create credible paths into product, engineering and leadership, and build enough bench strength that the function doesn’t break every time a strong performer moves on. The same breadth and ambition that makes great FDEs so useful to customers can also make them difficult to contain within a narrowly defined role.

That’s why we’ve written a data-backed guide for founders considering FDEs by answering four key questions:

  • How do you know if you genuinely need FDEs?
  • How do you go about hiring best-in-class talent, and what actually predicts success in the role?
  • How do you structure and scale your FDE motion as your company grows?
  • How do you develop and retain people when median tenure is about 2 years?

At MMC we’ve backed ex-Palantir founders building Frontier Health and Nexcade, and we’re always looking for the next high-agency technical generalist ready to be catapulted into company-building. If you’re a founder thinking about the FDE motion, or an FDE thinking about becoming a founder, reach out to Advika or Joanna – we’d love to chat.

What is an FDE and why should you care? The hottest role in tech, explained.

In simple terms, a forward deployed engineer is an engineer you embed inside a customer’s environment to build the product with them rather than for them.

Part engineer, part product thinker, part commercial operator and, on a particularly eventful Tuesday, part therapist. They sit between frontier technology and the inconvenient realities of getting anyone to adopt it – which explains both why everyone wants them and why they’re so hard to hire.
The role itself isn’t new. Palantir coined FDE in the early 2010s to sell complex products into regulated industries where nothing worked out of the box, but AI has pushed it mainstream. Job listings grew more than 800% in nine months, labs like OpenAI, Anthropic and ElevenLabs led the charge, and OpenAI, AWS, EY/Microsoft and Anthropic/DXC are collectively investing billions in forward-deployed delivery. Today, it’s hard to find a Series A enterprise AI company that isn’t hiring one.

So what changed? Counterintuitively, AI has made deployment harder.

Coding tools have made building faster and cheaper than ever, so the bottleneck has shifted to getting AI into production. SaaS mostly sold access to a standardised product; AI is increasingly sold on the outcome it delivers. But you can’t hand an enterprise an agent, wish them luck, and wait for the productivity gains to roll in. Someone still has to identify the right problem, wire it into messy systems, handle security and controls, drive adoption, and work out what should become repeatable product. That’s where the FDE comes in.

What does an FDE do?

An FDE’s job is to close the gap between what your product does and what your customer actually needs. The word doing the most work there is ‘needs’. Customers describe what they want, and what they ask for often addresses a symptom rather than the underlying problem. That’s why FDEs are embedded: onsite, observing existing workflows, understanding the workarounds people have built, and getting close enough to see what’s actually going on.

But diagnosis is only half of it. FDEs ship. They prototype, adapt the product and iterate with the customer through tight feedback loops, so the person who uncovers the problem is the one who solves it. Requirements don’t get passed through three layers before anything ships. Time-to-value collapses, and when customers see value quickly, adoption follows.

That’s also what separates them from the alternatives. Consultants diagnose and recommend but don’t build. Solutions engineers show how the existing product solves a problem, rather than building what’s missing. Implementation engineers get you live, but what they learn stops with them.

Because the point isn’t to build one customer a permanent bespoke solution. It’s to work backwards from their problem and ask: what should we build or change so that the next 10 customers can benefit from this too? In that sense, FDEs sit at a valuable intersection between customer delivery and product development.

That closeness to the customer creates commercial benefits as well. Because FDEs understand the customer’s workflows and problems so deeply, they often uncover entirely new ways the product can create value – which can drive greater adoption, expansion, cross-sell and retention.

That’s ultimately the idea behind the FDE model: leverage. You solve an important problem for one customer, turn what you learn into a better product, and make it easier to create value for many more.

“The forward-deployed model is much closer to what a founder does: working directly with customers, understanding what they need, and then building solutions that solve those problems in a way that can scale to future customers.”

– Katerina Pascoulis, Forward Deployed Product at Fin

Do you actually need FDEs?

A lot of companies don’t. For the majority of early stage startups, building a forward-deployed organisation is overkill, and the failure mode isn’t a wasted hire – it’s becoming a consultancy without noticing. The model makes sense when the economics, the product and the nature of the customer problem all justify putting highly technical people directly in front of customers.

Before hiring anyone, these are the questions you should ask yourself.

Question 1: do the economics support it?
FDEs make sense when average contract values are high and there’s real expansion potential inside the account. ‘High’ has a floor: the ACV needs to cover more than one FDE, because custom work concentrated in a single head is a liability. As Kevin Bai wrote, “When you’re doing custom work for a customer, the last thing you want is a single point of failure where one person holds all the context, goes on vacation, and you’re screwed.”

That makes the traditional embedded FDE model difficult to justify for most mid-market or commercial customers (at least after product-market fit is found), and almost certainly the wrong model for PLG or freemium businesses. Where ACVs are smaller but some forward-deployed work is still valuable, a pooled model (with FDEs shared across multiple accounts) can make much more sense.

Question 2: does the product need customising?
The test is a Goldilocks one. There needs to be some bespoke work involved. If every customer can essentially use the same implementation, you probably don’t need FDEs, and a solutions engineer or implementation team may be perfectly sufficient. But if everything has to be built from scratch for every new customer, you don’t have the right foundations for an FDE model either. Ideally, you should have a platform with shared primitives that FDEs can assemble, extend and improve rather than repeatedly reinventing the same underlying capabilities.

Question 3: how different are your customers from each other?
If different customer segments have very different workflows, technical environments or requirements, FDEs can bridge the gap between a common product platform and the specific outcome each customer is trying to achieve. A useful way to think about it is across two dimensions:

“If the product is largely ready out of the box, you may just need a solutions engineer or architect to integrate it into the customer’s environment; if it requires significant customisation, you need true forward-deployed engineers building alongside the customer. Then there’s the users: if only a handful of IT admins will use the product, a solutions engineering profile may be enough; if you’re trying to activate thousands of employees across an enterprise, you need an FDE engagement team to drive adoption. Where you sit on those two dimensions determines the team you need.”– Elina Mäkelä, Product Manager at Sana

Question 4: are you at the right stage?
There are arguably two points at which the model is particularly useful. The first is very early, when you’re still on the path to product-market fit. Your product isn’t fully developed yet, you’re still discovering what customers actually need, and having engineers work directly alongside customers dramatically compresses the distance between learning and building.

The caveat at that stage is that this usually isn’t a hire at all:

“I don’t think many startups need FDE roles. The founder is the original forward-deployed engineer. Your engineers are the original forward-deployed engineers. You still need to talk to your prospects and customers.”– Oskar McDermott, Senior Solutions Engineer at incident.io

The second is much later, once you have a mature and largely standardised platform but sophisticated customers still require meaningful customisation around it (à la Palantir). The common thread is the same, where meaningful technical work needs to happen alongside customer engagement. If there isn’t much customisation or product development happening at that interface, you probably don’t need FDEs.

And even once you’ve decided you need the model, not every customer deserves the same level of forward-deployed attention. It’s worth looking at three dimensions:

  • Revenue and long-term revenue potential.
  • Product learning (how much you’ll learn about your product and deployment model).
  • Strategic reference value (whether the customer will help open doors to future opportunities).

An FDE deployment doesn’t necessarily need to optimise for immediate revenue. A customer can be worth pursuing because of what they teach you, the capabilities they force you to build, or the credibility they give you with the rest of the market.

Where the model breaks

FDEs paper over problems well enough that you can run the model for a while without noticing it isn’t working. Three failures account for most of it.

No product thesis. One founder put it plainly: if you’re building a pile of custom software and nobody can explain what any of it has to do with anything else, FDEs are a band-aid, not a GTM model. You need core beliefs about what the product is for and what value you’re actually delivering. Without them there’s no test for which customer work deserves to become product.

The work doesn’t compound. FDEs can become incredibly expensive (and can accidentally turn your company into a consulting shop). Individual deployments can have really poor economics: teams duplicate work, build parallel tooling, or pour resources into pilots that go nowhere. But customer-level margins can be misleading, because some of that work is effectively R&D happening inside a customer deployment, and poor standalone economics (within reason) can be justified if the work produces valuable product learning or reusable capabilities. That means building shared primitives, and making sure recurring customer needs actually reach the roadmap.

The customer never becomes self-sufficient. Several people we spoke to described the same problem: the moment the FDEs came off an account, the account stalled. If adoption only holds while your engineers are in the room, you haven’t given the customer a capability – you’ve been renting them one.

How should you think about hiring and retaining FDEs?

We analysed 2,589 LinkedIn profiles, with 2,880 FDE stints across them (where 1 stint is an FDE role and subsequent promotions within the FDE function in the same company e.g. FDE and Technical Lead in Palantir will be part of the same stint). Of these, 1,166 stints are completed (with precise start and end points). Based on that, we uncovered:

Education. FDEs tend to have strongly technical educational backgrounds: 53% studied Computer Science, followed by various engineering disciplines like electrical or mechanical engineering (13%), Data Science/AI (8%), and Information Systems/IT (4%). Bachelor’s degrees are most common (50%), while 43% hold a Master’s, MBA or doctorate.

Pre-FDE work experience. There isn’t a standard route into the role. The median FDE had 4 years of prior experience, but hiring strategies vary dramatically by company. At Palantir (n=936), the median was just 2.0 years, reflecting a preference for recent graduates and early-career talent. At Databricks (n=164) and Salesforce (n=167), it was 10.8 and 13.7 years respectively.

That suggests two distinct hiring models are emerging: an early-career apprenticeship model, exemplified by Palantir, and a senior lateral-hiring model drawing from experienced solutions engineers, technical-commercial leaders and managers. Across the sample overall, software engineering was the most common immediate feeder role, followed by Data Science/ML/AI and Solutions and Customer Engineering. Internships have proven to be a significant contributor given this is a core hiring strategy for Palantir.

Which raises the obvious question for anyone building an FDE function from scratch: if the pipeline is that varied, what are you actually screening for?

What should you look for when hiring FDEs?

FDEs are an unusual hiring profile because you’re looking for much more than a strong engineer. They still need genuine technical depth (in many companies they should basically clear the same technical bar as a software engineer) but the best FDEs combine that with product judgement, business intuition and exceptional customer skills. They are, in many ways, mini-founders.

As part of our research, we interviewed over a dozen FDE hiring managers from ElevenLabs, Helsing, Sana, Anthropic and others – a handful of characteristics stood out as things they all look for consistently:

  • High agency in ambiguity. Strong FDEs take ownership of messy problems without waiting for perfect requirements or constant direction. They optimise for getting to a valuable customer outcome quickly, even when the first version is imperfect.
  • Grit: We loved how Rachel Finegold, Founder and CEO at Frontier Health described it, “You need somebody who’s willing to operate at extremely high velocity, and to break through the natural barriers that come up in the course of trying to do something hard – like deploying software into complex operational environments, or in Palantir’s case, into very high-stakes operational environments, whether in security, health, or elsewhere.”
  • Product judgement. Building the wrong thing brilliantly is still failure. Great FDEs can work backwards from what a customer asks for to the underlying problem, while constantly asking what can be generalised for the next customer. They demonstrate intellectual humility (i.e. can wipe their previous assumptions about how something works, and think from first principles).
  • Technical and commercial fluency. They need to move comfortably between engineers, product teams, users and senior customer stakeholders – explaining technical constraints, understanding business value and handling difficult conversations.
  • Breadth and adaptability. An FDE may move from debugging an implementation to gathering product feedback, onboarding users and discussing expansion in the space of a day. Autonomy, judgement and comfort with context-switching matter enormously.

“You need people with high agency who thrive when getting their hands dirty. When you’re deploying on an active build site at 7 AM in the pouring rain, resilience and a roll-up-your-sleeves attitude are what will make someone succeed here.”

– Jelmer Borst, Forward Deployed Engineering Lead at Monumental

Most importantly, don’t over-index on candidates who already have “FDE” on their CV. The role varies enormously between companies, so the title tells you relatively little about what someone has actually done. It’s often more useful to probe their day-to-day experience: have they owned ambiguous technical problems end-to-end? Worked directly with customers? Made product decisions? Built under time pressure? Balanced a short-term workaround against a better long-term abstraction?

The hiring data supports that emphasis on adaptability. An FDE Pulse analysis of 288 active FDE job descriptions found that Python was the only individual technology mentioned in a majority of postings, appearing in 51%. There’s no single technical template against which employers are hiring, because they need engineers with strong fundamentals who can move quickly across unfamiliar customer systems and emerging tools. Just as importantly, FDE Pulse identifies customer communication, executive stakeholder management, project scoping and context-switching as recurring requirements that distinguish candidates who pass the technical screen from those who ultimately receive offers.

“I would always look for AI-nativeness and what they’ve done with the latest tools. Working on side projects or hacky things is great, because a lot of FDE solutions initially are MVPs and have to be hacky and creative.”

– Taher Poonawala, Senior Forward Deployed Engineer at HappyRobot

Attrition and career progression

If you’re hiring FDEs, plan for roughly 2 to 2.5 years in the role, not a decade. Our Kaplan–Meier analysis across 2,856 completed and ongoing stints estimates a median tenure of 28 months (2.3 years), and that rises only slightly to 31 months when we look solely at more mature cohorts that began by 2023.

That matters because completed roles alone make tenure look shorter: their median is 1.7 years, with around 60% ending within two years. Kaplan–Meier is the better guide for founders because it also accounts for FDEs who are still in-role and may ultimately stay much longer. The completed-stint distribution is still useful, though, because it shows when exits actually occur among people who have already left, which helps you identify the periods when retention risk is highest (we discuss this in more detail later).

The practical implication is simple: don’t design your FDE function around permanent retention. Instead, work out when FDEs are most likely to move, why they do so, and what compelling next step you can offer before they start looking elsewhere.

We also found that attrition to roles outside the company is highest in the first three months, often because candidates enter with the wrong expectations of what the role actually involves. FDEs are defined more by outcomes than by function, so the role can span everything from product research and technical architecture to deployment, training and support. Founders therefore need to be unusually explicit during hiring about how technical the role is, where it sits in the customer lifecycle, how much time is spent building versus meeting customers, and whether they’ll be building deeply with a handful of customers or operating more broadly across many. When that expectation-setting is poor, disappointment follows quickly.

“Founders need to be honest about what they’re actually asking someone to do. A lot of founders simply say they want a ‘product-minded engineer who can do everything.’ But those people get bored if they’re not using all of their skills. If someone is highly technical and all you ask them to do is attend meetings, they’ll get frustrated. If someone is highly strategic but all you want is for them to sit in a corner writing code, they’ll also get frustrated. These are usually very multifaceted people, and you need to keep giving them opportunities to use those different strengths.”-Sravya Bhamidipati, Forward Strategist at Helsing (formerly FDE at Palantir)

A second danger point for attrition appears around 18-24 months, but for a very different reason: what comes next? FDEs tend to be ambitious generalists, and the role gives them exposure to engineering, product, commercial and customer problems unusually quickly. That breadth is part of the appeal, but eventually creates a career-path problem. If the only progression available is “more senior FDE”, some will start wondering whether they should instead become an engineering leader, product manager, commercial operator (or founder). Additionally, in 2-3 years FDEs have experienced enough deployments to feel that they aren’t learning that much, and that onset of ennui prompts a desire for a different role.

Our data suggests founders should take that possibility seriously. Across 1,166 completed stints, 66% ended with the FDE leaving the company altogether, while 28% moved internally into non-FDE roles.

But departure isn’t inevitable. Internal movers frequently went into software engineering, sales/BD, product management – suggesting that companies can retain strong FDEs by giving them somewhere meaningful to go next. Among those who leave, the destinations are similarly broad: software engineering and another FDE role are most common, while 10% go on to found a company.

In other words, your FDE function can become one of your best internal talent pipelines (if you design it that way). Rather than forcing ambitious generalists to choose between staying an FDE indefinitely and leaving the company, create explicit routes into product, engineering, management and commercial leadership. Rotations, increasing ownership and opportunities to build new functions can all give strong FDEs somewhere to go without losing them from the business.

That matters because FDE seems unusually effective at changing people’s career trajectories. Among the 1,055 completed journeys where we could identify roles both immediately before and after FDE, 81% emerged into a different kind of role from the one they held before becoming an FDE.

The recurring pattern looks something like:

High-agency technical generalist → FDE → cross-functional leader with meaningful business ownership

How should the FDE function scale?

FDEs rarely work alone. In most companies running this model, the FDE is one of three roles sitting around a customer, each with a distinct job to do:

  • The account executive owns the commercial relationship: qualification, pricing, negotiation, expansion and renewal. Their most useful contribution to the FDE model is often defensive, keeping procurement, contracting and commercial noise away from the people building.
  • The solutions architect, or deployment strategist, sits in the middle. They scope the customer problem before anything is promised, judge what is feasible with today’s product, design the pilot and define what success looks like.
  • The FDE owns delivery: they build the solution, get it into production, and feed what they learn back into the roadmap so that the next customer needs less custom work.

That division of labour is useful precisely because the three roles are optimising for different things. Those objectives will sometimes conflict, and that tension can be healthy.

How many of each do you need?

Your GTM motion sets the ratio. In a product-led organisation, the product itself does much of the selling and deployments tend to be more repeatable, so the applied team can stay relatively light: something closer to four or five account executives to two or three solution architects to a single engineer. That engineer may not be truly forward deployed at all, but rather a product engineer spread across several accounts to handle the technical requirements that do arise.

In a sales-led organisation, the pyramid can invert. Larger and more bespoke engagements justify much more technical capacity around each customer, pushing the ratio towards one AE to two solution architects to three FDEs. Once the AE has sold the engagement, they can move on to the next opportunity while the technical team stays with the customer.

That said, there is no universal ratio. Team composition should follow the problem rather than a fixed template. More technically demanding deployments warrant more engineering capacity; less sophisticated customers may require more support; and particularly strategic opportunities may justify longer or more flexible engagements. You also generally don’t want one FDE holding all the context, particularly on an important account, because that quickly creates a single point of failure.

What to watch out for:

The danger in a sales-led model is when headcount simply grows in line with revenue. An applied team can go from twenty people to several hundred within a year if every new customer requires another dedicated deployment team. A rule of thumb raised across several of our conversations is roughly $1m of annual contract value per person deployed, which helps keep the economics looking like software rather than consulting.

The more important test is whether that ratio improves over time. Roles tend to specialise as the organisation matures. As the product gets better and deployments become more repeatable, each person should be able to support more revenue and create more customer value. If every incremental dollar of revenue requires a proportional increase in applied headcount indefinitely, you have not really created product leverage.

“FDEs, if they are good, should work themselves out of a job. FDEs should be building such that the ratio of FDEs to customers decreases – the number of FDEs you need smooths out over time, if not decreases over time.”

– Rachel Finegold, Founder and CEO at Frontier Health (former Healthcare Lead at Palantir)

Roles also tend to specialise as the organisation matures. An early FDE or deployment strategist may span technical delivery, customer success and commercial conversations because there simply aren’t enough people to separate the work. At greater scale, those responsibilities can split across account management, customer success, solution architecture and deployment engineering.

That makes the organisation more scalable, but something is lost in the process: the customer no longer has a single person who understands the technical problem, the commercial context and everything that has happened in the account.

“As you scale, there’s a benefit to splitting the role out – it’s more scalable. But you lose something: customers really appreciate having one person who has all of the context.”– Matthew Blodgett, AI Consultant at Capsa AI

AI may push in the opposite direction. As more administrative work and lower-level technical implementation becomes automated, a single FDE may be able to own more of the customer relationship end-to-end and work across more accounts. The role may also become less about writing code and more about judgement: understanding what the technology can actually do, identifying the right end state, and deciding what is worth building in the first place.

Robotics is a slightly different kettle of fish. In software companies, it can make sense to keep FDEs organisationally distinct from core engineering, preserving a useful tension between customer-specific delivery and scalable product development. In robotics, engineering often is the product, so keeping FDEs inside engineering can be much more natural. It also creates a clearer technical career path and makes it easier for people to move between forward-deployed and core engineering roles as they develop.

There is also a more fundamental question behind the org chart: what are you actually asking your FDEs to own? Some companies use forward-deployed teams primarily to extend the go-to-market motion; others give them much deeper ownership of the customer problem itself, including the technical work required to solve it. That distinction matters not only for how the function operates, but for the kind of people it attracts. At Frontier Health, for example, FDEs own real operational problems for customers. The job naturally selects for a particular kind of person: high-agency, outcome-oriented, intellectually humble, gritty and unusually comfortable with ambiguity.

More generally, where FDEs sit matters less than the relationship they have with the core product team. FDEs should optimise aggressively for the customer problem in front of them; product teams should optimise for abstractions that can serve many customers. Those incentives are supposed to be different. What matters is creating a strong feedback loop between the two, so that FDEs surface and prove out real customer needs while the product team decides what should ultimately be generalised and productised.

The end goal is increasing product leverage. As the platform improves, FDEs should be able to solve more ambitious problems with less support from core engineering, while each deployed person supports more customer value and more revenue. The function scales not simply by adding more FDEs, but by increasing the leverage of each one.

How should you measure an FDE function?

It’s tempting to measure FDEs the way you’d measure an engineer or a salesperson, but the role is too cross-functional: FDEs influence deployment, adoption, product development, expansion and customer retention, often without owning any of those outcomes alone. The better approach is to measure the health and leverage of the FDE function as a whole, rather than trying to attribute every outcome to an individual engineer.

At a high level, the scorecard should cover four things:

  • Deployment outcomes: Are projects actually reaching production, and how quickly? Track time to first value, deployment success and, where relevant, the amount of production usage you capture.
  • Commercial impact: Are the customers your FDEs work with growing and sticking around? NRR, expansion ARR, churn and company-wide ARR per FDE are useful indicators.
  • Product leverage: Is customer work making the underlying product better? Look at reusable components, upstream product contributions and whether recurring customer problems are being productised. The number of FDEs required per customer should also decline as the product matures; if it doesn’t, you may simply be scaling services headcount alongside revenue.
  • Customer outcomes: Ultimately, did you solve the problem the customer hired you to solve? Renewal, expansion, satisfaction and willingness to act as a reference are useful signals here.
“The bottom line is revenue at the end of the day. But it’s hard to come up with one primary metric because the work is so interconnected and cross-functional.”– Lakshya Prakash Agarwal, FDE at Tavily/Nebius

Product feedback deserves particular care. You could measure how many FDE-discovered gaps make it onto the roadmap, but that risks rewarding the wrong behaviour: the product team may quite reasonably decide that a customer request shouldn’t become part of the core product. Better measures are whether FDEs are consistently surfacing valuable insights, whether reusable solutions are making their way upstream, and whether those learnings make future deployments easier.

Platform and outcome-driven models need different scoreboards. As Anjor Kanekar argues, a platform company should ultimately need fewer FDEs per dollar of revenue as the product becomes more powerful and customers become more self-sufficient. In practice, that means licence revenue growing faster than the delivery team required to support it. If instead you’re deliberately running an outcome-driven model, the scoreboard looks different: outcomes delivered, margin per FDE and the rate at which internal tooling makes each subsequent deployment cheaper. Kanekar also makes the case, in an earlier piece, that FDE economics should primarily be assessed at the company or team level rather than engagement by engagement. If every deployment is optimised for its own revenue or margin, FDEs are incentivised to maximise bespoke customer work rather than discovering capabilities that can eventually be productised.

“The moment it becomes a problem is when you’re really just selling services, and no incremental product improvements are coming out of it. That’s when you effectively become a consultancy: when being close to the customer isn’t actually improving your product, or helping you capture more value through it.”– Oswin Kruger Ruiz, FDE, ElevenLabs

Ultimately, don’t ask whether every FDE is busy or every deployment is profitable. At the individual level, the most telling signals are the ones hardest to put on a dashboard: the quality of the code an FDE ships, and the momentum they build inside the customer. At the level of the function, ask whether each successive deployment creates more customer value with progressively more product leverage. If revenue and deployments are growing but every new customer requires the same amount of bespoke engineering as the last, the model isn’t really scaling.

Build for what comes next

Hiring great FDEs is just the first step for founders. The bigger challenge lies in building a system where every deployment leaves behind a better product, more reusable capability and an easier path for the next customer (rather than more services headcount).

And the same principle applies to the people. Our data suggests FDE is often a role talented people grow through rather than grow old in. Done well, that isn’t a retention failure: it turns the FDE function into both a product-learning engine and a pipeline for future leaders. Build it for what comes next – for the product, the customer and the people doing the work.

Whichever side of that trade you’re on (whether you’re a founder thinking about the FDE motion, or an FDE thinking about becoming a founder) please reach out to Advika or Joanna, we would love to speak with you!

Methodology

We analysed the career histories of 2,589 people using publicly available LinkedIn profile data. Across these profiles, we identified 2,880 FDE stints. The data was collected up to 24 July 2026.

Eligibility of the 2,589 people for the sample was determined using role headings in each person’s full work history and recorded FDE-role progression. Profile descriptions, About sections and Skills were not used as evidence of eligibility.

Qualifying title evidence included:

  • Titles containing “Forward Deployed”, including Forward Deployed Engineer, Forward Deployed Software Engineer and related functional, leadership and hyphenated variants
  • The standalone acronym “FDE”
  • Deployment Strategist
  • Applied AI titles included within the qualifying FDE title family

Generic references to deployment (such as cloud deployment, product deployment, deployment services or field deployment) were not sufficient without one of the qualifying phrases above.

An internship was not counted as a completed FDE stint. Separately, when classifying experience immediately before or after an FDE stint, any title containing “Intern” or “Internship” (including “Forward Deployed Engineer Intern”) was classified as Intern before any other occupational classification rule was applied.

The study distinguishes between people and FDE stints. A stint is a continuous period in an FDE-related function at one company. Consecutive FDE titles, promotions or qualifying Technical Lead continuations at the same company were consolidated into a single stint.

The 2,589 people generated 2,880 retained FDE stints, because some individuals completed more than one FDE stint or worked in an FDE role at multiple companies. Unless explicitly labelled as people, the denominator used in the stint-based analysis therefore refers to stints rather than unique individuals.

An internal move was defined as a transition from the completed FDE stint into another role at the same canonical company. An external move was defined as a transition to a different company.

Analyses of what happens after FDE necessarily use completed stints, because an ongoing stint does not yet have an observable post-FDE role. The completed-tenure results consequently describe the duration of observed completed stints rather than the eventual tenure of all current and former FDEs.

To account for ongoing roles, we also used Kaplan–Meier survival analysis across 2,856 stints with a usable duration. This includes 1,294 completed stints as observed events and all 1,562 current stints as right-censored observations. In other words, ongoing stints contribute information for as long as they have been observed without assuming when they will eventually end.

This produced an estimated median tenure of 28 months (2.3 years). As a robustness check against the role’s recent growth, we repeated the analysis using only the 1,083 more mature stints that began by the end of 2023; the estimated median increased only modestly, to 31 months. Confidence intervals were calculated using Greenwood standard errors with a normal approximation.

Pre-FDE experience was reconstructed at the person level using each individual’s full recorded work history. The calculation was anchored to the earliest visible non-intern role matching the qualifying FDE-title rules, with the first retained source stint used as a fallback where necessary.

Qualifying professional-employment intervals before this anchor were identified and their durations calculated in months. Internships, summer analyst or associate positions, student programmes and qualifying roles held at the individual’s own university were excluded from the accumulated experience measure.

Where a pre-FDE bachelor’s graduation date was available, employment intervals were clipped to a June graduation midpoint so that work undertaken while completing the degree was not treated as full post-university experience. Where multiple qualifying jobs overlapped, their intervals were merged to avoid double-counting concurrent employment.

Dates reported only at year level were retained but flagged as lower-precision estimates. If no qualifying prior-employment interval could be identified, the calculated measure was recorded as zero. This means that no qualifying interval was visible in the available profile; it does not prove that the individual had no real-world work experience.

This accumulated-experience measure is separate from the classification of the role immediately before FDE. For example, a person whose immediate prior title contained “Intern” was classified as coming from an Intern role even though that internship was excluded from the accumulated years-of-experience calculation.

SampleDefinition and use
n = 2,589 people The full person-level research sample. Used for analyses of the population’s composition, education, location and estimated pre-FDE experience.
n = 2,880 stints All retained FDE stints, including current and completed stints. Used for the overall stint inventory and time-to-event analysis, where current stints can be treated as right-censored.
n = 2,856 stints Kaplan-Meier-eligible stints for retention analysis, which comprises of 1,294 completed stints with usable durations and 1,562 ongoing stints, censored at the 24 July 2026 cutoff
n = 1,166 stints The strict completed-stint sample: completed stints with exact month-level start and end dates and a calculable duration. Used where a reliable completed tenure or exit point is required.
n = 1,095 stints Strict completed stints with a usable role immediately after the FDE stint. Used for detailed post-FDE destination analysis. This comprises 326 internal moves and 769 external moves.
n = 1,055 stints Strict completed stints with usable roles both immediately before and immediately after the FDE stint. Used for the individual-journey Sankey analysis. These 1,055 stints were contributed by 1,005 unique people. These are the records for which the complete sequence (role before → FDE stint → role after) can be observed.

Job titles were standardised into broad occupational categories to make the transitions comparable. Internal moves were defined as transitions to another role at the same company, while external moves were transitions to a different company.

The initial title, company and occupational-category classifications were produced using ChatGPT Sol 5.6 with Extra High reasoning. These classifications were then manually vetted, with ambiguous or incorrect cases corrected before the final analysis was produced.

The analysis is based on publicly visible, self-reported LinkedIn histories. Profiles may be incomplete, out of date or inconsistent in the level of detail provided. People with private profiles, limited work histories or non-standard role descriptions may be underrepresented.

This is an observational sample rather than a probability sample of the global FDE workforce. It is particularly concentrated in companies that established or adopted explicit FDE and Deployment Strategist titles. Palantir alone accounts for 936 people, or 36% of the person-level sample. This is largely due to the fact that Palantir (having originated the role) provides the richest source of data and history around FDEs. Aggregate findings may therefore be strongly influenced by the recruitment and career structures of these companies and should not be presented as representative of every FDE organisation or of the entire global FDE workforce.

Requiring exact month-level dates for the strict completed-stint analysis may also introduce selection effects, because profiles with less detailed or older employment histories are more likely to be excluded. Likewise, the individual-journey analysis excludes stints without observable prior or subsequent roles. Missing next-role information was not interpreted as unemployment, a career break or continued employment.
