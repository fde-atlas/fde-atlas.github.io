AI Engineer Interview Questions: LLM APIs, RAG and Evals

Prepare.sh · 2026-09-20 · 13 min read

Summary

Drawing on real job postings, published interview loops, and first-person candidate accounts, this article compiles AI engineer interview questions (including Forward Deployed Engineer roles) with answers grounded in vendor documentation. It notes job titles are inconsistent—only Stripe uses "AI Engineer"—and that postings consistently demand prompts, agents, and evals, rarely specific frameworks, while backend fundamentals remain the baseline. Detailed answers cover distinguishing rate limits from spend-cap 429s, structured outputs that still need validation, separating retrieval from generation failures, pgvector's HNSW vs IVFFlat trade-offs, eval design principles, and how caching and batching cut cost and latency.

Key points

  • Filtering job boards on "AI Engineer" misses most of the market.
  • Postings demand prompts, agents, and evals; framework names are mostly absent.
  • A 429 can mean rate limit or exhausted spend cap—retrying the latter causes self-inflicted outages.
  • Measure retrieval recall and faithfulness separately before touching prompts.
  • Caching buys throughput as well as cost; batch APIs get 50% discounts.

Every question below traces back to something you can read yourself: a live job posting, a company's own description of its interview loop, or a first-person account with a name and a date on it. Nothing here is a "commonly asked question" I made up. The answers are grounded in vendor documentation, because that is where the interviewer's own mental model came from too.

One warning before the questions. The title for this work is unsettled. Of the postings I went through, exactly one, at Stripe, is called "AI Engineer". The rest are "Forward Deployed Engineer", "Applied AI Engineer", "Member of Technical Staff" or "Product Engineer, AI". If you filter job boards on the words "AI Engineer" you will miss most of the market.

What the postings actually ask for

Anthropic's Applied AI Engineer posting asks for "Production experience with LLMs, including advanced prompt engineering, agent development and frameworks, evaluation frameworks, transcript analysis, MCP, and deployment at scale". Glean's Founding Forward Deployed Engineer posting says almost the same thing in the same order: "You've shipped AI-powered solutions in the real world - prompt engineering, agent development, evaluation frameworks, deployment at scale. Not just prototypes."

That trio, prompts, agents, evals, is what keeps recurring. RAG shows up a little less often than its reputation suggests. Notion asks for work "with MCPs, APIs, LLM applications, prompt engineering, retrieval/RAG systems or workflow orchestration", and Pinecone's field engineering posting wants "Familiarity with the concepts of embeddings, similarity search, recommendation systems, and RAG". Stripe wants "Experience building and deploying AI agents, LLM-powered tools, or workflow automation beyond basic prompt engineering" plus "Strong backend engineering fundamentals".

Two things worth noticing, because they change how you prepare:

Backend fundamentals are still the floor. Cursor's posting asks whether you have "handled production reliability before (metrics, alerts, safe rollouts, incident response)". Intercom goes further and says the quiet part out loud: "No ML experience required. We believe you can be highly effective immediately by bringing excellent software engineering skills, and learn ML as you work on the team."

Framework names are mostly absent. LinkedIn's Jobs on the Rise 2026 list (published 7 January 2026) ranks Artificial Intelligence Engineer as the number one fastest growing US role and lists the top skills as LangChain, RAG and PyTorch. Yet LangChain appeared as a requirement in none of the postings I read, not even LlamaIndex's own. Learn the concepts, not a framework's API surface.

On money, treat every number you see as measuring something different. The Stack Overflow Developer Survey 2025 puts the US median total compensation for the AI/ML engineer role at $189,500, from 23,928 salary responses, self-reported. That is not comparable to a base salary range in a recruiter's guide, and neither is comparable to equity-heavy totals at large-cap tech.

The formats you will get

Janvi Kalra, who interviewed at 46 AI companies before joining OpenAI, described the state of things to Gergely Orosz in May 2025: "AI Engineering interviews are all over the place. There doesn't seem to be an 'AI Engineer interview' format." Expect some mix of algorithmic coding, system design, a take-home build, and a deep dive on LLM specifics.

The newer format worth preparing for specifically is the live build. Sierra published its loop in April 2026: a plan phase where "The candidate drives ideation, while interviewers ask questions to strengthen it", a build phase where "The interviewer steps out and the candidate brings the idea to life over 2 hours, using the AI tooling and frameworks of their choice", and a review phase where they "debate the key product flows and choices they made" and read the code.

Applied Intuition described a similar structure in July 2026: a "45-minute coding task with no AI", then a 45 minute open-ended system design question, then "two hours to build a working prototype of the system they just designed using any AI model they want, and any frameworks".

Note the pattern in both: AI tools banned in one round, mandatory in another. Anthropic's published candidate guidance says of live interviews, "This is all you--no AI assistance unless we indicate otherwise", while Datadog's guidelines say "For certain roles, we conduct AI-assisted coding interviews... You will be explicitly told in advance if you will be in an AI coding interview." Ask which kind you are in. Nobody minds the question, and guessing wrong is expensive.

"We started getting 429s in production. Walk me through it."

This is the question that separates people who have shipped from people who have prototyped, and it comes straight out of the "deployment at scale" language in the postings.

The answer. First, read the response, because a 429 is not one thing. The rate limit case tells you how long to wait. OpenAI's rate limit docs are explicit: "Follow

Retry-After

when it's present, reduce your request rate, and then increase it gradually", and "If

Retry-After

is missing, increase the delay between retries and add a small random delay." The headers tell you which budget you blew:

x-ratelimit-remaining-requests

and

x-ratelimit-remaining-tokens

, with matching

-limit-

and

-reset-

variants. Anthropic exposes the same idea under

anthropic-ratelimit-requests-remaining

,

anthropic-ratelimit-input-tokens-remaining

and

anthropic-ratelimit-output-tokens-remaining

, plus a

retry-after

documented as "The number of seconds to wait until you can retry the request. Earlier retries will fail."

Then the case most candidates miss. A 429 can also mean you are out of money, and retrying that one forever is a self-inflicted outage. Anthropic's spend cap response carries the same

rate_limit_error

type but no

retry-after

header, and an

error.details.error_code

of

enforced_spend_limit_reached

. OpenAI puts it plainly: "Don't retry quota, billing, or other errors that require you to take action."

What a strong answer adds: a queue in front of the provider so backpressure is yours to shape, jitter so your retries do not synchronise into a thundering herd, alerting on the remaining-token headers before they hit zero rather than on the errors afterwards, and a fallback model or a degraded path for user-facing requests.

Follow-ups to expect: How do you tell a rate limit from a spend cap in your metrics? What do you do with a streaming request that fails halfway through? Would you retry a non-idempotent tool call?

"How do you get output you can actually parse?"

The answer. Stop parsing prose. OpenAI's structured outputs feature "ensures the model will always generate responses that adhere to your supplied JSON Schema", and the docs are direct about the older approach: structured outputs "is the evolution of JSON mode", and "only Structured Outputs ensure schema adherence". Same idea on the tool side, where

strict: true

means function calls "reliably adhere to the function schema, instead of being best effort". Anthropic's tool docs mirror this: "Add

strict: true

to your custom tool definitions to ensure Claude's tool calls always match your schema exactly."

Then the part that gets you the job: say that you still validate. The docs list real failure paths, a refusal that arrives in a separate

refusal

field, a response cut off by a token limit, and schema features that are not supported. Schema-adherent is not the same as correct or complete, and your code has to handle a model that declines.

Follow-ups: What happens to your parser when the response is truncated? How do you version a schema that is already in production? Where do you put retries when validation fails, and how do you stop that loop from burning budget?

"Our support bot answered confidently and it was completely wrong."

The answer. Separate retrieval failure from generation failure before you touch a prompt, because the fixes are unrelated. OpenAI's accuracy guide names both retrieval failure modes: you can supply "the wrong context, so the model can't possibly answer", or "too much irrelevant context, which drowns out the real information and causes hallucinations". Measure first. Ragas splits this cleanly: context recall measures "how many of the relevant documents (or pieces of information) were successfully retrieved", while faithfulness "measures how factually consistent a

response

is with the

retrieved context

". Low recall is a retrieval problem. High recall with low faithfulness is a generation problem.

For generation, Anthropic's hallucination guidance gives concrete moves rather than vibes: "Allow Claude to say 'I don't know'", ask the model to extract word-for-word quotes before answering on long documents, and make claims auditable, "If it can't find a quote, it must retract the claim." Their Citations API does the same thing as a product feature, returning "the exact passages that support each claim". The docs are honest about the ceiling, and you should be too in the interview: these techniques "don't eliminate them entirely".

Follow-ups: How would you catch this class of failure before a user does? What does your eval set look like, and who wrote the expected answers? When is the right fix "show the source and let the human decide" rather than anything technical?

"Pick a vector index and defend it."

The answer. In pgvector the trade-off is documented in one paragraph each. HNSW "has better query performance than IVFFlat (in terms of speed-recall tradeoff), but has slower build times and uses more memory". IVFFlat "has faster build times and uses less memory than HNSW, but has lower query performance".

The operational detail that shows you have used them: IVFFlat has to learn its lists from data, so pgvector tells you to "Create the index after the table has some data", while with HNSW "an index can be created without any data in the table since there isn't a training step". That single difference decides which one fits a pipeline that reindexes nightly versus one that ingests continuously.

HNSW IVFFlat
Query speed at recall better worse
Build time slower faster
Memory more less
Needs data before building no yes

Bring up filtering unprompted if you want to stand out. Qdrant's docs warn that "the HNSW graph starts to fall apart when using filters that are too strict", which is exactly the case in every multi-tenant product: search this user's documents only. You can experiment with this in the Qdrant workspace rather than arguing about it in the abstract.

Follow-ups: How do you pick

m

and

ef_construct

? What is your recall target, and how did you measure it? What changes if the collection grows 100 times?

"How would you know if the new version is better?"

Eval design shows up in job requirements constantly (Anthropic, Glean and LlamaIndex all name it) even though it rarely gets its own named interview round. Expect it as a follow-up to everything else.

The answer. Start by refusing the vague version of the goal. Anthropic's docs contrast a bad success criterion, "The model should classify sentiments well", with a good one: "The sentiment analysis model should achieve an F1 score of at least 0.85... on a held-out test set of 10,000 diverse Twitter posts... which is a 5% improvement over the current baseline."

Then three rules that are cheap to state and hard to argue with. Mirror the real task distribution, including edge cases. Automate grading so you can run it on every change, and "Prioritize volume over quality: More questions with slightly lower signal automated grading is better than fewer questions with high-quality human hand-graded evals." And if you use a model as judge, it is "generally best practice to use a different model to evaluate than the model used to generate the evaluated output".

OpenAI's guidance points the same way, calling for eval-driven development, and names the two anti-patterns worth quoting back at anyone who asks: "Overly generic metrics: Relying solely on academic metrics like perplexity or BLEU score", and "Vibe-based evals: Using 'it seems like it's working' as an evaluation strategy."

One current fact that makes you sound like you read release notes: do not anchor your answer on a specific eval platform. OpenAI announced the deprecation of its Evals platform on 3 June 2026, with existing evals becoming read-only on 31 October 2026 and the dashboard and API scheduled to shut down on 30 November 2026, with a published migration path to Promptfoo. Tooling churns. The dataset of labelled examples you own is the asset.

Follow-ups: Where does the eval set come from on day one, when you have no traffic? How do you stop it from rotting? What is your offline-to-online correlation, and what do you do when they disagree?

"Cut this feature's cost and latency in half."

The answer. Go after tokens and round trips before you go after models. OpenAI's latency guide lists the levers in a way worth memorising: "Generate fewer tokens", "Use fewer input tokens", "Make fewer requests", "Parallelize", "Make your users wait less", and the one people forget, "Don't default to an LLM."

Then caching, which is where the real money is for anything with a long system prompt or a big document. Anthropic's pricing structure: cache writes cost 1.25 times the base input price at the 5 minute TTL and 2 times at the 1 hour TTL, while "Cache read tokens are 0.1 times the base input tokens price" on most models (0.05 times on Claude Opus 5.5 and Sonnet 5.5, 0.025 times on Claude Fable 5.1 and Mythos 5.1), and the cache "is refreshed for no additional cost each time the cached content is used". Same order of magnitude on the OpenAI side, where cached input is billed at a fraction of the uncached rate.

The detail that wins this question is that caching buys throughput, not just money. Anthropic's rate limit docs: "For most Claude models, only uncached input tokens count toward your ITPM rate limits", with their own worked example, "With a 2,000,000 ITPM limit and an 80% cache hit rate, you could effectively process 10,000,000 total input tokens per minute". If you are rate limited rather than cost limited, a cache hit rate is your capacity plan.

For anything that does not need an answer now, both providers discount batch work by 50%. Offline enrichment, backfills and eval runs belong there.

Follow-ups: What invalidates your cache, and how often does that happen in practice? How do you measure time to first token separately from total time? Which parts of this can you not cache, and why?

"Design a RAG system for us"

A real version of this prompt, from Mai Chi Bao's write-up of his LLM interview in May 2025: "Design a production-ready system for a customer support chatbot that uses fine-tuned LLMs to answer queries based on company documentation." The stated constraints were open-source models for data privacy, 100+ concurrent users, sub-2-second responses, and answers grounded in the documentation. The deliverables included an architecture diagram, model selection justification, retrieval details, deployment and scaling, a monitoring and evaluation plan, and cost estimation.

How to answer it. Work the constraints, not the diagram. Sub-2-second responses with 100 concurrent users is a serving question (batching, KV cache, streaming the first token early) before it is a model question. Data privacy rules out hosted APIs, which changes your cost model from per-token to per-GPU-hour. "Grounded in company documentation" means citations and an "I don't know" path, not a better prompt.

Say what you would not build. OpenAI's own guidance is that "Prompt engineering is typically the best place to start", and that you optimise context when "the model lacks contextual knowledge because it wasn't in its training set", but optimise the model itself when outputs are "inconsistent... formatting" or the tone is wrong. Reaching for fine-tuning to fix a knowledge gap is the classic wrong answer, and interviewers listen for it.

"Would you use an agent here?"

The answer. Define the terms first, because half the disagreements in this area are vocabulary. Anthropic's engineering post draws the line: "Workflows are systems where LLMs and tools are orchestrated through predefined code paths. Agents, on the other hand, are systems where LLMs dynamically direct their own processes and tool usage."

Then give the boring recommendation, which is also the one in the docs: "Start with simple prompts, optimize them with comprehensive evaluation, and add multi-step agentic systems only when simpler solutions fall short." Name the patterns you would try first, prompt chaining, routing, parallelization, and say what an agent costs you: nondeterminism, a wider blast radius from tool calls, and much harder debugging. If the task has a known set of steps, write the steps.

Follow-ups: How do you bound an agent's spend and step count? What does a tool failure do to the loop? How do you test something that takes a different path every run?

How to practise this

Read the postings for the specific companies you are targeting and steal their vocabulary. When Anthropic names MCP and Glean names evaluation frameworks, those are the words their interviewers use in debriefs.

Then get the reps in. We keep company-tagged question sets for the AI labs, including Anthropic and OpenAI, and the backend fundamentals that every one of these loops still tests sit in the backend engineering interview track. If the retrieval and storage side is where you feel thin, our article on using Redis as a memory layer for LLM and RAG applications covers the moving parts you will be asked to draw on a whiteboard, and the prompt engineering in cloud services piece covers the part of the job the postings put first.

Last piece of advice, and it comes from what the interview formats have become: build something small and ship it. A candidate who can say "I ran this in production, here is the eval set, here is what the 429 storm taught me" answers every question on this page without being asked.
