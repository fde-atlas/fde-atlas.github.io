How FDEs Handle Client Delays: Dependency Management and Escalation Communication

Original title: FDE 如何应对客户延迟：依赖管理与升级沟通

FDE Times · 2026-11-02 · 6 min read

Summary

This guide reframes client-side delays as project dependency risks to be managed actively rather than annoyances to endure. Drawing on Umbrex, Baton, Rocketlane and Alloy.ai examples, it shows how to log dependencies with named owners at kickoff, back-calculate true deadlines from the demo date, ask for the real cause before escalating, and then send the sponsor a message with several options, each with a date and scope. A worked example for an insurance claims-classification agent walks through the math day by day. It also lists six implementation steps, common mistakes (borrowing accounts, silently absorbing delays), and how to demonstrate this skill in FDE job interviews.

Key points

  • Treat client delays as dependency risks, not personal frustrations.
  • Back-calculate true deadlines for each dependency from the demo date.
  • Ask the owner why work is stuck before escalating.
  • Escalate with multiple options, each with a date and scope.
  • Avoid workarounds like borrowed accounts or silently absorbing delays.

Key Takeaways

  • Slow client progress is a risk to be managed, not an annoyance to be endured.
  • Work backward from deadlines, see which dependencies are already overdue, and before escalating, first ask what's really causing the delay.
  • A good escalation offers several viable paths, each with a clear date and scope.

The project is in its second week. You have a laptop, a badge, and a borrowed conference room, but no account for the client's system. The request has been submitted three times, the ticket still reads "pending security team review," and the demo date for the executive team hasn't moved.

When this happens, people tend to have two instinctive reactions: send yet another polite follow-up email, or wait it out in silent frustration. Both let project time quietly slip away without requiring anyone to make any decision. Getting out of this situation is a skill that takes practice, like debugging.

Umbrex's consulting playbook suggests treating repeated client delays as a project dependency risk rather than a personal annoyance, shifting from passive waiting to active dependency management. To do this, you need three things: a tracking table, a subtraction, and a message with options.

Put Delays into the Plan

Wind the clock back to the kickoff meeting, before the opening scene ever happened. The timeline is on the screen, the project sponsor and the security and IT folks are all present, and no one has delayed anything yet. This is the easiest moment to talk about delays, because no one needs to be held accountable.

Point to a specific box on the timeline: the security review of the integration plan. This is a classic blocker—Rocketlane has warned that a single department can hold up a project for weeks. Then ask: "Who owns this on your side, and how long does it usually take?"

The answer is a name and a number of days, both of which go straight into the timeline. This is also Baton's advice: build common client-side blockers into the plan from the start. A reminder with no named recipient won't get anyone to act.

If the contract says the same thing, the client will take what you say at the kickoff more seriously. So before the kickoff, read the statement of work (SOW) before the data schemas. Alloy.ai's SOW is a clear example: all timelines depend on timely data access, and delays may lead to extending the project through a change order.

When the client goes unresponsive, this document also gives the implementer the right to pause the integration and reassign staff. You need to know whether your contract has similar mechanisms before you can talk about escalation.

A Day-by-Day Walkthrough of a Hypothetical Project

Suppose the project is building an agent for an insurance company that classifies claims cases. The kickoff is D1, and the demo is set for the 20th business day, D20. Once you have read access to the real system, you estimate 10 days of development, plus 3 days for the business team to validate the results.

The math takes one line: 20 minus 13 equals 7. Access must be in place by D7 at the latest; if account provisioning takes 2 days after the review, the security team must approve by D5 at the latest. This is exactly what polite nudges never tell you: the real deadline for each dependency.

Umbrex recommends recording, for each dependency, the owner, the deadline, the affected deliverable, and the escalation contact. Applied to this hypothetical project (the names are hypothetical too), the register looks like this:

DependencyClient-side ownerDeadlineAffected deliverableEscalation contact
Security review of the integration planLan, security leadD5All work based on real dataProject sponsor
Read-only accountMinh, IT adminD7First version of developmentIT lead
Export of 12 months of claims data for evalsHa, data engineerD12Evals set for the demoData lead

The most important column is the owner: each cell contains a specific person, not "the client" or a vague job title. If you can't fill in a name in a cell, you've found the project's first risk.

At D6, the Review Still Isn't Done

It's now D6, and the D5 deadline has passed. Before writing to the sponsor, you ask Lan: "Where is the review stuck? Is anything missing on our side?" Umbrex treats this question as a pre-escalation step, because a curious tone makes the other party less defensive and surfaces what's really blocking the project.

Lan replies that all materials are complete, but the review board doesn't meet until D10. So the cause is the meeting schedule, not a vague request—another nudge or a rewritten request won't help. The account will be ready at D12 at the earliest, 5 days past the deadline; 12 plus 13 means the full demo slides to D25.

Umbrex stresses that an effective escalation helps the client choose a direction, rather than just telling them you're anxious. So the message to the sponsor should read:

The D20 demo is at risk: the account won't be ready until D12, 5 days late, because the security board doesn't meet until D10. Confirmed with Lan: the request materials are complete; the only blocker is the meeting date. A. Keep D20 and reduce scope: do the demo with an anonymized data export, contingent on the security team's approval and the export arriving by D7 at the latest. B. Keep the full scope and push the demo to D25. C. Have the security team schedule a dedicated review by D7: the account is ready by D9, and the full-scope demo happens on D22. Please choose by the end of D6.

The decision deadline is D6 because options A and C both depend on D7. Under option A, the 13 days of work only just make D20 if the export arrives on D7 itself; option C requires the review to happen that same day. Wait until the next day, and only option B remains.

This message blames no one and contains no adjectives. OpenProject's escalation guide calls this a decision template: send it in writing, send it early, and state the cause and the options.

The sponsor may not know the security team is holding up the project, but they can make a phone call you can't.

Whichever option is chosen, record the decision in the register and send a confirmation email. If the SOW contains clauses like Alloy.ai's and the timeline really does need to be extended, whoever handles contracts on your side needs to know early so they can act in time, rather than discovering it when it's too late.

Six Steps to Apply to Your Project

Step one is the kickoff meeting: state directly where things usually slow down, and require an owner for every dependency.

Step two is building the register. A spreadsheet reviewed regularly is enough to start.

Step three is working backward from deliverables to derive real deadlines, rather than picking dates by feel.

Step four is asking why the delay happened before escalating. If the request was vague, rewrite it with specific tables, specific permissions, and a rationale; if the owner is overloaded, the sponsor needs to reprioritize. These two causes need different solutions, and a generic nudge solves neither.

Step five applies when a real deadline can no longer be met: escalate with a multi-option message like the one above, each option with a date and a scope.

Step six is to keep doing all the work that doesn't require access while you wait: write tests, build data pipelines with synthetic data, and find out who already has an approved copy of the data.

Mistakes That Keep Projects Stuck

Don't wait long past the deadline to escalate, because by then you can no longer offer the sponsor low-cost options. And don't escalate the wrong way, such as forwarding a chain of emails straight to someone's manager without attaching consequences and options. That loses you an ally, and the sponsor still doesn't know what to do.

The more dangerous mistake is finding your own workaround, such as borrowing a friendly employee's account or requesting files through private channels. You save a few days, but you wager all the trust with the security team that is currently reviewing you. The last mistake is quietly working overtime to make up the time, so the client never sees the true cost of the delay.

How to demonstrate this skill when job hunting

Don't just write "strong communication skills" on your resume; that phrase doesn't show the reader what you actually did. Use your own real numbers to describe which dependency you identified, how many days of risk you calculated, what options you provided, and what the final outcome was.

At the interview stage, ask whether the Forward Deployed Engineer (FDE) can read the SOW and who initiates change orders. The answers will tell you whether the company empowers its frontline engineers or just sends them out to wait alone.

This week's exercise

Pick one thing you are currently waiting on from a client or another team within your company. Work backward from the deliverable to find the real deadline, ask the person in charge where things are stuck, and then write a three-option message using the template above, with a date and scope for each option. If you can't even come up with a second option, it means you don't understand your own project well enough.

The next time a ticket's status is still "waiting on the security team," your job is no longer to wait, but to calculate how many days remain and who needs to know that number.
