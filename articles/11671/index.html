Self-Refine (2023) Explained for FDEs

FDE Interviews · 2026-10-17 · 4 min read

Summary

This piece explains the NeurIPS 2023 Self-Refine paper for forward deployed engineers. The method uses one model with three few-shot prompts: generate an output, critique it with specific, actionable feedback, then rewrite it, looping up to four times. Across seven tasks with GPT-3.5, ChatGPT and GPT-4, six tasks improved; GPT-4's keyword coverage tripled from 15% to 45%, but math reasoning barely moved because the models' feedback rarely caught their own errors. External signals like test results or validators dramatically help, and preference-based win rates can exaggerate small gains.

The paper in one minute

People rarely get a piece of writing or code right on the first try; they reread it, notice problems, and revise. Aman Madaan, Niket Tandon, Peter Clark and colleagues at CMU, the Allen Institute for AI and elsewhere asked whether a language model can do the same with no training, using only prompts. Their method, Self-Refine, presented at NeurIPS 2023, uses one model in three roles.

First it generates an output from a few-shot prompt. Then it writes feedback on that output, using a prompt whose examples show feedback that is actionable (it proposes a concrete change) and specific (it names the part to change): "This code is slow as it uses a for loop which is brute force. A better approach is to use the formula n(n+1)/2." Then it refines: rewrites the output given the feedback and the full history of earlier outputs and feedback. The loop repeats up to four times, or stops when the feedback includes a stop signal, such as a quality score above a threshold.

They tested seven tasks: making dialogue responses more engaging, speeding up programs, improving variable names, math word problems (GSM8K), reversing the sentiment of reviews, generating acronyms, and writing a sentence that uses 20 to 30 given keywords. With GPT-3.5, ChatGPT and GPT-4, Self-Refine improved six of the seven, often by a lot. GPT-4's keyword coverage tripled from 15% to 45%. Math did not move: the model's feedback almost never noticed its own errors.

The analysis is as useful as the results. Specific feedback beat generic feedback, which beat none. Most of the gain came in the first round or two. And when refinement failed, the feedback was almost always to blame.

A model's first draft is often close but not right: a reply that ignores the user's feelings, code that works but is slow, a sentence that misses required keywords. Fixing that usually meant training a separate refiner or collecting human feedback.

Use the same model three times with three few-shot prompts: generate an output, write specific and actionable feedback on it, and rewrite it using the feedback and the history of earlier attempts. Repeat up to four times or until the feedback says to stop.

Across seven tasks with GPT-3.5, ChatGPT and GPT-4, refined outputs were preferred or scored higher on six, by large margins on constrained generation and code readability. Math reasoning did not improve, because the feedback rarely found the errors.

Why it matters to an FDE

A draft, critique, rewrite loop is easy to add to almost any generation step, and customers often suggest it. This paper tells you where it pays.

It works when the model can see the problem. Missing keywords, a cold tone, a nested loop, single-letter variable names: things a reader spots and fixes. It does not work when spotting the problem is the hard part, as with a subtle arithmetic slip.

The feedback step is the bottleneck. In the authors' review of failures, 94% came from feedback that pointed at the wrong place or suggested the wrong fix. Spend effort on the critique prompt, and give it external signals (test results, a validator, a checklist) whenever you can.

An outside signal changes everything. On math, telling the model only which answers were wrong raised GPT-3.5 by 4.8 points where its own feedback had added nothing. If you have a check in code, use it to decide when to refine.

Read preference numbers carefully. Half the tasks were scored by asking GPT-4 which of two outputs is better. A large win-rate gap can come from small but consistent improvements, so do not read it as a quality percentage.
