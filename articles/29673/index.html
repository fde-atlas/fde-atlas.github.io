Full text

What happens if there's a single wrong number in a bond prospectus?

It's not a rewrite—it's an incident.

Liao Wanli, founder of Jinzhiwei, has worked in the financial industry for so many years that he knows this bottom line all too well.

When large models arrived, everyone assumed AI could write reports—and it can. But can the reports it writes actually be used in real financial transactions? "Not a single number in there can be wrong," Liao Wanli said.

Financial customers' requirement for AI's "accuracy" is 100%.

01

Another "Do First, Name Later"

Liao Wanli first noticed the term FDE (Forward Deployed Engineer) in September 2024, when Jinzhiwei was preparing its Hong Kong IPO materials.

While researching benchmark companies, he came across Palantir. "My first reaction was, huh? What they do is remarkably similar to the way we've always worked."

Jinzhiwei started out in RPA, with over 1.8 million digital employees deployed in the systems of the six major banks and more than a hundred brokerages. RPA itself means a lot of on-site delivery—stationing staff, integration, tuning.

"I used to feel we hadn't mastered a complete methodology. We never truly formed a flywheel of knowledge and data the way Palantir did."

That remark reminds me of Li Jiarui, founder of Juzi Interactive, whom I interviewed last time. When she started doing Prompt Engineer work in 2023, she didn't know it was called FDE either.

Two founders—one in online education marketing, one in financial RPA—arrived at the same answer by different routes.

China's FDE practice was forced out by business needs, not learned from others.

02

Three Signals

That Forced Him to Change His Approach

Liao Wanli is not someone easily swayed by concepts. He paid attention to FDE because three signals were growing stronger and stronger.

The first signal: customers are no longer satisfied with the old stuff.

In the past, once you delivered an RPA automation workflow, customers would use it quietly for two or three years. Now AI has arrived, and customers feel the old model is "not intelligent enough" and demand rapid evolution.

The second signal: customers started asking "harder questions."

Customers who've been brainwashed by AI hype think large models can do anything. "They have higher expectations, but they themselves may not have figured out what's good and what's not."

The third signal: the competitive landscape has changed.

"In the past, RPA vendors competed on features, stability, and price. Now the competitors are AI companies—they move faster and can produce demos quickly." Even though the demos never land, that's enough to influence customers' decision-making psychology.

These three signals point to the same conclusion: the old organization and delivery model no longer work.

03

AI's Hardest Part Isn't Speed, It's Accuracy

I asked Liao Wanli a question: of the three elements you summarized on customer sites—"do it accurately, do it reliably, do it well"—which one breaks most easily?

He barely hesitated: "accuracy."

"Reliability is a technical problem; a good architecture and operations system can basically guarantee it. Doing it well is the outcome—if the first two are solved, it will most likely be done well. But accuracy—that's the hardest."

He gave an example. The same financial document is handled in completely different ways under different regulatory environments. General-purpose large models can't possibly understand this.

Every number in a bond prospectus has "cross-checking relationships"—data A and data B must conform to specific logical rules.

These rules aren't in any public document. They're hidden in the experience of business staff, in the approval habits of compliance departments, in the records of every revision that was rejected.

"Without deep industry knowledge and accumulated business logic, it's very hard to be accurate."

This is the real value of FDE in financial scenarios—not the person stationed on-site writing code, but the person who can dig out these implicit rules, systematize them, and feed them to AI.

04

The CEO Personally Bets on an Experiment

But Liao Wanli didn't stop at the "we should have a methodology" stage.

He is running an organizational experiment: he set up a small independent team that reports directly to him, consisting of himself, a newly created CKO (Chief Knowledge Officer), and the CTO.

In his words, "it's basically simulating the operations of a small company."

This team is to use a new "knowledge-ontology-driven" methodology to rebuild from scratch a product line that's already running. Goal: get it working in two months.

"If we can make this path work, it will be a revolutionary change," Liao Wanli said.

What does making it work mean? It means that for any customer's business need in the future, what you organize becomes a knowledge ontology directly linked to the model—"rapidly building business capability while being precise and reliable."

An entrepreneur with over a decade of experience, at a critical juncture of AI transformation, chose the dumbest approach: get in the trenches himself, take a real customer's real need, and redo it with the new method.

This is not a technical experiment. This is an organizational experiment.

05

"The First Project Will Definitely Lose Money"

But the cost of the experiment is real.

"The first project will definitely lose money," Liao Wanli is clear-eyed about this. Silicon Valley's FDE model rests on American customers' ability to pay. The reality in China is—customers are willing to spend money on AI, but margins can't support a large number of on-site engineers.

Liao Wanli's confidence lies in the replicable network of financial customers. "In our financial document scenario, once we finish with one brokerage, over a hundred brokerages have similar needs. The key is whether we can productize the scenario and scale it in the market."

This is not a technical question; it's an organizational design question: can the knowledge FDE digs out become replicable product capability? If yes, FDE is an investment; if no, FDE is a cost.

He made an appointment with me to talk again in two months. If the experiment works, I'll come back to fill in this answer.

Liao Wanli's definition of himself is interesting: "We're definitely not the company with the strongest AI technology, and probably not the one that has deployed the most AI capabilities—but we just want it to truly create value in business processes."

Not the flashiest, but the one that gets the work done.

I asked what reputation he hopes Jinzhiwei will have in AI delivery three years from now. He said: "Truly getting the customer's work done and creating real value. If you create value for them, you can naturally share in the fruits of that value."

This remark may come closer to the essence of FDE than any technical white paper.
