How FDEs Identify Internal Champions at Customer Organizations

Original title: FDE 如何识别客户内部拥护者（champion）

FDE Times · 2026-10-09 · 5 min read

Summary

This guide argues that an FDE project's survival depends on an internal champion who will defend it in meetings the engineer never attends. Champions differ from supporters: they stake their professional reputation, speak up under internal politics, and open doors to budget owners. The author suggests finding candidates among frontline end users rather than executives, testing them by asking for an introduction to the budget decision-maker, giving value before asking, pairing champions with an executive sponsor, sharing bad data to keep them objective, and building a trained champion group instead of relying on one person.

Key points

  • Champions stake their reputation; supporters go quiet when politics or budget cuts hit.
  • The fastest test: ask the candidate to introduce you to the budget owner and see if it happens.
  • Look for candidates among respected frontline end users, not the highest titles.
  • Champions need an executive sponsor above them or they burn out or get sidelined.
  • Share failure data to keep champions objective, and grow a champion group, not one person.

Key Takeaways

  • A true champion stakes their professional reputation on the project; a supporter goes silent the moment internal politics appear.
  • The fastest test: ask the person to introduce you to whoever controls the budget, then see if it actually happens.
  • Champions need executive backing above them, and someone to keep them objective, otherwise they become evangelists for an outdated system.

The meeting that decides your pilot's fate is one you're almost never in. It's the client's regular management meeting, where someone asks: is that AI agent still worth continuing? The answer depends on exactly one thing: whether anyone in the room speaks up for you.

At that moment, your coding skills can't save you. The integration can be clean, the evals can look great, but if no one on the client's side stands up to defend the project when budgets tighten or other departments push back, your work ends at the demo.

The person who does this is called an internal champion. Finding, testing, and cultivating champions is a learnable skill, much like debugging: form a hypothesis, run tests, and watch for signals that prove you wrong.

People who like you aren't necessarily champions

According to UserGems, a champion combines three things: belief that the solution should succeed, real influence, and access to decision-makers. But per Spotlight.ai, a champion's most fundamental trait is the willingness to stake their professional reputation on the project's success or failure. Without that, all you have is someone who's pleasant to deal with.

So the most critical dividing line is between champions and supporters. Once a project gets caught up in internal politics, supporters go silent while champions step up to defend it. And during the demo phase, the two look exactly the same.

Supporter

  • Praises the demo, replies to messages quickly
  • Goes quiet when the project faces opposition
  • Apologizes that they can't help introduce you upward
  • Loses nothing if the project fails

Champion

  • May start out skeptical, asking only hard questions
  • Speaks up when the project gets caught in politics
  • Personally opens the door to the budget owner for you
  • Ties their personal reputation to the outcome

Where to look: on the front lines, not at the top

Many engineers' instinct is to seek out the person with the biggest title. In reality, the backing of a respected colleague often carries more weight than a meeting with a C-level executive, because that person can open up stakeholder channels invisible on the org chart, as UserGems also emphasizes.

The first place to look is end users: identify them early, and you'll discover who might become your strongest advocate. This advice was originally written by Close.com for salespeople. Forward Deployed Engineers (FDEs) are in an even better position: you sit next to end users every day, and you can see who is tormented by the old process and whose opinion the whole team seeks out.

In Tryolabs' FDE model, the first week is spent immersing in the client's real environment: mapping systems and aligning stakeholders. These two things happen simultaneously. Follow a data flow downstream and you'll meet the people who actually run it—they're your first candidate list.

Example: two candidates at a logistics company

Suppose you're deploying an AI agent that helps dispatch vehicles for the night shift. The first candidate is Minh, an IT team lead who always replies to Slack messages within five minutes and publicly praised the demo. The second is Ha, a shift supervisor who only asks hard questions, but every dispatcher with a tricky shift goes to her for advice.

Cultivating a champion is a two-way relationship: following the approach Close.com describes, treat the person as a colleague and an expert, give value first, then make requests. So your first move with Ha isn't a request at all—it's helping her solve the shift handover report she still has to manually assemble every morning.

Two weeks later, with pilot data in hand, it's time to test. The most practical method is to ask the candidate to introduce you to the budget owner, and see if it happens. The conversation might go like this:

You: Next week we'll have two weeks of night-shift data. Do you think the operations director should take a look before the quarterly review? Ha: Yes. He meets with the shift supervisors on Thursday; I'll ask for fifteen minutes. I'll cover the operations part, you handle the technical part.

Ask Minh the same question and you get: "He's pretty busy, I'll see." That's exactly the dividing line Spotlight.ai draws: a true champion creates access, while a supporter apologizes that they can't. Minh is still useful for system permissions, but the person worth investing in is Ha.

Champions need someone backing them from above

Finding Ha isn't the end. According to a study by the Construction Industry Institute, champions during implementation often feel that management provides very little support for adopting new ways of working. A champion fighting alone burns out, or gets sidelined by leadership at the first round of cuts.

The VP of Forward-Deployed Engineering at Salesforce says that to secure real commitment, you must take the conversation to the right level. The champion and the executive sponsor are two different roles: one pulls from below, the other protects from above. So your first request to the champion should be to arrange a meeting with that sponsor.

Don't let the champion become an evangelist

There's a risk in the opposite direction too. A case study presented at the 2005 AMCIS conference warns that a champion quietly turning into an evangelist harms business outcomes, especially when that person is an executive. They'll keep defending a system that no longer fits.

The safeguard is keeping the champion objective. Give Ha the bad numbers too, including cases where the AI agent failed, so she defends the project with data rather than belief.

A more durable approach is turning one person into a team. Tryolabs explicitly writes that "an adoption metrics baseline and a trained group of internal champions" is a deliverable of the adoption phase. A team with data outlasts one enthusiastic individual.

Mistakes that cost you your champion

The most common mistake is treating the fastest responder as the influential person, like Minh. The second is asking before giving: demanding a meeting with the director in week one, before you've solved any problem for them.

The third is letting the champion carry the project alone without a sponsor. The last is handing off to only one person. The moment that person changes jobs, the project becomes an orphan.

In FDE job descriptions, phrases like "stakeholder management" or "drive adoption" usually refer to exactly this skill. Prepare a concrete story for your resume and interviews: how you identified who the champion was, what test you used to confirm it, and what the champion group you left behind could accomplish.

Your code runs on the client's servers. But whether the project survives depends on what someone on the client's side thinks, at precisely the moment you're not in the room.
