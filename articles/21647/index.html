Product development refers to the entire set of activities that consistently drive a product (product or service) to solve user or market problems, from planning through design, development, release, and improvement.

It is not simply "building software"; the essence of product development is defining "whose problem will be solved, and how," and continuously realizing it as a product.

The main components of product development are as follows.

ElementDescription
Why (why build it)Defining the problems to solve and business goals
Who (for whom)Identifying target users and personas
What (what to build)Designing product features and value propositions
How (how to build it)Technology selection, development methods, team structure
When (when to deliver)Roadmaps, sprint planning, release strategy
How much (how to grow it)Setting KPIs and operating improvement cycles

Integrating and managing all of these is the overall picture of product development.

"Product development" and "system development" are easily confused, but they are fundamentally different concepts.

Comparison itemProduct developmentSystem development
Main purposeSolving user problems and creating business valueBuilding systems that meet client requirements
Origin of requirementsMarket and user researchClient's RFP and specifications
Definition of completionValue is delivered to users and KPIs improveMeeting the specifications in the requirements document
Involvement after developmentContinuous improvement and growthTransitions to delivery and maintenance
Typical rolesPdM, product owner, UX designerPM, SE, programmer
Definition of failureNot used by users, KPIs don't improveDoesn't work as specified, many bugs

For example, when a company orders work from an SIer with the goal of "streamlining internal attendance management," that is system development. On the other hand, an activity such as "launching a SaaS to solve attendance management problems for small and medium-sized businesses" falls under product development.

The biggest difference lies in "whether you define for whom and what to build yourself." In product development, the team continuously makes decisions proactively, from problem discovery to KPI improvement.

Why are so many companies emphasizing product development now? There are three structural changes behind it.

1. Spread of the SaaS business model The shift from a "sell the product outright" business to a "continuously charge for it as a service" model is accelerating. In SaaS, users' continued use directly affects revenue, so the product development philosophy of continuously improving the product even after release has become essential.

2. Acceleration of market change Competitor entry cycles have shortened, and cases where requirements defined three years ago diverge from current market needs are increasing. The waterfall approach of "building exactly what was specified" can no longer keep up with market changes, so product development methods that quickly iterate on hypothesis testing are valued.

3. Shift to in-house development driven by DX promotion The movement to "bring system development that was outsourced to vendors in-house and own the product ourselves" is spreading even among large enterprises. With in-house development, companies need to make product decisions themselves, and organizational capability for product development has become a source of competitive advantage.

Product development has no fixed "finished form." Its essence is a cycle of continuous improvement while engaging with users and the market. However, there is a clear flow, especially in the early phases. Below, it is organized into 6 steps.

[Problem discovery] → [Planning and design] → [UX/UI design] → [Requirements definition and design] → [Development and implementation] → [Release and improvement]

The starting point of product development is not "building what you want to build," but "finding the problem to solve."

The main activities in this phase are as follows.

  • User interviews: Talk directly with target users to understand latent problems and behavioral patterns
  • Observational research (ethnography): Observe users' actual behavior to discover problems that have not been verbalized
  • Quantitative data analysis: If an existing product is available, form hypotheses about issues from access logs, exit rates, and other data
  • Competitive research: Analyze the strengths and weaknesses of similar products to identify differentiation points

Common failure: Skipping this phase and starting a project on a vague "hunch." Products built without user research tend to end up as "functional, but unused by anyone."

Once the problem is clear, design the concept of "what product will solve it."

In this phase, create documents that answer the following questions.

  • Product vision: The worldview this product aims for and its ultimate goal
  • Value proposition: What value it provides to users and how it differentiates from competitors
  • Target user definition: Who will use this product, and in what situations
  • Business model: How it will be monetized and what the KPIs are

An effective framework for concept design is the Lean Canvas (explained in detail in Chapter 4). By organizing the nine elements of a business model on a single sheet, you can quickly align the direction of the entire team.

Once the concept is solidified, design the actual user experience (UX) and interface (UI).

The key point of this phase is to "fail fast and cheap."

  • Information architecture (IA): Organize screen structure, navigation flows, and content priorities
  • Wireframes: Represent screen layouts as simple line drawings to confirm feature placement
  • Prototypes: Build interactive mockups using tools such as Figma or Adobe XD
  • User testing: Have target users try the prototype to identify UX issues early

By conducting user testing before writing a single line of code, you can greatly reduce the cost of rework caused by UX problems discovered after development.

Once the UX/UI direction is set, define the "requirements" and "technical design" for engineers to implement.

  • Functional requirements: Describe specifically what the product can do (e.g., users can log in with an email address and password)
  • Non-functional requirements: Define quality-related requirements such as performance, security, and scalability
  • Technical design: Decide on the architecture, technology stack, API design, database design, and more
  • Development plan: Set sprint plans and milestones, and break tasks down according to team capacity

In this phase, collaboration between the PM (product manager) and the engineering lead becomes especially important. They are expected to adjust priorities based on an understanding of both business requirements and technical constraints.

This is the phase where code is actually written and the product is built. In modern product development, the following practices have become standard.

  • Sprint development: Develop in short cycles of one to two weeks and continuously deliver a working product
  • Code review: The team reviews implemented code to ensure quality and share knowledge
  • CI/CD (continuous integration/continuous delivery): Build automated testing and deployment pipelines to lower release costs
  • Feature flags: Gradually release new features to specific user groups to minimize risk

Release is not the goal; it is the beginning of hypothesis validation.

  • Staged release (beta, closed beta): Release early to a subset of users and collect feedback
  • Data measurement: Monitor KPIs such as DAU, retention rate, and conversion rate
  • A/B testing: Randomly serve variations of features or screens to verify their effectiveness
  • Collecting user feedback: Gather qualitative information through in-app surveys, customer support, and interviews

Based on the collected data and feedback, form the next improvement hypothesis and keep cycling through steps 1 to 6. This is the feedback loop, the essence of product development.

Product development methodologies are broadly divided into two types: "waterfall" and "agile." Neither is inherently superior; it is important to choose based on the nature of the project.

Waterfall development is a methodology that proceeds through the stages of requirements definition → design → implementation → testing → release in order, from upstream to downstream. Its name comes from the image of a waterfall flowing from top to bottom.

Advantages

  • Deliverables (documents) for each phase are clear, making quality control easier
  • Even with large teams, roles are clearly divided, making parallel work on stages easier
  • Progress is easy to manage, and explanation and approval processes with clients are easier to go through
  • Well suited to large-scale, long-term projects where requirements do not change

Disadvantages

  • Difficult to accommodate specification changes after requirements definition, and change costs are high
  • Since user reactions cannot be checked before release, there is a high risk of "building something no one uses"
  • Problems tend to surface late in testing, making rework costs large

Suitable cases

  • Industries such as government and finance, where specification changes are rare and strict process management is required
  • Core system renewal projects with clearly defined requirements
  • Cases where outsourcing to an external vendor requires contractually defined deliverables

Agile development is an iterative methodology that repeats planning, development, release, and improvement in short cycles (sprints). It originated from the "Agile Manifesto" published in 2001, and multiple practical methods exist, including Scrum, XP, and Kanban.

  • Can flexibly respond to specification changes and market shifts
  • Since a working product is delivered in short cycles, user feedback can be obtained early
  • Problems are easy to detect and address early, keeping risk low
  • Team autonomy increases, making it easier to boost motivation and productivity
  • Overall scope and costs are hard to see, making it difficult to explain to stakeholders
  • Team members need an agile mindset and practical skills
  • The product owner must continuously commit to decision-making
  • Documentation tends to be sparse, creating challenges when handing over to a new team
  • Projects with fluid requirements that require hypothesis testing, such as startups and new businesses
  • Products that are continuously improved based on user reactions, such as SaaS and BtoC apps
  • Small to mid-sized autonomous product teams

The current mainstream approach is "a hybrid model based on agile, combined with waterfall-style planning phases as needed." For example, an approach where the overall direction and technical architecture are decided in a waterfall manner, and then feature development is run using Scrum, is widely adopted.

Frameworks are not "magic tools"; they are tools for organizing a team's thinking and speeding up decision-making. This section explains 14 frameworks actually used in the field, organized by product development phase.

Definition: The classic framework for organizing your company's or product's Strengths, Weaknesses, Opportunities, and Threats.

Internal environmentExternal environment
StrengthsOpportunities
WeaknessesThreats

Use case: Used to align understanding within the team when discussing market entry strategy for a new product.

Example: When developing SaaS for small and medium-sized businesses, organize it as "Strengths = deep relationships with existing customers," "Weaknesses = low brand recognition," "Opportunities = increased IT investment by SMBs through DX subsidies," and "Threats = price competition from major SaaS providers."

Definition: A framework that visualizes the positioning of your product and competitors in the market using a two-axis matrix.

Use case: Used to identify differentiation points for your product based on competitive research results.

Example: By plotting competitors with the vertical axis as "price (high/low)" and the horizontal axis as "feature richness (feature-rich/simple)," you can discover an open space such as "low price × simple."

Definition: A one-page business model design tool developed by Ash Maurya, based on the methodology of Eric Ries's "The Lean Startup." It consists of 9 blocks.

BlockContent
ProblemThe top 3 problems the target audience faces
Customer SegmentsDefinition of target users
Unique Value Proposition (UVP)A one-line definition of value differentiated from competitors
SolutionKey features and services that solve the problems
ChannelsReach and distribution paths to users
Revenue StreamsRevenue model and pricing
Cost StructureKey cost items
Key MetricsKPIs for measuring success
Unfair AdvantageAn advantage that cannot be easily copied

Use case: In the early stages of launching a new product, the whole team organizes business model hypotheses on a single page. Because the business plan can be expressed on one page instead of a lengthy business document, decision-making speed increases dramatically.

Definition: A framework developed by Alex Osterwalder to verify the fit between "what users want" and "what the product can provide."

It matches the "Product Map" on the left (value proposition, pain relievers, gain creators) with the "Customer Profile" on the right (jobs, pains, gains).

Use case: Used when verifying whether the product truly solves users' problems, and in discussions on prioritizing new features.

Definition: A definition of the typical persona of target users as a concrete profile.

Main items to include in a persona:

  • Basic attributes (age, occupation, job title, place of residence, etc.)
  • Behavioral characteristics (workflows, frequently used tools, information gathering methods)
  • Problems and concerns (what specific problems they face)
  • Goals and motivations (what they want to achieve)
  • Values and decision criteria (what they prioritize when making choices)

Example: When developing BtoB SaaS, define a persona such as "Makoto Tanaka, 38 years old, IT department manager at a manufacturing company with 300 employees, feels pain from spending 8 hours a week on Excel-based aggregation work, wants to streamline reporting to his boss."

Caution: A persona should be "collective knowledge based on data about real people"; if created from assumptions, it becomes meaningless. Always create personas based on user interviews and behavioral data.

Definition: A framework for visualizing what users "see, hear, think, and feel" in order to gain deep empathy.

The 6 blocks of an empathy map:

  • Think & Feel: What the user is thinking and feeling internally
  • See: What environments, information, and people the user sees
  • Hear: What the user hears from people and information around them
  • Say & Do: What they actually say and how they act
  • Pain: The fears, frustrations, and obstacles users experience
  • Gain: The outcomes, pleasures, and sense of achievement users want

Use case: Effective for deepening team understanding of personas through a workshop after user interviews. Particularly used to help UX designers and PMs build a shared understanding.

Definition: The concept that users "hire" a product to get a certain job done. Proposed by Clayton Christensen of Harvard University and others.

Famous JTBD example: "People don't buy a drill because they want a drill; they buy it because they want a hole in the wall." Similarly, by focusing not on a product's "features" but on the "job" users want to get done, unexpected competitive alternatives become visible.

Three types of jobs:

  • Functional Job: What users actually want to accomplish (e.g., quickly aggregate data)
  • Emotional Job: How users want to feel (e.g., be seen as competent by their boss)
  • Social Job: How users want to be perceived by others (e.g., be seen as driving DX initiatives)

Use case: Used when planning new features to dig deeper into "what users' real needs are."

Definition: A framework that visualizes, in chronological order, the entire series of experiences (journey) a user goes through from noticing a problem to continuing to use the product.

Key elements to include in the map:

  • Phases (awareness, comparison/consideration, first use, continued use, advocacy, etc.)
  • User actions (what they do)
  • Touchpoints (where they come into contact)
  • Emotions (how they feel)
  • Pains and gains (problems and satisfactions at that stage)

Use case: Used to get an overview of the product's overall UX and identify which phase has the biggest problems. Also effective when discussing improvement priorities for an existing product.

Definition: The most widely adopted practical framework for agile development, systematized by Ken Schwaber and Jeff Sutherland in the 1990s.

Key elements of Scrum:

ElementDescription
SprintA 1-4 week development cycle
Product backlogA prioritized list of features and tasks to be developed
Sprint backlogThe list of tasks to be handled in the current sprint
Daily scrumA daily 15-minute meeting to share progress and obstacles
Sprint reviewA meeting at the end of the sprint to demo the working product
Sprint retrospectiveA meeting to reflect on and improve the team's process

Roles:

  • Product Owner (PO): The person responsible for maximizing business value. Manages the backlog.
  • Scrum Master (SM): Supports the practice of Scrum and removes obstacles for the team.
  • Development team: The self-organizing team that does the actual development (ideally 3-9 members).

Example: A typical use case is a startup mobile app development team building an MVP (minimum viable product) over 8 sprints (4 months) using 2-week sprints.

Definition: A product that can be released with minimal functionality. The accurate definition of an MVP is "a product with the minimum features needed for validation that can still deliver value to users."

Common misconceptions about MVPs:

  • ❌ "A compromised product with lowered quality" → ⭕ "A strategic product focused on the purpose of validation"
  • ❌ "Building only 20% of all features" → ⭕ "A product that can validate one core value hypothesis"

MVP design process:

  • Identify the single hypothesis (risk) you most want to validate
  • Identify the minimum features needed to validate that hypothesis
  • Implement only the essential features and release
  • Collect user data and feedback to validate the hypothesis
  • Decide whether to "pivot or persevere" based on the validation results

Related article: To learn more about MVPs, see 3 Reasons MVP Development Is Suited to New Business Development and Keys to Success

Definition: A framework developed by Google and systematized by Jake Knapp that takes an idea through to validation in 5 days.

The 5-day process:

  • Monday (Map): Define the problem and organize long-term goals and obstacles
  • Tuesday (Sketch): Individually sketch solution ideas
  • Wednesday (Decide): Evaluate the ideas and choose the most promising one
  • Thursday (Prototype): Build a realistic-looking prototype in one day
  • Friday (Test): Test the prototype with 5 target users

Use case: When you want to quickly validate the direction of a new product, or solve a major problem in an existing product in a short time. It's an intensive process said to deliver "months' worth of answers in one week."

Definition: A framework for quantitatively evaluating the priority of features and tasks. It calculates a "RICE score" by combining scores from four metrics.

RICE score formula:

RICE score = (Reach × Impact × Confidence) / Effort

MetricDefinitionHow to evaluate
ReachNumber of users affected within a given periodActual number (e.g., 1,000 per month)
ImpactDegree of impact per user3 = high, 2 = medium, 1 = low, 0.5 = minimal, 0.25 = tiny
ConfidenceConfidence in the estimate100% = high, 80% = medium, 50% = low
EffortPerson-months required for implementationActual number (e.g., 2 person-months)

Concrete example: If "improving the dashboard UI" has Reach=500, Impact=2, Confidence=80%, and Effort=1, the RICE score = 500 × 2 × 0.8 / 1 = 800. Comparing numbers prevents prioritization based on subjective feelings like "this seems kind of important."

Definition: A goal management framework that originated at Intel and became widespread through Google's adoption. It defines "what you want to achieve (Objective)" together with "metrics to measure achievement (Key Results)."

Characteristics of OKR:

  • Set quarterly, with weekly check-ins on Key Results progress
  • Objectives are set qualitatively and ambitiously, while Key Results are set quantitatively and measurably
  • A 60-70% achievement rate is considered the "appropriate difficulty" (100% achievement means the goal was too easy)
  • Not directly tied to compensation (to maintain psychological safety)

Example of use in product development:

  • Objective： ユーザーが毎日使いたくなるプロダクトにする
    • KR1: Improve weekly active user rate from 40% to 60%
    • KR2: Improve NPS (Net Promoter Score) from +15 to +35
    • KR3: Improve tutorial completion rate from 35% to 65%

Definition: A visual work management framework that applies the Toyota Production System to software development. Tasks are managed on a board divided into columns such as "Todo, In Progress, Done."

Differences between Scrum and Kanban:

Comparison itemScrumKanban
CycleSprints (fixed periods)Continuous flow (no periods)
Role definitionsYes (PO, SM, development team)None
WIP limitsIndirectly limited by the sprint backlogExplicitly limited per column
Suitable teamsNew product development teamsOperations, support, and maintenance teams

Use case: Suited for teams managing randomly occurring tasks such as bug fixes and customer support. Many teams practice it using the board views of Jira, Trello, or Notion.

I have introduced 14 frameworks, but that does not mean you should use all of them. Choosing according to the situation is what matters.

Project phaseRecommended frameworks
New product idea stageLean Canvas, SWOT analysis, VPC

Ongoing feature developmentScrum, RICE, OKR

Also, frameworks are merely "tools to aid thinking." What matters is not "filling in" the framework, but "raising the quality of team discussions" through the framework.

The market value of PMs and engineers involved in product development is rising rapidly. Behind this lies the following structural gap between demand and supply.

Demand-side factors:

  • Expansion of companies' needs for in-house development driven by DX promotion
  • Maturation of the startup ecosystem (increasing funding amounts)
  • A chronic shortage of product-thinking talent due to the mainstreaming of SaaS business models
  • Acceleration of product development speed through AI and generative AI (and the scarcity of talent who can oversee it)

Supply-side constraints:

  • The absolute number of "people who understand products from both technical and business perspectives" is small
  • Talent with practical experience in Scrum, JTBD, OKR, etc. takes several years to develop through new-graduate hiring
  • Many people have contracting or SES experience at large companies, but those with product development ownership experience are rare

This supply-demand gap is driving up the market value of talent with product development skills.

For engineers and PMs who want to "leverage product development skills to win high-rate freelance projects," here is a roadmap for building up skills.

First, gain experience overseeing the entire product development process.

  • Development experience on a Scrum team (ideally also serving as PO or SM)
  • Experience conducting user interviews
  • Practice with frameworks such as Lean Canvas and RICE scoring
  • Experience designing, measuring, and improving KPIs

Next, create a specialized area by combining your strengths.

  • For those from engineering: technical understanding × product strategy
  • For those from design: UX design × product thinking
  • For those from business: market analysis × product planning

At this stage, building one or two "track records of driving product development proactively" will be your greatest asset in winning your first project after going freelance.

There are three key factors for raising your rates after going freelance.

  • Articulating your track record: Can you speak concretely not about "what you did" but about "how you improved specific KPIs"?
  • Choosing the right agent: Choose an agent that is strong in product development projects and has a high percentage of direct transactions with end clients.
  • Signaling your expertise: Broaden your recognition through LAPs, speaking at events, posting technical articles on Zenn, and more.
