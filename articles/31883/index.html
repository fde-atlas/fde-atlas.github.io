Project economics: getting the numbers straight for both sides

Original title: 项目经济性：把双方的账算清楚

pafa.github.io · 2020-08-25 · 14 min read

Summary

Using a hypothetical 800,000 RMB teaching contract, the article shows how to keep three separate ledgers for an FDE project: customer value, supplier project economics, and product investment. A 27.5% direct margin turns into a loss once pre-sales and shared costs are included. Customer time savings count as cash benefit only when they replace real spending, with breakeven around 417 outsourced hours per month. The author also covers scope changes, sunk-cost decisions to continue or exit, cash-flow gaps, hidden costs, and why the second customer best reveals whether earlier investment was reusable.

Key points

  • Three ledgers must be kept separately: customer, supplier project, and product.
  • A 27.5% direct margin becomes a 50,000 RMB loss under a wider cost scope.
  • Time savings count only when replacing real spending; breakeven is ~417 hours/month.
  • Efficiency gains may be passed to customers or their customers via repricing.
  • Decisions should compare future income and costs, not total project losses.

A project has finally gone live, the customer is willing to renew, and the engineering team has received praise. Operators still need to ask a plain question: what exactly did we spend to earn this revenue?

The answer is often scattered across different places. On-site engineers are booked to the delivery department, product engineers helping with troubleshooting are booked to R&D, pre-sales trials never enter the contract, and the customer's own staffing investment certainly never appears on the vendor's books. If you only look at contract value and headcount on site, a project that seems profitable may have been borrowing resources from other teams all along.

The FDE model deserves careful accounting precisely because it combines services, product learning, and long-term customer relationships. The investment may be reused in the future, or it may become a lasting maintenance burden. The two outcomes look similar at the start of a project: in both, people are busy solving the current customer's problems.

First, put the three ledgers on the same table

The first is the customer's ledger. What did the new way of working save, what did it add, and did it improve the outcomes the customer truly cares about? The second is the vendor's project ledger. Can the revenue from this delivery cover the costs incurred for it? The third is the product ledger. Can the capabilities left behind by the project help other customers, and how much shared R&D budget should carry them?

The three ledgers cannot stand in for one another. The customer saving time does not mean the vendor is profitable; the vendor winning a high-gross-margin contract does not mean the customer received corresponding value; and a project losing money this period cannot automatically be counted as successful R&D just because the team says it "learned a lot."

Palantir: How Field Problems Enter the Product notes that Palantir's 2020 listing documents examined customer acquisition, relationship expansion, and operations at scale separately. What is borrowed here is the mindset that early-stage investment and mature customers should be analyzed separately; the company's self-defined metrics cannot be used directly as the profit in this chapter's project ledger, nor do they represent any individual FDE's output. 1

Phase division guarantees no outcome for any project. Whether the early investment is worth bearing still comes down to verifiable costs and subsequent usage.

A project ledger that can be checked line by line

Everything below uses hypothetical numbers to illustrate the method; they are not industry quotes, salary levels, or any company's real contract. This is a standalone 800,000-yuan contract example, not combined with the earlier maintenance team's one-week schedule into the same project. Assume a vendor takes on one phase of delivery, starting with a baseline that adds no new equipment types: contract revenue of 800,000 yuan, with the delivery scope and payment terms already clear. For simplicity, taxes and revenue-recognition timing are set aside for now.

Three engineers each invest 50 working days, at a fully loaded personnel cost of 3,000 yuan per person-day, totaling 450,000 yuan. The per-day rate here includes salaries, employer-paid benefits, and related personnel costs, and excludes the shared facilities and administrative expenses listed separately below; it is not the same as an employee's daily wage. In addition, product support and customer coordination by people outside these three total 25 person-days, priced at 2,400 yuan, or 60,000 yuan. Project-specific cloud resources and model calls cost 40,000 yuan, and travel plus dedicated reviews cost 30,000 yuan.

22 divided by 80 gives 27.5%. At this point, the operator can say "under the currently listed scope, the project has 27.5% remaining," but cannot yet call it net profit. Upfront customer acquisition, shared R&D, administration, capital costs, and other expenses not yet listed still need to be handled in their respective ledgers.

Now consider the expanded-scope branch: the customer adds one equipment type, requiring one engineer to work 20 more days, plus an extra 20,000 yuan in model and cloud costs. If the original contract is unchanged, the incremental investment is 60,000 yuan in personnel costs plus 20,000 yuan in resource costs. Project costs rise to 660,000 yuan, leaving 140,000 yuan, and the corresponding ratio drops to 17.5%.

The interface may show just one more option, but the business outcome has already changed. How hard the engineers worked cannot explain the 80,000 yuan. It comes from a change in delivery scope and resource investment, and it needs to be identified, recorded, and decided upon.

Return to the baseline without a scope change: there are also 120,000 yuan of pre-sales trials and customer-acquisition investment not yet counted, plus 150,000 yuan of shared facilities and administrative expenses allocated according to a pre-agreed basis. The 270,000 yuan here explicitly excludes the personnel, dedicated resources, and support work in the earlier table, and the same invoice or the same hours must not be counted twice.

Both columns can stand, because they answer the same question under different scopes. A scope change does not disappear when the accounting scope widens. These are still teaching business ledgers; they do not cover taxes, capital, or all company-level projects, and are not called statutory net profit.

A real project does not need to build a complex system first; it can start by attaching to every investment a date, the work object, the amount of effort, the attribution, and a confirmer. For example, if a product engineer spends two days fixing an interface this customer must use, and the project lead confirms it, it goes into the 60,000-yuan support line in the earlier table; if another three days are approved by the product lead to build a general capability other customers could also use, only those three days are listed separately as product investment. You cannot report all five days to delivery and then allocate the three days back from R&D.

Ongoing maintenance should be confirmed by the operations lead for service period and resources; support that has not yet occurred should be listed as a future budget, not mixed in as incurred cost. For shared costs, first remove the portions already directly attributed, then allocate the rest by usage, person-days, or another reasonable basis chosen before the project, keeping any unallocated balance. Methods may differ, but do not keep changing the method within the same period just to improve a particular project table.

This way, if management accepts a phased loss in exchange for reusable capability, it must be clear what the capability is, who maintains it, and when to check whether others are using it—not just rename the expense to a different department.

How the customer's "time saved" becomes value

Return to the 800,000-yuan contract without the added equipment type, but now account from the customer's side. Assume 12 months of observation after the phase-one launch: 10,000 work items processed per month, each saving six minutes of old steps but adding two minutes of review. Net time released is about 667 hours per month, or 8,000 hours a year. This is not an automatically reduced payroll: staff may still be on duty, and the time may go toward clearing backlogs.

The customer pays 800,000 yuan for the contract, plus 80,000 yuan of internal preparation and training investment, of which 60,000 yuan is existing staff time occupied and 20,000 yuan is new training spend. Another 120,000 yuan is paid for operations and support in the 12 months after launch, explicitly excluding the 40,000 yuan of deployment-period resources already listed in the vendor's phase-one project. The customer's first-period economic investment is thus 1,000,000 yuan, with new cash outlays of 940,000 yuan; the 60,000 yuan of internal hours carries an opportunity cost but generates no new salary payments in this example.

How do time gains enter this ledger? Suppose observation records show that 400 hours per month of the released time replaced outsourced work that would genuinely have been purchased and for which procurement has since stopped, at an outsourcing rate of 200 yuan per hour; the remaining roughly 267 hours went to clearing backlogs and are not recorded as cash gains. Verifiable annual savings are 960,000 yuan. It is not all 8,000 hours multiplied by the rate, nor is the shortened backlog counted a second time as revenue.

Same contract, first-period investment plus 12 months after launchAmount or result

This set of teaching observations allows two statements to hold simultaneously: the customer's new cash inflows and outflows have improved slightly, and the initial economic investment has not yet been fully recovered. The supplier's broad-basis remaining surplus in the earlier table is negative 50,000 yuan. Neither party can use the other's numbers to declare its own project profitable. Whether to continue depends on whether the backlog improvement is worthwhile, whether subsequent costs decline, and whether service quality has suffered.

Change one more condition and the conclusion may reverse. If the outsourced work actually stopped being procured is only 300 hours per month, the annual savings drop to 720,000 yuan, the customer's economic difference becomes negative 280,000 yuan and the cash difference negative 220,000 yuan; to cover the 1 million yuan initial economic investment at this outsourcing rate, about 417 hours per month would need to be genuinely cut. 417 is the result of this example's calculation, not an industry threshold that FDE projects must meet.

This is also why research findings need to keep their original scope. The 2024 revised summary of "Generative AI at Work" uses data from 5,172 customer service agents, reporting an average 15% increase in issues resolved per hour, while noting that the changes differ across experience groups. This result cannot be directly turned into "profits rose 15% after adopting AI," let alone into a universal return on investment for the FDE model. 2

For an FDE, important work is helping the customer put measurement back into the process. How a piece of work originally started, when it counted as complete, which exceptions were excluded, and whether it was passed to others—all should be kept as comparable as possible. Otherwise the team may simply move the difficult parts outside the scope of the statistics.

When costs come down, who keeps the gains

The accounting above also assumed one thing: the price for completing the same work has not changed. But once a technology becomes easy to obtain, customers will renegotiate, and competitors may lower their quotes. The engineering team created the efficiency; how much of it the operating entity can retain is the next question.

Still using the baseline without expanded scope, this time we compare only the supplier's direct project surplus. The original quote was 800,000 yuan, direct costs 580,000 yuan, leaving a surplus of 220,000 yuan. If tool progress brings the same-scope cost down to 480,000 yuan, with the quote unchanged the surplus becomes 320,000 yuan; but if the next contract of the same scope can only be sold for 700,000 yuan, the surplus returns to 220,000 yuan. Delivery has indeed become cheaper, yet the added 100,000 yuan benefit is passed to the customer through price. These figures are assumed conditions, not a forecast of market price cuts; the direct surplus still does not deduct the aforementioned customer acquisition and shared investments.

The customer needs to run this check too. Paying less for outsourcing improves current cash flow; if the customer's own products also drop in price with competition, the measured savings may not all be retained as profit. Conversely, shorter waits and more stable service may help the customer win more orders. Which one happens depends on actual prices, volumes, and service quality—you cannot simply multiply saved hours by a unit price once.

Therefore, this book suggests adding another column to the benefits table: Who can reprice at the next transaction? The supplier, the customer, and the customer's customers may each take away different portions in turn. An FDE cannot decide the profits of the entire industry chain, but can make commitments concrete: whether this improvement is in processing capacity, cash expenditure, or already observed business gains. If renewal or the peak season has not yet been crossed, leave the question of whether value can be retained for the next round of checks.

Model fees are conspicuous; hidden costs deserve more searching

API bills are easy to notice because they appear regularly. But in a project's early stages, checking permissions, interpreting data, clarifying requirements, and handling exceptions can also consume substantial resources. Not all of these should be eliminated—some are precisely the work of understanding what the customer needs. Operations management must distinguish: which are one-time learning costs, which recur with every additional customer, and which keep growing with usage.

Whether one-time learning costs can be reused must be proven by subsequent projects. If a connector—software that lets two systems exchange data—still requires major rework for the second customer, the commonality originally identified may have been insufficient. Costs that grow with usage need corresponding capacity and pricing. If revenue is fixed while model calls, manual reviews, and support requests rise with transaction volume, successful adoption could actually worsen the supplier's profit.

Another kind of cost comes from the commitment itself. Providing rapid response for critical systems means retaining the capacity to mobilize people. Staff not currently handling tickets does not mean this commitment is free. If everyone is fully booked and then response commitments are sold, the organization can only interrupt other customers when failures arrive.

The customer's internal investment should likewise be made visible at project initiation. Business experts need to provide examples, data owners need to organize fields, and frontline staff need to learn new steps. Excluding these costs makes the supplier's proposal look cheap, but the customer may be unable to cooperate and thus slow to obtain results.

Billing models themselves also change. In Microsoft's Copilot announcement of September 25, 2026, everyday subscription licensing was separated from usage-based billing for long-running tasks; Home, Code, and Autopilot each launched in phases, with Autopilot still in private preview at the time. 3 Having bought seats does not mean continuously executing tasks can be treated as having no incremental cost. Customers need to know who can add tasks, who sees usage, and how to continue or pause once budget conditions are reached.

Model unit prices should also be recalculated per task, not just compared against price lists. For GPT-6.1 Sol, launched on September 29, the announcement listed standard API prices per million tokens (the unit by which the model measures input and output): input 2 dollars, cached input 0.10 dollars, output 10 dollars. 4 Caching is the reuse of already-processed input; input, reuse ratio, output, tool execution, and failed retries together determine how much a job costs. The price assumptions in a teaching account need not be quietly updated to the latest rates, but real projects should record the date of the quoted prices and compare old and new options on the same workload.

Why the second customer is the most revealing

The first project shows whether the team can get something done. The second project begins to show what the previous investment actually left behind.

The second delivery should first return to the earlier investment table. Suppose the data integration work within the previous period's 150 engineering person-days actually took 20 fewer person-days at the second customer—at the original teaching rate, that is 60,000 yuan less investment. But if the shared tools also need 10 person-days of maintenance, the 60,000 yuan cannot all be counted as net gain. The second project's actual reduction in work should be listed separately from new ongoing maintenance, and the product owner should confirm the scope of shared investment coverage.

Development costs already paid in the previous project still exist when reviewing total investment; since they did not recur in the second project, they cannot be counted again as new cash expenditure. Conversely, future ongoing maintenance cannot be zeroed out just because "the code already exists." Which capabilities are worth extracting into products is left to Every Customer Is Different: How Products Grow.

The UK government's digital procurement guidance requires considering knowledge, data, and handover arrangements before a contract ends. Although it addresses public procurement, this full-lifecycle perspective also reminds commercial projects: handover and exit have costs, and they cannot vanish from estimates just because they occur after the first launch. 5

From today, is continued investment worthwhile

How much a project has lost since its start, and which path should be chosen today, are two different questions. Past investments that have already occurred and cannot be recovered must be acknowledged in the retrospective; when deciding the next step, one must compare what each option will bring from now on.

Return to the branch where equipment types have increased: planned revenue was 800,000 yuan and the broader-scope costs above were 930,000 yuan. Suppose that at a decision point, work corresponding to 600,000 yuan of revenue has been completed and 730,000 yuan of costs have been incurred; the remaining 200,000 yuan of revenue can only be obtained by completing the remaining delivery. The table below compares only revenue and investment from the current point onward; all incurred items are unrecoverable; it does not indicate when payments arrive—cash is listed separately below.

Comparing from the current pointRevenue obtainable laterInvestment still needed laterLater revenue minus investment

Both parties agree to terminate and complete the necessary handover0 yuan30,000 yuan-30,000 yuan

Offsetting the remaining contracted future revenue against investment, an agreed exit results in a net outlay of 30,000 yuan; counting from the project's start, the former leaves a negative 130,000 yuan and the latter a negative 160,000 yuan. Therefore, a "full-scope loss" alone is not enough to conclude "stop immediately." The second path is more attractive, but it presupposes the customer agreeing to adjust the contract; the vendor cannot unilaterally shrink its commitments to create profit.

This table has not yet finished the decision for the manager. One must confirm whether the remaining payments are reliable, what work the occupied staff would miss, and whether the new investment can deliver verifiable product capability. If waiting for customer approval requires continued weekly payments, the waiting period must be counted in; if an expense must be borne even after exit, you cannot pretend it drops to zero once you stop.

Whether the money is enough to keep cash flowing is another hurdle

The end-of-period net cash of 100,000 yuan cannot cover or refute the negative 50,000 yuan in the cost account; the difference between the two tables is the 150,000-yuan allocation scope. The company still has to pay for shared resources; only the timing and increment of those payments are not laid out in this table. Based on these month-end balances, the project's deepest shortfall is 120,000 yuan; the order of receipts and payments within a month is not listed, so actual cash preparation must check specific payment dates. If the second month's 360,000 yuan is delayed to the fourth month, the shortfall at the end of the third month becomes 460,000 yuan, while the end of the period remains 100,000 yuan. The project amount has not changed, but the company needs 340,000 yuan more in working capital.

Another sensitivity comes from rework and usage volume. Taking the 580,000 yuan of direct and allocated costs as the baseline, adding 10 engineering person-days shifts the broad-scope remainder from negative 50,000 yuan to negative 80,000 yuan. If, of the 40,000 yuan in deployment-period resource fees, 10,000 yuan is fixed and 30,000 yuan varies with usage, then doubling usage with everything else unchanged also turns the remainder into negative 80,000 yuan; if both happen at once, it becomes negative 110,000 yuan. The linear changes here only serve to expose assumptions; real usage may cross capacity tiers, so the multiples cannot be copied directly.

The 800,000-yuan contract work account puts both parties' scopes, the payment schedule, the sensitivities, and the three paths of continuing, shrinking, or exiting into downloadable CSV and JSON. First recompute with the original numbers, then change one condition. The more valuable discussion is often not arguing over a single ROI figure, but pointing out: which cost belongs to whom, which benefit has already been realized, and which payment arrived too late.

The three accounts of the same contract can now be placed side by side: whether the customer's benefits are realized, whether the vendor's costs are complete, and who bears the product investment.
