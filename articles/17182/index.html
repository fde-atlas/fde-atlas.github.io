How an FDE Builds an Ontology from Utterance Logs | Turning a Dictionary of Nouns and Verbs into an AI-Traversable Form

Original title: FDEが発話録からオントロジーを作る手順｜名詞と動詞の辞書をAIが辿れる形にする

TIMEWELL · 2026-09-15 · 12 min read

Summary

In the sixth article of TIMEWELL's FDE practice series, Hamamoto explains how to turn the noun-and-verb dictionary gathered from field interviews and observations into an ontology that AI agents can traverse. Citing Gruber's 1993 definition and Palantir Foundry's ontology structure, the author describes extracting five elements—nouns, verbs, relations, states, and exceptions—from interview transcripts, converting them into a graph, and updating the dictionary weekly against a working product. He contrasts this with conventional contracted data modeling, where verbs and exceptions live in separate documents and models are frozen after design. AI can propose candidates, but humans must resolve same-word-different-meaning cases. Handing dictionary ownership to the customer is the completion condition for FDE work.

Key points

  • An ontology structures a company's vocabulary so agents can act on it.
  • Extract five elements from transcripts: nouns, verbs, relations, states, exceptions.
  • Graph form lets agents traverse states, relations, and exceptions to answer queries.
  • Unlike contracted data modeling, verbs and exceptions are recorded with nouns and updated weekly.
  • Ownership transfers to the field; FDE becoming unnecessary marks completion.

Hello, this is Hamamoto from TIMEWELL, Inc.

This is the sixth article in the FDE practice series. In the previous five articles, I have written about the techniques for gathering information from the field. You take notes of nouns and verbs in interviews, verify them through observation, deepen their meaning with ethnography, structure them with the KJ method, and add numbers with video. In all five articles, a "dictionary of nouns and verbs" appears. This time, I will write about how to turn that dictionary into a form that AI can traverse.

In a previous article on the difference between FDEs and AI co-piloting support, I wrote that an agent cannot run without being taught nouns and verbs. For an agent to perform the act of "allocating an order," it needs to hold, in a machine-readable form, what an order means at that company, what allocating entails, and what comes before and after allocation. That form is an ontology. It sounds like a difficult word, but what it does is simply carve up the world using that company's own vocabulary and hand it to the machine. If you want to check first how much of your company's business terminology is in a form that can be handed to AI, please use the AI literacy diagnostic.

What is an ontology? Carving up the world in that company's own words

The word ontology comes from philosophy, but in information science, Tom Gruber defined it in 1993 as "an explicit specification of a conceptualization." It is something that explicitly describes, in a shareable form, what exists in a domain and how those things relate to each other 1. In short, it is a dictionary that writes down the nouns, verbs, and relationships of a domain without ambiguity.

Palantir is the company that placed this concept at the core of its enterprise products. According to the company's documentation, the ontology is a layer placed on top of integrated data that binds data to their real-world counterparts, and its components include object types, properties, links, action types, and functions 2. Objects correspond to nouns, links to relationships, and actions to verbs. A person who worked as an FDE at Palantir for eight years lists this "ontology" as one of the company's old internal terms 3, and you can see the structure here: when FDEs return the business meanings they learned in the field back into the product, the ontology is the receptacle for it.

Why is this needed in the era of AI agents? The reason is that agents act. An AI that merely summarizes text can work even if the definitions of a company's vocabulary are vague. But an agent told to "allocate orders awaiting approval and issue shipping instructions" cannot act unless it knows what "awaiting approval" means, what "allocating" means, and who has the authority to issue shipping instructions. Even if it acts, it will allocate incorrectly. The definitions of nouns and verbs are the basis of an agent's actions.

From utterance records to a dictionary. Extracting five things: nouns, verbs, relationships, states, and exceptions

Here comes the procedure. The materials are what was gathered in articles one through five of this series: interview transcripts, five-column observation records, three-layer ethnography notes, the names of KJ method clusters, and video counts. From these, we extract five things.

What to extractWhat it isExample from utterance recordsHow to write it in the dictionary
NounsNames of things used at that company"When an order comes in, we first check the inventory"Order, inventory
VerbsActions people perform on nouns"We allocate the inventory and secure it"Allocate (inventory to an order), secure (verbally by sales)
RelationshipsConnections between nouns"Every order is tied to a shipment"An order has a shipment (one-to-one or more)
StatesStages a noun can take"We don't touch anything awaiting approval"The states of an order are: received, awaiting approval, approved, shipped
ExceptionsUnusual routes and conditions"When it's urgent, the section chief stamps it"The approver is the manager, but for urgent classifications it is the section chief

Nouns are written as they are in the company's own words, without translating them into system terminology. If "allocation" and "securing" have different meanings, they are written as separate verbs. This is where the cluster names from the KJ method article come in handy. A cluster name like "the reality of approval has two stages: the section chief's judgment and the manager's stamp" becomes, as is, the definition of the verb "approve." The dictionary writes: "To approve means that the section chief makes the judgment and the manager stamps it." If the two-stage nature is written in the dictionary, the agent will not stop at the first stage.

Relationships and states are verified through observation and video. The utterance record says "every order is tied to a shipment," but when you look at the screen recordings, there may be orders marked complete without a shipment. In that case, you revise the relationship to "an order has a shipment, except for sample shipments." The gap between what is said and what is observed improves the dictionary's precision.

Exceptions are the most important and the most easily dropped. "When it's urgent, the section chief stamps it" will not appear in a feature list, but it goes into the dictionary. Whether the agent halts an urgent-classified order awaiting the manager's approval or routes it to the section chief is decided by this single line.

Use AI for the extraction. Feed it the transcripts and observation records and have it produce a list of candidates for the five categories. Machines are faster at producing candidates; dozens of nouns and verbs emerge from hundreds of fragments within minutes. However, humans make the final decisions. The reason is that the same word can carry different meanings depending on the department. "Securing" in sales and "securing" in the warehouse are different actions, and machine extraction will merge them into one. The dictionary writes them separately as "secure (sales)" and "secure (warehouse)," with a rule attached for deciding which meaning applies from context. Only someone who has thickened the meaning through ethnography can perform this splitting.

Turning the dictionary into a form machines can traverse. Graphs and agents

Once the dictionary is complete, turn it into a form machines can traverse: a graph, with nouns as nodes and relationships as edges. States are held as node attributes, verbs as operations permitted on nodes, and exceptions as conditions on those operations.

The reason for this form is that agents do not merely answer questions; they traverse and act. To answer the question "Can this order be shipped?", you must traverse in order from the order node through its state (is it approved?), its relationships (is inventory allocated?), and its exceptions (does section chief approval suffice for urgent classifications?). Simply searching a pile of documents cannot perform this traversal. GraphRAG, a method combining large language models with knowledge graphs to answer questions spanning entire documents, was systematized in 2024 4. Our ZEROCK is a product that holds internal knowledge in this form so that agents can traverse it, and the dictionary built in the field goes directly into the product's structure.

This connects to the "reusable primitives" discussion that a16z wrote about in its January 2026 essay. That piece frames the true FDE model as building on reusable primitives—such as data model and workflow components—rather than creating new functionality for each customer 5. The ontology is the most fundamental primitive. Structures like "orders have a status," "approvals can have multiple steps," and "exceptions branch by category" do not change from company to company. What changes are the status names, the number of steps, and the categories. So we put the structure into the product's standard schema, and make the names, step counts, and categories per-customer settings. The "approvals have two steps" we saw at three companies becomes the product's schema, and "at this company, the urgent category is handled by section chiefs" becomes a setting. The way of separating patterns and branches that I wrote about in my KJ method article lands here as the product's structure.

I wrote the details of how to run agents in my article on context engineering. Within the context passed to agents, the dictionary is the part that changes least and hurts most if wrong.

How this differs from contracted data modeling

Contracted development also has a data modeling phase. You identify entities, define relationships, and translate them into database design. At first glance, writing out nouns and relationships looks the same. Here is a table of the differences.

PerspectiveContracted data modelingFDE ontology
PurposeDesign the system's databaseCreate the basis on which AI agents act
SourceRequirements specificationOn-site utterances, observations, videos
Verbs and exceptionsWritten separately as functional specificationsWritten alongside nouns, with conditions
UpdatesFrozen at the design phaseUpdated weekly, in response to working software
AttributionThe customer's databaseThe pattern goes into the product's standard schema; branches are customer extensions
OwnerThe development sidePlaced with the business side, so it can be updated after the FDE leaves

The biggest difference is the handling of verbs and exceptions. Data modeling deals with nouns and relationships; verbs are written in a separate document as functional specifications, and exceptions are noted as "other." An ontology writes verbs and exceptions in the same place as nouns, with conditions. For an agent, the verb "approve" and the exception "urgent items go to section chiefs" are just as important as the noun "order."

Another difference is ownership. The owner of a contracted data model is the development side, and changes become modification contracts. An FDE's ontology places the owner on the business side. When a person on site edits the dictionary to say "the decision-maker for the urgent category has changed," the agent's behavior changes. Once that state is achieved, the FDE leaves the site. The FDE organization AWS launched in June 2026 is designed around leaving customers able to run on their own at the end 6, and for us, the condition for self-sufficiency is that the dictionary's owner is on the business side.

The on-site procedure: update the dictionary weekly and reflect it in working software

The procedure runs weekly. From Monday to Thursday we gather material on site, on Friday we form bundles with the KJ method, pick five names from the bundles to update the dictionary, on Monday of the next week feed the updated dictionary to the agent and deploy the working software, ask the person in charge "what's different?", and fix the dictionary. That one loop is one week.

The dictionary carries versions. It is normal for the definition of "approve" to change from "the manager stamps it" in week one, to "the section chief decides and the manager stamps it" in week two, to "the urgent category is section chiefs only" in week three. Keeping versions lets you trace later why the agent's behavior changed. When someone says "it behaved differently from last week," showing the dictionary diff ends the explanation.

Then we designate the dictionary's owner. While our engineers are on site, we update it, but by the end of the engagement we hand it over to the person in charge. What we hand over is the procedure for picking five, the way of splitting meanings by department, and how to keep versions. Once the person in charge can edit "the decision-maker for the urgent category changed from section chief to supervisor" themselves, the FDE is no longer needed. Becoming unnecessary is the completion condition of this process.

Our practice and its limits

I will write honestly. The dictionary is a living thing and is never finished. On-site language changes, organizations change, exceptions multiply. When a dictionary we took home is outdated six months later, it is because we failed to place the owner on the business side. That is why we make the handover of ownership the completion condition.

I will also write the limits. AI extraction only goes as far as producing candidates. "The same word with a different meaning" and "different words with the same meaning" are things machines cannot tell. When "secure" appears 40 times in the utterance log, the person who was there sorts out how many are sales "securing" and how many are warehouse "securing." Also, a dictionary never exceeds the quality of the fragments collected. If the interviews happen only in meeting rooms, the dictionary becomes a dictionary of "how work should be," and the agent stalls on actual work. This is also why the techniques from parts one through five come first.

Finally, I will write honestly about the relationship to our product. Our ZEROCK is a product that holds this dictionary as structure. So our FDEs grow the product while building the dictionary. This is not a conflict of interest; it is the FDE model itself. If the learning does not flow back into the product, it is contracted work. That said, the dictionary's owner is the customer, and the dictionary's contents belong to the customer. What flows back into the product is the structure, not the company's nouns and verbs themselves.

Summary

A dictionary of nouns and verbs gathered on site cannot be used by an agent as a dictionary alone. From the utterance logs and observation records, you extract five things—nouns, verbs, relationships, states, and exceptions—shape them into a graph, and make them traversable by the agent. That is the ontology: the work of dividing up the world in that company's own language and handing it to the machine.

The differences from contracted data modeling are: writing verbs and exceptions alongside nouns with conditions, updating weekly, returning the pattern to the product's standard schema while keeping branches as customer extensions, and placing the owner on the business side. The FDE becoming unnecessary is the completion condition of this process.
