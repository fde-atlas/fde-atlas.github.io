Customer Expectation Management Playbook: The Boundary of Commitments Between Demo and Production

Original title: 客户期望管理 Playbook：Demo 到生产之间的承诺边界

FDE中国 · 2026-09-04 · 7 min read

Summary

This article addresses the expectation gap between demos and production in enterprise AI projects, where misalignment typically surfaces only at acceptance. It lays out an eight-step playbook: inventorying all commitments, delivering a demo positioning statement on the spot, writing non-goals into acceptance documents, designing numeric milestones, establishing a bad-news protocol, aligning internal teams, managing scope changes through a formal process, and holding a pre-acceptance calibration meeting. The core thesis: expectations management means written commitments, symmetric presentation of boundaries, and gradual release of gaps throughout the project—not lowering expectations. In the Chinese context, written boundaries also help the client's contact person report upward.

Key points

  • Eight numbered steps cover commitments from demo to acceptance, each with deliverables and pitfalls.
  • A demo positioning statement should be spoken at the demo's end, not hidden in an appendix.
  • Non-goals deserve equal wording with promises and should include reasons to be accepted.
  • Bad news must be self-reported within 24 hours, always with at least one option attached.
  • Milestones need verifiable numeric criteria, not just dates, to keep expectations grounded.

The most common failure path in enterprise AI projects goes like this: at the demo meeting the customer keeps saying how great it is, two weeks later expectations balloon into "fully replacing manual work," and three months later at acceptance both sides discover they were never thinking of the same thing. The problem isn't that customers are irrational, but that no one manages the gap between Demo and production. As an FDE standing at the front line, this responsibility naturally falls on you. This piece gives a follow-along expectation management method in eight numbered steps, each covering what to do, deliverables, and common pitfalls. It builds on the question list produced by customer interviews, is used alongside the project kickoff document, and closes out at the PoC acceptance checklist.

Step 1: Commitment inventory — first figure out what you've already promised

What to do: The first thing after taking over a project or demo is to search through all commitments already made: statements in the sales proposal, verbal answers at the demo meeting, "no problem, we can do that" in emails, even the "we'll support all of that later" you casually said during the demo. Write each one down, note the source, and classify into three tiers: written commitment, verbal commitment, and the customer's own assumptions. The third tier has to be dug out by replaying the demo meeting and going through chat logs.

Deliverable: A commitment inventory table (commitment content, source, current judgment: deliverable / needs negotiation / needs proactive clarification).

Common pitfall: Only inventorying what you yourself said — what sales and pre-sales said in front of the customer also shapes customer expectations; if you don't know about them, you'll hear about it for the first time at some acceptance meeting. When inventorying, don't adopt the defensive posture of "I didn't say that"; record the facts first.

Step 2: Demo positioning statement — clarify things on the spot when the customer is most excited

What to do: Prepare three sentences before every demo and deliver them at the end of the demo, before the customer's questions begin: 1. What prepared data and processes today's demo scenarios are based on; 2. What work remains before production (data integration, permissions, concurrency, exception handling, etc. — pick three to five real items); 3. What the PoC phase intends to validate and what it does not intend to validate. Reading from a single page is fine, with the written version sent afterward. It's worth sacrificing a bit of applause at the end of the demo for three months of peace afterward.

Deliverable: A one-page demo positioning statement (demo prerequisites, gaps to production, PoC validation scope), sent to the customer along with the meeting minutes.

Common pitfall: Hiding the positioning statement in a PPT appendix — hidden in an appendix means it was never said; or vaguely saying "we'll make it more complete in production," which the customer hears as "all problems will be solved," actually raising expectations.

Step 3: Write boundaries into acceptance — "what we won't do" needs a written version

What to do: In the PoC plan or acceptance document, state non-goals with the same clarity as commitments: input types explicitly not covered, accuracy floors explicitly not guaranteed, explicit trigger conditions for falling back to manual work. Give reasons when writing non-goals, e.g., "handwritten receipts are out of scope for this phase because they account for roughly 10% and recognition costs are too high" — boundaries with reasons are easier for customers to accept. This step should be walked through item by item with the customer, with verbal confirmation obtained, and archived in the kickoff document.

Deliverable: A mutually confirmed list of scope and non-goals, forming the scope section of the acceptance document.

Common pitfall: Writing non-goals vaguely ("extreme cases are out of scope" equals not writing anything); or writing them only once at the beginning while the acceptance criteria section is full of positive commitments, so the customer forgets the boundaries by the time they read further. Boundaries should sit alongside the acceptance criteria for easy side-by-side reference.

Step 4: Milestone design — cut "eventually usable" into numbered steps

What to do: Cut the path from Demo to production into three to five milestones, each with verifiable completion criteria: e.g., "Milestone 1: on 300 real samples provided by the customer, core scenario processing success rate reaches X%, over two weeks." Set the metric numbers jointly with the customer, using the methods from eval baseline building. The point of milestones is to let customer expectations follow the steps rather than hang on the single suspense of "will it work in the end."

Deliverable: A milestone plan table (milestone, completion criteria, date, dependencies), signed or confirmed by email by the customer.

Common pitfall: Setting milestones by time only, without criteria — a milestone like "see initial results in two weeks" can't be judged as met or not; or steps that jump too far apart, so for three months in between the customer sees no perceptible progress and expectations start fermenting on their own.

Step 5: Bad news delivery mechanism — bad news should be delivered by you first, early, and with a plan

What to do: Pre-write an internal rule: any bad news that affects customer expectations (schedule delays, metrics falling short, dependent parties slipping) gets proactively communicated within 24 hours, in the format "current status — impact — our recommendation — what we need from the customer." Always bring at least one option when reporting, even if the options are "accept the delay" and "reduce this phase's scope." Beyond your direct contact, consider who they need to answer to above them, and proactively help them prepare their talking points.

Deliverable: A fixed template for bad news communications + every actual communication record sent.

Common pitfall: Waiting for a technical breakthrough before reporting — "we'll have results next Monday" dragged into four consecutive "next Mondays" erodes far more trust than the bad news itself; or reporting problems without solutions, throwing the decision pressure straight at the customer. There's also a reverse pitfall: using overly technical language to dilute the severity of bad news, which the customer doesn't understand at the time and later holds against you.

Step 6: Internal alignment — make the boss, sales, and delivery all say the same thing

What to do: Half of expectation management failures come from internal messaging splits. Before each milestone, hold a fifteen-minute alignment meeting with sales and the project manager: what's the unified external message, which commitments can't be given right now, what are the customer's recent moods and pressures. Write the "current external commitment version" as a short document of one line per item that anyone can recite. The alignment meeting should also clarify: for out-of-scope requests from the customer, who takes them and through what evaluation process.

Deliverable: A quick-reference page of the current external messaging + an internal routing agreement for out-of-scope requests.

Common pitfall: Alignment meetings turning into blame-shifting sessions — the focus should be "what the customer is hearing," not "whose fault it is"; or only the engineering team aligning while sales keeps piling on with contract-signing talking points on the customer side, leaving your boundary management sidelined.

Step 7: Scope change management — new requests aren't refused, they're handled by process

What to do: When the customer raises a new request mid-project, neither refuse nor agree on the spot; catch it with a fixed phrase: "That can be done. Let me first assess the impact on the current milestones and cost, and I'll give you three options tomorrow." After assessment, usually offer a choice of three: add it to this phase and extend the schedule, put it in the next phase, or partially satisfy the request at low cost with a workaround. Write each change as a one-page change record (request, impact, chosen option, confirmers from both sides); accumulated, these are the best self-protection evidence.

Deliverable: A change record book + an updated milestone plan.

Common pitfalls: Accepting every small request just to keep the client happy—small requests won't kill the project, but after two or three months of accumulation, the schedule is ruined and all boundaries are gone; another pitfall is verbally agreeing to a change without updating the written plan, leaving both sides with conflicting accounts at acceptance. Change management should be linked with delivery metrics: after each change, re-estimate cost and schedule metrics so decisions are grounded in data.

Step 8: Pre-acceptance calibration meeting—hold a "pre-acceptance" before the formal acceptance

What to do: One to two weeks before formal acceptance, schedule a calibration meeting and walk through the PoC acceptance checklist item by item: show the client the current metric numbers, actual performance inside and outside the boundaries, the list of known issues, and the follow-up plan. Make it clear that "our goal is no surprises at this meeting." Record on the spot any items the client still disputes, sorting them into two columns: "resolve before acceptance" and "follow up after acceptance." Most acceptance conflicts stem from seeing the real numbers for the first time at the acceptance meeting—the purpose of the calibration meeting is to move that first exposure of the numbers earlier.

Deliverables: Calibration meeting minutes (disputed items and their handling categories) + updated acceptance materials package.

Common pitfalls: Turning the calibration meeting into yet another demo—showing only flattering cases just postpones the conflict; or not reserving enough time, leaving no room to digest disputed items and turning the calibration meeting into a failed dress rehearsal of the acceptance meeting. The calibration meeting should be held at least one week before formal acceptance, giving both sides room to work through their differences.

Summary and next steps

The core of expectation management is not rhetoric but three things: putting commitments in writing (everything said is documented), presenting boundaries symmetrically (what is promised and what is not promised get equal treatment), and releasing gaps gradually throughout the process (using milestones and a bad-news mechanism to spread the gap across the whole project rather than piling it up at acceptance). In the context of Chinese enterprises, the client's point of contact also carries pressure to report upward, and your written commitment boundaries are exactly the tool they need to communicate downward—helping them save face helps the project save face.
