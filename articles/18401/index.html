Summary

This article by FDX株式会社 defines AX (AI Transformation) as a management-level transformation that redesigns business processes, organizations, and decision-making with AI as a given premise, positioning it as a stage beyond DX (digital transformation), which mainly digitized and optimized existing workflows.   • Only ~5% of generative AI projects reach production, per MIT NANDA research cited.
  • Most Japanese companies stall at maturity level 1 (tool adoption without process change).
  • AX Factory: three principles and five phases, 3–6 months per business area.
  • Success requires upfront ROI targets, production deployment, and in-house capability transfer.

What is AX? (3-line summary)

  • AX is a transformation that rebuilds business and organization with AI as the premise. It differs from DX, which digitizes existing operations, in both its starting point and its destination.
  • The goal lies beyond efficiency. AI takes on part of the work proactively, human roles themselves evolve, and the organization aims to acquire outcomes and capabilities.
  • It does not end with tool adoption. Only by designing business redesign, talent development, operational embedding, and horizontal expansion as a continuous process does the investment convert into business value.

What is AX (AI Transformation)?

AX (AI Transformation) refers to a management transformation that makes AI a premise of business operations and redesigns business processes, organizational structures, and decision-making mechanisms themselves. "Introducing AI tools" is merely one component of AX and cannot be called AX on its own.

Whereas DX (Digital Transformation) was a transformation that "replaced analog operations with digital ones for efficiency," AX is a transformation that "rebuilds operations on the premise that AI proactively takes on part of the work processes that humans used to judge and handle." AI is embedded not as a mere tool but as a member that constitutes the business. This is the difference from DX.

Specifically, AX requires answering the following questions.

  • Which work processes should be assigned to "humans," "AI," or "human-AI collaboration"?
  • How is the quality of AI-handled processes ensured, and who monitors them?
  • To which higher-value work should the human time freed by AI be reallocated?
  • How do you retain in-house the talent and mechanisms to keep making these decisions continuously?

What AX aims for is to rebuild the way the company works with AI as the premise and to hold that capability as an internal asset. Becoming "a company that can use AI" is achievable through tool adoption. AX's destination lies beyond that: "a company that can continuously rebuild its own operations with AI as the premise," which requires acquiring organizational capability. This difference is the dividing line between AI adoption and AX.

Following the thinking of METI's "DX Report," AX lies on its extension. If DX is "transforming business models and operations with data and digital technology," then AX is "transforming operations, organization, and decision-making with AI"—the stage where the protagonist of transformation has shifted from digital to AI.

Distinguishing AX from terms it is often confused with

AX is often confused with several terms used in similar contexts. To ensure accurate understanding, let us distinguish them.

  • AI adoption: Contracting generative AI tools and making them available to employees. It is part of AX, but on its own it does not change the structure of operations, so it cannot be called AX. AI adoption is a means of AX, not its purpose.
  • Operational efficiency: Making existing operations faster and cheaper. Efficiency gains do occur in AX, but AX's essence lies in "rebuilding the operations themselves." Efficiency is merely one of the results.
  • DX: Transformation through digital technology. It is the preceding stage that forms the foundation of AX, and AX lies beyond it. The two do not conflict; they are continuous.
  • AI utilization: A broad term referring to using AI in general. AX refers to the more structural transformation limited to "utilization at the level of redesigning operations and organization."

In short, if you merely want to "use" AI, AI utilization or AI adoption suffices. Only when you go as far as "rebuilding operations and organization with AI as the premise and retaining that capability in-house" can it be called AX. If management does not share this distinction, there is a risk of mistaking initiatives that stopped at AI adoption for AX and continuing to invest without results.

Why AX now? | The limits of DX

Behind the emergence of AX on the management agenda are two structural changes: the limits DX has faced, and the arrival of generative AI and AI agents.

DX hit a ceiling with "efficiency"

Many Japanese companies have worked on DX from the late 2010s through the early 2020s. Digitizing paper documents, moving operations to the cloud, visualizing data. These certainly improved efficiency. But efficiency is an effort that "assumes the existing business processes and makes them faster and cheaper"; it does not change the structure of the operations themselves.

What METI's "DX Report" has repeatedly pointed out is the reality that many companies' DX has stopped at "digitization (converting paper to digital)" or "digitalization (digitizing individual operations)" and has not reached true "business transformation (transforming operations and organization)." Tools have increased, but the structure of how people work has not changed. This is the true nature of DX's plateau.

Generative AI and AI agents created a "tool that judges"

Just as companies were facing the limits of DX, generative AI and AI agents appeared. Companies now possess technology that goes beyond a "tool for processing."

Traditional IT tools were "processing instruments" that executed procedures defined by humans quickly and accurately. Generative AI, by contrast, can understand text, summarize it, create drafts, and present options. AI agents can even autonomously advance multiple steps toward a given goal. In other words, AI has become capable of taking on part of the "understanding," "generation," and "judgment" that only humans could previously do.

What this change means is that the scope of transformation has expanded from "tasks" to "business processes themselves." Processes that have occupied the middle stream of corporate activity—document creation, aggregation, coordination, first-line response, drafting, and classification—are now being significantly replaced or supported by AI.

The "just add tools" mindset does not produce results

This is where many companies stumble. They try to add generative AI to existing operations as a "convenient tool." They contract for a chat tool and distribute it to employees saying "give it a try." Adding only a tool while leaving business processes unchanged limits the results to productivity gains for a few employees and does not translate into company-wide outcomes.

AI's true value is realized when operations are rebuilt from the ground up with AI in mind. If AI handles first-line response, you must rebuild the response flows, escalation criteria, and even the quality-check framework. If AI creates drafts, you must redesign the review and approval processes. AI adoption without this rebuilding stalls—stuck at the PoC (proof of concept) stage where it was confirmed as "seemingly useful" but never moves into production. This is the implementation wall that Japanese companies face today.

Some surveys show that only a small fraction of generative AI projects pass the PoC stage and reach production with real results (MIT NANDA's "The GenAI Divide: State of AI in Business 2025" reports that only about 5% reach production; various other industry surveys exist). Most of the causes of failure are not technical but stem from the absence of design that rebuilds operations. That is precisely why we need the concept of AX, which goes beyond the dimension of tool adoption and rebuilds operations, organizations, and talent together. For concrete steps to overcome PoC stagnation, see "How to Approach Generative AI Adoption".

This is also a rerun, in AI form, of the failure many companies experienced in the early days of DX: "we installed systems, but operations never changed." Companies that were satisfied with merely installing tools back then never reached true business transformation. The structure is the same with AI. To avoid repeating the same mistake, we need the AX way of thinking, which starts from operations rather than tools.

The difference between AX and DX

AX and DX are often confused. But the two are different stages, differing in both the agent of transformation and the goal. Clarifying the differences is essential both for building internal consensus and for investment decisions.

DX centers on "improving operational efficiency through digitalization," while AX centers on "redesigning operations and organizations with AI as the premise." The relationship between the two, compared across key dimensions, is as follows.

PerspectiveDX (Digital Transformation)AX (AI Transformation)
AgentDigital technology (cloud, data, systems)AI (generative AI, AI agents)
TargetDigitalization and automation of existing operationsRedesign and reallocation of business processes themselves
Role of peopleUse tools to make operations more efficientCollaborate with AI and focus on judgment and value creation
GoalOperational efficiency, cost reduction, visibilityMaximizing results, evolving people's roles, building organizational capability
DeliverablesDigitalized operations, systems, dashboardsAI-embedded business processes, operational frameworks, in-house talent
How success is measuredReduced man-hours, processing speed, paperless rateImproved business KPIs, shift to value-added work, establishment of in-house AX implementation capability
Failure patternsTools installed but operational structure unchangedPoC stagnation, tool-first approaches, outsourcing everything with no in-house capability left behind

What this comparison reveals is that AX does not negate DX; it is the stage beyond it. The data infrastructure and digitalized operations built through DX become the foundation for AX. The more thoroughly a company has executed DX, the more easily it can move to AX.

On the other hand, note that approaching AX with a DX mindset leads to failure. DX was based on the idea of "improving efficiency while keeping existing operations as the premise." If you stop at simply "adding AI to existing operations" with the same mindset, you will end up stuck at the PoC stage, as described in the previous chapter. AX requires a shift to the mindset of "questioning existing operations and rebuilding them with AI as the premise." This is not a technical issue but a matter of management decision-making.

What stage is your company's AX maturity? | Five levels

Before considering how to proceed with AX, there is something to confirm first: "where your company currently stands." AX is not a state reached company-wide all at once; it advances step by step. Misjudging your current position leads either to overly ambitious plans that stall, or conversely to underestimating yourself and missing opportunities.

As a yardstick for management to gauge their company's current position, AX maturity is organized into five levels.

  • Lv.0 Not started: Generative AI use in operations remains at the level of individual trial and error, with no organizational policy. Start with aligning management's understanding and narrowing down to one area.
  • Lv.1 Tool adoption: Generative AI tools have been contracted and employees have started using them. But business processes have not changed, and the effects remain limited to individual productivity gains. Many companies stagnate at this stage.
  • Lv.2 Operational integration: AI has been embedded into specific business processes and workflows redesigned. One area is running in production, with effects measured in business KPIs. This is the state of having crossed the entrance to AX.
  • Lv.3 In-house operation: In-house staff can independently operate and improve AI-embedded operations. Things keep running even if the vendor withdraws, and improvement cycles run internally.
  • Lv.4 Horizontal Expansion: The stage where patterns established in one domain can be rolled out to other departments and business processes under internal leadership. AX shifts from "one-off successes" to "a mechanism that can be mass-produced internally."
  • Lv.5 AX Native: The stage where business processes, organization, and decision-making are designed with AI as a given, and new operations launch with AI woven in from the start. AX has become the organization's default.

Many Japanese companies are stuck at Lv.1. They have introduced tools, but their business processes have not changed, and the benefits remain confined to individual productivity. The transition from Lv.1 to Lv.2 is precisely the first hurdle in moving beyond the extension of DX into AX, requiring the most difficult step: business process redesign. The approach for overcoming this hurdle is the five phases of AX Factory described next.

AX Focus Points by Industry

The basic approach to AX is common across industries, but which business processes to start with for the easiest wins varies by industry characteristics. Below is an overview of focus points for representative industries. If there is an area close to your own company, it can serve as a clue for narrowing down your first AX target.

  • Manufacturing: Focus on quality control, equipment maintenance, and production planning, which depend heavily on veteran workers on the floor. There is ample room to draw out experts' tacit knowledge and replace it with AI-driven anomaly detection and decision support. At the same time, routine tasks in technical departments, such as creating estimates and reading drawings, also tend to yield quick results.
  • Finance and Insurance: Focus on screening, compliance checks, and inquiry handling. Because regulatory requirements make operational design heavy, if quality assurance and monitoring are designed carefully, the automation benefits of AI-driven first-pass judgments and document checks are substantial.
  • Retail and E-commerce: Focus on inquiry handling, product data maintenance, and demand forecasting. The classic design is for AI to handle the first response to high-volume, routine inquiries, while humans concentrate on exception handling and improving customer experience.
  • Healthcare and Elder Care: Focus on peripheral tasks of professionals, such as record creation, schedule coordination, and first-line inquiry handling. By shifting peripheral administrative processes to AI so professionals can concentrate on their core work, this directly alleviates labor shortages.
  • Professional Services (Consulting, Legal, Accounting, etc.): Focus on the mid-stream processes of knowledge work, such as document creation, research, drafting, and meeting minutes. A collaborative design works well, where experts retain judgment while AI supports the surrounding information organization and draft generation.

The common principle is to start with "routine, high-volume processes with relatively clear decision criteria" and create the first success story in areas where results are easy to measure quantitatively. Industry characteristics are a factor in prioritizing where to start, but they do not change the approach itself.

How to Proceed with AX | The Five Phases of AX Factory

So how exactly should AX be carried out? FDX Inc. calls its methodology for implementing AX not as a "one-off project" but as a "mechanism that can be mass-produced internally" AX Factory. AX Factory consists of three design principles and five phases. First, let's look at the flow of the five phases.

The five phases of AX Factory proceed in the order of Diagnose → Design → Implement → Enable → Horizontal Expansion. For each business domain, production deployment takes roughly three to six months, after which an internal AX promotion structure is left in place before moving to the next domain. This ensures that AX does not end as a "one-off project in a specific department" but spreads across the entire company.

Phase 1: Diagnose

The first step is to observe the current business processes, data, and organization, and define the areas where AX should be applied and their priorities. Not all operations can be AI-ified. Separate the processes where AI is effective from those that should be handled by humans.

  • Understand the current business processes, data locations, system architecture, and organizational structure
  • Conduct direct interviews with management, frontline leaders, and practitioners to identify the "real bottlenecks"
  • Identify the processes where AI can deliver results (routine, high-volume processes with clear decision criteria)
  • Deliverables: Current business process map, priority matrix for AX application areas, quantitative baseline

Phase 2: Design

Next, narrow down the problems to solve and redesign the business processes and AI capabilities as one. This is the core of AX and the step that differs most from DX. Rather than simply deciding which tools to introduce, redraw the boundaries between "processes done by humans," "processes done by AI," and "processes where humans and AI collaborate."

  • Carry out business process redesign (BPR), AI capability design, and integration design with existing systems simultaneously
  • Design the framework for quality assurance, monitoring, and exception handling for processes delegated to AI
  • Estimate return on investment (ROI) and operational burden to solidify the prerequisites for production deployment
  • Deliverables: To-Be business flow, AI capability specifications, system architecture diagram, ROI model, KPI design

Phase 3: Implement

Implement the design in a form that works on the ground. What matters here is inserting the AI layer non-destructively, without breaking existing core systems. If large-scale system overhauls are assumed, production deployment will be years away, delaying AX's benefits.

  • Add AI capabilities to existing systems using a non-destructive architecture
  • Select the necessary capabilities from the AI capability catalog (FDX Tools) and deploy them in your own environment
  • In parallel, begin training for frontline users and preparing operational documentation
  • Deliverables: Operational AI-embedded business processes, operational documentation, training materials

Phase 4: Enable

Implementation is not the end. Developing frontline staff to a state where they "can operate and improve it themselves" is the key to turning AX into an internal asset. If this step is skipped, everything stops the moment the vendor leaves.

  • Design adoption with a three-tier structure: promoters, company-wide literacy, and frontline practice
  • Develop in-house talent who can redesign, operate, and improve work with AI (merely being able to use it is not enough)
  • Document judgment criteria, troubleshooting procedures, and improvement roadmaps
  • Deliverables: internal promotion structure, an operations and improvement knowledge base, and a self-sufficiency checklist

Phase 5: Scaling (Scale)

Transplant the patterns that succeeded in one business area to other departments and domains. This is where AX shifts from "one-off success" to "a mechanism that can be mass-produced in-house."

  • Turn the patterns of work redesign, AI implementation, and talent development established in one area into templates
  • Leave behind a mechanism that lets internal promoters lead the transplantation to other job types and business units
  • Establish AX as an internal operating model that runs continuously
  • Deliverables: scaling templates, an internal AX promotion playbook, and a mass-production structure

Only when all five phases can be run in sequence does AX translate into results. If even one is missing, AX remains just an efficiency tool or stalls at the PoC stage.

Three principles for making AX succeed

The foundation that drives the five phases of AX Factory is a set of three design principles. If the phases define "what to do," the principles define "with what mindset to do it." Deviating from these three principles means no results even if you follow the five phases correctly.

Principle 1: Deploy First (get it running in the field first)

AX must not end as proposals or concept documents. Put AI that actually works in the field in place from the start.

Many AI projects proceed with "first build a perfect plan" or "first establish company-wide rules," postponing production deployment by months. In the meantime, enthusiasm on the ground cools and the technology becomes obsolete. Deploy First is the opposite. Working backward from production deployment, it launches work redesign, AI capabilities, data, and talent development simultaneously and gets things running in a short period. Only with something running do you get candid feedback from the field, and improvement begins.

In the generative AI space, technology evolves quickly. Even if you spend six months building a perfect plan, models and tools will be replaced in the meantime and the plan itself becomes obsolete. That is why a cycle of deploying to production small and fast and learning while running is more rational than pursuing perfection on paper. Deploy First is also a discipline for decision-making in highly uncertain domains.

Principle 2: ROI Accountability (take responsibility for return on investment)

AX does not end with delivery. We take responsibility until results are achieved.

Results here include not only business KPIs such as time savings, quality improvement, sales contribution, cost reduction, and decision-making speed, but also "whether internal AI implementation capability has taken root." A project that deploys AI without measuring results cannot justify the investment and cannot secure the next budget. ROI Accountability requires defining these KPIs upfront and visualizing them through operations. Areas not captured in numbers cannot be improved.

This principle matters because AX investments easily end as "we tried it." Without measuring effects, you cannot judge success or failure, nor justify scaling to the next area. Conversely, if you define KPIs and capture a baseline upfront, post-deployment improvements, reporting to management, and decisions on additional investment can all proceed based on numbers. ROI Accountability is the mechanism that lifts AX from a one-off experiment to a continuous management initiative.

Principle 3: Transfer Skills (transfer technology toward in-house capability)

The ultimate goal of AX is to create a state where AX can run in-house without external support.

What should be developed is not people who can operate AI tools, but people who can redesign work with AI and run it through operation and improvement. Leave such talent in-house and create a state where the company can mass-produce AX even after the vendor leaves. This is a design philosophy that deliberately avoids vendor lock-in. By incorporating Transfer Skills into success metrics, handover quality is structurally guaranteed. "Finishing and leaving" is the proper form of AX support.

Why include in-housing in scope? Because AX is not a one-time transformation but a continuous effort that changes business areas and keeps scaling. Relying on external parties every time inflates both cost and time, and the speed of transformation becomes constrained by external circumstances. The ability to mass-produce AX in-house is the greatest competitive asset in an era premised on AI. The role of external supporters is complete when that capability has been fully transferred in-house.

These three principles systematize, as the overall design philosophy of AX, the model that Forward Deployed Engineers (FDEs) have proven in customer environments: "enter the field, deliver results, enable self-sufficiency, and leave." The FDE role is explained in detail in "What is a Forward Deployed Engineer (FDE)? Defining the implementation partner for the AI era".

Common AX adoption failures and how to avoid them

AX is powerful, but a wrong approach wastes the investment. Here we organize the failure patterns repeatedly seen in Japanese companies and how to avoid them.

Failure 1: Stalling at PoC | Settling for "it seems useful"

The most common failure is confirming in a PoC that "AI seems usable" and never moving to production operation. Although the technical validation succeeded, there is no design for embedding it into business processes; permission management, audit logs, and quality assurance become afterthoughts, and the field cannot use it and it is left abandoned.

Countermeasure: Design from the PoC stage with production operation in mind. Decide upfront "who will use it, when, for what purpose, and what the fallback is when it fails." In the diagnosis and design phases of AX Factory, build a plan premised on production deployment and ROI, so this gap never arises structurally.

Failure 2: Tools first | Adding tools without changing the work

The thinking goes: "We signed up for a trendy generative AI tool, so things will change once employees start using it." But if business processes stay as they are, adding tools only yields marginal reductions in individual work time before hitting a ceiling.

Countermeasure: Redesign the work before selecting tools. Design "which steps AI handles, which steps people own, and where they collaborate," then choose tools with the features required by that design. Get the order wrong and you will not see results.

Failure 3: Full outsourcing | No internal capability remains, and everything stops the moment the vendor leaves

A company asks an external vendor for AX support but hands everything over, leaving no knowledge in-house. The moment the vendor exits, the project stalls; neither improvement nor horizontal rollout is possible, creating a cost structure of perpetual external dependence.

Countermeasure: At the contracting stage, include "developing internalization candidates" and "handover completion criteria" as outcome metrics. Run external supporters alongside internal candidates so the company retains people who can redesign, operate, and improve work with AI. Building Transfer Skills into the design from the start is key.

Failure 4: Confined to IT or the promotion office | Authority for work redesign never circulates

If AX is kept entirely within the IT department or the DX promotion office, the authority needed for work redesign never circulates, and the project becomes a formality. AX is a transformation that rebuilds the work itself, so the involvement of business divisions that own frontline operations is essential.

Countermeasure: Assign AX directly under a business division sponsor (at the general manager level) and drive it as a management agenda item. Have IT and the promotion office run in parallel as technical supervisors and drivers of company-wide rollout.

The first step in AX that leadership should take

We have looked at the definition of AX, how it differs from DX, how to proceed, and how implementation works. Finally, let us lay out the "first step" leadership can act on starting tomorrow. AX may look like a grand transformation, but the first step can be small and concrete. Indeed, the bigger the framing, the harder it becomes to start moving.

Step 1: Align leadership's understanding

AX is a transformation that rebuilds the work itself and moves frontline authority. That is why top-down alignment is the starting point. Within leadership, align on the recognition that "distributing AI tools" and "rebuilding work with AI as the premise" are different things. If this remains vague when pushed down to the frontline, everything stalls at tool adoption.

Step 2: Narrow the target work to one

"Company-wide AX" is a classic failure. At first, narrow it down to a single routine, high-volume work process whose impact is easy to measure. Candidates include first-line inquiry handling, meeting minutes and CRM integration, quote creation, and draft document generation. Building a success story in one domain becomes the foothold for company-wide rollout.

Step 3: Decide production deployment and ROI upfront

A PoC that only confirms something "might work" won't move things forward. From the start, decide on "deploying to production in 3–6 months" and "which business KPIs will measure the impact," and plan backward from there. Simply making production deployment and ROI the premise changes the quality of the design.

Step 4: Decide who will take over in-house

The exit of AX is internalization. Decide "who will operate and improve this AX" before starting. If there is no designated successor, everything stops the moment external support leaves. Having internalization candidates work alongside the project from the beginning is the condition for leaving behind internal assets.

None of these four steps require large-scale investment or company-wide reform. They can start with executive decision-making and narrowing down to a single area. AX is not "transformation to do big someday," but "transformation you can start small this week."

Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ)

Q1. What is the difference between AX and DX?

A. The subject and goal of the transformation differ. DX is transformation that streamlines existing operations with digital technology, with goals such as cost reduction and visibility. AX is transformation that redesigns the business processes themselves with AI as the premise, with goals of maximizing outcomes, evolving people's roles, and building internal AX implementation capability. DX forms the foundation of AX, but approaching AX with DX's mindset of "streamlining existing operations" merely adds AI as a tool and yields no results. For details, see the section "The Difference Between AX and DX" in this article.

Q2. Is AX possible for small and medium-sized businesses?

A. Yes, and in fact, faster decision-making can be an advantage. AX does not need to be a company-wide, simultaneous, large-scale transformation. A realistic approach is to focus on one business area (e.g., first-line inquiry handling, quote creation, meeting minutes and CRM integration), deploy to production in 3–6 months, confirm results, and then expand. The key is not scale, but narrowing down the business and designing with production deployment and ROI as the premise.

Q3. Should AX talent be built in-house or outsourced?

A. A hybrid of "launch with outsourcing, run with internal teams" produces the best results. Trying to do everything in-house from the start takes time and lacks know-how. On the other hand, outsourcing everything means everything stops the moment the vendor leaves. The ideal model is for external supporters to lead business redesign and advanced implementation, while internal candidates work alongside to accumulate know-how, with in-house talent becoming the operational leaders within six months to a year. For criteria on deciding between outsourcing and internalization, see "Using External FDEs vs. Developing In-House | 5 Criteria for Executive Decision-Making".

Q4. How do you measure the ROI of AX?

A. Measure both business KPIs and organizational capability. Business KPIs—time savings, quality improvement, revenue contribution, cost reduction, decision-making speed, and so on—are defined according to the target business. In addition, it is important for AX to include "whether internal AI implementation capability has taken hold (can it be operated, improved, and scaled in-house?)" as a success metric. Evaluate based on business impact and acquisition of internal assets, not on unit price or man-hours. Define measurement metrics first in the design phase, and visualize them while operating.

Q5. What is the relationship between AX and AX Factory?

A. If AX is "the desired state (a state where business and organization have been rebuilt with AI as the premise)," then AX Factory is the "methodology and operating model" by which FDX implements it. AX Factory consists of three design principles (Deploy First / ROI Accountability / Transfer Skills) and five phases (Diagnosis → Design → Implementation → Development → Scaling). Following AX Factory ensures that AX doesn't end as a one-off project, but remains as a mechanism that can be replicated in-house.

Q6. Our DX is still in progress. Can we still move on to AX?

A. You can start AX even if DX isn't complete. In fact, with the advent of generative AI, there are increasingly more cases where it is more efficient to advance digitalization simultaneously while restructuring business with AI as the premise, rather than pursuing DX and AX separately in sequence. However, in areas where data is not maintained at all, there is no information to feed the AI, making results hard to achieve. The standard practice is to sort out "areas where AI is effective" and "areas that need data preparation first" in the diagnosis phase, and start with the effective areas.

Q7. What business areas should AX start with?

A. It is easier to succeed by starting with processes that are routine, high-volume, and have relatively clear decision criteria. Examples include first-line inquiry handling, meeting minutes creation and CRM updates, generating documents and drafts, quote creation, and data aggregation and classification. These are areas where AI can easily produce results and outcomes are easy to measure quantitatively, making it easy to create the first AX success story. The standard path is to establish a success pattern in one area and then scale it out.

Q8. What is the relationship between AX and AI agents?

A. AI agents are one of the key technical means of realizing AX. Because AI agents can autonomously advance multiple processes toward a given goal, they can replace or support a wider range of business processes that were previously handled by people. If AX is transformation that "redesigns business and organization with AI as the premise," then AI agents are "the executing entities that take over processes in place of people in that redesign." However, simply introducing agents does not make it AX. Only when there is business design—deciding which processes to delegate to agents, how to ensure quality, and what people will handle—does the technology turn into results.

Q9. How much data preparation is needed before starting AX?

A. You don't need to perfectly organize all company data before starting. Rather, it's enough if the data required for the one area you tackle first is in place. Since AX proceeds one area at a time, it's fine as long as the data used in that area (inquiry history, meeting minutes, deal information, etc.) can be referenced. In the diagnostic phase, check "does this area have the data it needs" and start with the areas that do. For areas with almost no data, either start with business process design that generates data first, or postpone them. The assumption that "AX is impossible without a perfect data foundation" is often the biggest factor delaying the start.

Q10. How long does it take for AX to produce results?

A. As a guideline, one business area takes roughly 3 to 6 months to reach production deployment, when the first results become visible. At AX Factory, we spend 1 to 2 months on diagnosis and design, 2 to 3 months on implementation, and 1 to several months on training and adoption, then confirm improvement in that area's business KPIs. The priority is to produce early results in one area, without waiting for company-wide transformation to complete. Early success builds internal buy-in and accelerates horizontal expansion to the next area. Conversely, starting with a company-wide simultaneous rollout and long-term plan tends to lose momentum before results appear and stall easily. Building up results in short cycles is the realistic path to sustaining AX.
