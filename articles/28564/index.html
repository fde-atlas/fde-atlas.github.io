Build vs buy AI: a layer-by-layer framework for FDEs

FDE Times · 2026-11-16 · 6 min read

Summary

This article gives forward deployed engineers a framework for the build-versus-buy question: split the system into layers (model platform, data processing, business logic, interface) and judge each with four criteria—differentiation, speed, total cost including licenses and maintenance, and organizational readiness. Citing a 2025 MIT NANDA study (vendor-bought AI succeeds about 67% of the time versus roughly a third for internal builds), it works through an insurer example with a break-even calculation around 42,500 documents per month, and argues that candidly advising clients to buy—even against your own product—builds lasting trust.

Key points

  • The real question is which layers to buy and which to build, not a binary choice.
  • Four criteria: differentiation, speed, total cost, and people/data readiness.
  • MIT NANDA 2025: buying AI succeeds ~67% of the time; internal builds about a third as often.
  • Illustrative break-even: buying wins below ~42,500 documents/month, building wins above.
  • FDEs should raise vendor-dependency risk themselves and prioritize client outcomes over product sales.

Four tests let a forward deployed engineer split a system into layers and give an honest verdict on each, even when it costs their own product the sale.

In brief

  • The question worth answering is which layers to buy and which to build, rather than picking one option for everything.
  • Four criteria matter: whether the layer differentiates the client, how fast they need it, total cost including licenses and maintenance, and whether they have the people and data.
  • An FDE who defends their own product loses trust; one who advises on the outcomes the client needs keeps the client for the long run.

Sooner or later there will be a meeting where someone on the client side looks straight at you and asks: should we build this ourselves, or just buy it from your company? You are the vendor’s engineer, so everyone in the room can guess what you will say. That is why your next answer decides how far they will trust you.

The easiest reflex is to defend your company’s product. The second reflex, just as wrong, is to say “it depends” and change the subject. There is a third way: apply four criteria, take the system layer by layer, and state your conclusion even when it does not favor your product.

Why defending the product loses you the client

Clients do not pay to have you sitting next to them. They pay for results. An analysis on the Substack Enterprise Context Management argues that clients accept hands-on help when it gets them to value faster, but they will not mistake that help for the outcome itself.

An FDE who is product-centered rather than outcome-centered will soon be found out.

The data does not support building on instinct either. A 2025 study by MIT NANDA, as reported by Fortune, found that buying AI tools from specialized vendors or partnering with them succeeds about 67% of the time. Internal builds succeed only about a third as often.

None of this means buying is always right. The same Substack analysis warns of a risk an FDE should raise unprompted: an engineering team embedded at the client can gradually become a permanent implementation department, quietly woven into how the client operates.

Being candid about that risk, even when it concerns you, is the fastest way to make the client believe everything else you say.

The right question is which layers to buy and which to build

According to “Build vs. buy AI: A CIO’s decision matrix” on TechTarget, the real question is rarely a binary choice. It is which parts of the AI stack to buy and which to build in order to keep a differentiating edge.

HatchWorks describes the most common hybrid: buy the platform, then build the customization and the client’s proprietary AI on top.

So the first thing to do when you hear the question is to hold back an answer. Sketch the system as layers, usually the model platform, the data processing layer, the business logic layer and the user interface. Then apply the four criteria to each one.

Four criteria, in order

Differentiation. An EY executive advises that if a capability is tightly bound to the client’s industry and is what sets them apart from competitors, the default should be to build the data and proprietary AI. HatchWorks offers a quick test: if the client’s competitors can buy the same thing, it is unlikely to become a competitive advantage.

Speed. The TechTarget piece observes that when speed is decisive, buying almost always wins. HatchWorks cautions, though, that a quick demo can mislead decision-makers. The question to ask is how long until it runs on the client’s real data; time to a demo tells you little.

Total cost. Buying has its own cost curve, since license and subscription fees grow with scale. Building means carrying maintenance, and HatchWorks stresses that maintenance is the very price of owning software, far from a side issue. A comparison is fair only when it counts both kinds of cost.

Readiness. Darshan Naik of Capgemini argues that people, business readiness and data are the biggest determinants of success, and are routinely underestimated. A layer worth building is still not worth building now if the client has nobody to maintain it.

Applying it to a real case

Take a hypothetical insurer that wants to use AI to process claims. They ask whether to build the whole thing themselves. You split the system into four layers and work through each criterion:

Layer Differentiating? Speed Cost to scrutinize Recommendation
Model platform No, competitors can buy it Available immediately if bought License grows with claim volume Buy
Document extraction Low Faster to buy Per-document fees Buy, test on real claims
Claims rules and data High, industry-specific Slower but worth it Maintenance borne by the client Build
Staff interface Medium Depends on the team Light maintenance Use off the shelf, customize over time

The claims rules and data layer is where the client can pull ahead of competitors. This insurer’s experience in handling claims is something no rival can buy, so that is the layer worth investing people and time in. But the fourth criterion forces a further question: who on the client side will maintain these rules after you leave?

The break-even point matters more than today’s price

Document extraction is the layer where the arithmetic deserves care. The figures below are purely illustrative assumptions, meant to show the method. Say buying costs $0.20 per document and the client processes 10,000 claims a month, or $2,000 a month.

The build option, also assumed: $60,000 up front, plus $6,000 a month for maintenance and infrastructure. Spreading the build cost over 24 months of use adds $2,500 a month. The total cost of building is therefore about $8,500 a month, roughly constant regardless of claim volume.

Break-even comes when $0.20 times the number of claims equals $8,500, or about 42,500 claims a month. At 10,000 claims, buying costs less than a quarter of building.

If the client rolls out company-wide and reaches 100,000 claims, the purchase fee becomes $20,000 a month, more than twice the cost of building.

The right recommendation is therefore neither a flat “buy” nor a flat “build”: buy now, and revisit once volume passes about 40,000 claims a month, provided the client has maintenance staff by then. You hand the client a threshold to watch, which is far more useful than an opinion.

A script for the meeting

When the client asks, move the conversation to outcomes: “Before we discuss buying or building, what results do you need to have this quarter?” That tells you how much speed matters.

Next: “If your competitors had exactly this tool, where would you still beat them?” This reveals which layers truly differentiate.

The next two questions target cost and readiness: “When volume grows tenfold, who pays for the increase?” and “When the vendor’s engineers pull out, who on your team will maintain what you built?” Finally, raise the risk of depending on the vendor’s engineering team yourself, including your own.

Common mistakes

The first mistake is answering for the whole system with a single word, “buy” or “build”. The second is letting a demo decide what a test on real data should. The third is comparing license fees with the upfront build cost while forgetting maintenance, or the reverse, forgetting that licenses grow with scale.

The hardest mistake to spot is advising a client to build a layer that genuinely differentiates them when they have neither the people nor the data. Right on strategy but wrong on timing is still wrong.

Turning it into an edge when job hunting

Before an FDE interview, prepare a story about a time you put the client’s outcome ahead of your product’s short-term interest. A four-criteria table you actually used, with a recommendation for each layer and a break-even point, turns that story into evidence.

On your CV, write in terms of outcomes, for example: “Advised the client to buy the platform and build the business rules layer, with a plan to hand maintenance over to the client’s team.”

This week’s exercise: take a project you know well, split it into layers, fill in the four-criteria table, then defend each cell in front of a colleague playing a difficult client.

An FDE who has once said “you should buy this part from someone else” will be trusted more when advising that the rest go to their own team.
