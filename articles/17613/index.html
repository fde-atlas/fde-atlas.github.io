Success Criteria Contract Clauses: Writing "What Counts as Success" into the Contract

Original title: 成功标准合同条款：把「什么算成功」写进合同

FDE中国 · 2026-09-04 · 1 min read

Summary

This FDEChina article explains how to write success criteria into a contract appendix to avoid disputes at acceptance time. It argues an enforceable success criterion needs four elements: a measurable metric, a baseline, a data source, and a judgment window; missing any one makes the clause useless. It warns against vague wording like customer satisfaction and against treating model metrics such as accuracy as business success criteria. It distinguishes success criteria, which govern business outcomes and renewals, from acceptance criteria, which govern features and interfaces, and recommends drafting during scoping with written client confirmation.

Key points

  • Success criteria clauses belong in the contract appendix to prevent scope creep and acceptance disputes.
  • Four required elements: measurable metric, baseline, data source, judgment window.
  • Avoid undecidable phrases like customer satisfaction and conflating accuracy metrics with business outcomes.
  • Success criteria govern renewals and expansion; acceptance criteria govern features and interfaces.
  • Draft during scoping, keep it to five lines, and get written client confirmation.

Full text

Save yourself three months of acceptance disputes with a one-page contract annex. The core breakdown in this article is the four elements of success criteria: measurable metrics, a baseline, a data source, and a judgment window—if any one is missing, the clause becomes ineffective during acceptance, as if it were never written. There are two common types of misuse: writing non-verifiable statements like "customer satisfaction" into the clause; and treating model technical metrics (such as accuracy) as business success criteria—customers buy business outcomes, and accuracy is only an internal quality measure; mixing the two makes acceptance feel like both a technical review and a business negotiation. Compared with the common confusion of equating success criteria with acceptance criteria, this article clearly separates the two layers: acceptance criteria are at the engineering delivery granularity, governing features and interfaces; success criteria clauses are at the commercial and business outcome granularity, governing renewals and expansion—the former gets you live, the latter gets you retained. We suggest you write down "what counts as success" for your current project in no more than five lines, send it to the customer for written confirmation, and only proceed to contract signing once the definition is confirmed without ambiguity. — FDEChina Editorial Team · Practitioner's Perspective

Definition

Success criteria contract clauses refer to the mechanism of writing a project's "what counts as success" as measurable metrics, baselines, and judgment processes, and attaching them as a contract annex to bind both parties. It is one of the most worthwhile clauses to fight for in a delivery contract.

Details

It is a core tool in FDE delivery for defending against scope creep and acceptance disputes. Executable success criteria have four elements: measurable metrics, a baseline, a data source, and a judgment window—missing any one of them renders the clause ineffective during acceptance. Draft it during the scoping phase rather than on the eve of signing; the definition needs written confirmation from the customer, and ideally the data extraction method should also be locked down.

There are two common types of misuse: writing non-verifiable statements like "customer satisfaction" into the clause; and treating model technical metrics (such as accuracy) as business success criteria—customers buy business outcomes, and accuracy is only an internal quality measure. The difference from acceptance criteria: the latter are at the engineering acceptance granularity, governing features and interfaces; success criteria clauses are at the commercial and business outcome granularity, governing renewals and expansion. The two layers coexist in the same contract and cannot replace each other.
