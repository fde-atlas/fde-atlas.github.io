Hypercare Post-Launch Support Period: FDE Scheduling and Exit Criteria

Original title: Hypercare 上线保障期：FDE 排班与退出标准

FDE Times · 2026-10-27 · 5 min read

Summary

This guide explains how forward deployed engineers should run hypercare, the post-launch stabilization period. Citing sources including Supportbench, ERP Research, Panorama and Featurebase, it argues that exit timing should be governed by measurable exit criteria agreed before go-live, not by the calendar: all conditions must hold simultaneously for five consecutive business days, including ticket volume at or below 120% of business-as-usual, documented workarounds with owners, and customer sign-off on the handover. Staffing should start at 2–3x BAU levels in the first two weeks, then taper. Withdrawal should be gradual, unresolved incidents handed over person-to-person, and for financial systems the FDE should stay through the first month-end close.

Key points

  • Hypercare typically lasts 2–8 weeks but ends when exit criteria are met, not when weeks run out.
  • Criteria must be set before go-live and hold for five consecutive business days.
  • Ticket volume threshold example: no more than 120% of BAU.
  • Staff weeks 1–2 at 2–3x BAU levels, then taper down.
  • Hand over unresolved incidents face-to-face; stay through the first month-end close for financial systems.

The departure date should be determined by exit criteria agreed before go-live and the "five consecutive business days" rule, not by the calendar.

Key takeaways

  • Hypercare typically lasts several weeks (ERP Research cites a range of 2–8 weeks), but it ends when the criteria are met, not when the planned number of weeks runs out.
  • Agree on the criteria before go-live; you can only leave once all conditions have held simultaneously for five consecutive business days.
  • The first week requires 2–3 times BAU staffing, which then tapers off, with the handover completed in person.

The system goes live on Monday morning. By Wednesday afternoon, tickets are piling up, and your manager is already asking: can you move to the new client next week?

The right answer is rarely "yes" or "no" — it's "once the exit criteria are met." The period between go-live and your actual departure is called hypercare.

Forward Deployed Engineers (FDEs) who handle hypercare well do three things: agree on exit conditions in advance, staff enough on-call coverage, and ensure nothing falls through the cracks between the two teams during handover.

How long it lasts is not up to you

Supportbench defines hypercare as a time-boxed stabilization period after go-live, typically lasting 2–6 weeks. It ends when a set of specific criteria agreed in advance is met, not when the calendar says so.

Every source gives different numbers. ERP Research says 2–8 weeks after go-live. Featurebase says most last 1–8 weeks, while complex ERP or platform migrations can run 12 weeks or longer. Such a wide range shows that the number of weeks is an outcome, not something you can fix in advance.

What you can control is the criteria. The endpoint must also be clear: according to ERP Research, hypercare ends with formal handover of long-term support to a service desk or managed services team. Someone has to take over the system; the FDE cannot simply walk away.

Agree on exit criteria before go-live

Panorama recommends setting exit criteria before go-live and using measurable metrics, such as transaction accuracy, support request volume, and the decline in backlog tickets. The reason is practical: after go-live everyone is exhausted, and criteria set at that point are easily adjusted to accommodate the departure date everyone wants.

Supportbench is stricter: all conditions must hold simultaneously for five consecutive business days. One specific condition is that ticket volume stays at or below 120% of expected day-to-day operations (BAU) levels. Two others concern people: every workaround is documented and has an owner, and the relevant client stakeholder signs off on the handover summary.

Example: an invoice reconciliation system deployed for a finance team

Suppose you deploy an invoice reconciliation system that goes live on the 10th of the month. Before go-live, you and the head of accounting estimate BAU at about 5 tickets per business day, so the 120% threshold is 6 tickets per day.

Suppose that by week three, tickets have dropped to 5–6 per day. But on Thursday, a file import bug pushes them up to 9. The five-consecutive-day count is broken and restarts on Friday. That's frustrating, but the point of the rule is precisely to prevent leaving early on a "close enough" basis.

For systems serving finance, Panorama's checklist asks one more question: can the tasks of the close cycle be completed without interruption?

If the system goes live on the 10th, the month-end close is the first real test. Even if five consecutive days have already accumulated before then, wait until the first close is complete before leaving.

Overstaff early, then taper off

Supportbench points out a common mistake: staffing hypercare at exactly BAU levels. It recommends 2.0–3.0x staffing in weeks 1–2 and 1.3–1.5x in weeks 3–4, with daily standups in the first two weeks that then taper off.

Do the math. If 2 people normally support the system, weeks 1–2 require 4 to 6 people, and weeks 3–4 require 2.6 to 3. In practice, roughly 2.6 people can mean 2 full-time staff plus one FDE spending more than half of each day on the system.

Featurebase stresses that hypercare works best when one specific person owns it, rather than the whole team covering it on the side of their day jobs. If you're the project's FDE, put your own name on the on-call schedule with clear time slots; don't run it as "whoever is free takes a look."

Taper off and hand over in person

Featurebase recommends gradually reducing support and logging every open risk, rather than cutting support off all at once. FDEs can try this approach: in the final week, have the service desk take primary on-call duty while you shadow them, then step back a little further each day.

OneUptime is explicit: unresolved incidents must be handed over from person to person; posting them in documentation is not enough. So if the file import bug is still unresolved on handover day, hold a short meeting with the person taking over, walk through the logs together, and have them confirm they're taking ownership.

Mistakes that prolong or sink hypercare

The costliest mistake is leaving by the calendar: four weeks are up, so you go, regardless of ticket levels. The second mistake is setting criteria only after go-live, when everyone is eager for it to end and the criteria get loosened to accommodate that wish.

Next come spreading the load across the whole team with no one truly accountable, and withdrawing support all at once without a risk list. For finance systems, there's one more mistake: leaving before the first period-end close. Bugs exposed during the close will find you eventually, sometimes after you've already moved on to another project.

Turning hypercare into an advantage in interviews

When reading FDE job descriptions, watch for phrases like post-go-live support, hypercare, or handover. If you see them, ask directly in the interview: how long would you stay on-site with the client after go-live? Who takes over the system after you leave?

Don't just write "post-go-live support" on your resume. Spell out how you designed exit criteria, how you brought ticket volume down to the BAU threshold, and how you completed a client-signed handover. Use real numbers from your own projects, because recruiters will follow up on how you measured them.

Clients rarely remember the go-live date. What they remember is the week the system first ran smoothly on its own, when nobody needed to call you.
