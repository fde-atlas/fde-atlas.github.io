Crossing the Chasm Book Notes: How FDEs Deploy Mainstream Customers

Original title: 跨越鸿沟读书笔记：FDE 如何部署主流客户

FDE Times · 2026-10-09 · 4 min read

Summary

This reading note treats Geoffrey Moore's Crossing the Chasm (3rd edition) as an operations manual for FDEs. It argues that visionary customers buy transformation while pragmatic mainstream customers want productivity gains without disruption, so a deployment that wins the first customer often fails with the second. Mainstream customers will not fill product gaps themselves; connectors, training materials, and support processes—the whole product—fall to FDEs. The author recommends focusing on one problem in one market segment, completing it fully, then expanding, and learning to defer other requests. It also cites Everett Rogers' skepticism, advising readers to treat the chasm as a lens rather than a law.

Key points

  • Visionaries buy change; pragmatists want productivity without disruption.
  • Mainstream customers expect a whole product; FDEs must fill the gaps.
  • Document what early customers did themselves as a whole-product checklist.
  • Focus on one problem in one market, then expand from that beachhead.
  • Everett Rogers disputed the chasm, so treat it as a perspective, not a law.

Key Takeaways

  • Moore argues that early customers buy change, while mainstream customers want productivity improvements to their existing business.
  • Early adopters will fill in the missing parts of a product themselves; mainstream customers will not. Those gaps usually land on the FDE.
  • Everett Rogers pointed out that prior research does not support the "chasm" claim, so this book should be treated as a lens, not a law.

In 1991, Geoffrey Moore wrote that a huge chasm lies between visionary customers and pragmatic customers, and almost everyone ignores it. More than thirty years later, many Forward Deployed Engineer (FDE) teams still step right into it. They deploy successfully for their first customer, apply the same approach to the second, and it fails.

The problem is not the code. According to Moore, the two types of customers buy different things, so the deployment approach must differ too. If you are an FDE at a startup moving from a handful of pilot customers to broader sales, this book can help you articulate the problems you are facing right now.

A marketing book that FDEs should read as an operations manual

We recommend the third edition, *Crossing the Chasm, 3rd Edition: Marketing and Selling Disruptive Products to Mainstream Customers*, published by Harper Business on January 28, 2014.

Moore's official website describes the book as a set of strategies for bridging the gap between visionary early adopters and the pragmatic majority. It is written for marketing and sales people, but the three most important ideas in the book map exactly to what FDEs do on-site with customers every day.

Idea 1: The first customer buys change

Moore draws a clear line between two types of customers. Visionary customers buy change; in his words, the early majority wants to improve the productivity of their existing business. For the former, deployment is a transformation project; for the latter, deployment must be incremental improvement that causes no disruption.

Imagine deploying a document-processing AI agent for two logistics companies. The operations director at Company A wants to rebuild the entire process.

He accepts changes to data entry forms, expects employees to learn new tools, and can tolerate errors in the first three weeks. Company B only wants its finance team to spend two fewer hours a day on data entry, and the ERP system and approval workflows must not change at all.

If you take Company A's solution as-is to Company B and propose "redesigning the process," Company B will only see risk. So during requirements gathering, ask the customer two things: what they want to change, and what must not be touched under any circumstances. The answer to the second question tells you which type of customer you are dealing with.

Idea 2: The whole product is the FDE's job

Moore writes that pragmatic customers evaluate and buy the whole product, not the core technology. Innovators and early adopters are usually willing to fill in the missing pieces themselves; the early majority is not.

This explains why the first customer happily accepts a deployment held together by hand-written scripts, while a mainstream customer sees the same thing as half-finished.

Returning to the earlier example: at Company A, the ERP connector was written by the customer's own IT team, and they also ran employee training. At Company B, no one will do any of that, so the connector, training materials, and support processes must be ready before you arrive.

The first step is to write down everything the early customers did for you. That list is the first draft of the whole product your next customer needs.

This is also worth putting on your resume. A line like "turned an ERP connector written by the first customer's IT team into installation steps that subsequent customers could use directly" shows employers that you think about the product, not just writing code to spec.

Idea 3: One problem, one market, then expand

Moore recommends focusing on a single customer segment at a time and treating each success as a springboard into the next market. Specifically, build a complete solution for one problem in a single market first, then expand outward. This is the "beachhead" or "bowling alley" strategy.

A common FDE mistake is the exact opposite. Imagine three logistics customers: the first wants customs document processing, the second wants warehouse management, and the third wants invoice reconciliation.

If you say yes to all three, six months later you will have three half-finished features, none complete enough to sell to a pragmatic customer.

Following Moore's approach, do it in four steps. First, consolidate all requests from customers in the same industry into one list. Second, pick the problem that appears in the most customers, such as customs documents.

Third, make that piece truly complete: connector, training materials, and support processes — the whole product described in Idea 2. Fourth, once logistics customers are using it reliably, use that success as a springboard into warehousing or another industry.

The hardest step is saying "not for now" to the second and third requests. An FDE who can explain to a customer why their request is queued and give a concrete timeline earns more trust from the product team than one who accepts everything.

Does the chasm really exist?

The chasm concept has its critics. Everett Rogers, the originator of diffusion of innovations theory, pointed out that prior research does not support the idea of a gap between technology adopter groups. So treat this book as a diagnostic lens, not a law.

The third edition covers the technology adoption lifecycle, how to choose a beachhead segment, how to build the whole product with partners, and how to position against competitors.

FDEs should read the whole product and beachhead sections first, because everything in the book directly relevant to deployment is there. The lifecycle section can wait, as background on the theoretical framework; the positioning section is more useful for those preparing to move into solutions or product roles.

The book was first published in 1991 and revised in 1999 and 2014; the case studies are no longer new, but the framework still holds.

The next time a deployment that ran smoothly at your first customer is suddenly judged "not mature enough" by your second, the product itself is probably fine. The more likely situation is that you have just entered a different customer group and need to deploy in a different way.
