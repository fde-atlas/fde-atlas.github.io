What Is an Ontology? Meaning, Differences from Knowledge Graphs, and How to Build One with AI

Original title: オントロジーとは？意味・ナレッジグラフとの違い・AIでの作り方

FDX株式会社 · 2026-08-17 · 20 min read

Summary

This Japanese-language article by FDX株式会社 explains ontologies as machine-readable definitions of business concepts, attributes, relations and constraints—a 'semantic map' written in operational language rather than table names. It distinguishes ontologies (templates) from knowledge graphs (templates filled with real data) and semantic layers (read-only metric definitions), and contrasts these with Palantir's ontology, which adds kinetic elements like action types for executing operations. Citing benchmarks, it shows accuracy gains: 16.7% to 54.2% with knowledge graphs, 72.55% with ontology-based query checks, and continued gains on 2026 models. It lists five failure patterns, Japan-specific challenges, and a five-step approach starting from one use case, ending with FDX's FDE plus Deployment Strategist delivery model.

Key points

  • Ontology is the template; a knowledge graph is that template populated with actual data.
  • AI agents now execute business actions, making strict semantic definitions essential.
  • Benchmarks: SQL direct 16.7% vs knowledge graph 54.2%; with ontology checks 72.55%.
  • Failures stem from governance: modeling source systems, missing definition owners, company-wide scope.
  • Start with one decision use case, limiting object types to 10–30 nouns.

What Is an Ontology? (3-Line Summary)

  • An ontology is a "map of meaning." It is a systematized, machine-interpretable representation of the concepts (things) that exist in a domain, along with their relationships and constraints. It is best understood as the work of redefining data in the business language used on the front lines, rather than in table or column names.
  • Its role is as the glue between data integration and frontline operations. The real cause of "we have data but can't use it" is that the meaning (context) of the data is not shared with anyone. An ontology fills in that missing layer.
  • The concept itself is an old technology dating back to the 1990s, but it regained attention in 2026 because AI agents have advanced into actual execution, and LLMs have dramatically lowered construction costs.

In the sections that follow, we will go through, in order, the differences from similar terms, the Palantir-style structure, empirical data on LLM accuracy, and common failure patterns.

Why Ontologies "Now"? | Three Forces

The term ontology originally comes from philosophy's "study of being," and in information science it has been studied since the 1990s in the context of the Semantic Web. Standards such as RDF and OWL were established more than 20 years ago. In other words, it is not a new concept at all.

Nevertheless, it surged into practical business discourse between 2025 and 2026 because three forces converged.

Force 1: AI Has Shifted from "Reading" to "Acting"

While generative AI was limited to chat and summarization, slight ambiguity in data meaning caused little real harm, because humans could always visually correct things at the end.

But once AI agents began handling business execution—placing orders, credit approvals, inventory reservations, ticket creation—everything changed. If "customer" refers to different granularities in the sales management system and the accounting system, an agent will casually run processes against the wrong party. For AI that executes, rigorous definitions of meaning became indispensable. This is the biggest driving force behind the reevaluation of ontologies.

Force 2: LLMs Broke Down Construction Costs

The biggest reason ontology construction never spread was that the cost-benefit ratio didn't work out. Even when experts spent months writing conceptual systems in OWL, the applications that could use them were limited.

LLMs broke this premise twice over. First, they can now perform the drafting work of extracting concept candidates and relationship candidates from existing schemas and documents at a fraction of the human cost. Second, a completed ontology can now be used as a translator between natural-language questions and data, dramatically expanding the avenues for return on investment. Building became cheaper, and use cases multiplied. The conditions for widespread adoption were finally in place.

Force 3: Data Platform Vendors Converged on the Semantic Layer All at Once

In early 2026, enterprise data platforms all began making the same promise: giving business context to AI agents at scale.

  • Microsoft Fabric IQ: Introduced a semantic layer on Fabric that defines business terms, attributes, and relationships and links them to actual data
  • Databricks Unity Catalog Business Semantics: Reached GA (general availability) in early 2026. Defines business semantics starting from the catalog
  • Snowflake Cortex: Similarly passes context to AI centered on semantic models

What was once seen as a Palantir-exclusive concept is becoming a standard feature of major platforms. It is fair to say that ontologies are shifting from "a luxury for a few advanced companies" to "a standard component of the data platform."

Clarifying Four Easily Confused Terms

Most discussions break down because of confusion over terminology. Let's align on this first.

TermWhat It Refers ToAnalogy
OntologyThe "definition (schema)" of concepts, attributes, relationships, and constraints itselfBlueprint / template
Knowledge graphThe entity created by pouring actual data into an ontologyA building constructed according to the blueprint
Semantic layerA "reading" layer that aligns metric definitions into a single company-wide standardA common measuring stick
Data catalogAn inventory of what data exists and whereAn index / library list

The most important distinction is ontology vs. knowledge graph. The ontology is the "template," while the knowledge graph is "the finished product made by pouring real data into that template." The definition "a customer has one or more orders" is the ontology; the actual instance "Yamada Shoji has orders A, B, and C" is the knowledge graph.

It is also worth understanding the difference from a semantic layer. A semantic layer is often a read-only layer that unifies metric definitions such as sales or inventory turnover. The Palantir-style ontology discussed later differs decisively in that, on top of this, it has a layer for "acting."

Palantir-style ontology | Mapping the world through "things" and "actions"

A representative example of bringing ontology up to the level of real-world practice is Palantir's Foundry / AIP. The company describes its ontology as an organizational digital twin. It sits on top of datasets and models, connecting them to their real-world counterparts (from physical assets such as factories, equipment, and products to concepts such as orders and transactions).

The structure broadly divides into two groups of elements.

Semantic elements | "Describe" the organization

  • Object types: Schema definitions of real-world entities and events. For example, there is an object type "airport," and JFK and LHR are its individual objects
  • Properties: Attributes that objects possess
  • Link types: Definitions of relationships between object types, such as "this order is linked to this customer"

Kinetic elements | "Change" the organization

  • Action types: Take input from frontline operators and connect to existing systems to drive decision-making processes. A mechanism for making changes while following controls and governance
  • Functions: A mechanism for writing and growing business logic of arbitrary complexity
  • Dynamic security: Control over who can view which objects and execute which actions

This is the essence. While a typical semantic layer remains a layer for "reading," a Palantir-style ontology serves as the foundation of applications that handle both reading and writing.

Put simply, an ontology is a translation of business operations into things (nouns) and actions (verbs). "Orders," "inventory," and "delivery runs" are things; "approve an order" and "reroute a run" are actions. Once this mapping is in place, both humans and AI agents can make decisions and execute on the same worldview.

And this also implies a change in the capabilities demanded of engineers. In addition to the ability to write code, the design skill to understand frontline business flows and translate them into digital things and actions is now required. This is also why FDX values the role of Forward Deployed Engineer (FDE) (What is an FDE).

How much does an ontology improve LLM accuracy?

Rather than stopping at "defining meaning seems useful," let's look at quantitative evidence. Over the past few years, multiple independent benchmarks have produced results pointing in the same direction.

LLM accuracy roughly tripled with a knowledge graph

An enterprise benchmark published by data.world in 2023 and presented at the 2024 GRADES-NDA workshop has become the reference point in this field. In question answering against a real company's SQL database, the following differences emerged.

  • Accuracy when querying the SQL database directly: 16.7%
  • Accuracy when querying the same data as a knowledge graph representation: 54.2%

Same data, same LLM (GPT-4); the only difference was whether a semantic layer existed, yet accuracy roughly tripled. Especially in the "complex questions × complex schema" domain, accuracy with direct SQL dropped to nearly zero, while querying via the knowledge graph maintained significant accuracy.

Adding ontology-based checks raises accuracy to 72%

Follow-up research (Sequeda et al., 2024) proposed a method that uses the ontology as a mechanism for inspecting generated queries.

  • OBQC (Ontology-Based Query Check): Deterministically checks whether the SPARQL query generated by the LLM is consistent with the ontology's semantics
  • LLM Repair: Feeds the error explanations from the check back to the LLM to repair the query

With this combination, overall accuracy reached 72.55%, 4.2 times that of direct SQL. Notably, about 8% of that was correctly answering "I don't know." The error rate dropped to 19.44%. An ontology not only increases correct answers but also gives AI the ability to say "I cannot answer." In enterprise use, this property is often the more valuable one.

Even with 2026 models, the semantic layer still matters

To the question "won't the semantic layer become unnecessary as models get smarter?" the benchmark published by dbt Labs in 2026 provides an answer. The table below compares results against the same modeled data, with only the interface differing.

PathGPT-5.3 CodexClaude Sonnet 4.6
Via semantic layer100%98.2%
Having AI write SQL (text-to-SQL)84.1%90.0%

Even with the latest generation of models, accuracy is clearly higher when going through the semantic layer. In addition, when context-rich metadata was provided, SQL generation accuracy was 3 times higher than with schema-only prompts, and a separate benchmark of 522 questions reported a 38% relative improvement.

The evolution of models has not made the semantic layer unnecessary. On the contrary, the more agents take on execution, the more the remaining few percent of errors surface as operational incidents, which raises the value of the semantic layer.

Five common failure patterns in ontology adoption

That said, building an ontology is also a highly difficult project. It is worth knowing the typical ways implementations break down in advance.

1. Modeling the source systems instead of the business

This is the most common failure. If you copy existing tables directly into object types, you end up with names derived from table names, producing a model that no one outside the data department can recognize. Since the value of an ontology lies in "redefining things in the language of the field," this makes the entire investment a waste. The correct approach is actually the reverse: start the design from business processes and connect existing schemas afterward.

2. Bloat and decay

In a neglected ontology, optional fields proliferate, nearly duplicate object types pile up, and actions that "some team needed as a workaround in month three" linger on. The more definitions accumulate, the harder it becomes to know which one is correct.

3. No owner for definitions

Healthy operation requires a named owner for each object type who has the authority to arbitrate disputes over definitions. If you proceed without being able to decide "who is a customer" between sales and accounting, the ontology becomes a graveyard of unagreed definitions.

4. Trying to model the entire company at once

Building and maintaining an ontology that accurately represents the data, processes, and models of an entire organization is extremely complex and requires continuous updating. The approach of "first create a complete map of the whole company" almost certainly stalls.

5. Vendor lock-in

Embedding business logic, semantic models, and operational workflows into a specific product makes migration costs extremely high. Moreover, Foundry's ontology has no OWL-style logical inference engine and is not designed with multi-vendor agent interoperability in mind. How much of your company's semantic definitions to keep in your own hands is a point you should consciously decide at selection time.

These share the same structure as the five structural reasons AI PoCs fail. The failures occur not in the technology, but in consensus building and operational design.

Issues specific to Japanese companies

1. The problem of "revenue" being defined differently by each department

When Japanese companies start on an ontology, they often stall before it. Basic terms such as revenue, utilization, customer, and deal are used with different meanings in each department. This may look like a problem that predates the ontology, but aligning these definitions is itself the greatest value of adopting an ontology. The side effect of unifying company-wide terminology for the first time, using AI adoption as the impetus, is by no means small.

2. A culture of negotiation and verbalizing "things" (processes)

Defining "things" (nouns) is relatively easy. The hard part is "happenings" (verbs). In Japanese companies, approval processes and exception handling are often not documented and run on informal coordination in the field. Defining this tacit knowledge as action types is laborious, but the differentiation that competitors cannot imitate lies precisely here (see also What is Company Brain on structuring internal knowledge).

3. Inheriting permission structures

Who can see which objects, and who can execute which actions. In Japanese companies, how faithfully you can inherit existing permission structures such as Active Directory determines the success or failure of the implementation. This is difficult to retrofit and should be decided early in the design.

4. Cross-departmental consensus requires executive sponsorship

Unifying definitions always involves reconciling interests between departments. It cannot be settled by field-led bottom-up efforts alone. Whether you can establish a system in which management can rule "we will go with this definition" becomes the project's watershed.

How to build an ontology | 5 steps

As the flip side of the failure patterns, a realistic approach looks like this.

  • Work backward from a use case: Instead of "a map of the whole company," pick one decision you want to improve. Start from a concrete question like "when a delivery is delayed, which shipment do we reroute?"
  • Limit things to 10-30: Define only the nouns needed for that decision. Prioritize precision that the field can say "we understand that term" over comprehensiveness.
  • Define the happenings and decide execution authority: For each action, write down "who" can execute it and "under what conditions." Only when you have written this far can you delegate to an AI agent.
  • Connect real data to eliminate ambiguity: When you flow real data through, contradictions such as "there are three kinds of customer" will inevitably appear. This friction is the real work and cannot be found in desk-based design.
  • Connect AI and run an evaluation loop: Build an evaluation set of real business questions and measure the accuracy rate and the "I don't know" rate. Aim for a state where the numbers move every time you revise a definition.

And successful operations share common practices: there is an owner with arbitration authority for each object type, action types go through review before release, and the procedure for retiring unused properties is documented. An ontology is not something you build and finish; it is something you cultivate and prune.

FAQ

Q1. What is the difference between an ontology and a knowledge graph?

An ontology is the "definition (schema)" itself of concepts, relationships, and constraints, while a knowledge graph is the entity created by pouring real data into that definition. It's easiest to think of it as the relationship between a "template" and "a building constructed according to that template." "A customer has one or more orders" is the ontology; "Yamada Shoji has orders A, B, and C" corresponds to the knowledge graph. In practice they are discussed together, so they are easily confused, but the object of design is the ontology side.

Q2. Are an ontology and a semantic layer the same thing?

They overlap, but they are not the same. A semantic layer often refers to a "reading" layer that unifies metric definitions such as sales or inventory turnover across the company. In contrast, an enterprise ontology, exemplified by Palantir's Ontology, additionally has a "moving/acting" layer with action types and functions, serving as the foundation for applications that handle both reading and writing. If you want AI agents to execute business operations, you need the scope of the latter.

Q3. Does building an ontology really improve LLM accuracy?

Multiple independent benchmarks show that it does. In data.world's enterprise benchmark, direct querying of a SQL database achieved 16.7%, while a knowledge graph representation of the same data achieved 54.2%, roughly a threefold improvement. Adding ontology-based query checking (OBQC) and LLM-based repair brings it to 72.55%. Even in a benchmark published by dbt Labs in 2026, going through a semantic layer on the same modeled data achieved 98-100%, versus 84-90% for having AI write SQL directly, leaving a clear gap.

Q4. Won't ontologies become unnecessary as models get smarter?

Current evidence suggests the opposite. Even with the latest models as of 2026, accuracy is clearly higher when going through a semantic layer. What's more important is that once AI agents take on execution, the remaining dozen or so percent of errors translate directly into business incidents. For read-only use cases, humans can correct errors, but at the stage of automatically executing orders or credit decisions, this is unacceptable. It is reasonable to conclude that the more agent-driven things become, the more important the semantic layer is.

Q5. Can't you build an ontology without adopting Palantir?

You can. As of early 2026, major data platforms such as Microsoft Fabric IQ, Databricks Unity Catalog Business Semantics (GA), and Snowflake Cortex offer equivalent semantic layers. There is also the option of building on open standards like RDF/OWL. The design work of translating your business into things and processes matters more than product selection. That said, embedding business logic deeply into a specific product raises migration costs, so decide in advance how much of the definitions you want to keep in-house.

Q6. At what scale should you start?

An approach that tries to model the entire company at once will almost certainly stall. It's realistic to start by choosing one decision you want to improve and narrowing the necessary nouns down to 10-30. Once you connect real data, contradictions like "we have three kinds of customers" will inevitably surface, so it's ultimately faster to encounter that friction early. The standard practice is to expand from areas with results to adjacent operations.

Q7. What kind of talent is needed to build an ontology?

Data modeling skills alone are not enough. You need the design ability to understand on-site business workflows and translate them into digital things and processes. In addition, since a framework for arbitrating definitions that have fractured across departments is essential, you need a role that can connect both management and the front lines. FDX handles this with a pair of FDE (implementation) and DS (strategy and consensus building) (see: Differences between DS and FDE roles).
