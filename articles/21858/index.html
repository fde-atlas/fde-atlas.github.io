How to Debug Robot Fleet Failures with MCAP Logs

FDE Times · 2026-11-07 · 6 min read

Summary

Physical Intelligence describes how its Forward Deployed Robotics Engineers debug warehouse robot failures, using a dropped box at station 7 as the running example. The workflow: prioritize tickets by fleet-wide impact rather than arrival order, reproduce the fault on site (e.g., 6 drops in 40 picks) to get exact timestamps, then read MCAP logs—ROS 2's default format—by slicing a few-second window around each drop and comparing gripper commands with feedback. The timeline points to one of three causes: worn hardware, system latency, or a model choosing a bad grasp point. The article warns against diagnosing before reproducing, blaming the component you know best, or closing tickets without checking other robots, and stresses reporting recurring patterns to the design team. It closes with advice on demonstrating these skills in job applications.

Key points

  • Prioritize tickets by fleet-wide impact, not arrival order.
  • Reproduce the fault on site before diagnosing it.
  • Slice MCAP logs into short windows around each drop.
  • Attribute failures to hardware, latency, or the model.
  • Report recurring patterns to the design team after fixing.

In brief

  • At Physical Intelligence, the Forward Deployed Robotics Engineer is the robot fleet's first line of technical defense: analyzing tickets, reproducing faults and collecting diagnostic data.
  • Reproduce the fault at the robot station before diagnosing it; MCAP, the default log format of ROS 2, is what you will open to read.
  • A fault is only truly fixed once you have checked whether it recurs on other robots and reported the pattern to the design team.

A new ticket lands in the queue at the start of a shift: “Robot at station 7 dropped a box.” No photo, no timestamp, just an impatient warehouse operator. You could guess straight away at a worn gripper, a dirty camera or a model gone haywire. All three sound plausible. Guessing like that is also the quickest way to lose two days.

Physical Intelligence describes its Forward Deployed Robotics Engineer role as the first line of technical defense for a growing robot fleet. The robots work in warehouses and in external data-collection environments.

The duties are listed plainly: analyze tickets from the field, reproduce faults, collect diagnostic data and follow each issue through to resolution.

The skill is not confined to robotics. Reading several data streams along a single timeline, narrowing down a cause and turning a one-off incident into a lesson for the whole system is the job of any FDE who has to debug production on real data.

What differs with robots is that a wrong guess leaves visible consequences straight away: the box on the warehouse floor.

First in is not always first fixed

The job description asks for the ticket queue to be prioritized by impact on the whole fleet and by operational goals, not by order of arrival. So the first question about the station 7 ticket is not “why” but “where else”.

Take a case where you filter the past week’s queue for “drop” and “gripper” and find three other stations reporting something similar, all running the same firmware version. The station 7 ticket is no longer one robot’s problem.

It is a symptom of a fleet-wide issue and goes to the top of the queue. If, on the other hand, only station 7 is affected, you are looking for something specific to that station: hardware, layout, lighting or the type of box.

Bash is enough for this step. A

grep -il "fell\|drop" tickets/*.txt | xargs grep -h "station"

followed by

sort | uniq -c

gives you a first picture in thirty seconds. No dashboard required.

No reproduction, nothing to diagnose

The next step is the one many software engineers want to skip: going to the station. Physical Intelligence explicitly lists physical inspection and functional testing at the robot station to confirm that a fault can be reproduced. A fault that cannot be reproduced may be a box placed off-center just once, or an operator reporting the wrong robot.

At station 7, you have the robot pick the same type of box repeatedly. Suppose that over 40 picks, 6 end in a drop. That figure is one you produced under conditions you control, and, more important, you know exactly when each of those 6 drops happened. Those timestamps are the key to the log file.

Logs are where the data streams sit side by side

On a robot running ROS 2, what you open is usually an MCAP file. MCAP is an open-source container format for multimodal log data and the default log format of ROS 2.

It records and replays binary messages in any format. A single file can hold camera images, joint states, gripper commands and a force-sensor stream at once.

A common mistake is to open the whole file in a visualization tool and scrub aimlessly along the timeline. Do the opposite: cut a window of a few seconds around each drop and read only the relevant topics. The script below shows the idea. Topic and file names are examples; substitute the ones from the real system.

from mcap.reader import make_reader
from mcap_ros2.decoder import DecoderFactory

TOPICS = ["/gripper/command", "/gripper/state"]
DROPS = [1730955612.4, 1730955987.1]  # timestamps of the drops during reproduction
WINDOW = 3.0  # seconds before and after each drop

with open("station7_repro.mcap", "rb") as f:
    reader = make_reader(f, decoder_factories=[DecoderFactory()])
    for _, channel, message, msg in reader.iter_decoded_messages(topics=TOPICS):
        t = message.log_time / 1e9
        if any(abs(t - d) <= WINDOW for d in DROPS):
            print(f"{t:.3f}  {channel.topic:18}  {msg}")

The output is a short timeline in which commands and feedback sit right next to each other. Read it like a conversation: the robot says “close the gripper”, and what does the gripper answer, and after how long?

Three stories a timeline can tell

Consider three outcomes. In the first, the close command goes out, but the gripper state reports a clamping force well below that of successful picks. That points to hardware: a worn gripper pad or a weak motor.

In the second, the close command arrives late relative to the moment the gripper touches the box. That points to latency in the system. Compare that delay across the drops and the successful picks.

The third is the hardest: the hardware responds correctly, latency is normal, but the gripper chooses a grasp point well off the edge of the box. Now you have to ask about the model.

Physical Intelligence writes that π0.5 aims to generalize to new environments, and that its experiments were run in homes not present in the training data.

When it open-sourced π0, the company also said plainly that the release was experimental, since π0 had been developed for its own robots.

For the person diagnosing a fault, those statements are a reminder about expectations: the field environment can be very different from anything the model saw in training. A new type of box, glossy tape or unfamiliar lighting can all change the model’s behavior without a single component failing. Save the camera images from each drop.

Physical Intelligence says that 1 to 20 hours of data was enough to fine-tune π0 for a range of tasks, so carefully recorded failure scenes can be useful to the training team as well as to your own ticket.

Closing the ticket is only half the job

The job description includes a duty that is easy to forget: identify recurring failures across the fleet and point out durability weaknesses so the design team can improve them.

If the gripper pad at station 7 wears out after a certain number of cycles, the next question is how many cycles the other stations have run. Replacing the pad fixes one robot. Telling the design team the pad needs to last longer fixes the fleet.

A good root-cause report needs only one page: the symptom, how it was reproduced and the failure rate, the log excerpt as evidence, the cause, the on-site fix, and the pattern reported to design. Write it to the same template every time, and after a few months you have a dataset that shows which failures keep coming back.

The mistakes that drag a diagnosis out

The most common is diagnosing before reproducing: fixing on a hypothesis, watching the fault vanish for five minutes and closing the ticket, when at a failure rate of 6 in 40, five minutes proves nothing.

The second is concluding “the model is wrong” or “the hardware is broken” simply because that is the part you know best. The third is reading the entire log instead of isolating the right time window, and the last is closing a ticket without ever asking whether other stations have the same problem.

How to prove this skill when applying

Physical Intelligence’s requirements list at least 2 years of experience in debugging and root-cause investigation, along with experience analyzing logs and writing diagnostic scripts in Python or Bash. Against a job description like that, “experienced in debugging” on a CV convinces no one.

Be specific. For example: “Wrote a Python script that slices logs around incidents, cutting the time to find the cause of an intermittent fault”, together with a root-cause report with sensitive details removed.

In the interview, walk through one fault in the order set out here: how you prioritized it, how you reproduced it, what the logs showed, and what you reported back to the design team.

The robot at station 7 will drop boxes again, and so will station 12. The engineer trusted in the field is the one who can find, in the logs, the exact moment the box fell.
