The FDE concept has fallen into misuse in China's AI application industry, while the capabilities it truly requires are extremely scarce.

Original title: FDE概念在中国AI应用行业陷入滥用，真正所需能力极为稀缺

Summary

The author argues that the FDE title is being diluted in China as software companies rebrand implementation consultants as FDEs, mistaking a job title for capability building. Real FDEs must understand business at the SOP level—specifying what information to gather, what conditions to judge, and what actions to trigger—since AI agents deliver outcomes, not tools. Excellent FDEs are extremely scarce: even a leading AI product team has only two or three. Consulting experts know industry strategy but not delivery constraints, and many refuse field work. Low project budgets in China make expensive FDE teams unprofitable, so the realistic path is training young, willing-to-learn people through real projects and accumulating their experience as company assets.

Key points

  • Chinese firms are rebranding implementation consultants as FDEs, confusing titles with capability
  • True business understanding means extracting executable SOPs: information, conditions, actions
  • Agents deliver results, not tools, so FDEs must encode business judgment into SOPs
  • A leading AI team leader admits only 2-3 of his staff are truly excellent FDEs
  • Low domestic project budgets make hiring costly FDE teams unprofitable; internal training is the realistic path

Full text

This article comes from the WeChat official account ToB Laorenjia, author: Wang Daiming

Finally, FDE is about to become a cliché too.

Similar things have actually happened many times before.

When SaaS was the darling of capital, all software companies transformed overnight into SaaS companies. Still charging with a one-time buyout? No problem. Just have customers pay what they would have paid upfront in installments over 5 years, and it can be packaged as "annual subscription billing." This approach has another benefit: a renewal rate as high as 100%.

When AI became a global trend, all SaaS companies transformed overnight into AI application companies. No competitive AI product? No problem. Quickly release a demo, slap on a few fancy labels like "China's Palantir," and then reclassify all traditional software revenue as AI revenue. Just like that, a high-purity AI company is born.

Well, now it's finally FDE's turn.

It seems that overnight, a large number of traditional implementation consultants disappeared. At the same time, the market suddenly saw a flood of FDEs.

Of course, software companies doing this isn't entirely without reason. After all, the FDE title is naturally tied to AI projects. To customers, it also sounds more like someone who can solve AI implementation problems than "implementation consultant."

But the problem is, when customers have high expectations of the so-called FDE, they may quickly discover: this fancy FDE seems no different from the original implementation consultant, and may even be worse than a senior implementation consultant.

When everyone starts packaging implementation consultants and solutions consultants as FDEs, we are actually making a serious mistake: treating a job title as capability building.

Let me first briefly explain what an FDE is.

FDE, or Forward Deployed Engineer, is a role first popularized by Palantir. In Palantir's early startup days, it actually didn't know what customers really wanted. So it sent FDEs to customer sites to understand customer needs, deploy systems, and feed field experience back into the product.

In other words, an FDE is not just someone who delivers projects—they also need to distill product capabilities from projects.

In the AI era, FDEs have become important again because in most cases, neither software companies nor customers know what AI can actually do, or how AI should be implemented. Especially for enterprise AI projects, it's often not as simple as the customer stating a clear requirement and the vendor building a feature.

It's more like both sides exploring together: Does this scenario actually have value? Can the data be obtained? Can it ultimately produce business results?

So you'll see that AI companies like OpenAI and Anthropic are all hiring FDEs. Because enterprise AI implementation doesn't end with selling a model. The model is just the capability foundation; the real challenge is putting the model into the enterprise's business context.

This is also why I believe that whether it's AI projects going from 0 to 1 or AI projects already in the scaling stage, FDEs directly determine project success or failure.

However, excellent FDEs are extremely scarce.

I know a leader of an AI product team. He has been working on enterprise AI implementation since 2023 and now manages a sizable team. The commercial AI agent he is responsible for also holds a top position in its niche market.

But he told me something very candid: out of his entire team, only 2 or 3 people can truly be called excellent FDEs.

This is not a newcomer to AI complaining about how hard it is to hire talent. It's someone who has been doing enterprise AI implementation for years, manages a sizable team, and whose product has reached the top of its niche, admitting that this role is genuinely difficult.

So what exactly makes the FDE role so hard?

Many people would say that FDEs need to be too well-rounded: they must understand business, AI, and IT. That's certainly true. But I think it doesn't go deep enough.

Because for many enterprise AI projects, AI technology and IT capabilities are actually not the hardest parts. In the process of AI implementation, AI capability is mainly prompts, and IT capability is mainly interfaces and system integration. These things certainly have a learning curve, but at least they can be trained.

Especially now, many software companies hiring FDEs recruit young people with strong learning abilities, even graduates from top universities and master's degree holders. As long as they are willing to learn, AI and IT capabilities are not the biggest barriers.

The truly hard part is understanding the business.

There is a big misconception here: in software companies, many people think they understand the business.

Implementation consultants think they understand the business because they know how customer processes work, how fields are filled in, and how permissions are configured. Product managers think they understand the business because they have drawn process diagrams, written PRDs, and participated in requirement research. Consulting experts think they understand the business because they can explain methodologies to customers, build frameworks, and deliver presentations.

But none of this necessarily means truly understanding the business.

The business can actually be divided into three layers.

The shallowest layer is processes and forms.

For example, in a CRM project, a nurtured lead needs to be converted into an opportunity. When filling in opportunity information, fields such as estimated amount, opportunity stage, expected close date, and customer contact need to be entered.

Many traditional implementation consultants understand this layer. Because a large part of traditional software delivery work is configuring these processes, fields, permissions, approvals, and reports.

One layer deeper is strategy and mechanisms.

For example, how do you motivate salespeople to follow up on opportunities in a timely manner? This might involve a lead recycling mechanism. If a lead has no follow-up activity for a period of time, it gets recycled back into the public pool and reassigned to another salesperson. This is where some industry know-how starts to come in.

For example, how long should a lead go without follow-up before it should be recycled? Behind this question may be the visit cycles of different customer types, and the reasonable daily visit volume for salespeople. These questions are no longer simple field configuration, but business strategy.

But understanding only to this layer, I believe, is still not enough.

Because the biggest difference between AI agents and traditional software is that traditional software mainly carries processes, while AI agents begin to participate in judgment and action. Traditional software delivers tools. AI agents deliver results.

So in the AI era, truly understanding the business requires entering the third layer: the business SOP layer.

For example. If a salesperson is facing a potential customer, what kind of proposal should they provide to increase the win rate? This judgment is not simple. First, you need to know what information to collect. Then you need to identify the key variables from that information. Finally, you need to make specific judgments based on these variables.

For example, when a salesperson faces a parts machining customer, they can proactively collect one piece of information: how many times per year does this customer experience unplanned downtime due to equipment failures? If downtime exceeds a certain number of times, the salesperson can focus on recommending the "predictive equipment maintenance" solution.

This is a business SOP.

It's not a vague statement like "recommend providing solutions based on customer pain points." It's specific down to: what information to collect, what conditions to judge, what actions to trigger, and what solutions to recommend. This is exactly what an AI agent truly needs.

Otherwise, if you let AI assist sales, it will most likely just output a pile of correct nonsense: recommend gaining a deeper understanding of customer needs, recommend providing targeted solutions, recommend continuously following up on customer feedback. These statements are certainly not wrong. But they are of no help to business results.

So, if business SOPs cannot be broken down, AI will struggle to give recommendations that can truly change business results. And this heavy responsibility often falls on the FDE.

Since FDEs require such specialized skills, can we find them from consulting experts or business experts?

Of course we can. But this raises new problems. The issue with consulting experts and business experts is that they tend to stay at the macro level. They may know the industry well, understand the general direction customers should take, and excel at solution design. But they may not understand interfaces, system boundaries, and delivery constraints. They can explain clearly "how things should improve," but may not be able to turn that into a capability that an AI agent can execute, test, and deploy.

More realistically, many consulting experts are unwilling to switch to FDE roles. Because FDE work is dirty and exhausting. You have to communicate extensively with clients, handle all kinds of on-site uncertainties, take responsibility for delivery outcomes, and face project pressure. Many people in consulting simply don't want to jump into the delivery trenches.

Behind the difficulty of finding talent, there is also a deeper problem: the contract values of AI projects at domestic companies are generally not high.

I've come across some AI projects where, even when they're relatively sizable, the contract value still can't sustain the long-term investment of a high-cost FDE team.

A project of the same complexity might command a higher budget in overseas markets. But domestically, once project values are low and FDE costs are high, companies easily fall into an awkward position: without strong FDEs, project results suffer; with strong FDEs, the project isn't profitable.

So in the short term, it will be hard for China to quickly build up a batch of excellent FDEs with high salaries the way Silicon Valley does.

The more realistic path is to develop them in-house.

Recruit young people with a sense of responsibility, strong learning ability, and willingness to go on-site, and let them enter real projects and grow out of the delivery loop. Young people learn fast, have drive, and their salary expectations are relatively lower.

But their biggest weakness is often poor business understanding. They easily assume that once the system goes live, the project is delivered.

But AI agent projects don't work that way. Agents deliver results. System go-live is only the beginning. Afterward, you still have to watch how clients use it, how the business outcomes are, how the knowledge base gets supplemented, and how the SOPs get revised.

So the key to developing FDEs is not teaching young people technology, but having them continuously track clients' real usage.

Continuously observe what happens after clients go live. Continuously observe which suggestions work and which don't. Continuously observe which SOPs can be reused and which are just special cases of a single client.

Finally, turn these project experiences into company assets.

So back to the beginning: the term FDE will definitely become overused. You'll soon see more and more companies calling their implementation consultants FDEs.

That's not surprising. Every new concept, once it enters China's software industry, will most likely go through a round of packaging, misuse, and bubble-ization.

But while the term gets overused, the capability doesn't.

Real FDEs will remain very scarce. Because the role requires a person to go into the client's site, understand the real business, break out the business SOPs, push the client's interfaces to be ready, complete the delivery loop, and finally feed the project experience back into product assets. This can't be solved by simply changing a job title.

If Chinese AI application companies truly want to take the FDE path, they will inevitably return to a few very plain things:

Whether there are people willing to go on-site, whether there are people who can truly understand the business, whether there are people who can break business experience into SOPs that agents can execute, and whether there are mechanisms to turn the experience from every project into company assets.

This path is definitely not easy, but it's most likely one that can't be avoided.
