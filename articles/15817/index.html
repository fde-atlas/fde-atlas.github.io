Screen-recording tutorials teach AI to do the work: Desktop agents are replacing highly paid Forward Deployed Engineers

Original title: 录屏教学让AI学会干活：桌面Agent正在替代高薪前沿部署工程师

满银网 · 2026-06-16 · 4 min read

Summary

This article explains "screen-recording teaching": instead of writing prompts, users record themselves completing a task, and desktop agents learn the underlying task logic rather than fixed clicks. Unlike macro-style script tools, these agents can adapt when interfaces change. The author describes a "brain plus cerebellum" architecture where a large model handles planning and exceptions while a small model executes interface actions, cutting a two-minute data-entry task to under a minute in tests. Enterprises prioritize stability, requiring training convergence, multi-layer verification, and rule constraints. The approach lowers automation costs and reduces reliance on expensive forward deployed engineers (FDEs), with the most repetitive, standardized work likely automated first.

Key points

  • Screen-recording teaching lets AI learn task logic, not fixed coordinates.
  • Desktop agents split work: large models plan, small models execute clicks.
  • Tests cut a two-minute entry task to under one minute.
  • Enterprises need convergence, multi-layer checks, and rule constraints.
  • The method lowers costs and reduces reliance on costly FDEs.

AI is moving from "being able to chat" to "being able to work." In the past it answered questions; now it is starting to take over desktop tasks like filling out forms, entering data into systems, and organizing files. As desktop agents keep evolving, the workflows of ordinary people are being redefined.

What's more interesting is that what's really driving this isn't just model capability, but the method that teaches AI to learn processes. "Screen-recording teaching," which has been mentioned frequently recently, is turning AI from understanding text into understanding operations.

1. Why has "teaching AI to work" always been hard?

Many people assume that if you write a prompt for AI, it will execute accordingly. Reality isn't that simple, because real workflows often can't be explained in a single sentence—they're a chain of details:

- Which system to open first - What to fill in at each step - Whether to skip or retry when something goes wrong - When to upload attachments - Which pages require jumping across systems

These details are often handled by experienced staff through intuition, but they're hard to explain fully to others. If humans can't articulate them clearly, AI naturally has an even harder time learning them directly. This is why companies employ FDEs—Forward Deployed Engineers who work on-site to map out business processes, translating "things people can do but can't explain" into tasks AI can execute.

2. What is screen-recording teaching?

Screen-recording teaching means users don't first learn prompts; instead, they simply record their computer operations. You complete a task as you normally would, and AI learns the entire process from the recording, then attempts to reproduce it.

This differs from the traditional "teach the machine where to click" approach. What it learns isn't individual click actions, but task logic: why to open this page first, when to type, and when to switch steps when certain situations arise.

This approach is especially suited to work with fixed processes but many details, such as back-office data entry, document organization, system form filling, and repetitive reviews. For companies, the biggest value isn't showing off technology, but automating repetitive labor.

3. Why is it more like AI than "macro recorders"?

Many people's first reaction is to associate these tools with the script automation software of years past, but the two are very different.

Macro recorder tools are more like "remembering coordinates and actions"—if a button moves, the script easily breaks. Desktop agents take an approach closer to "understanding the task": they don't just know where to click, they know why to click there.

So even if the interface changes or buttons move, it can keep reasoning and executing based on the process, rather than mechanically memorizing a sequence of operations.

4. Why does AI need a "brain + cerebellum" division of labor?

Many desktop agents are slow not because they can't do the task, but because every step is forced through a general-purpose large model, resulting in something like watching, thinking, and operating all at once—which is naturally slow.

A more efficient approach is to split up the task:

- The large model handles understanding the screen recording, breaking down goals, planning paths, and handling exceptions - Specialized small models handle recognizing the interface, clicking, typing, and executing high-frequency actions

This division of labor resembles how humans operate computers: the brain handles judgment, the cerebellum handles movement. For back-office tasks that require frequent execution, this structure is usually faster and more stable.

In real-world testing, some products can compress a data-entry task that originally took over two minutes to under one minute. For companies, what's saved isn't just time, but also substantial costs from repetitive manual work.

5. What companies really care about isn't "can it be done," but "is it stable"

In production environments, what AI fears most isn't occasionally being slow, but occasionally being wrong. Because writing one wrong data record, missing one attachment, or clicking one wrong button can all have amplified consequences.

So companies value stability more. A usable desktop agent typically needs to accomplish three things:

1. Training convergence, reducing execution divergence 2. Multiple verification layers, where planning, execution, observation, and review cross-check each other 3. Rule constraints, writing key steps into fixed processes

Simply put, it means having it do the same task repeatedly, following the same path as much as possible, to get the same result. For scenarios like finance, customer service, operations, and supply chain, this matters more than "looking smart."

6. Why are these products starting to get attention?

Over the past two years, the AI industry has been competing over bigger models and stronger conversational abilities. But now, a more practical question has emerged: can AI truly enter the workflows of ordinary people?

If only a few large clients can afford expensive Forward Deployed Engineers, automation will be hard to popularize. The significance of the "screen-recording teaching" route is that it lowers the learning barrier, allowing more ordinary teams to hand their processes over to AI.

This means future desktop agents may no longer just be lab demos, but will gradually become "AI employees" that actually go to work. They may not replace everyone, but they will likely first replace the most repetitive, most standardized, and most time-consuming parts of the work.

7. How should ordinary teams understand this?

If a team's daily work involves many fixed steps, it's worth thinking about three questions:

- Are these steps already sufficiently standardized - Which parts are best suited for a first AI trial run - Which critical checkpoints must retain human review

From an implementation perspective, what's most suitable for automation is usually not the most complex task, but the most repetitive one. Starting with tasks like data entry, information organization, and cross-system data transfer tends to show results more quickly.

For individuals, you can also start organizing your own workflows now, writing down and recording "how it's done" clearly. Because it will become increasingly evident: people who can't write code will also be able to train their own dedicated desktop agents through process recordings.

AI is learning to operate computers, and "screen-recording teaching" may be the first key to its truly entering the office.
