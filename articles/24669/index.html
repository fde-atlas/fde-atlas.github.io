How to Run a Pre-mortem Meeting: Risk Assessment Before Launch for FDEs

Original title: Pre-mortem 事前验尸会议怎么开：FDE 上线前风险排查

FDE Times · 2026-11-03 · 6 min read

Summary

This guide explains how forward deployed engineers can run a pre-mortem, the method Gary Klein introduced, to surface risks before a launch. Using an insurance LLM Agent deployment as an example, it walks through a 30-minute session: announce the project has already failed, have everyone silently write causes, take turns speaking, dot-vote on the top three risks and opportunities, and assign owners and deadlines. It also covers common mistakes and how to present the experience on a resume.

Key points

  • Pre-mortems assume failure is certain, which rewards sharp risk-spotting.
  • Klein's format needs only 20-30 minutes, based on prospective hindsight research.
  • The flow: silent writing, round-robin sharing, dot voting, task assignment.
  • Invite real users and hold the session about two weeks before launch.
  • Common mistakes: hedged wording, senior people speaking first, no owners assigned.

First declare that the project has already failed, then ask why: Gary Klein's method breaks the mutual silence both sides keep to save face.

Key Takeaways

  • Project kickoff meetings often mask risks, because no one wants to spoil the team's good vibes.
  • The pre-mortem starts from the assumption that "the project has already failed"; whoever voices the sharpest cause is seen as the one with the clearest view.
  • A meeting can be as short as 20-30 minutes, but it must end with a vote to select a handful of risks, each with an owner and a deadline.

Two weeks before launch. At the steering committee meeting, everyone reports on their piece: the integration is working, the evals passed the threshold, and the operations team has been trained. No one raises an objection. But experience tells you that a meeting this smooth usually misses something.

Psychologist Gary Klein proposed the pre-mortem method about 30 years ago (as of 2021), and he put this phenomenon bluntly.

In meetings like this there is always an unspoken pressure: no one wants to voice doubts for fear of disrupting team harmony. For Forward Deployed Engineers (FDEs), the pressure is even greater, because people from two organizations sit at the same table, and neither wants the other to lose face.

You stand between both sides, which makes you the best person to break this silence. The pre-mortem is the cheapest tool for the job. It requires no special authority, only the ability to run a meeting.

Change the question, and the discussion changes

Ask "where might this project go wrong?" and you usually get safe answers. The pre-mortem asks a different question.

As Klein describes it, the facilitator tells everyone that the plan just discussed has failed, then asks each person to write down the reasons independently. His 2007 article in Harvard Business Review rests on the same assumption: the project being planned has just failed.

Wikipedia offers a medical analogy. A conventional plan review asks how the patient might fare; a pre-mortem assumes the patient has already died, then asks for the cause of death.

The method draws on Mitchell, Russo, and Pennington's 1989 research on "prospective hindsight": looking backward from an imagined future.

The cleverest part is the participants' motivation. Klein observed that once failure is treated as an established fact, people demonstrate their competence through the quality of the issues they raise. Pointing out risks is no longer seen as a lack of confidence in the project, but as a contribution.

Rehearse a 30-minute meeting with your client

Picture a scenario. You are deploying an LLM agent for an insurance company to triage claims cases. Present are the client's project sponsor, the operations lead, an IT engineer responsible for the core system, a claims specialist, and you.

Klein believes such a meeting can be as short as 20-30 minutes. The process below keeps Klein's silent writing and round-robin sharing. The last two steps, voting and handoff, come from Atlassian's template and the UK government digital health team's practice.

Minutes 0-3, opening. Read the prepared opening statement word for word: "It is three months after launch. The project has failed: the agent has been decommissioned, and staff have gone back to manual processing. Please write down the reasons that led to this outcome."

The opening statement must use the past tense and be specific. The moment you say "suppose it might fail," the effect evaporates.

Minutes 3-8, silent writing, no discussion. Everyone writes down reasons independently. This quiet step is critical: it prevents frontline staff from being swayed by the sponsor's views.

The UK digital health team added a step here: split the participants into a "disaster group" and a "success group." In a five-person meeting, ask one or two people to write the opposite scenario: three months later, the agent succeeded. Why?

Answers like "the claims specialists trust the agent because they can see the reasoning behind every triage decision" tell you what must stay in scope, even when the schedule gets tight.

Minutes 8-20, round-robin sharing. Klein suggests the facilitator start candidly to set an example, and that each person share only one reason per round. You might start with a weakness in your own work: "My eval set was built from last year's claims cases, and this year's triage rules have changed."

Then the claims specialist might say that staff don't trust the agent, so they re-verify every case and no time is saved. The IT engineer might point out that the core system throttles API calls during peak hours. Such statements rarely surface in an ordinary steering committee meeting.

Minutes 20-25, voting. The list produced is usually longer than what you can handle. The UK digital health team has everyone vote with dots for the three biggest risks and the three biggest opportunities. Follow that: the three risks become items to fix, and the three opportunities become things to protect in the launch plan.

Minutes 25-30, assigning tasks. The top three risks must each have an owner and a deadline. In the insurance scenario, it might look like this: by Friday, you add 200 claims cases processed under the new rules to the eval set.

The operations lead picks two employees to trial the agent in parallel for a week. The IT engineer confirms the API limits, and you add a queue.

Templates exist, but adapt them for the client

For large, multi-party projects, Atlassian's Team Playbook offers a longer ready-made version: 3-11 people, about 60 minutes, including silent brainstorming, voting on the main risks, and assigning action items with owners and deadlines.

Using it with a client requires two adjustments. First, invite the actual users, because sponsors rarely know where the agent will break down in day-to-day work. Second, hold the meeting early, about two weeks before launch, so there is time to fix things; meeting the day before launch only makes everyone more anxious.

The UK digital health team's lesson: the value lies in the actions. Colin Pattinson wrote that the meeting surfaced risks no one had anticipated, and the team addressed several of them in time. In his view, the pre-mortem forces you to confront problems before they later destroy the project.

Mistakes that turn the pre-mortem into a gripe session

The most common mistake is saying "might fail" instead of "has failed." It's a tiny change in wording, but listeners retreat into a defensive posture, and you're back to safe answers.

The second mistake is letting the most senior person speak first, offering a trivial reason like "maybe there wasn't quite enough time."

Klein wants the facilitator to speak first so that others dare to be candid too. Opening with a trivial reason sends the opposite signal: speak politely here. So agree with the sponsor in advance that the first reason must be something genuinely uncomfortable, or say it yourself first.

The third mistake is ending the meeting with 30 risks and no owners. A list without owners and deadlines is just a record of everyone's worries. The last mistake is inviting only your own team. Then you'll only hear risks you already knew about.

Turn this meeting into evidence on your resume

If the FDE job description you're targeting mentions collaborating with stakeholders or risk management, a pre-mortem you facilitated is good evidence of that skill, provided you can articulate what it changed.

Back to the insurance scenario. A weak resume line is: "Organized a pre-mortem meeting before launch."

A strong line states who attended, what was found, and what was fixed: "Facilitated a pre-mortem with 4 client stakeholders two weeks before launch; identified that evals were out of sync with the new rules and that the core system had API limits during peak hours, and resolved both issues before release."

In interviews, "tell me about a time you prevented an incident" is exactly the place to share details. Name the opening line you used, the reason the claims specialist raised that the sponsor had never heard, and the parallel pilot that followed.

What interviewers want to see is that you proactively look for problems, rather than just firefighting after they erupt.

The next time a steering committee meeting goes suspiciously smoothly, you don't need to argue with anyone. Just ask one question: if the agent gets decommissioned three months from now, what do you think the reason will be?
