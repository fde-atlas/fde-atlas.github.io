How to Create a Data Source Inventory: The FDE Seven-Step Data Audit Method

Original title: 数据源清单怎么做：FDE 数据盘点七步法

FDE Times · 2026-10-08 · 7 min read

Summary

This guide shows forward deployed engineers how to build a data source inventory during their first week on site, using read-only access only. Walking through a hypothetical retail chain with POS, CRM, and a marketing survey form, it demonstrates a seven-step process: start with seven basic fields, scan by source category, assign a named owner per source, record ingestion mode and CDC support, tag sensitivity levels, flag duplicate ingestion and quality issues, and note schema drift risk. The inventory underpins all later pipelines and agents, so owners must be individuals, sensitivity tagging must precede copying data to dev environments, and the list needs quarterly reviews with a clear client-side maintainer after handoff.

Key points

  • Build the inventory in week one with read-only access, in seven steps.
  • Start with seven fields; every source needs a named individual owner.
  • Record batch vs streaming, CDC support, and sensitivity per source.
  • Tag sensitivity before copying any data into dev environments.
  • Review quarterly and name the client-side maintainer in handoff docs.

Spend your first week with read-only access and build a data source inventory in seven steps, recording owners, lineage, and usage restrictions before you build pipelines and agents.

Key takeaways

  • Seven fields are enough to start. The owner column must name a specific person.
  • For every data source, record whether it is batch or streaming, whether it supports CDC, and how sensitive it is. These three points determine how the pipeline is designed.
  • The inventory needs regular review, at least quarterly in the early stages, and should specify which person on the client side will maintain it after handover.

In your first week on site, you ask five people "where is the customer data stored?" and very likely get five different answers. Each answer is partially correct, but no one has the full picture.

Your first task is to consolidate those five answers into a single file that the client team agrees on. IBM defines data ingestion as collecting data from multiple sources for storage, processing, and analysis. Data sources no one lists are ones you cannot collect from.

Therefore, the data source inventory is the foundation for everything that follows; pipelines, dashboards, and agents are all built on top of it. The seven steps below show you how to build an inventory from scratch, using a hypothetical client throughout so you can see how the inventory fills in step by step.

What you will have by the end of the week

The deliverable is a spreadsheet, or a CSV file in the project repository, with one row per data source. GeekyAnts' data discovery guide sets the goal as a complete, trustworthy inventory in which every data source has an owner, data lineage, and usage restrictions.

You need to prepare three things: a file storage location both parties can view, a contact list from the client side, and read access to the systems you are permitted to see. In the first week, request read-only access only. You are there to observe; you are not changing anything yet.

The example running through this article is a hypothetical retail chain with three data sources: a point-of-sale (POS) system in its stores, a customer relationship management (CRM) system, and a survey form the marketing team built themselves. All data rows below are illustrative only.

Step 1: Start with a small set of fields

On Monday morning, the retail company's head of operations sends you a list of "major systems" with only two names on it: POS and CRM. You will be tempted to immediately open a spreadsheet and add a pile of columns. Hold off.

According to IF4IT, the initial record only needs seven fields: ID, display name, description, structure, data type, domain, and sensitivity classification. Beyond those seven fields, add a few columns that directly serve the deployment work, and leave them blank until you have the information.

The column names in the example are, in order: ID, name, description, structure, type, domain, sensitivity level, owner, ingestion method, CDC support, data lineage, usage restrictions, number of ingesting teams, quality notes, and schema drift risk.

id,name,description,structure,type,domain,sensitivity,owner,ingest_mode,cdc_support,lineage,usage_constraints,ingest_team_count,quality_notes,schema_drift_risk

After the first day, the first seven columns look like this (the two rows describe POS orders as checkout transactions and CRM as customer profiles and contact records, with sensitivity levels still "unclassified"):

id,name,description,structure,type,domain,sensitivity
SRC-001,POS 订单,门店交易,关系表,交易数据,零售,unclassified
SRC-002,客户 CRM,客户档案和联系记录,带自定义字段的记录,客户数据,市场,unclassified

Before moving to the next step, check: you must be able to fill in the first six columns for every row. Leave "unclassified" in the sensitivity level column until Step 5. If you cannot describe a data source in one sentence, you do not yet understand it. Mark it as an open question.

Step 2: Scan by source category, not by memory

The head of operations' two-system list is almost certainly incomplete. To avoid omissions, use the four typical data source categories listed by Qlik as an interview checklist: databases, IoT devices, SaaS applications, and data lakes. Go through each category with every department.

Ask about SaaS in the most detail, because tools departments have signed up for on their own may not be on the list of systems you are authorized to scan. At this retail company, the survey form only surfaced when you asked the marketing team "do you use any other SaaS applications?", and the inventory gained one more row:

SRC-003,调查表单

。

For systems you have access to, do not enter data by hand. GeekyAnts recommends using tools to generate the initial catalog, roughly 80% automated and 20% manual. Scanning tools handle the information that can be read from the systems themselves: names, structures, data types.

The manual part covers what machines cannot know on their own, such as who owns a data source and what it is used for. This can only come from interviews.

Step 3: Every row needs a person's name

Ask "who owns the CRM?" and the answer is "IT". That is not an answer. IF4IT requires each record to have its own owner and steward, and the inventory itself must also have a named owner. Writing "the IT department" or "the data team" is the same as writing nothing.

id,owner
SRC-001,Nguyen Van A
SRC-002,Tran Thi B
SRC-003,市场团队

Check: filter the

owner

(owner) column and count the blank cells and cells that only name a team. In the example above,

SRC-003

does not qualify. Compile these rows into a list of questions and bring it to the meeting with the client's project sponsor.

Step 4: Batch or streaming? Any CDC?

The POS pushes transactions to the central data warehouse almost in real time, while the CRM only exports a file every night. These two data sources need two different kinds of pipelines. IBM distinguishes two ingestion methods: batch ingestion collects data first and processes it all at once, while real-time ingestion processes data as it is generated.

Therefore, the

ingest_mode

(ingestion method) column will drive most of the subsequent design. At the same time, record which data sources support change data capture (CDC). As Qlik describes it, this mechanism tracks transaction logs and only sends changed data to target systems:

id,ingest_mode,cdc_support,lineage
SRC-001,stream,yes,POS -> 中央数据仓库
SRC-002,batch,unclear,CRM -> 每晚导出文件
SRC-003,batch,no,表单 -> 手动下载的 CSV 文件

Here yes means supported, no means not supported, and unclear means not yet known. "Unclear" is not an error; it is another question to ask the owner identified in Step 3.

Step 5: Label the sensitivity level of every data source

A colleague on the client side offers to send you "a sample CRM file so you can test quickly". Don't take it yet. According to IBM, the more data exposed during ingestion, the greater the risk of sensitive data leaking. So the

sensitivity

(sensitivity level) column is mandatory, and data sources that look harmless are no exception.

GeekyAnts recommends labeling the sensitivity level of every dataset and running automated personally identifiable information (PII) / protected health information (PHI) detection tools, supplemented by manual review. In the example, POS is only "internal"; CRM contains phone numbers and email addresses, so it is "PII"; the survey form has free-text fields and is tentatively labeled "may contain PII".

This step must be completed before any data is copied into a development environment, not after. Discovering customer phone numbers in a test file in week three is too late.

Step 6: Find duplicate ingestion and quality issues

During interviews, the CRM came up three times: the marketing, customer service, and BI teams all said "we are pulling data from the CRM". This is exactly what Qlik warns about: when multiple teams ingest from the same data source, data easily becomes fragmented and duplicated.

ingest_team_count

One column records how many teams pull data from a given data source. A value of 2 or more means multiple teams hold different copies of the same data. The CRM cell is 3, which is the first risk worth raising. The names of the three teams can go into

quality_notes

(quality notes).

Qlik also recommends incorporating data quality and integrity checks into data governance. At the inventory stage, you do not need detailed measurements. Writing one observation in

quality_notes

, such as "some POS rows are missing the store code", is enough to know what to measure later.

Step 7: Record schema drift risk and schedule reviews

The go-to-market team mentioned that they modify survey questions almost every month. That's where schema drift comes from. IBM describes it as changes to the structure of source data—such as fields being added, removed, or modified—that cause the source and target to no longer be consistent.

Classify each data source as low, medium, or high based on a single question: does this data source frequently change its columns without anyone being notified? Survey forms should be rated high. POS can probably be rated low.

The inventory itself also goes stale. IF4IT recommends verifying it at least quarterly in the early stages, then monthly once the organization matures, or whenever a new integration is added. Write this cadence into the handover documentation, and make clear who on the client side is responsible.

Common mistakes that turn the inventory into a "dead document"

One common mistake is piling on a huge number of fields from the start and giving up by the tenth data source. Another is listing a department name as the owner, or keeping the inventory on your own laptop instead of somewhere the client can view and edit it.

The most dangerous mistake is treating the inventory as paperwork. Every blank cell is a project risk that no one has flagged yet.

How the inventory helps you handle clients and strengthen your resume

Bring this inventory to the review meeting at the end of the first week. It shows the client how deeply you understand their systems, and it gives you the confidence to say "we can't do this part yet, because the survey form has no owner."

On your resume, don't just write "worked with data." Be specific: how many data sources you inventoried, how many instances of duplicate ingestion you found, and how you completed sensitivity-level tagging before the data left the client's systems. This is also a concrete story you can tell in interviews.

Pipelines can be rewritten, and AI agents can be tuned. But a data source missed in the first week may not surface until the tenth week.
