Why are OpenAI and Anthropic both hiring FDEs now? The reason AI companies are rushing to adopt this role.

Following the moves of OpenAI and Anthropic, a striking commonality has emerged over the past few months. Both companies have begun pouring billions of dollars not only into refining their own models, but also into acquiring engineers who embed themselves directly in customer companies' operations. Why, at a time when the competition over model performance has by no means settled, is so much capital flowing toward implementation talent? Pursuing this question from primary sources reveals the structural challenges that generative AI companies themselves are facing.

In the companion article "What Is an FDE?", we examined the definition of the Forward Deployed Engineer (FDE) role and the substance of the working style systematized by Palantir, based on primary sources. As a follow-up, this article verifies, using primary sources and overseas media reports from 2026 onward, why generative AI providers themselves are now investing heavily in FDEs, and organizes what this means for Japanese companies. The corporate developments covered here are all based on information confirmed as of August 2026.

OpenAI and Anthropic are investing in FDE-style businesses one after another

First, let us confirm what is actually happening.

In May 2026, OpenAI announced the establishment of a new company called the "OpenAI Deployment Company" (commonly known as DeployCo). This is a company whose very purpose is a business model in which, rather than selling models as APIs, engineers specializing in implementing cutting-edge AI are placed inside customer companies. Denise Dresser, OpenAI's Chief Revenue Officer (CRO), made remarks to the effect that "the challenge is no longer model performance, but how to integrate these systems into companies' infrastructure and business workflows" (see: Winbuzzer, "OpenAI Launches Deployment Company With Tomoro Acquisition" (2026)). This new company acquired Tomoro, a London-based applied AI consulting firm, and plans to welcome approximately 150 Forward Deployed Engineers and deployment specialists—carrying existing clients such as Tesco, Virgin Atlantic, and Supercell—from day one. Nineteen investment and consulting firms, including TPG, Advent, and Bain Capital, participated in its establishment, and the initial investment is said to exceed $4 billion (see: same source).

The reporting also touches specifically on what these 150 FDEs will actually do. They are to be embedded directly into customer companies' production environments, taking on the unglamorous work of keeping AI systems actually running: building data pipelines, serving models, and monitoring operations (see: same source, Winbuzzer). What "implementation" means here is not flashy prototype development, but the work of getting inside a customer company's systems and shaping them into something that can withstand daily operations. The tech media outlet MarkTechPost also characterized this move as "an investment to bridge the knowledge gap between customers' business understanding and the AI labs' production operations experience," and reported at the same time that Anthropic had established an enterprise AI services company valued at roughly $1.5 billion (per reports) in a joint venture with Blackstone, Hellman & Friedman, and Goldman Sachs (see: MarkTechPost, "What is a Forward Deployed Engineer: The AI Role OpenAI, Anthropic, and Google Are Hiring in 2026" (2026)).

Anthropic's joint venture is called "Ode with Anthropic," and it received major coverage in July 2026. According to TechCrunch's reporting, the starting point of this concept was that Blackstone, when trying to introduce AI into its portfolio companies, perceived a gap: "there is no implementation partner of just the right size" between the major consulting firms and small AI-specialized studios. Blackstone arrived at the concept of Ode to fill this gap, and the joint venture Ode acquired US-based Fractional AI as its foundation. Chris Taylor, co-founder of Fractional AI and CEO of Ode, said of the business that "it is entirely conceivable that it becomes a trillion-dollar company," and Ode already has roughly 100 engineers and is expanding by embedding them in customer companies as Forward Deployed Engineers (see: TechCrunch, "Anthropic and Blackstone bet the next trillion-dollar AI business is implementation, not models" (2026)).

In the title of its article introducing these OpenAI and Anthropic moves, MarkTechPost deliberately used the phrase "the role OpenAI, Anthropic, and Google are hiring for in 2026." The very fact that the major generative AI providers have begun raising the same job title at the same time, without any coordination among themselves, shows that this movement is not a passing trend (see: MarkTechPost, cited above).

These moves are not limited to OpenAI and Anthropic. Citing a survey by executive search firm Christian & Timbers, TechCrunch reports that the percentage of companies planning to hire FDEs surged from 5-10% in early 2026 to 70% by the end of the first half of the year. The article also touches on the scarcity of this talent, noting that there are only about 2,000 immediately deployable "elite-tier" FDEs in the entire United States, and roughly 17,000 in the market overall, most of whom are already employed somewhere. Some major consulting firms report needing to increase their FDE headcount tenfold, and a movement to build teams of 20 to 100 people is said to be spreading. The article also cites predictions that demand for FDEs will expand further by the end of the year (see: TechCrunch, "Forward-deployed engineers are the AI industry's latest talent obsession" (2026)). Taken together with the Indeed job posting data reported by LeadDev in a companion article (a 729% increase year-over-year), multiple independent sources corroborate the same trend: investment in the FDE way of working is expanding rapidly as 2026 begins.

The article identifies three motivations for AI companies investing in FDEs: (1) converting the massive sums they have poured into model development into actual revenue, (2) accelerating penetration into enterprise customers, and (3) differentiating from increasingly cheap competing models through the quality of implementation. On the other hand, Chris Taylor, CEO of Ode with Anthropic, commented to the article that "many FDEs can help deploy tools, but only a small minority can build flagship-worthy features from scratch," pointing out that the talent capable of actually generating tens of millions of dollars in ROI is extremely limited (see: ibid., TechCrunch). Here we see a supply-demand mismatch: while demand for such talent is expanding rapidly, people with implementation skills at a level that produces results remain scarce.

The "last mile" between model capability and real-world work

Why do the AI companies that build the models themselves invest in FDEs on such a scale? The answer lies not in model performance itself, but in the implementation process that sits in front of it.

In a July 2026 report, research firm TBR (Technology Business Research) describes this situation as the "AI last-mile problem." According to the report, while agentic AI platforms are proliferating rapidly, the areas actually generating revenue remain concentrated in a few domains such as coding assistance, and many enterprise customers remain unconvinced of AI's benefits while taking a cautious stance on expanding token consumption. In this environment, the report frames the FDE's role not as mere deployment support, but as encompassing the following three functions.

The first is use case discovery: embedding oneself inside the customer's organization to determine which business processes AI can be applied to for high value. The second is supporting organizational change that goes as far as redesigning workflows and permission/approval processes—not just configuring tools, but including work that modifies how the business itself operates. The third is the process of moving technical proofs of concept (pilots) into production operation; beyond the technical validation of whether the code works, this delves into the question of adoption—whether frontline staff can actually keep using the system in their daily work. The report points out that technical validation alone is insufficient, and that whether change management can also be handled is what determines whether a pilot takes hold in production. It also states that whether the FDE model is viable as a business depends on how much reusable machinery can be created, rather than repeating one-off custom development for each engagement (see: TBR, "Forward-deployed Engineers: The Last Mile of the AI Value Chain" (2026)).

MarkTechPost explains a similar structure in terms of the knowledge gap between customers and AI labs. Customer technical teams deeply understand their own business and operational data but have little experience operating AI models in production, while AI lab engineers know the ins and outs of large-scale operations but are unfamiliar with customers' specific legacy systems and compliance requirements. After presenting survey findings that many enterprise generative AI adoption projects have not translated into measurable business outcomes, the article locates the problem not in model performance but in the deployment and implementation process itself. While this figure is just one survey cited by the article and cannot be definitively applied uniformly to all AI-adopting companies, the fact that AI companies themselves are making massive investment decisions on the premise that "simply handing over a model does not produce results" is worth taking seriously as corroboration of this structure (see: ibid., MarkTechPost).

In other words, no matter how intelligent models become, the process of connecting them to a customer's specific business data, systems, and decision-making processes does not close automatically. The FDE was designed as the role of manually filling this "last mile" by embedding into the customer's operations, and the very fact that AI companies continue to invest massive sums in this role is the strongest evidence that model performance improvements alone cannot overcome the barrier to adoption.

As a concrete example supporting this picture, MarkTechPost highlights Palantir's track record. In its Q1 2026 earnings, total revenue grew 85% year-over-year, with US commercial revenue up 133% and US government revenue up 84%—a series of figures demonstrating that an implementation-first model works as a business. The article also mentions a case where agricultural machinery giant John Deere, after deploying the company's AI solutions in the field, was able to reduce chemical usage by up to 70%. This is one example showing that measurable results emerge only when the implementation process—building to fit frontline business processes—is fully addressed, not just the technology of the model itself (see: MarkTechPost, op. cit.).

How should we interpret this trend?

Note that all the figures introduced so far reflect trends that spread rapidly after the start of 2026, and the market has not yet fully solidified. The "70% of companies planning to adopt FDEs" figure reported by TechCrunch is also based on a survey of only a subset of companies, and does not indicate the proportion of companies that have actually completed adoption and are seeing results. Moreover, companies like OpenAI and Anthropic, which can deploy capital on the scale of billions of dollars, differ fundamentally from ordinary operating companies in the scale of resources they can invest.

Even so, the essential insight to be drawn from this trend remains unshaken: the very companies leading the development of generative AI capabilities have concluded that model improvements alone cannot fully deliver results to customers' operations, and are rapidly increasing their investment in talent responsible for implementation. This is not a temporary hiring strategy of a particular company; since multiple independent surveys and reports point in the same direction, it is reasonable to view it as a structural change across the industry.

What the Scale of the Investment Indicates

Lining up the amounts we have seen so far, their sheer scale stands out. OpenAI's Deployment Company has received over $4 billion, and Ode, the joint venture between Anthropic and Blackstone and others, has received roughly $1.5 billion (reported valuation). Combined, these two companies mean that billions of dollars in capital and organizational capacity are being directed not at model development but at "talent responsible for implementation." This is on par with the annual R&D spending of a typical enterprise software company or the new-business investment scale of a major consulting firm.

What is significant is that the ones making these investment decisions are the companies themselves that develop generative AI models and should understand their capabilities better than anyone. The AI companies themselves have judged that continuing to invest solely in model improvement has limits, both in monetizing revenue from customer companies and in penetrating organizations, and this use of capital was chosen as a result. This is also instructive for companies considering AI adoption. While it is important to spend time selecting and comparing models, the sheer size of these investments tells us that even greater attention must be paid to designing who will carry out implementation and how it will be sustained.

The Lineage of a Working Style That Began with Palantir

The FDE working style we have examined was not invented by OpenAI or Anthropic. As noted in the companion article, the first to systematize this role was Palantir Technologies. The company quickly established, in the field of data analytics, a way of working that involves embedding itself in customers' operations and tackling customer-specific challenges by combining existing product lines, handling the complex business requirements of government agencies and large enterprises on top of general-purpose products.

What is interesting is that this lineage is not merely a story of the past; it is being proven in numbers right now. As mentioned earlier, Palantir's Q1 2026 revenue growth rate shows that this implementation-first model can continue generating revenue over the long term. The reason OpenAI and Anthropic are adopting this model can be read as their running into, once again with generative AI, the same wall Palantir faced: simply handing over a general-purpose product does not take root in customers' operations. The historical background of how Palantir came to create this working style and why it proved effective is a theme worth exploring in greater depth, so I plan to cover it in a separate article. Here, it is enough to note that "FDE" is not merely a buzzword of today's AI industry, but sits atop a lineage of companies that have grown their businesses around implementation.

What This Means for Japanese Companies

What does this trend mean for Japanese companies?

Until now, many companies have viewed AI adoption as an extension of purchasing behavior: "select a tool, sign a contract, and deploy it." The typical approach has been to sign up for a generative AI plan, embed chatbots into a few business processes, and then wait for staff to figure out how to use them. However, the fact that OpenAI and Anthropic are pouring vast sums into their own implementation-focused engineering organizations shakes this very premise. They are in the position of understanding better than anyone in the world that simply handing over the models they built does not translate into results in customers' operations. Yet they are still building organizations that support implementation with human labor, because the essence of AI adoption lies not in "choosing good tools" but in "continuously building things that work, tailored to the data and workflows of the field."

This dynamic emerges as an even more pressing problem for B2B companies in the midst of business growth. As a business grows, the list of "things we want to build" keeps growing daily—pipeline generation, CRM improvements, automation of content production, revamping sales materials—while the engineering resources to turn them into reality are chronically scarce. Meanwhile, traditional outsourcing that includes requirements definition, specification finalization, and delivery scheduling carries the weight of re-obtaining estimates with every decision, and is often too heavy for the pace of the business. Even if you introduce tools, without people to embed them into actual workflows and keep them running as priorities shift, they become wasted assets. In fact, many companies that have introduced AI tools hit a wall when trying to keep using them in practice: "no one is available to touch the settings" or "we lack the capacity to adjust them to our business data."

From the standpoint of a marketing or business development leader, this wall takes an even more concrete form. Hiring engineers does not happen quickly enough, and outsourcing carries the weight of specification finalization and delivery scheduling that does not match the pace of the business, so things stall in a state of "we got the tools in place, but no one is keeping them running in line with daily operations." If what OpenAI and Anthropic face is the "last mile" of connecting cutting-edge technology—models—to customers' operations, then what operating companies face is a "last mile" of the same structure: connecting the tools they have adopted to daily work such as sales meetings, content, and customer interactions.

The implication for Japanese companies is to stop treating AI adoption as an extension of "purchasing tools" and instead shift to the question of how to maintain a "system that keeps building in the field." This shift in thinking does not necessarily require massive investment. What matters is designing the people who will enter the field, rebuild implementations whenever priorities change, and keep delivering working products. Whether to hire these people as in-house engineers or entrust them to external specialists depends on the business, but whether you can take away the very idea of "designing the people responsible" will determine whether AI adoption actually leads to results.

Options for companies that cannot build this model in-house

That said, ordinary Japanese operating companies cannot simply imitate the approach of OpenAI or Anthropic, which launched new companies on the scale of $4 billion and hired FDEs en masse. Only a limited number of companies can build an in-house FDE specialist organization from scratch, and as TechCrunch reported, FDE talent capable of delivering results is scarce even globally. Especially now, with fierce competition for engineering talent, securing skilled implementation talent solely in-house can be a high hurdle depending on the stage of the business.

One realistic option in this case is to receive FDE-style support from outside. Rather than a binary choice between hiring in-house or outsourcing with fixed specifications, this is a third model: dedicated engineers embed themselves in the field and work alongside you, rebuilding working products whenever priorities change. With this model, you can supplement the "system that keeps building in the field" externally without bearing the fixed costs of maintaining an in-house engineering organization.

In particular, for companies at a stage where pipeline generation and business growth speed are demanded, there is often no room to wait for the hiring period required for in-house recruitment or the specification-finalization effort required for traditional outsourcing. In such cases, securing a dedicated team at a fixed monthly fee and having them work in the field only for the period needed can be an option that balances decision-making speed with implementation continuity. Where to start

You do not need to match the scale of OpenAI or Anthropic, but the question to take away from this trend is common regardless of business size: "Within our own operations, where is the 'last mile' that AI's capabilities have not yet reached?"

Typical examples include: having sales meeting data entered in a CRM but no mechanism in place to determine which leads to call next; being able to draft content with AI but still finishing it manually to match the company's tone and brand context; and having a mountain of dormant leads but no prioritization to reactivate them. Identifying one specific task where this "final step" is missing, and thinking about how to place people who keep building in the field there, is the first step toward implementation beyond tool adoption.

Rather than trying to change all operations at once, the approach of starting with the single highest-value area, delivering working products, and then deciding the next priority is itself the very method of the FDE way of working we have examined. Instead of perfecting estimates before starting, first build something small that works, then decide what to build next based on field reactions. This reversal of order is the essence of the implementation model that OpenAI and Anthropic are investing in, and a way of thinking that can be adopted regardless of scale.

Summary

OpenAI launched a $4 billion-scale Deployment Company in May 2026, and Anthropic launched Ode, a joint venture with Blackstone and others on the scale of $1.5 billion (valuation per reports), around the same time. The percentage of companies planning to hire FDEs has also surged from 5–10% to 70% in just the first half of 2026. Behind this trend lies a structure: no matter how much model performance improves, the "last mile" of connecting it to a customer's specific operations, data, and decision-making processes can only be filled by human hands. This way of working, systematized by Palantir, is now being adopted by AI companies themselves as a pillar of their own businesses.

What is important to note here is that this is not a temporary hiring boom, but a business decision made by the very companies that build the models. The fact that companies with the deepest understanding of generative AI performance are, alongside investing in performance improvements, increasing investment in implementation talent on the same scale suggests that the success of AI adoption will increasingly depend on "how implementation is carried out." For Japanese companies, the meaning lies in reframing AI adoption not as "purchasing tools" but as "building a system that keeps building in the field." Whether to hold this system in-house or receive external support depends on the stage of the business.
