The Ground-Force Implementer of the AI Era: A Comprehensive Analysis of the FDE (Forward Deployed Engineer)

Companies worldwide have invested over $30 billion in generative AI, yet 95% of projects fail to generate positive profit—technology is no longer the bottleneck; the last mile between technology and real business has completely broken down. The FDE (Forward Deployed Engineer), the only hybrid role in the AI era that connects standardized technology with non-standardized scenarios, is becoming the core focus of the tech hiring market with million-yuan salaries. From a product manager's perspective, this article breaks down the underlying logic and complete closed loop of the FDE.

Many AI newcomers share a common confusion: with large models and AI algorithms emerging endlessly, open-source models readily available, and enterprise AI teams constantly expanding with growing budgets, why do the vast majority of enterprise AI projects end up as "demo products" that never truly land and generate business value?

The NANDA team at MIT gave a painful answer in its 2025 report "The GenAI Divide: State of AI in Business 2025": global enterprises have cumulatively invested over $30–40 billion in generative AI, but 95% of AI projects produce no measurable positive impact on corporate profit statements.

The industry's core bottleneck is no longer "AI technology isn't strong enough," but rather the complete breakdown of the last mile between technology and real business—models that run perfectly in the lab immediately fail and struggle to adapt when deployed in enterprises' complex, non-standardized, and legacy real-world business scenarios.

In traditional internet and software industries, the product delivery loop is relatively smooth: standardized products + remote iteration + online updates complete value delivery. But the AI industry is entirely different. The core of AI value is "scenario-based enablement"—there is no universal, all-purpose AI product, only AI solutions tailored to specific businesses, specific processes, and specific data.

Under this industry transformation, the division-of-labor barriers among traditional R&D, product, and delivery roles have completely collapsed, giving rise to a brand-new core role—the FDE (Forward Deployed Engineer).

Especially in recent years' tech hiring market, top global AI companies have been aggressively expanding FDE hiring, with salaries far exceeding those of ordinary engineers. ByteDance offers monthly salaries of 35,000–70,000 yuan with 15 months' pay per year, reaching up to 1.05 million yuan annually; Alibaba Cloud Intelligence offers 20,000–50,000 yuan monthly with 16 months' pay per year; in Silicon Valley, the median total annual compensation for senior FDEs at frontier labs reaches $485,000.

Meanwhile, OpenAI has partnered with 19 PE firms to invest over $4 billion in establishing a deployment company; Anthropic has partnered with Blackstone, Goldman Sachs, and others to establish an enterprise services company valued at $1.5 billion; Amazon Web Services announced a $1 billion investment to build an FDE team of thousands. FDE-related positions surged from 643 in April 2025 to 5,330 in April 2026—a year-over-year increase of nearly 729%.

The FDE has become one of the scarcest and most critical roles in the AI industry. Yet the vast majority of AI newcomers, working professionals, and even business managers cannot clearly define the FDE, cannot distinguish it from ordinary development, implementation, or pre-sales roles, and fail to understand the underlying logic behind companies hiring FDEs at high salaries.

Is this a brand-new golden profession born of the AI era, or just "old wine in new bottles" concept hype?

As a product manager, I want to use a complete product-thinking framework to help AI newcomers truly understand the FDE—what it is, what it does, why it's hot, and whether it's worth it.

Chapter 1: What Is an FDE?—Redefining It from a Product Manager's Perspective

1.1 Explaining the FDE in One Sentence

If I had to explain the FDE to an AI newcomer in one sentence, I would say:

An FDE is "an engineer who goes to the front lines with a toolbox"—stationed at the customer's site, understanding the business while writing code, breaking down a vague AI idea into a production system that is runnable, evaluable, and sustainably maintainable.

Breaking it down, this definition contains four key phrases:

  • "Going to the front lines"—not writing generic code at headquarters, but going to where the customer is
  • "Carrying a toolbox"—possessing complete engineering and technical capabilities
  • "Understanding the business while writing code"—spanning business understanding and technical implementation
  • "Breaking it down into a production system"—not just building demos, but delivering something that actually works

Taken literally, "Forward Deployed" is a military term whose core meaning is leaving the rear headquarters, going deep into the front-line battlefield, and making decisions, executing, and iterating on the spot. This is the most fundamental difference between FDEs and all traditional technical roles: traditional engineers work "in the rear office, delivering remotely," while FDEs are "stationed at the front line, tackling problems on-site, closing the loop on delivery."

Combining the product manager's value closed-loop theory, I offer a precise definition of the FDE that newcomers can fully understand:

The FDE is a hybrid value-delivery role exclusive to the AI era, the sole core bridge connecting an enterprise's standardized AI technical capabilities with customers' non-standardized real business scenarios. It breaks the fragmented division of "product defines requirements, R&D builds features, delivery handles deployment," with one person handling the entire chain of "requirement discovery, on-site implementation, problem-solving, product feedback, and value validation." The core goal is not "delivering a product" but "landing value."

Simply put: traditional engineers are responsible for "building the product," while FDEs are responsible for "getting the product used, making it effective, and iterating it to work better."

1.2 The Origin of the FDE: From Palantir's "Necessity" to the AI Era's "Inevitability"

The FDE was not invented out of thin air in the AI era. To understand it, you must return to its source—Palantir.

Palantir was founded in 2003, initially serving US intelligence and defense agencies. These clients faced a fundamental problem: their work was highly classified, making it impossible to describe their requirements in detail to external engineers. After Palantir's founders showed demos to potential clients, the feedback was: "This product is terrible—it has nothing to do with what we do."

The traditional software development model completely failed here—it was impossible to first write requirement documents, then develop, then deliver. Palantir's solution was to have engineers directly embed at the client's site. Engineers were not just technical support staff, but people who truly embedded themselves in the client organization, understood business pain points, and directly wrote programs to solve problems.

This model gradually became Palantir's core competitive advantage and gave rise to the title "Forward Deployed Engineer." Within Palantir, FDEs are called "Delta," complementing the "Dev" (Software Engineer) role responsible for product development. By around 2016, Palantir's FDE headcount even exceeded that of traditional software engineers.

Palantir officially describes this model as "the human equivalent of backpropagation"—engineers stay as close to the problem itself as possible, collaborating with the core engineering team to continuously synthesize and transform on-site feedback into new features.

After the AI wave emerged, companies like OpenAI and Anthropic began widely adopting this model. Why? Because the deployment of generative AI faces the same dilemma—demos look beautiful, but once they enter real enterprise environments, they hit all kinds of practical problems. The FDE, this "old approach," has found a brand-new stage in the AI era.

1.3 The Core Underlying Logic of the FDE (Product Duality Theory)

The vast majority of FDE explainers on the market merely list job responsibilities, ignoring its most essential duality—which is precisely why 90% of AI newcomers can't understand the FDE.

From the perspective of product manager role evolution and value accumulation, the FDE is inherently endowed with a bidirectional closed-loop capability of delivery implementation + product iteration—two forces that check and empower each other, both indispensable:

Left hand · Delivery side (immediate value): Facing customers' real business scenarios directly, solving non-standardized implementation problems, adapting to customers' legacy systems, complex processes, and special rules, rapidly completing AI feature deployment, debugging, and trial runs, taking full responsibility for landing business value for individual customers, and solving the problems of "usable and easy to use."

Right hand · Product side (long-term value): During on-site implementation, continuously uncover common pain points, shared needs, and implementation pitfalls, and abstract and distill non-standard solutions from individual customers into reusable product capabilities, general templates, and standardized processes, feeding them back to the product and R&D teams in the rear. This role is responsible for the overall iteration and upgrade of the product, solving the problem of "replicability and scalability."

This dual logic is the core moat that distinguishes FDEs from pre-sales, implementation, outsourcing, and ordinary R&D roles, and it is the underlying reason for their high salaries and scarcity. Ordinary technical positions only handle work in a single dimension, while FDEs take on the dual closed loop of "short-term delivery value + long-term product value," making them the core hub for enterprises' AI commercialization at scale.

1.4 Understanding the FDE's Positioning in One Diagram

The core logic of this diagram: the FDE is the bridge connecting "customers' vague needs" and "a runnable production system," while also feeding frontline insights back to the product team, forming a positive cycle.

Palantir has officially given a precise and classic positioning: the FDE's working model is highly consistent with that of a startup CTO. In a small frontline team, the FDE takes end-to-end full responsibility for high-risk, high-difficulty AI implementation projects—no cumbersome division of labor or handoffs, no layers of approval, and the ability to make decisions, implement, and iterate quickly on-site.

Unlike traditional roles where "everyone does their own job and delivers in stages," the FDE is the owner of the entire chain: if requirements aren't understood, they break them down themselves; if the system doesn't run, they debug it themselves; if processes don't fit, they adapt them themselves; if the product has flaws, they give feedback themselves; if results fall short, they optimize themselves. This "one-stop closed-loop capability" is precisely the core capability most needed in complex AI implementation scenarios.

Chapter 2: The FDE's Scope of Responsibilities — A Complete Closed Loop from Entry to Exit

2.1 The Full Picture of Responsibilities

The complete work cycle of an FDE can be broken down into five phases:

2.2 Detailed Breakdown of Each Phase

Phase One: Entry and Discovery

The first thing an FDE does after arriving at the customer's site is not to write code, but to understand. They need to map out the customer's existing systems—which data sources, which legacy systems, what permission structures, what compliance requirements. More importantly, they must understand how business people actually work—the operations that happen in practice, outside of what's written in process documents.

The biggest pitfall in AI implementation is "implementing pseudo-needs." Customers often cannot clearly articulate their pain points and only put forward vague feature requests; traditional pre-sales and product teams are easily misled by surface-level needs, ultimately resulting in "features that no one uses, while users' real pain points go unsolved."

The FDE's primary responsibility is to go deep into the business frontline and complete separating real needs from false ones and deep decomposition:

1. Immersive on-site research: fully embed in the customer's business scenario, follow business staff through the entire workflow, and understand the real business logic, process bottlenecks, and efficiency pain points, rather than judging needs based on the customer's verbal descriptions or written documents;

2. Translating vague needs: translate vague requests like "we want intelligent efficiency gains" or "we want intelligent analytics" into AI technical requirements that are implementable, quantifiable, and verifiable;

3. Prioritizing needs: based on business value, implementation difficulty, and cost, filter out high-value essential needs and eliminate invalid pseudo-needs to avoid wasting resources;

4. Stakeholder alignment: engage with multiple roles across the customer's business, IT, and management to unify implementation expectations and clarify the core metrics and acceptance criteria for AI implementation.

A typical scenario: the customer says "I want an AI customer service system." After arriving on-site, the FDE discovers that the real pain point is that customer data across multiple business systems is completely disconnected, and low customer service efficiency is only a surface symptom. If you build an AI customer service system directly, the core problem remains unsolved. The FDE must first penetrate the surface and find the problem truly worth solving.

Phase Two: Solution Design

After finding the real problem, the FDE must translate vague business requests into concrete technical solutions. This requires breaking down empty ideas like "I want to use AI to cut costs and boost efficiency" into implementable technical paths.

Standardized AI products can only fit generic scenarios, while 90% of enterprises' real business scenarios are non-standard: incompatible legacy systems, messy data formats, unusual business processes, strict security permissions, and numerous customization rules. This is also the core reason ordinary AI products fail in implementation.

The FDE's core engineering responsibility is to solve the last-mile implementation challenge of non-standard scenarios:

1. On-site system adaptation: connect with the customer's on-premise systems, legacy ERP, CRM, and industrial control systems, solving problems like disconnected interfaces, data isolation, and format incompatibility;

2. Rapid prototyping and iteration: based on the company's core AI models and standardized platforms, quickly develop customized adaptation modules and build implementation prototypes suited to the customer's scenario, without waiting for remote scheduling from the rear R&D team;

3. Production environment deployment and debugging: independently complete on-site deployment, stress testing, bug debugging, and security adaptation of the AI system, ensuring stable integration into the customer's production environment and true business usability;

4. Immediate problem-solving: resolve unexpected implementation issues on-site, such as data anomalies, model accuracy deviations, process adaptation conflicts, and permission errors, preventing project stalls and failures.

Phase Three: Rapid Building

The FDE's core deliverable is not a PPT or solution document, but runnable production-grade code. They typically need to go from a vague customer problem to a working prototype (Proof of Concept) within days, and to production-grade deployment within weeks.

Unlike sales, whose core goal is "closing deals," the FDE's core goal is "deepening value." Through immersive on-site implementation, the FDE understands the customer's business pain points and potential needs best, making them the core role in mining existing customer value and increasing customer stickiness:

1. Answer customers' technical questions, resolve long-term usage issues, and build deep trust;

2. Uncover customers' potential business scenarios, promote cross-scenario reuse of AI capabilities, and achieve project expansion, renewals, and additional purchases;

3. Collect customers' long-term strategic needs, providing frontline input for the company's long-term product planning and industry positioning.

The FDE is the core hub between the enterprise's frontline and rear, needing to connect sales, product, R&D, algorithms, and after-sales teams: facing forward to engage with the customer's business, facing backward to sync implementation progress, issues, and needs, and coordinating rear resources to solve core technical challenges the frontline cannot overcome, achieving an efficient closed loop of "frontline implementation + rear iteration."

Phase Four: Deployment and Launch

This is the most easily underestimated phase. An AI model may run perfectly in a demo environment, but once it enters a real enterprise environment it hits: legacy systems without standard interfaces, uneven data quality, incompatible permission systems, and failed compliance audits. The FDE must solve these problems one by one to get the system truly running.

A core product manager principle: implementation without value validation is ineffective delivery. Many AI projects end once deployment is complete, but never verify whether they truly improve business efficiency or reduce costs—this is the key reason AI projects "seemingly land but are actually ineffective."

One of the FDE's core responsibilities that distinguishes them from ordinary engineers is taking full responsibility for business outcomes:

1. Business user training: guide customer employees in using the AI system, establish standardized usage processes, and resolve user operation bottlenecks;

2. Implementation effect monitoring: track the AI system's operational data and business usage data in real time, measuring core metrics such as efficiency gains, cost reductions, and error rate declines;

3. Value closed-loop validation: verify the real business value of AI implementation against the acceptance criteria agreed upon earlier, producing a quantifiable implementation report;

4. Experience optimization and adjustment: based on user feedback and business operational data, fine-tune system features and process logic on-site to improve product fit.

Phase Five: Consolidation and Feedback

The FDE's responsibilities don't end with delivery. They must also train the customer's team so they can operate and maintain the system independently after the FDE leaves; at the same time, they feed lessons learned on-site back to the product team to drive product iteration.

This is the FDE's most core, most scarce, and most easily overlooked responsibility, and it is the key criterion for distinguishing a "real FDE" from a "fake rebranded FDE." Ordinary delivery roles serve only a single customer and end once the work is done, whereas the FDE bears the responsibility of being the core data source for the enterprise's product iteration at scale.

The FDE needs to convert non-standard deployment experience from individual customers into reusable, general-purpose product capabilities for the enterprise:

1. Distilling common pain points: aggregating deployment pitfalls, high-frequency requests, and adaptation difficulties across multiple customers, and filtering out industry-wide common problems;

2. Abstracting non-standard solutions: abstracting a single customer's customized adaptation schemes, interface modifications, and process optimization logic into general templates, configurable components, and standardized adaptation tools;

3. Feeding product requirements back: delivering precise iteration requirements to the product and R&D teams behind the scenes, driving the core product to improve model accuracy, add adaptation features, and refine industry solutions;

4. Accumulating industry methodology: summarizing AI deployment processes, adaptation standards, and pitfall-avoidance guides across different industries, and building an enterprise industry deployment knowledge base to reduce deployment costs for future customers.

2.3 How FDEs Spend Their Time

Based on industry practice, an FDE's working time can roughly be allocated as follows:

  • 25%: pure coding
  • 50%: integration and debugging (connecting to customer systems, handling data, resolving compatibility issues)
  • 25%: communication and project management (aligning requirements with customers, coordinating resources, driving decisions)

Chapter 3: How the FDE Differs from Other Engineers — One Table to Understand It All

To attract candidates, many companies package traditional implementation or pre-sales roles as FDEs, leading to a flood of "fake FDEs" in the market. This section makes a precise distinction across five dimensions — product division of labor, value orientation, work scenarios, competency model, and salary logic — and also provides criteria for identifying real vs. fake FDEs, completely eliminating cognitive confusion.

3.2 In-Depth Interpretation of Key Role Differences

3.2.1 FDE vs Algorithm Engineer: The Division Between a Model That "Works" and One That "Works Well"

The two roles AI beginners most easily confuse: the algorithm engineer is responsible for building good models, while the FDE is responsible for using models well.

The algorithm engineer's core work is optimizing model parameters, improving general accuracy, and training datasets in the lab, pursuing "maximizing the model's theoretical performance," completely detached from specific business scenarios. As a result, many models score perfectly in the lab but, when deployed in real scenarios, become completely unusable due to messy data, mismatched processes, and scenario-specific quirks.

The FDE, by contrast, does not train models or tune core algorithms; the core work is adapting the general-purpose models built by algorithm engineers to specific business scenarios, solving the adaptation, process, data, and experience problems of model deployment.

In short: the algorithm engineer solves the "technical ceiling," while the FDE solves the "deployment floor." Without an FDE, even the best AI model is a castle in the air.

3.2.2 FDE vs Ordinary Development Engineer: Building Products in the Back Office vs Creating Value on the Front Line

Palantir has an incisive internal summary:

  • Dev (traditional software engineer) focuses on "one capability, many customers"
  • Delta (FDE) focuses on "one customer, many capabilities"

Ordinary backend/frontend developers are typical "back-office executors": all their work is based on requirement documents produced by product managers; they are only responsible for feature development and need not care whether requirements are genuine, whether they fit the business, or how deployment turns out. Once development is complete and the code is tested and launched, the work is done — they never touch customers or understand the business.

The FDE is a "front-line decision-maker + executor": there is no fixed requirement document; the FDE must discover requirements, judge them, design custom solutions, implement them, and iterate. The ordinary developer "builds one standardized feature for a massive number of customers," while the FDE "builds end-to-end business capabilities for one customer" — and then reuses those capabilities for a massive number of customers.

3.2.3 FDE vs Implementation Engineer: Closed-Loop Delivery vs Executing Deliveries

This is the role most easily confused, and the one with the most "fake FDEs" on the market. Many companies simply rename implementation engineers as FDEs — essentially job repackaging.

The implementation engineer's core is standardized execution: after the product is finalized, they follow a fixed process to complete deployment, installation, and training. They can only solve standardized problems — no custom development, no product iteration, no handling of complex non-standard scenarios. When they encounter system incompatibility, process conflicts, or requirement changes, they can only report back to the team behind the scenes and cannot resolve issues on-site.

The FDE's core, by contrast, is non-standard problem-solving + value closure: they have the ability to develop independently, modify on-site, and iterate immediately, can solve all non-standard deployment challenges, and their core goal is not "completing the delivery" but "generating business value," while also bearing the core responsibility of feeding product iteration.

3.2.4 FDE vs Pre-Sales Architect: Accountable for Deployment Outcomes vs Accountable for Closing Deals

The pre-sales architect's entire work has one core goal: helping sales close deals. Their work is concentrated in the early project stage; once the deal is signed, they immediately exit the project, taking no part in deployment and bearing no responsibility for outcomes. Their compensation includes sales commissions and performance bonuses, strongly tied to deal size.

The FDE's work is concentrated after the deal is signed; they are accountable for deployment outcomes, business value, and long-term iteration. They do not participate in deal-making and receive no sales commission; their compensation structure is "base salary + performance bonus + stock," strongly tied to deployment value and product iteration results.

3.3 A Broader Perspective: The FDE as a "Tri-Hybrid Talent"

Palantir has officially given a precise and classic positioning: the FDE's working model is highly consistent with that of a startup CTO. In a small frontline team, the FDE takes end-to-end full responsibility for high-risk, high-difficulty AI implementation projects—no cumbersome division of labor or handoffs, no layers of approval, and the ability to make decisions, implement, and iterate quickly on-site.

Unlike traditional roles where "everyone does their own job and delivers in stages," the FDE is the owner of the entire chain: if requirements aren't understood, they break them down themselves; if the system doesn't run, they debug it themselves; if processes don't fit, they adapt them themselves; if the product has flaws, they give feedback themselves; if results fall short, they optimize themselves. This "one-stop closed-loop capability" is precisely the core capability most needed in complex AI implementation scenarios.

Overall, the FDE is a highly hybrid role:

  • 20% sales: understanding customer needs, building trust, driving collaboration
  • 30% product: requirements analysis, solution design, product thinking
  • 50% engineering: writing code, doing integration, deploying to production

It sits at the intersection of product management, software engineering, and strategic consulting.

Based on the JDs and deployment models of leading industry companies, here are 5 stress-test criteria that beginners can use directly to tell real FDE roles from fake ones:

1. Salary structure (the hardest criterion): a real FDE has no sales commission; compensation is base salary + performance bonus + equity. Any role with performance commissions or deal bonuses is a rebranded pre-sales/implementation position.

2. Work content: a real FDE spends over 70% of their time writing production code and doing on-site development and debugging; those who only write proposals, make PPTs, and demo prototypes are architects/pre-sales.

3. Product feedback channel: a real FDE has a well-established mechanism for distilling field experience into general product capabilities; roles with no product feedback loop that leave once the work is done are outsourcing/implementation.

4. Timing of engagement: a real FDE comes in after the deal is signed and is accountable for deployment outcomes; those who come in before signing to help close deals are pre-sales.

5. Requirement decision-making authority: a real FDE can independently judge whether requirements are genuine, optimize solutions, and reject invalid requirements; those who can only passively execute customer requests are basic delivery roles.

Chapter 4: Why Enterprises Need FDEs — Pain Points, Necessity, and Value

4.1 Product Strategy Perspective: Solving the Core Pain Points of the AI Industry

From the product manager's pain point–solution–value matching theory, the birth of the FDE is not a case of companies following trends, but an inevitable strategic choice as the AI industry has reached its current stage. The traditional division-of-labor model can no longer solve the three core pain points of AI commercialization, and the FDE is the only solution that fits.

4.1.1 Pain Point One: The Inherent Contradiction Between Standardized AI Technology and Non-Standardized Business Scenarios

Traditional internet products (social media, e-commerce, office software) have highly standardized user needs and usage scenarios. A single product can serve tens of millions of users, and value delivery can be completed through "back-end R&D iteration + online updates."

But AI products are completely different, especially enterprise AI and industry AI: there are no standardized scenarios, only non-standardized needs. The business processes, data standards, system architectures, and core pain points of different industries, companies, and departments are all completely different. General-purpose AI models must go through scenario adaptation, custom modification, and process integration before they can generate value.

Under the traditional division of labor, back-end R&D doesn't understand frontline business, and frontline delivery doesn't understand technical iteration, resulting in "features built by R&D go unused, while features customers need are never built"—a dead loop for implementation. As the frontline technical hub, the FDE perfectly resolves this contradiction, achieving precise adaptation between standardized technology and non-standardized scenarios.

4.1.2 Pain Point Two: Overly Long Traditional Division-of-Labor Chains Lead to Extremely Low AI Implementation Efficiency and Extremely High Costs

The traditional AI project implementation chain: customer raises requirements → sales records them → product breaks them down → R&D schedules them → development and testing → delivery and deployment → after-sales operations. The entire chain involves 5–6 roles, with layer-by-layer handoffs and losses; requirement deviations, communication delays, and slow iteration are the norm.

The core requirement of AI implementation is fast trial-and-error, fast iteration, fast validation. Vague requirements and dynamic scenarios are the norm, and an overly long division-of-labor chain directly leads to project delays, failures, and wasted investment.

The FDE's full-chain closed-loop model completely cuts out redundant intermediate steps—one person handles requirements, development, implementation, iteration, and feedback—greatly improving implementation efficiency and reducing communication costs, labor costs, and trial-and-error costs.

4.1.3 Pain Point Three: Implementation Experience Cannot Be Accumulated, So Companies Cannot Build Scalable Moats

The fatal problem of most AI companies: every customer implementation "starts from zero." Once one customer project is done, all the experience is lost; the next customer means stepping on the same pitfalls again, redeveloping, and readapting. Scalable replication is never achieved, and companies can only pile up labor to do projects, unable to build a product moat.

The core strategic value of the FDE is to transform one-off project delivery into continuous product assets. By accumulating general capabilities, industry templates, and adaptation solutions through frontline implementation, the company's products grow stronger, implementation costs keep falling, replication speeds keep increasing, and a core industry moat is built.

4.1.4 Pain Point Four: AI Projects Emphasize Delivery Over Value, Leading to Extremely Low Customer Retention

The traditional delivery model only cares about "whether deployment is complete," not "whether business value is generated." Many AI projects, after delivery, are shelved or abandoned by customers due to poor fit, complexity of use, or failure to solve core pain points—ultimately leading to non-renewals and no additional purchases, so company revenue cannot grow sustainably.

The FDE takes "business value implementation" as its core goal, following up throughout on usage outcomes, optimizing the experience, and digging deeper into scenarios, ensuring AI capabilities truly empower the business—greatly improving customer satisfaction, retention, and repurchase rates, and achieving a sustainable closed loop of commercial value.

4.2 When Must a Company Deploy an FDE

The FDE is not a universal role; not all software and internet projects need one. Based on the product scenario adaptation theory, I have summarized 6 core scenarios where an FDE must be deployed—also the core tracks of current FDE expansion at AI companies—precisely distinguishing business scenarios that "need an FDE" from those that "don't."

4.2.1 Scenario One: Enterprise AI and Industry AI Non-Standardized Implementation Projects (Core Rigid-Demand Scenario)

AI transformation projects in traditional industries such as industrial manufacturing, finance, government, healthcare, energy, and transportation feature high industry barriers, complex business processes, legacy systems, non-standardized data, and stringent security and compliance requirements. Standardized AI products simply cannot fit; an FDE must be stationed on-site to tackle the work, completing scenario adaptation, custom iteration, and value delivery. This is currently the most core and mainstream application scenario for FDEs.

4.2.2 Scenario Two: Frontier AI Model Commercialization Projects

Frontier AI technologies such as large models, multimodal AI, AI agents, and intelligent decision-making currently have no mature implementation standards, no fixed usage scenarios, and highly uncertain requirements. Such projects cannot rely on back-end standardized R&D for implementation; they need FDEs to go deep into the field, explore implementation scenarios, validate business models, and accumulate implementation standards, paving the way for product scale-up. OpenAI and Anthropic's large-scale FDE hiring is precisely to serve the commercialization of these frontier models.

When a company enters a brand-new AI track or incubates a brand-new AI product, there are no mature requirements, no implementation cases, and no standardized solutions. An FDE is needed to go deep into the field, completing 0-to-1 scenario exploration, requirement validation, model refinement, and case accumulation, helping the company validate its business model and product implementation path.

4.2.5 Scenario Five: Strategic Projects Requiring Deep Customer Co-Creation

AI customization projects for a company's key accounts and strategic partners require deep binding with the customer's business, co-creation of solutions, and long-term iterative optimization. 5.1 Case One: AI-Driven Intelligent Risk Control Implementation in Banking

5.1.1 Project Background: The Extreme Pain Points of AI Implementation in Finance

Banking is the core scenario with the highest requirements for AI commercialization, the strongest barriers, and the greatest ability to demonstrate the FDE's core value. Compared with industrial scenarios, AI implementation in banking has five core characteristics: strict regulatory compliance, highly sensitive data, extremely complex business logic, legacy system architecture, and near-zero fault tolerance. It is also the disaster zone where 90% of general-purpose AI products fail to implement.

This case is based on the retail credit intelligent risk control AI upgrade project of a domestic joint-stock commercial bank, fully reproducing the industry's real implementation situation. The bank's original traditional credit risk control system relied on manual review plus a static rules engine, with serious business pain points: high volume of small loan applications, extremely low manual review efficiency, poor user experience due to no manual staffing at night, and traditional rules unable to identify new types of fraud, resulting in annual bad debt losses exceeding ten million yuan.

The bank procured a general-purpose intelligent risk control large model system from a leading domestic AI company. The model achieved an accuracy rate of 98.7% on test datasets and standardized financial samples, and in theory could fully replace traditional manual risk control review, intercept fraudulent orders, and reduce the bad debt rate.

However, after the project team entered the site, the traditional R&D and delivery teams were completely unable to make progress, and the project stalled, on the verge of being scrapped. The core implementation pain points were all non-standard scenario adaptation issues, which are also the common challenges of all financial AI projects:

1.Non-standard data and compliance barriers: The bank's core credit data is entirely isolated in an intranet classified environment and cannot be transmitted externally or connected to cloud models; the cloud invocation architecture of general-purpose AI models is completely unable to adapt to the bank's localized, private, and intranet/extranet-isolated deployment requirements. Meanwhile, financial data is regulated by the banking regulator, with strict compliance standards for data desensitization, field access, and log retention, which general-purpose products cannot accommodate.

2.Legacy system compatibility barriers: The bank's core credit system, user credit reporting system, and transaction flow system are all legacy architectures over ten years old, with no standardized API interfaces, chaotic data formats, and inconsistent fields, making it impossible for the general-purpose AI risk control system to complete data integration and business linkage.

3.Highly non-standard business rules: The bank has exclusive differentiated risk control rules for small loans, young customer groups, and lower-tier customer groups, which differ from industry-wide standards. The risk control decision logic of general-purpose AI models is too standardized to adapt to the bank's differentiated credit granting and interception rules; direct implementation would cause a large number of misjudgments, wrongful rejections, and wrongful approvals, triggering financial risks.

4.Overly long iteration cycles, unable to adapt to real-time financial risk control: In the traditional back-end R&D iteration model, a single rule optimization or model fine-tuning takes 1-2 months, while financial fraud tactics iterate in real time and new scam schemes emerge constantly—slow iteration is completely unable to meet the needs of real-time risk control interception.

5.Business value cannot be realized: Simply deploying a general-purpose model can only achieve simple data display; it cannot truly integrate into the credit approval process, cannot automatically complete risk control decisions, and cannot reduce the bad debt rate. It can only serve as a demonstration tool with no actual business value.

Chapter 6 Summary: The Essence and Future of FDE

6.1 Understanding FDE Through an Analogy

If we compare an AI company to a weapons manufacturer:

  • AI researchers: research more advanced gunpowder formulas
  • Software engineers: build standardized weapons in the factory
  • Sales: show customers how powerful the weapons are
  • FDE: goes to the front line with the troops, adjusting weapons based on actual battlefield conditions, teaching soldiers how to use them, and repairing malfunctions on the spot

No matter how powerful AI capabilities are, if no one brings them to the battlefield and makes them truly work, they are just a pile of technology sitting in the lab. FDE is the person who brings technology to the battlefield.

6.2 The Core Essence of FDE

Looking back at this article, the essence of FDE can be summed up in three points:

First, FDE is a "results-oriented" engineer. He is not accountable for his lines of code, nor for the number of features delivered — only for the customer's business outcomes.

Second, FDE is an engineer with "closed-loop thinking." He doesn't just build something and stop; he feeds frontline lessons learned back into the product to make it better.

Third, FDE is an "amphibious" engineer. He must have solid engineering skills (able to write production-grade code), business understanding (able to communicate with non-technical executives), and the ability to independently drive work forward in ambiguous environments.

Companies are willing to pay a high premium for FDEs precisely because this "amphibious" capability is extremely scarce.

6.3 The Future of FDE

FDE is evolving from a standalone role into a standard capability of ToB teams. A major tech company in Hangzhou has already implemented a policy of "new hires spend their first three months as FDEs," and even directly dispatches product managers on-site for requirements research and demo development.

Against the backdrop of rapid AI technology iteration, customer demand for on-site engineering support that "understands both technology and business" is growing sharply. The FDE role will not disappear — it will only become more important.

But a word of caution: FDE is not a model that can be casually copied. It requires high-value customers, a mature product platform, and talent that truly possesses "amphibious" capabilities. Blindly following the trend of hiring FDEs will only become an expensive mistake.

6.4 Advice for AI Beginners

If you are an AI beginner trying to understand FDE, remembering these three sentences is enough:

  • FDE is "an engineer who goes to the front line with a toolbox" — not writing generic code at headquarters, but solving problems at the customer's site
  • FDE is accountable for results, not lines of code — the goal is to get the customer truly using it and generating value
  • FDE is "the personification of the product discovery loop" — discovering problems on the front line, solving them, and bringing the experience back into the product

If you are considering whether to pursue FDE as a career, ask yourself three questions:

  • Can I accept working long-term at customer sites (rather than sitting in a comfortable office at headquarters)?
  • Can I push work forward when information is incomplete and goals are vague?
  • Do I have engineering skills and am I willing to deeply understand the business?

If all three answers are "yes," FDE may be one of the best-fit careers for you.

6.5 Closing Remarks

The essence of FDE is: in the most complex environments, with the shortest feedback loops, turning the vaguest problems into working systems, and then turning frontline experience into reusable product capabilities.

The AI industry has completely left behind the era of wild growth where the game was "competing on technology and model parameters," and has entered an era of refined competition centered on deployment, scenarios, value, and scale.

In the past, a company's core competitiveness was "whether it could build advanced AI technology"; in the future, it will be "whether it can deploy advanced AI technology, use it well, create value, and replicate it at scale." And FDE is the key role that holds this core competitiveness.

For AI beginners, understanding the essence of FDE means understanding the underlying business logic of the AI industry: technology is the foundation, deployment is the core, and value is the ultimate goal.

The rise of FDE is not an iteration of job roles, but a landmark transformation of the entire AI industry from "technical hype" to "industrial practicality." Only by grasping the core understanding of FDE can you truly see the future trends of AI commercialization, avoid industry misconceptions, and precisely seize career and business opportunities in the AI era.

In the AI era, the FDE role will only become more and more important. Keep going, everyone!
