FDE bench time: what to ask before you sign an offer

FDE Times · 2026-11-04 · 7 min read

Summary

Using TCS's new policy as a case study, this article examines the risk FDEs face when a client engagement ends. Since June 2025, TCS caps bench time at 35 working days per year alongside a 225-day billable target; staff who exceed the cap reportedly face discipline, a PIP, or termination, and the IT union NITES has complained to India's Labour Ministry. The author advises candidates to ask before signing how the company handles gaps between projects, what local labor law permits (using Vietnam's termination and severance rules as an example), and to build multi-person support at the client so a project's value does not depend on one champion.

Key points

  • TCS caps bench time at 35 working days a year, with a 225-day billable target.
  • Exceeding the cap can lead to discipline, a PIP, or termination.
  • Union NITES alleges benched staff were pressured to resign.
  • In Vietnam, billable-day criteria could be used as grounds for dismissal.
  • Ask interviewers what happened to the last FDE after an engagement ended.

Before signing, ask what the company does with you in the gap, what local law allows and how much standing you have built with the client.

In brief

  • TCS limits bench time to 35 working days a year, alongside a target of 225 billable days. According to press reports, staff who go over can face disciplinary action, a PIP or termination.
  • Vietnamese law does not treat the end of a project as grounds for restructuring. If performance criteria are tied to billable days, though, that gap can still become grounds for dismissal.
  • Ask about the gap between projects before you sign, and make sure more than one person at the client knows the value of your work.

Since June 12th 2025, TCS has put a hard limit on the gap between projects: no employee may spend more than 35 working days a year on the “bench”. Indian press reports say that anyone who exceeds the limit without landing a project can face disciplinary action, be placed on a performance improvement plan (PIP) or even have their contract terminated.

People holding FDE offers share the same worry. An anonymous post on Blind says FDEs placed at clients were let go as soon as the engagement ended, while another poster compared the model with consulting, where bench time is already built in. Both are opinions, with no data behind them.

The project will end. The questions that matter are what the company does with you in the gap that follows, what the law where you sign allows it to do, and how much standing you have built for yourself at the client.

At TCS, the bench runs on a countdown

TCS’s policy goes beyond the 35-day cap. It comes with a target of at least 225 days a year of work billed to clients for every employee.

Add the two numbers and you get 260. TCS does not publish that figure, but a year of 52 five-day weeks also comes to exactly 260. By that arithmetic, the working year is almost entirely allocated, even before public holidays and annual leave are taken out.

Take an engineer who rolls off a project in September and waits six weeks for the next one. Six weeks is 30 working days, so the bench allowance is nearly used up, and for the rest of the year that person can barely afford to wait any longer.

Time on the bench is no holiday either. Under the new policy, staff without a billable project can no longer work remotely and must take mandatory upskilling courses. Someone waiting for a project is therefore more tightly constrained than someone who has one.

Unions have made more troubling allegations. The IT union NITES has filed a formal complaint with India’s Ministry of Labour, saying that employees who could not be placed on projects were repeatedly sent emails pressing them to resign, and has asked the regulator to investigate.

These remain union allegations, but they show how the bench can be used to push people out without a formal round of layoffs.

Experts consulted by The Week said that letting go of benched staff is a last resort, and that a company as large as TCS can give people time to reskill, but that the risk of cuts further down the line is real.

An FDE title does not tell you what you are measured on

The policy applies to TCS’s IT services staff and sets no standard for the FDE profession. It is simply the clearest example of how billable-days logic works once it is written into rules.

Chris Degnan, who has publicly criticized the FDE role, called FDEs “glorified professional services people”, placing the role close to professional services. You do not have to agree with him. The criticism is worth hearing because it lands on the profession’s most vulnerable point.

The difference lies in the metric. If a company assesses you on billable days, you are working in a billable-days model whatever your title says. The TCS logic can then apply to you too, perhaps never written down so plainly, but working the same way.

So read the job description closely and listen carefully in interviews. If you hear words such as “utilization”, “billable target” or “staffing pool”, you are most likely being hired into a staffing system.

If the interviewer talks at length about feeding lessons from clients back into the product, that is a different model, and the gap between projects means something different there too.

How far does the law protect you?

Labor law varies from country to country. Take Vietnam as an example: an employer may terminate a contract unilaterally when an employee repeatedly fails to complete their work according to the performance criteria the employer has set. The end of a project, on its own, is not a ground for restructuring recognized by law.

If a company wants to lay people off for restructuring, it has to demonstrate economic difficulty or technological or organizational change, and give at least 30 days’ notice. Those who lose their jobs receive severance of one month’s salary for each year worked, with a minimum of two months. So one year of service still earns two months’ pay, and four years earns four.

The weak point is the word “criteria”. If the performance criteria a company issues include a number of billable days, time spent over the bench limit could be recast as “failing to complete work”.

The company could then take that route instead of having to prove grounds for restructuring. This is an inference from how the law is structured, and no such case has been reported, yet this is the clause you most need to read carefully.

Mechanism What is known What to ask before you sign
Bench cap TCS: at most 35 working days a year Is there a limit on how long you can wait for a project, and what happens if you exceed it?
Billable target TCS: at least 225 days a year Are you assessed on billable days or on results at the client?
Conditions while waiting No remote work, mandatory training While you have no project, what do you do and where do you do it?
Valid grounds for termination Vietnam: repeatedly failing to complete work according to set criteria Where are the performance criteria written down, and do they mention utilization?
Restructuring Vietnam: requires economic, technological or organizational grounds, 30 days’ notice, severance of at least two months’ salary What does the law where you sign say about grounds, notice periods and severance?

If you sign a contract in another country, the last two rows of the table still apply: find out which grounds for termination the law there allows, how much notice is required and how severance is calculated.

When the terms are too vague, an hour with an employment lawyer usually costs far less than the consequences of signing the wrong contract.

Does your project depend on one person?

A contract tells you only what happens after a project ends. Whether it ends early or late usually depends a great deal on whether the client sees its value. If all the support rests with one person, their departure means you have to rebuild the relationship almost from scratch.

Say you are deploying an agent that processes applications for a bank. Your champion is the head of operations, and only he understands how many hours of work the system has saved.

If he changes jobs mid-quarter, his successor sees a running cost with no visible value, and your engagement can easily become the first thing cut.

The safeguard is part of the daily work. From the first month, take your results to at least two or three people at different levels on the client side.

Record the project’s value in numbers that someone newly handed the account would grasp at once. The project then depends less on one person, and if it still ends, you have evidence to take to the next client.

What to bring to the interview

The most useful question appears on no list of sample interview questions: “The last time an engagement ended, what did the FDE in charge do the following month?” A concrete answer, such as moving to another client, returning to the product team or building internal tools, is a good sign.

If the interviewer hesitates, that is an answer too.

On your CV, show that you have worked across several engagements and carried something from one project to the next, such as a library, a process or a set of evals.

Recruiters read that as proof that you create value beyond billable days. When you need a new job, it also keeps you from being seen as someone who is worth something only while placed at a client.

Every client project ends eventually. Choose an employer that already has a clear answer for that gap before you need one.
