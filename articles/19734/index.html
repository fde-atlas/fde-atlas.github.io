Data Engineer to FDE: Practical Guide to Customer Data Ingestion and Isolation

Original title: 数据工程师转 FDE：客户数据接入与隔离实战

FDE Times · 2026-10-12 · 7 min read

Summary

This guide targets data engineers moving into forward deployed engineer (FDE) roles, drawing on job postings from Valdera, Rowspace, Vori, and Paraform. It contrasts internal pipelines, where you own the schema and bad data halts jobs, with customer data work, where sources like SAP exports, spreadsheets, and scanned PDFs arrive uncontrolled. The core practices: validate and quarantine bad rows instead of failing the whole run, treat mapping and reconciliation as ongoing operations, and turn per-customer solutions into reusable features. It includes a code skeleton for ingestion with quarantine, a five-step process for onboarding new sources, common mistakes, and advice on rewriting resumes in FDE terms, citing an 800% growth in FDE job postings in 2025.

Key points

  • Customer data has no schema owner; discovery comes before pipeline building.
  • Quarantine bad rows with reasons rather than failing the entire load.
  • Mapping and reconciliation are ongoing operations, not one-time migrations.
  • A five-step process: discovery, measure errors, prototype with quarantine, hand off to business owners, harden.
  • Resumes should show uncontrolled data sources, quantified graceful degradation, and reusable outcomes.

Key Takeaways

  • Customer data doesn't follow your rules, so the first job is figuring out where the data lives and what rules it follows.
  • Customer-side data pipelines need validation, retries, and the ability to keep running when some data is bad: load the good rows, quarantine the bad ones, and document why.
  • Mapping and reconciliation are ongoing operational work; the best work done for one customer should become a feature that all customers can use.

Imagine your first week on-site at a customer. You need to load supplier invoice data, and you're handed three things: an SAP export, an Excel file maintained manually by the finance team, and a folder full of scanned PDFs. No one owns the schema, there's no data contract, and the demo for management is already on the calendar.

For data engineers used to internal data pipelines, this is a real shock. At your old company, you controlled the data sources and formats; when data was wrong, you filed a ticket with the upstream team. Here, there's no upstream to blame, because the upstream is the customer.

The good news is that most of your skills still apply; only the order of priorities changes. And job postings for Forward Deployed Engineers (FDEs) spell out this shift with surprising clarity.

The first job is finding data, not writing pipelines

Valdera's FDE job posting states that every customer is different: data lives in different systems, with different structures and different rules. What you're integrating are the customer's ERP, procurement, and finance platforms—like SAP, Oracle, Coupa, and Ariba—not a data lake managed by your own team.

Rowspace goes a step further. Its FDEs start work after the contract is signed, sitting down with customers to build integrations and data pipelines directly in the customer's environment, on the customer's data.

They're looking for people who won't be intimidated by PDFs, Excel models, and inconsistent formats. When Vori hires a Forward Deployed Data Engineer, the listed inputs range from CSV and EDI to PDFs, and even scanned paper documents.

So the first question isn't "Airflow or dbt?". You need to figure out where the data lives, who's modifying it, and which business rules were never written down. For example, the supplier codes in the Excel file might be legacy codes from before the SAP migration.

Internal data pipelines

  • You define the schema
  • Bad data stops the job and triggers a ticket
  • Design carefully first, then run
  • Ends when the migration is complete

Customer data

  • The schema already exists, and it often contradicts itself
  • Load the good parts, quarantine the bad parts, and explain why
  • Prototype quickly, then harden what's actually used
  • Mapping and reconciliation require ongoing maintenance

Bad data must not bring down the entire run

Valdera states plainly the problems FDEs must handle: messy formats, mismatched schemas, and pipelines that need validation, retries, and graceful degradation. Those three words capture the biggest gap from internal work habits.

Do the math. Suppose the first batch has 1,000 invoices, of which 60 rows are missing supplier codes, or record the amount as "1.250,00" instead of "1250.00".

The internal approach: an assertion fails, the job stops, 0 invoices are loaded, and you have nothing to show at the demo. The FDE approach: 940 rows load successfully, 60 rows go into quarantine, each with a specific reason attached, sent to the finance team.

The code below is a minimal skeleton that handles the two tabular data sources in this scenario: the SAP export and the finance team's Excel file, assuming they've already been read into rows. Each data source has its own mapping, kept in configuration rather than hardcoded. This is also the part you'll change the most in your first few weeks.

REQUIRED = ["vendor_id", "invoice_no", "amount", "currency"]

# 每个数据源一份映射；这是配置，不是代码
SAP_MAPPING = {"vendor_id": "LIFNR", "invoice_no": "BELNR",
               "amount": "WRBTR", "currency": "WAERS"}

# 财务团队的 Excel：目标字段相同，列名不同
EXCEL_MAPPING = {"vendor_id": "Supplier Code", "invoice_no": "Invoice #",
                 "amount": "Total", "currency": "Curr"}

def normalize(row: dict, mapping: dict) -> dict:
    out = {target: row.get(src) for target, src in mapping.items()}
    amt = out.get("amount")
    if isinstance(amt, str):
        amt = amt.strip()
        if "," in amt and amt.rfind(",") > amt.rfind("."):
            amt = amt.replace(".", "").replace(",", ".")  # 1.250,00 -> 1250.00
        out["amount"] = amt.replace(",", "")
    return out

def validate(row: dict) -> list[str]:
    errors = [f"缺少 {f}" for f in REQUIRED if not row.get(f)]
    try:
        float(row.get("amount") or "x")
    except ValueError:
        errors.append(f"金额不是数字：{row.get('amount')!r}")
    return errors

def ingest(rows, mapping):
    good, quarantine = [], []
    for raw in rows:
        row = normalize(raw, mapping)
        errs = validate(row)
        if errs:
            quarantine.append({"raw": raw, "errors": errs})
        else:
            good.append(row)
    return good, quarantine

# 同一个函数，两个数据源
sap_good, sap_bad = ingest(sap_rows, SAP_MAPPING)
xls_good, xls_bad = ingest(excel_rows, EXCEL_MAPPING)

That folder of scanned PDFs isn't covered by this skeleton. It needs a separate extraction step to turn pages into rows before it can get its own third mapping and go through the same

ingest

function. Worth remembering is this shared entry point: all data sources pass through the same validation and quarantine layer.

Retries belong at the system-call layer, such as when the ERP's API occasionally times out. You add backoff to the call and cap the number of attempts. Quarantine belongs at the data layer. Don't mix the two: retrying a malformed row ten times won't make it correct.

Prototype first, harden next, reconcile forever

Valdera describes the working rhythm as: prototype quickly, then harden the parts that are actually used into something production-ready. Paraform lists building custom integrations and data pipelines on deployment-driven timelines as one of the FDE's core responsibilities.

For people used to designing carefully before running, this rhythm can seem sloppy at first. It's really just the order reversed: you learn the data's rules by running against real data.

The reason is that demos don't reflect reality. A guide from Stackmatix points out that customer data is messy, and real data pipelines are full of edge cases that no demo will surface. According to that guide, evals happen in production: nothing is proven until it runs on real systems.

Hardening isn't the end of the work either. Vori treats configuring and maintaining data mappings and reconciliations as day-to-day work for the operations team, not a one-time migration project. Customers will change supplier codes, the finance team will add a column to the Excel file, and your mappings must keep up.

Rowspace adds one more loop: the best work done for one customer should become a feature that all customers can use. The configuration-driven

normalize

function above is the seed of exactly such a feature.

In practice: five steps for onboarding a new data source

When you encounter an unfamiliar data source, follow this order.

Step 1: Data discovery. Write a one-page brief: which system the data lives in, who owns it, what format it's in, and which business rules exist only in users' verbal descriptions.

Step 2: Measure before transforming. Take a real sample and, before writing any transformation logic, count the bad rows by error type.

Step 3: Prototype with quarantine. Build the prototype to separate valid rows from quarantined rows from the start, so you have something to show the customer within days.

Step 4: Hand the quarantine list to the business owners. They can fix at the source the errors you'd never guess.

Step 5: Harden and package the mappings. Harden only the paths the customer actually uses, and move the mappings into configuration so they can be reused next time.

Common mistakes made by career changers

The most common mistake is treating a single bad row as a failure of the entire job, the way you guarded quality at your old company. The second mistake is guessing business rules instead of asking. An empty "status" column might mean "paid" in one department's process.

The third mistake is investing heavily in an elegant architecture before touching real data. The fourth mistake is about people. Vori's requirements state that candidates need empathy for customers and the ability to stay calm while handling incidents in chaotic, high-pressure environments.

Palantir, which pioneered this role, says FDEs often work directly on-site at customers. There, how you explain those 60 quarantined rows matters as much as the code that separated them.

Translating your past experience into an FDE resume

When describing the day-to-day work of an FDE, Palantir mentions handling massive-scale data. In other words, a data engineering background is a genuine advantage—no need to hide it. Paraform, citing a Financial Times report, notes that FDE job postings grew by more than 800% from January to September 2025, showing that the market truly demands this skill set.

But your resume must speak the language of this role. Instead of writing "built a Spark pipeline processing 2 TB of data per day," describe an experience integrating data sources you didn't control: how you isolated errors, and who you sat down with to clarify the business rules.

Take the 1,000-invoice scenario above as a hypothetical example. The old-style resume line would be: "Built an ETL pipeline to load invoice data into a data warehouse." That's not wrong, but an FDE recruiter sees no client, no dirty data, and no outcome.

Rewritten in FDE language: "Integrated invoice data from SAP and Excel files managed by the client's finance team; designed an isolation layer that loaded 940 of 1,000 rows on the first run, and sent the 60 failed rows along with the reasons to the finance team to fix at the source; packaged the mappings as reusable configuration for a second data source."

The second version carries three signals at once: data you didn't control, graceful degradation backed by numbers, and a reusable deliverable. Using this template, write your own version with real projects and real numbers. When reading job postings, watch for phrases like "messy data," "reconciliation," "post-sale," and "on site": they tell you how much of your time will be spent dealing with clients.

Internal data pipelines teach you to get the data right. Client data teaches you to keep the system useful even when the data isn't right yet. That's exactly the capability companies are willing to pay to deploy on site.
