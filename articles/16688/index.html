How to Hire Your First FDEs: Profiles, Channels, and Trial Design

Original title: 如何招聘你的第一批 FDE：画像、渠道与试岗设计

FDE中国 · 2026-09-04 · 9 min read

Summary

This Chinese-language guide lays out a five-step playbook for hiring a company's first forward deployed engineers (FDEs): defining the role profile, understanding the talent market, prioritizing sourcing channels, designing a work trial, and structuring the first team. It argues FDEs combine engineering, customer, and domain skills that no single person fully has, so teams should be built by complementing gaps. It cites Anthropic's stated role mix (roughly 40% full-stack building, 30% architecture, 30% customer discovery) and ByteDance salary ranges of 35,000–70,000 RMB monthly. It recommends internal transfers over external hiring, a two-week paid trial on real desensitized data with a real business stakeholder as client, and three 90-day milestones for the first lead. The key warning: don't mistake strong pre-sales people for FDEs, since winning deals and landing delivery are different capabilities.

Key points

  • FDEs need engineering, customer, and domain genes; hire for ratios, not unicorns.
  • Chinese candidates hide under titles like solutions engineer; screen for delivery evidence.
  • Channel priority: internal transfers first, ecosystem recruiting second, open hiring last.
  • Replace interviews with a two-week real task: narrow scenario, three deliverables, real client.
  • First 90 days: scenario inventory, one small end-to-end delivery, formal retrospective.

When hiring the first batch of FDEs, most companies only get one chance to get it wrong, and the cost is six months to a year. This batch determines the first-impression label of this function in your organization: "ace problem-solvers" or "rebranded on-site outsourcing"—the outcome is often decided at the hiring stage. This article provides a directly executable hiring plan in five steps: profile, market reality, channels, trial engagement, and team formation. For question-level design of interviews, use it together with the on-site FDE Interview Guide; this article focuses on team formation and process from a decision-maker's perspective. The assumption is that you have already completed the needs assessment and organizational decision, covered respectively in Does Your Company Need FDEs: A Decision Framework and Build an In-House FDE Team or Buy Delivery Services.

First, define the profile: three genes and their ratios

An FDE is a compound of three genes: the engineering gene—able to independently deliver full-stack from prototype to production in a customer environment; the customer gene—able to guide business stakeholders toward convergence amid ambiguous requirements and manage expectations; the domain gene—understanding the processes, terminology, and data shapes of your industry. People who possess all three genes are extremely rare; the practical approach is to set explicit ratios and fill gaps by weakness, rather than fantasizing about all-rounders.

A reference industry benchmark: according to third-party tracking (tryexponent.com), Anthropic's FDE role describes its work composition as roughly 40% full-stack building, 30% architecture, and 30% customer discovery, and explicitly does not hire new graduates, requiring years of customer-side engineering experience and production-grade LLM application experience. Two implications of this benchmark are worth borrowing: first, the ratio of the three types of work is deliberate organizational design, not something that emerges naturally; second, this role is not for career newcomers—your first batch must have evidence of having independently delivered complete projects; for how to evaluate such evidence, see the FDE Portfolio Guide.

The practical implication of ratios: if the first batch is three people, the recommended combination is one lead strong in both customer and domain genes, plus two members strong in the engineering gene, with domain knowledge supplied to the team by the lead through requirements translation. Don't try to form a team of three all-rounders—that would stretch the hiring timeline beyond acceptability. Also, write these three genes directly into the JD's competency requirements, replacing "what you need to be" with "what you need to have done"; resume screening signals will be much cleaner.

Talent market reality: titles, compensation, and talent flows

First, the title trap. According to analysis from the Tencent Cloud Developer Community (cloud.tencent.com), among those in China recruiting or job-hunting under the FDE label, actual work content varies enormously, and a substantial portion corresponds to pre-sales Solutions Engineers, AI implementation engineers, or private deployment engineers. Conversely, the person you're looking for is probably not under the "FDE" title, but hidden under titles like Solutions Engineer, Sales Engineer, implementation consultant, or full-stack engineer. When searching, it's advisable to cover synonym titles as well: FDSE, Applied AI Engineer, Solution Architect, Customer Engineer, etc., are all commonly used near-synonymous job titles in the industry. The first action in resume screening is therefore "title restoration": translate FDE and solutions expert back into what the candidate actually did—how long on-site, what was delivered, whether it went live, and who signed off. The value ranking of titles is far below delivery evidence.

Now, the compensation reality. According to 36Kr (36kr.com), ByteDance's FDE roles have been listed at monthly salaries of 35,000 to 70,000 RMB with 15 months' pay per year, while Alibaba Cloud Intelligence's FDE is 20,000 to 50,000 RMB monthly with 16 months' pay; according to Xinhua Daily Media Group (xhby.net), Zhipu's FDE lead reaches 60,000 to 80,000 RMB monthly. You are competing with these employers for the same pool of people. The observation from the Tencent Cloud Developer Community (cloud.tencent.com) is that in China, FDE salaries are generally higher than same-level R&D roles, and salary inversion is common. The advice for decision-makers is that managing expectations matters more than squeezing on price: if you can't offer top-of-market pay, differentiate on scenario quality and growth space—a scenario with open data, real business, and executive support is at least as attractive to strong candidates as a pay gap, often more so. One expectation to calibrate along the way: "ready-to-use" perfect candidates almost don't exist in the market; the sensible strategy is to hire people strong in two genes with the third trainable, filling the missing one with scenarios and training.

Channel priorities: internal transfers, ecosystem recruiting, and external hiring

Internal transfers are the top priority. Your organization most likely already has quasi-FDEs: Solutions Engineers or senior implementation consultants who have spent years honing at customer sites and have just taken over AI project pilots. Transferees' advantages are ready-made domain genes and organizational trust; what they need to add is engineering methodology and AI application methodology; for a systematic discussion of the transition path, see the on-site FDE Transition Path. Internal transfers also have a commonly overlooked value: they know how your organization actually works, something external candidates may not learn in a year. The operational recommendation is to first run an internal inventory: ask each business line to nominate people who "understand customers best and can also write code"; usually one round of inventory will surface two or three candidates previously outside your view.

Ecosystem recruiting is the second priority. Model vendors' ecosystem partners, top-tier delivery staff at integrators, and frontline backbone at AI application service providers are all in the same ecosystem, making background checks and capability verification relatively easy. The key action when screening is distinguishing "platform halo" from "individual capability": successful delivery on a big platform doesn't mean someone can deliver independently off-platform, and the trial engagement design (see below) exists precisely for this.

External hiring is the third priority, and the only channel that can fill top-tier engineering genes, but with the fiercest competition. According to The New Stack (thenewstack.io), in 2026 Google Cloud posted dozens of FDE positions within a week and plans to hire hundreds; leading platforms are systematically absorbing the market's existing talent. According to Xinhua Daily Media Group (xhby.net) citing platform data, the number of FDE positions rose roughly 42-fold in two years, rapidly diluting the existing talent pool. Rather than clashing head-on in the external hiring market, domestic companies should turn internal transfers and ecosystem recruiting into long-term compounding, reserving external hiring budget only for the most critical headcount slots (e.g., the first team lead). If external hiring is indeed your main channel, budget the hiring cycle by quarter and prepare your employer narrative in advance—qualified candidates will inevitably ask three questions: is the scenario real, will you get data access, and who takes the blame when things go wrong. If these three answers are weak, no salary will retain them.

Trial engagement design: replace interview imagination with a two-week real task

FDE core capabilities can't be measured in an interview room: requirements clarification, on-the-spot adaptation, dancing with business stakeholders—these are behavioral capabilities that can only be verified behaviorally. A viable alternative is to make the trial engagement a small real delivery, with four design essentials.

  • Real data and a narrow scenario. Provide a desensitized real business dataset and a narrow scenario—for example, intelligent Q&A for a certain type of report, or automatic initial triage for a certain type of ticket—not a fictional brain teaser. The narrower the scenario, the cleaner the capability signal.
  • Two weeks, time-boxed, three deliverables. Require a requirements clarification memo (tests requirements discovery ability), a runnable prototype (tests engineering ability), and a briefing deck for the business owner (tests communication and expectation management). The discovery-phase task design can be reused directly from the site's FDE Requirements Discovery Handbook.
  • A real business stakeholder plays the client. Assign an actual business owner to role-play the customer, not an interviewer doing an imitation. Business stakeholders' reactions can't be faked—whether the candidate truly understands what the business side is saying becomes clear in a single briefing.
  • Four evaluation dimensions. Quality of requirements definition, prototype completeness, briefing and expectation management, and judgment about when to ask for help independently—when to ask, whom to ask, and at what level of granularity. The last one is the most easily overlooked, yet the most predictive of on-site performance.

A two-week trial engagement is a responsible investment for both sides: the candidate tests the quality of your scenarios, and you test their true level. For candidates currently employed, the trial can be compressed into one paid consulting weekend plus a single briefing, with the principles unchanged: real data, real business stakeholders, real feedback. Choosing the trial scenario also takes care: don't pick the scenario with the most complete data—that won't reveal how the candidate handles incomplete data; and don't pick the biggest mess—no one would pass that. Pick a real scenario of medium complexity where the business side is willing to cooperate; that yields the cleanest signal.

The First 90 Days Before Onboarding: What Milestones to Set for the Lead

The first 90 days after the first cohort of leads joins determine whether this function survives. Recommend agreeing on three milestones with each lead. First 30 days: complete a scenario inventory and a data-readiness assessment, producing a scenario list ranked along two dimensions, "business value and technical feasibility"—note, do not rush into customer sites during these 30 days. Second 30 days: complete one end-to-end small-scale delivery on the top-ranked scenario, running the full path from requirements to launch with real data; the delivery can be small, but no step can be skipped. Third 30 days: complete a formal retrospective and report three things to decision-makers—the playbook that worked, the organizational friction exposed, and the resource asks for next quarter. At the end of 90 days, decision-makers should hold a reusable delivery template and one validated scenario, not three in-progress project plans. This cadence aligns with the site's project kickoff methodology (FDE Project Kickoff Document Template) and connects naturally with the biweekly retrospective mechanism (see Managing an FDE Team).

The First Cohort and Four Pitfalls to Avoid

For the first cohort, three people is the recommended minimum unit: one lead (strong customer DNA and domain DNA) plus two members with strong engineering DNA, forming backup and internal review. The rationale for this configuration is discussed fully in FDE Team Design; this article emphasizes only four high-frequency pitfalls. First, title engineering: giving a single star employee the FDE title without a team or scenarios behind it neither retains the person nor validates the model. Second, hiring only externally: an all-external team lacks organizational trust and domain knowledge and hits walls on arrival; the right structure is a mixed internal-external team. Third, treating pre-sales as FDE: pre-sales' success criterion is winning deals, while FDE's success criterion is landing implementations; misaligned evaluation will distort the whole team's behavior—see The Difference Between FDE and Sales Engineer for the boundary between the two. Fourth, mismatched compensation systems: evaluating a frontline team on a pure R&D track decouples incentives from customer outcomes; see FDE Career Development Paths for a structured discussion of career tracks and incentives. One final reminder: the goal of the first hiring round is not to hire perfect people, but to have the model complete one real validation within 90 days—use this standard to work backward on your timeline, and use it to decide when to abandon a channel.

Summary and Next Steps

To close: set the profile's ratios by the three DNAs; the two premises of market reality are title confusion and inverted compensation; prioritize channels as internal transfers, ecosystem recruiting, then open hiring; replace interview speculation with a two-week real-task trial; start the team at three people with the lead first. Recommended next steps: this week, write your company's first profile draft by the three DNAs and align with HR on the search scope for synonymous titles; then arrange one trial engagement for each of your top two candidates.
