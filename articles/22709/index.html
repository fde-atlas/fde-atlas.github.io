Cost structure and contract formats for FDE-type support. Why are arrangements such as quasi-mandate contracts and fixed monthly fees chosen?

Original title: FDE型支援の費用感と契約形態。準委任・月額定額という仕組みはなぜ選ばれるのか。

株式会社Respectify · 2026-08-04 · 15 min read

Summary

This article by Japan's Respectify explains why FDE-type support engagements almost always use quasi-mandate (jun'inin) contracts with fixed monthly fees rather than fixed-price contractor-style agreements. Under Japan's Civil Code (Articles 632 and 644), contractor contracts promise completed deliverables with liability for defects, while quasi-mandate only requires diligent performance. Because FDE work involves weekly shifting priorities and evolving scope, fixed-spec contracts fit poorly. Drawing on IPA's multi-stage model contracts, McConnell's Cone of Uncertainty, Mike Cohn on change costs, and a METR study showing AI tools slowed experienced developers by 19% on large codebases, the author argues monthly fixed pricing reduces uncertainty for both sides. The article lists three pre-contract checks: no completion obligation, ownership of interim deliverables, and who controls prioritization. The author discloses Respectiveify STUDIO's pricing: from one day per week at 600,000 yen monthly (excluding tax).

Key points

  • Quasi-mandate contracts require diligent performance, not guaranteed deliverables.
  • FDE work's weekly shifting scope makes fixed-price contractor contracts impractical.
  • Monthly fixed pricing balances flexible scope with predictable client costs.
  • Pre-contract checks: completion obligation, interim deliverable rights, prioritization control.
  • Respectify STUDIO prices from one day weekly at 600,000 yen monthly, tax-excluded.

When procurement managers begin considering FDE (Forward Deployed Engineer) style support, the first thing that usually trips them up is the question of cost. If they have experience requesting estimates from production companies or SIers, they tend to proceed on the assumption that they can hand over requirements and receive a written estimate in return. However, when they ask the same thing of an FDE-style support company, the answer they get is, "Let's start working first and decide as we look at something that actually runs." Signing a contract without first receiving even a single sheet of paper in the form of an estimate leaves the ordering party feeling uneasy.

The true nature of this unease ultimately comes down to the difference in contract types. This is also touched on in our companion article that clarifies what FDE is, but most FDE-style support adopts a contract type called "quasi-mandate" (jun'inin) with a fixed monthly fee pricing structure. This differs fundamentally, in legal terms, from the "contract for work" (ukeoi) model familiar to production companies. If the business-side representative proceeds with the contract without understanding this difference, they may find themselves unable to explain things well when asked in internal approval processes or management meetings, "Why is there no estimate?" or "Where is the guarantee of completion?" In this article, we will organize, based on official sources, what legally distinguishes ukeoi from quasi-mandate, why FDE-style support almost invariably chooses quasi-mandate with a fixed monthly fee, and the points the ordering side should examine before signing a contract.

What legally distinguishes ukeoi from quasi-mandate?

Under the Civil Code, ukeoi is defined as a contract in which "one party promises to complete a certain work, and the other party promises to pay remuneration in exchange for the results of that work" (see: Moooka Law Office, "Article 632 of the Civil Code (Ukeoi)" (as of August 2026)). The key point here is that the object of the remuneration is the result itself, namely the "completion of the work." The contractor bears the obligation to complete the deliverable, and if the deliverable is defective, they bear liability for non-conformity with the contract.

In contrast, under a quasi-mandate, the purpose of the contract is the performance of the tasks themselves, and it does not promise the completion of a deliverable. What the mandatary under a quasi-mandate bears is the obligation to process the tasks with the care of a good manager in accordance with the purpose of the mandate—the so-called duty of due care as a good manager (see: Moooka Law Office, "Article 644 of the Civil Code (Duty of Care of the Mandatary)" (as of August 2026)). As long as the work is performed faithfully, the mere fact that the results did not reach the initially envisioned outcomes does not, by itself, constitute a breach of obligation. Whether one bears an obligation of completion, or merely an obligation of faithful performance—this is the most fundamental line that separates ukeoi from quasi-mandate.

In the practice of system development, a widely adopted approach is to use different contract types for different phases, taking this distinction into account. The "Information System Model Transactions and Contracts" is a model contract template published on December 22, 2020 and last updated on April 8, 2025, which was a revision by the Information-technology Promotion Agency (IPA), an independent administrative institution, of the model contract released by the Ministry of Economy, Trade and Industry in 2007, published as the second edition (see: IPA, "Information System Model Transactions and Contracts (Second Edition)" (2020)). According to an article by a legal specialty media outlet explaining the thinking behind this model contract, a multi-stage contract is recommended in which phases such as requirements definition, where "the deliverable cannot be concretely envisioned at the time the phase begins," are handled as quasi-mandate, while phases such as detailed design, development, and testing, where the form of the deliverable is already fixed, are handled as ukeoi (see: BUSINESS LAWYERS, "Choosing Between Ukeoi and Quasi-Mandate in System Development Contracts" (2023)). The rationale is that if requirements definition is made a ukeoi contract from the start, disputes such as "that is outside the requirements, so additional costs will be incurred" are likely to arise later, so phases where the deliverable cannot yet be seen should be handled as quasi-mandate.

Why FDE-style support does not fit ukeoi

This idea that "phases where the deliverable cannot be concretely envisioned should be handled as quasi-mandate" overlaps strongly with the very nature of FDE-style support. FDE-style support is not work that delivers a specific deliverable based on a specification fixed in advance. Backlog priorities are reviewed weekly, and what to build next changes as the team observes the business situation and user reactions. One week may be spent improving CRM workflows, and the next week may begin automating content generation—the target domain itself is in motion.

Attempting to handle this under a ukeoi contract first requires an extensive back-and-forth of requirements definition and estimation. Work cannot begin until the specifications are fixed, and once they are fixed, every specification change triggers additional estimates and contract amendment procedures. The premise of changing priorities weekly and the ukeoi premise of fixing specifications and then bearing an obligation of completion fundamentally do not mesh. This is precisely why most FDE-style support chooses quasi-mandate, whose contractual purpose is the performance of the tasks themselves. Under a quasi-mandate, even if the target domain changes from week to week, there is no need to re-conclude the contract each time, and changes in priority can be treated as events within the scope of the contract.

Why a fixed monthly fee pricing structure is chosen

Pricing structures for quasi-mandate contracts include time-and-materials billing, where hours worked are accumulated and invoiced, and fixed monthly fees, where a predetermined amount is paid each month. A fixed monthly fee tends to be chosen for FDE-style support because it makes it easier to reconcile the "moving scope" nature of the quasi-mandate contract type with the ordering side's demand for "predictable costs." Under time-and-materials billing, monthly invoice amounts fluctuate as hours accumulate, making budget planning difficult from a management perspective. With a fixed monthly fee, the monthly payment itself remains constant even if the areas covered or priorities change from week to week.

In our own case, Respectify STUDIO clearly states its minimum unit pricing as "the equivalent of one day per week, from 600,000 yen per month (excluding tax, varies with workload)." Plans with greater workloads are quoted individually depending on the circumstances of each project, and we do not publish uniform prices in advance. The contract type is quasi-mandate, with monthly renewal and termination possible, and the workload covered is disclosed in monthly reports (primary information based on the terms of our own service). This "equivalent of N days per week" phrasing directly reflects the quasi-mandate nature of conveying a rough indication of workload while not fixing the coverage area itself, in the pricing structure.

Note that what can be shown here is only our own pricing and does not indicate market rates for FDE-style support in general. Even among companies adopting the quasi-mandate, fixed monthly fee contract type, the pricing structure changes if the scope of coverage or workload assumptions differ. We do not cover other companies' market rate information in this article.

The uncertainty embedded in estimates, and what the contract promises

Feeling anxious about not receiving an estimate up front is not so much about the size of the amount as it is about having to commit money to something that has not yet been built and verified. The client bears the risk of discovering only after completion that "it's not what I expected." The vendor, too, has no choice but to estimate effort before doing any work, and pads in a buffer to cover the uncertainty. Because both parties agree on an amount that incorporates their respective risks, an estimate can be described as the act of converting uncertainty into money.

This uncertainty is also known in the field of software estimation as the "Cone of Uncertainty." Popularized by Steve McConnell, it holds that estimates made in the early stages, when requirements are not yet settled, can be off by multiples of the final actuals, and that this margin of error narrows as the project progresses and details become clear (see: Construx, "Software Development's Cone of Uncertainty" (2019)). The paradox is that estimates are least accurate at exactly the stage when time is being spent trying to pin down the specifications.

The quasi-delegation (jun-iin) model with a fixed monthly fee can be understood as a contractual mechanism that lightens this upfront commitment. A quasi-delegation contract, which can be renewed or terminated on a monthly basis, commits money not to a finished product that does not yet exist, but to the work performed over the coming month. By shifting the object of the commitment from "an unseen finished product" to "a certain amount of work," it reduces the uncertainty itself that both parties carry.

Behind the viability of a "build it, then fix it" approach lies the declining cost of change. Mike Cohn, a longtime writer in the agile field, has pointed out that the traditional curve in which change costs rise sharply as development progresses is becoming a thing of the past. In addition to tools and automated testing, AI-driven code generation and faster fixes are pushing this trend forward, and the view is that the main driver of change cost is shifting from development effort to delayed feedback (see: Mountain Goat Software, "The Cost of Change Curve Is Outdated" (2026)). That said, AI does not always make development faster. In a randomized controlled trial conducted by METR in 2025, experienced engineers maintaining a complex existing open-source project of over one million lines took 19% longer to complete their tasks when using AI tools (see: METR, "Measuring the Impact of Early-2025 AI on Experienced Open-Source Developer Productivity" (2025)). Since this study targeted the maintenance of a large, long-lived codebase, it does not share the same premises as an approach of building and iterating in small units. It is closer to reality to understand that AI's benefits emerge when the scale and tempo of the cycle are well matched.

Another thing to check before signing a contract is the substance behind the claim that "we work in an agile way." What the 2020 Scrum Guide defines is the way of working of a Scrum Team consisting of a Product Owner, a Scrum Master, and Developers, with sprints no longer than one month. On the other hand, it contains no description of the contractual relationship between the client and the vendor (see: Scrum.org, "The 2020 Scrum Guide" (2020)). Even if the development team's internal practices follow Scrum, it is possible that, from the client's perspective, the experience remains no different from a traditional fixed-scope contract. Before signing, it is reassuring to verify the following points.

  • Whether changes in priority are reflected without going through change request forms or additional estimates
  • Whether results are reviewed not only at monthly milestones, but also on a weekly basis as opportunities to see working software
  • Whether the client's decision-makers are in a position to make decisions on the spot

The last point is also a prerequisite for making the most of the flexibility of quasi-delegation. In a setup where decision-makers cannot attend review sessions, priority adjustments stall and the process tends to become a mere formality. Moreover, in domains such as financial institutions' core systems, where regulations and safety considerations require requirements to be strictly fixed before building, an approach that keeps shifting priorities under quasi-delegation may itself be unsuitable. We recommend choosing a contract form after considering both the nature of the work you want done and your own organization's decision-making structure.

Monthly work reports substitute for the obligation of completion

We noted that quasi-delegation carries no obligation of completion, but this does not mean "costs accrue with no idea of what was delivered." In exchange for not bearing an obligation of completion, it is especially important in quasi-delegation contracts to maintain a state in which the client can understand what work was performed and in what quantity. In our case, we operate by disclosing the amount of work performed and the areas addressed in monthly reports, and the client uses these reports to judge "whether that month's work was reasonable." Whereas acceptance testing in a fixed-scope contract is an act of inspecting the deliverables themselves, the monthly report in a quasi-delegation contract is an act of making the substance of the work visible, rather than the deliverables. Understanding this difference reveals that much of the anxiety about quasi-delegation contracts stems not from "the lack of a completion guarantee" but from "the invisibility of the work performed." As long as the visibility of the work is assured, the absence of a completion guarantee itself is not a major obstacle.

Key points the client should examine before signing

The quasi-delegation model with a fixed monthly fee is not all advantages for the client. There are three main points to keep in mind before signing.

The first is the very nature of having no obligation of completion. Since it is quasi-delegation, delivering or completing a specific deliverable is not a contractual obligation. The client needs material for judging "whether that month's work was sufficient." How the content and volume of work are disclosed monthly is a point to confirm before signing.

The second is how the work completed up to that point will be handled, in exchange for being able to stop midstream. Because quasi-delegation can be renewed or terminated monthly, there is no clear-cut endpoint like a fixed-scope contract's "contract ends upon delivery of the finished product." The handling of rights to code and content created up to that point must be made explicit in the contract.

The third is who actually holds the authority to set priorities. Quasi-delegation is a contract form that allows the scope of work to be flexibly changed according to the client's business situation, but at the same time it presupposes that the client takes the lead in prioritizing the backlog. If the role of deciding priorities is entirely delegated to the vendor, the flexibility of quasi-delegation can instead lead to results being harder to see.

Fixed-scope or quasi-delegation: the axis for deciding

Based on the review so far, the criteria for making a decision are relatively simple. If the specifications of what you want built are already fixed and your goal is to receive a finished deliverable, a contract for work (ukeoi) is the more reassuring arrangement for the client. The nature of a contract for work—where the counterparty bears the obligation of completion and liability for non-conformity with the contract—is well suited to one-off deliverables with fixed specifications.

On the other hand, if the premise is that target areas and priorities keep changing according to the state of the business, and you want to decide next steps while watching things in motion, a quasi-mandate (jun'inin) better matches the reality. FDE-style support chooses the quasi-mandate with fixed monthly fee format precisely because the work has this latter nature. This is not a question of which is superior; it is a matter of determining which one is closer to the nature of the work you want to commission.

Concrete examples make this easier to understand. For a corporate site renewal with finalized design specifications, or a one-off landing page build with fixed requirements, a contract for work—where you inspect and accept the deliverable and are done—is appropriate. Conversely, for work such as CRM operational improvement, continuous content production at scale, or automation of sales and marketing operations, where priorities shift after work begins and the very definition of "completion" changes over months, a quasi-mandate is a better fit. Even within the same company, it is entirely feasible to commission one-off deliverables under contracts for work while contracting ongoing insourcing support under quasi-mandates. If you view the choice of contract type not as "which one the company adopts" but as "which one is closer to the nature of the work you want done," the decision becomes easier.

Summary

  • A contract for work is a contract that promises completion of a deliverable and bears the obligation of completion and liability for non-conformity with the contract, while a quasi-mandate is a contract whose purpose is the performance of the tasks themselves and which is held only to the duty of due care as a prudent manager (Articles 632 and 644 of the Civil Code).
  • Even the model contract supervised by the Ministry of Economy, Trade and Industry recommends a multi-stage contract in which phases where the deliverable is not yet visible are handled as quasi-mandates, and phases where it is fixed are handled as contracts for work.
  • Since FDE-style support assumes that target areas and priorities shift on a weekly basis, a quasi-mandate fits the reality better than a contract for work, which presupposes finalized specifications.
  • The fixed monthly fee is a pricing structure designed to reconcile the quasi-mandate's "moving scope" nature with the client's desire to "predict costs." In our case, we clearly state one day per week at 600,000 yen or more per month (excluding tax) as the minimum unit.
  • The absence of an obligation of completion, ownership of interim results, and who holds the authority to decide priorities are points you should confirm before signing a contract.
