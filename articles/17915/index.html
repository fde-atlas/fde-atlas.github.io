FDE Offer Negotiation: Discuss Level First, Then Equity and Base Salary

Original title: FDE offer 谈判：先谈职级，再谈股权和底薪

FDE Times · 2026-10-15 · 5 min read

Summary

Drawing on four sources including getperspective.ai and Formation, this article argues that FDE offer negotiations should follow a strict order: level first, then equity, then signing bonus, with base salary last. Public data shows median FDE base pay around $195K, capped by level-based salary bands, making it the least flexible component. Equity at the same level can vary by $50K-$200K depending on compensation committee decisions. The author recommends justifying level with deployment scope, stakeholder count, and autonomous technical decisions, requesting four data points (share count, fully diluted shares, 409A price, preferred stock price) to value equity, and using a real competing offer as the strongest lever—while warning against fabricating one.

Key points

  • Base salary (~$195K median) is locked by level salary bands; negotiate it last.
  • Equity at the same level can vary $50K-$200K; it's where committees have room.
  • Justify level with deployment scope, stakeholders, and autonomous decisions.
  • Value equity using share count, diluted shares, 409A price, and preferred price.
  • A real competing offer is the strongest lever; never fabricate one.

Among Forward Deployed Engineer (FDE) roles with public salary data, base salaries cluster around $195,000 and are essentially capped by level. But at the same level, equity can vary by $50,000 to $200,000 depending on the compensation committee's decision. If you spend all your effort asking for a higher base salary, you're pushing hardest on the least flexible part of the entire compensation package.

Both figures come from getperspective.ai's 2026 FDE salary negotiation guide. Combined with other guides' discussions of salary bands and levels, a sensible priority order emerges: set your level first, then negotiate equity, and leave base salary until near the end.

If you're about to receive your first FDE offer, or are considering moving over from a software engineer role, this order will affect your income far more than asking, "Can the base salary be bumped up a bit?"

This is exactly the kind of skill needed at customer sites: figuring out who has the authority to make the call, what constraints they face, and then making a request they can actually say yes to.

Why does asking for a higher base salary usually go nowhere?

Formation, in discussing negotiations for FAANG offers versus startup offers, explains the mechanics clearly: each level has its own salary band, and recruiters can only operate within that range. Formation also points out that base salary is typically the least flexible part of the compensation package. Asking for a higher base salary means asking the person across the table for something they have almost no authority to loosen.

Suppose the recruiter has already set your base salary near the top of the band. Pushing further on base salary will win you at most a few percentage points, at the cost of spending all your negotiating leverage on the least worthwhile item.

Any FDE who has worked with customers knows this situation well. When a customer says the budget is fixed, you don't haggle on price; you renegotiate scope. Offer negotiations work the same way: find where there's still room, and don't push hard on what's already locked.

Level determines the entire compensation package, so negotiate level first

Formation argues that level determines the entire compensation package: it sets both the base salary range and the equity range. Machine Learning at Scale, in an article about offer negotiations in machine learning, likewise puts level first, with the subheading "Level Matters Most." Leon Staff puts it even more bluntly: if your level is set wrong, no amount of base salary negotiation can close the gap.

The reason lies in how salary bands work. Moving up one level shifts both the base salary and equity ranges upward together. Leon Staff argues that negotiating your level is what opens up a higher income ceiling; negotiating within a band just moves you between a floor and ceiling that were fixed long ago.

For FDEs, the case for a higher level should rest on scope of work, not just years of experience. Specifically: deployment projects where you owned everything from requirements gathering to production launch, the number of customer stakeholders you dealt with directly, and the technical decisions you made on your own without waiting for approval.

Ask the recruiter what level the offer is and what the requirements are for the next level up, then map each criterion against what you've actually done.

Equity is where the committee has room to give

Once the level is set, the biggest variance is in equity. According to getperspective.ai, equity at the same level can vary by $50,000 to $200,000 depending on the committee's decision. Machine Learning at Scale also notes that equity is easier to increase than base salary. That's why getperspective.ai's guide recommends prioritizing equity, valuing it on a fully diluted basis, and checking both the 409A valuation and the preferred stock price.

Run the numbers with hypothetical figures. The offer states a grant of 40,000 shares, and the company says total fully diluted shares outstanding are 200 million, so your ownership stake is 0.02%. If the preferred stock price is $10 per share, the grant looks worth $400,000; but if the 409A price is $3, the same grant is worth only $120,000.

The same equity produces two figures that differ by more than three times. So don't accept the total the recruiter quotes over the phone; instead, get four data points: share count, total fully diluted shares, the 409A price, and the preferred stock price from the most recent round.

Only with all four figures can you compare two offers, and only then do you have grounds to push for more equity within what the committee can approve.

Your strongest leverage is another offer

getperspective.ai holds that a competing offer is the most effective leverage, even if you have no intention of accepting it. The logic is simple: the committee has the authority to increase equity, but it needs a reason to exercise that authority. A real offer on the table gives it that reason.

According to Formation, the signing bonus is the most flexible part of the compensation package. Save it for the end of the negotiation: once level and equity have been pushed to their limits, any remaining gap can still be closed with a signing bonus. One practical tip: don't fabricate an offer. The recruiter may ask for details, and trust lost before signing is hard to win back.

Start preparing from your resume, not when the offer arrives

Since level determines the entire salary band, you should prepare your case for a higher level well before the recruiter calls with an offer. The safe approach is to make sure the experiences described in your resume and interviews all reflect your scope of work.

"Participated in deploying systems for customers" isn't enough. "Owned the deployment for a customer, from requirements gathering to production launch" is the kind of evidence that carries weight when arguing for a higher level.

When reading the job description, check whether it states a level or salary range. If it does, treat that range as a clue to the salary band and the achievable base salary ceiling; if it doesn't, the level should be one of the first questions you ask the recruiter, and you should ask it before discussing any numbers.

A great FDE doesn't fight against a customer's constraints; they find the space that can still be opened within them. Negotiating your own offer should work the same way.
