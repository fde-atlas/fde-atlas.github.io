Operational dependency: the FDE lock-in risk to avoid

FDE Times · 2026-11-14 · 7 min read

Summary

The article examines operational dependency, which Cohere identifies as the lock-in risk specific to forward deployed engineering: a client can own the code and infrastructure yet be unable to run the system, calling the vendor whenever it breaks. Cohere argues this is not inevitable but the result of decisions made during the engagement. Insight Partners advises designing every engagement to end, and Databricks' Jason Martin describes handing over IP along with the ability to use it. A 12-week insurance RAG case study shows tracking the count of tasks only the FDE can do, moving one task per week from 6/6 to 0/6. Product gaps should return to owning teams with context, per PostHog's handbook, so every future customer benefits. For job seekers, the article recommends telling handover stories with concrete numbers.

Key points

  • Operational dependency is the FDE-specific lock-in risk, and it is avoidable.
  • Design the exit from day one; build client capability throughout the engagement.
  • Track weekly the count of tasks only the FDE can perform, aiming for zero.
  • Send product gaps to owning teams with context instead of patching on site.
  • In interviews, tell stories of making clients self-sufficient, with concrete handover numbers.

In brief

  • Insight Partners advises designing every FDE engagement to end: solve the hard problem, then leave.
  • Cohere calls operational dependency the FDE-specific lock-in risk, and argues it is entirely avoidable.
  • Leaving the client does not mean dropping the problem: product gaps must go back to the owning team with full context.

A practitioner quoted by Insight Partners set their team a goal that sounds self-defeating: to “literally work ourselves out of a job”. In a profession paid to sit next to the customer, that is what doing the job well looks like.

Many engineers have the opposite reflex. When you are the only person who understands the pipeline, knows why the prompt reads the way it does and how to rerun the evals, you feel valuable. The client calls you whenever something goes wrong, and every call feels like confirmation.

This is the trap. For anyone working as an FDE or hoping to become one, grasping early why being irreplaceable counts as a risk will shape how you design each engagement, and how you talk about it in interviews.

FDE lock-in does not live in the code

When engineers talk about lock-in, they usually mean proprietary data formats or APIs that are hard to swap out.

Cohere points to another kind, more specific to this line of work: “The more FDE-specific lock-in risk is operational dependency.” Cohere puts it in a category of its own, separate from technical lock-in, so the check you run has to change as well. The question is who knows how to operate the code, wherever it happens to sit.

Cohere spells out the consequence plainly: an enterprise can own a deployment without having the capability to operate it. The code sits in the client’s repository, the infrastructure runs on the client’s cloud, and the contract names the client as owner.

Yet when the system breaks at 2am, the only person who knows what to do works at another company.

More important, Cohere does not treat this as an unavoidable cost of the FDE model. “Operational dependency is not an inevitable consequence of working with FDEs,” Cohere writes, and it goes further: deep vendor involvement ought to leave the client more independent, never less.

Dependency, in other words, is the product of specific decisions taken during the work, so different decisions can avoid it.

Design the exit from day one

Insight Partners compresses its advice into a single line: “Solve the hard problem, then leave.” In its view, every engagement should be designed to end from the outset. Once the results are proven, ownership passes to the teams that will productize the system and run it at scale.

Jason Martin of Databricks describes the model through the promise his team makes to clients: bring us your hardest problem, we will solve it, and then “the IP is yours, we’ll hand it over to you”.

That promise only means something if the client also gets the ability to use the IP. Handing over a repository that nobody on the client side can read is like handing over the keys to a car nobody knows how to drive.

That is why Cohere stresses timing. Building the client’s capability has to run through the whole engagement instead of being treated as a handover exercise at the last minute. A two-hour training session in the final week rarely turns anyone into an operator; weeks of working side by side can.

Count the tasks only you can do

Take a hypothetical 12-week engagement. You build a RAG system that helps an insurer’s claims team answer questions about policy terms.

The system has six recurring operational tasks: ingesting new documents, rerunning the eval suite, adjusting prompts when quality drops, monitoring cost, fixing failures when document formats change, and updating the list of test questions.

In the first version, you do all six yourself because it is faster. At week 12, the count of “tasks only the FDE can do” still stands at six out of six. You leave, three weeks later the legal department changes its contract template, accuracy drops, and the client’s first move is to call the vendor.

In the second version, from week two you ask the client to assign an engineer to work alongside you. From week three, one task a week moves from “FDE does, client watches” to “client does, FDE watches”, starting with low-risk work such as document ingestion and leaving the hardest, fixing failures when formats change, for week eight.

By week eight the count is zero out of six, and you spend the last four weeks mostly observing, revising the operating documentation and handling the genuinely hard cases.

Both versions deliver the same working system. Only the second delivers capability. Six out of six or zero out of six is a number you can track every week, put in the status report and use to show the client how close they are to not needing you.

Leaving the client does not mean leaving the problem

There is a reasonable worry here: if the client no longer needs you, where does an FDE’s value lie? Rajkumar Irudayaraj of Alteryx answers by turning the question around. In his view, FDEs should be “a continuous learning loop for the company”, not “a permanent crutch for the customer”.

The value does not vanish when you leave; it moves elsewhere. Every time you have to write a patch yourself because the product lacks a feature, that is a signal for the product team.

PostHog’s FDE handbook asks that gaps like these go back to the owning team with good context, because good context makes them faster to fix and the next customer will never run into the same problem.

Back to the insurance example. If failures caused by a changed contract format keep needing manual fixes, the right move is to stop teaching the client to patch them by hand and write a ticket for the team that owns document ingestion: which documents broke, how they broke, and what the impact was.

Once the product handles it, the fifth task on the list disappears for every customer at once.

Two ways to run the same engagement

Set side by side, the two approaches differ in small decisions; the engineering skill is the same.

Aspect The “irreplaceable” FDE The FDE who makes the client self-sufficient
Who operates the system Only the FDE knows how to run evals, fix prompts and handle incidents The client’s engineers do the work; the FDE observes and revises the documentation
When capability is built One handover session in the final week Throughout the engagement, one task moved each week
What the client owns at the end The deployment and the IP, but no capability to run them The deployment, the IP and a team that knows how to run them
Product gaps Patched by hand on site; only this client benefits Sent to the owning team with context; the next client never hits them
Sign of success The client calls the FDE whenever something goes wrong The client no longer needs to call

The left column is not technically wrong: the system runs, and the client is happy in the short term. But every cell on the left is a debt, and it is paid by you, by the colleague who comes after you, or by the client at the worst possible moment.

The story to tell in interviews

If you are preparing to move into an FDE role, look through your current experience for a time you made people stop needing you.

It might be the runbook you wrote so the on-call team could handle a class of incident on its own, or the time you taught another team to run a data pipeline you had built.

A story like that is far more persuasive than “I’m the only one who can fix it.”

On your CV, state the result of a handover in concrete numbers instead of just “deployed system X”. A line such as “handed all 6 operational tasks to the client’s 2-engineer team; client running evals independently from week 8” shows a hiring manager that you understand how an engagement ends.

When reading job ads, watch for words such as handover, enablement or working with the product team: they hint at how the company thinks about FDE work.

If you are already an FDE, the first thing to do at your next client is write down the list of operational tasks in week one and ask the client who will take on each one. The question can sound as if you are planning your exit. In practice, it shows the client you are planning for the day they can stand on their own.

The measure worth chasing is the team you leave behind that can do the work itself; the number of times the client calls you is the wrong score.
