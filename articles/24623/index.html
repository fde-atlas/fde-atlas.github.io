How to Formulate an AI Strategy: 5 Decisions for Leadership and the Order for Rolling Them Out to the Front Lines

Original title: AI戦略の立て方｜経営が決める5つのことと現場に落とす順番

FDE Consulting · 2026-10-31 · 9 min read

Summary

The author argues an AI strategy is not a vision document but a set of five decisions management must make: purpose, target business processes, responsible owners, budget and staffing, and permitted scope of use. Citing Japan's 2026 (Reiwa 8) Information and Communications White Paper, the piece notes 68.9% of Japanese firms now have generative AI policies, up from 49.7%, yet still lag the US, Germany, and China, and about 27% report no organized efforts. IPA's DX survey shows 91.6% of AI gains are efficiency-related while only 3.9% improve revenue or profit. The article proposes a six-step sequence for landing strategy in operations and guidance on what to outsource versus keep in-house.

Key points

  • AI strategy = five management decisions: purpose, target processes, owners, budget/staffing, allowed scope.
  • Japanese firms set AI policies (68.9%) but stall at assigning who does what by when.
  • Only 3.9% of AI adopters report revenue or profit gains; most gains are efficiency.
  • Rollout: one-page decisions, decompose tasks, set metrics and stop criteria upfront, run in real operations, quarterly reviews.
  • Purpose and ownership must stay in-house; external help suits analysis and implementation only.

When management tells the person in charge to "create an AI strategy," the first thing that troubles them is not knowing what to write for it to count as a strategy. Looking at other companies' announcements, they see a list of vision statements and use cases. Even if you imitate that and produce a document, it says nothing about what will actually change on the ground starting next month.

In my view, an AI strategy is not a document about "what to do," but a combination of five things management should decide in advance: the purpose, the target business processes, the person responsible, budget and staffing, and the permitted scope of use. If these are decided, even a one-page document gets the field moving. If not, even dozens of pages won't move anything.

Here, using the Ministry of Internal Affairs and Communications' Reiwa 8 (2026) Information and Communications White Paper and the national AI Basic Plan as reference points, I will examine where Japanese companies' AI strategies tend to stall, and then describe the five decisions and the order in which to translate them into frontline work. The very concept of transforming business and organizations around AI is covered in What is AX (AI Transformation)?, and the role of FDE (Forward Deployed Engineer) is summarized in What is an FDE? The Meaning and Work of a Forward Deployed Engineer.

Where Japanese companies' AI strategies are stalling

The Ministry of Internal Affairs and Communications' Reiwa 8 Information and Communications White Paper surveyed companies about their policies on using generative AI. The combined total of "we have a policy of actively using it" and "we have a policy of using it in limited areas" was 68.9% in Japan. This is a large increase from 49.7% in the previous year's survey, but remains low compared to the US, Germany, and China. By company size, it was 74.0% for large companies and 58.1% for SMEs.

More companies are setting policies. The problem is what comes next. In the same white paper, the share of Japanese respondents saying they are organizationally working on business transformation through generative AI was 65.6%, while "no organizational initiatives" stood at 27.0%, nearly 30%, which is markedly higher than the other three countries.

Furthermore, in the question asking about the content of strategies and policies, Japan scored highest on "the purpose and policy of AI use are explicitly stated in the management strategy," followed by "management executives are communicating the significance of AI use internally and externally." In contrast, in the other three countries, answers such as "management executives have presented implementation items and achievement targets to each department" and "the department or person responsible for promoting initiatives is clearly defined" were also prominent.

The right column is my own mapping. Arranged this way, it can be read that Japanese companies' AI strategies have reached the point of "stating a purpose," but stall at the point of deciding "who does what by when."

There is also a gap on the environment side. In the white paper, Japan scored highest on "there is an environment for learning the skills and know-how needed to review business processes and consider generative AI use." In the other three countries, "training generative AI on internal data or building databases that generative AI can reference" exceeded 50%, far higher than Japan. In my view, this reads as: places to learn have been provided, but AI is not yet in a state where it can use internal information.

On results, according to IPA's "DX Trends 2026," the combined total of AI implementation effects being "better than expected" or "as expected" was 31.8%. As for the content of the effects, "work was made more efficient or faster" was 91.6%, while "sales or profits improved" remained at just 3.9%. In my view, this reflects the fact that many strategies stop at the purpose of "efficiency" and are not connected to business numbers.

What the national plan and guidelines require of companies

On the national side as well, the direction of treating AI as a management issue has become clear. The AI Basic Plan (Phase II), approved by Cabinet on July 14, 2026, states at the start of the concrete initiatives in Chapter 3, Section 4, "Continuous Transformation Toward an AI Society," that to promote organizational management reform centered on AI, efforts will be made to visualize the status of initiatives by companies, including mid-sized and small enterprises, and to develop and secure talent.

The same plan organizes AI talent profiles into five categories. Among them, AI implementation talent is defined as those who provide AI solutions adapted to the field, rather than generic AI solutions, and who are required to understand both industry/administrative operations and AI. Unless it is decided who will fill this role, separate from those writing the strategy, the strategy will remain just a document.

The plan also introduces, as ways of dividing responsibility between humans and AI, the concepts of Human in the loop (humans directly involved in the task execution flow), Human on the loop (execution is left to AI, while humans handle overall design and monitoring), and Human in the lead (humans hold the initiative in decision-making and value creation). Choosing which applies to each business process can be used directly as the vocabulary when deciding the strategy's "permitted scope of use."

The AI Business Guidelines from the Ministry of Internal Affairs and Communications and the Ministry of Economy, Trade and Industry state that, regarding AI governance, management's responsibility is significant and exercising leadership is important. As an approach, they present "agile governance," which continuously and rapidly cycles through environment/risk analysis, goal setting, system design, operation, and evaluation. The same applies to strategy: don't create it once and be done, but build it on the premise of continuous iteration.

The five things management must decide

From here on is my own framework. What management must decide as an AI strategy can be narrowed down to the following five.

DecisionWhat happens if you don't decideExample of how to decide
1. What to use AI forOnly efficiency counts get reported, and business numbers don't moveChoose one or two of the numbers reviewed at management meetings, such as sales, gross margin, lead time, or churn rate, to move
2. Which business processes to start withSmall trials scatter across departments, none of which reach productionChoose one or two processes that affect the numbers chosen in 1, have data available, and have cooperative staff
3. Who is responsibleNo one ends up making decisions between the IT department and business departmentsDecide by name, one person each for the business owner and the AI implementation owner
4. Budget and staffingIt becomes "start with the free tier first," and judgments are made on shallow validationDecide on a six-month budget and, if it's a side duty, how many hours per week to allocate
5. Permitted scope of useEither staff are too afraid to use it, or they use it on their own and accidents happenDecide what information may be entered, which steps require human review, and which uses are prohibited

1 is the core of the strategy. If it is only phrased as something like "improve productivity," the remaining four items cannot be determined. How to measure costs and benefits is covered in AI's ROI.

The organizational form for placing a person in charge of item 3 varies with company size. Committees and role divisions are covered in Building an AI Governance Structure, and considerations for appointing an executive are summarized in What Is a CAIO (Chief AI Officer)?. In a small company, the president can double as the business owner, and simply appointing one implementation lead is enough to get started.

For item 5, putting it in writing as an internal policy keeps frontline staff from being unsure. A template is available in How to Create Internal Guidelines for Generative AI Use.

The order for translating the decided strategy into frontline work

Once the five items are decided, roll them out to the frontline in the following order.

1. Put the five decisions on a single sheet and get approval at the management meeting

Rather than a vision statement, frontline teams can act when the five answers are laid out on one page. The fact that it was approved at the management meeting becomes the basis for requesting cooperation across departments.

2. Break down the target work down to the level of individual procedures

For the selected work, write out who does what, on which screen, looking at what, and making what decisions. Only after this breakdown can you decide where to introduce AI. How to select and break down work is covered in Steps to Improve Work Efficiency with AI.

3. Before starting, decide the metrics to measure and the criteria for stopping

Measure the numbers before implementation, and decide in advance under what conditions to expand and when to stop. If you set criteria afterward, any result becomes "there was some effect."

4. Start small, in real production work

Checking accuracy only in a test environment will not reveal frontline exception handling or permission issues. Narrow the target to one department or one task, and run it within actual work. Why projects stall and how to move past it is summarized in Breaking Out of AI PoC Stagnation.

5. Review the five decisions quarterly

In my view, AI capabilities and pricing change faster than annual planning cycles, and some things only become clear once you run them. Each quarter, review whether the target metrics moved, whether the selected work was right, and whether budget and staffing were sufficient, then rewrite the one-pager.

6. Turn the successful work pattern into a template and expand it to the next task

Once the numbers move in one task, turn the data preparation, review process, and training approach used there into a template and carry it to the next task. How to spend the first three months internally is covered in How to Roll Out AI Internally.

When outsourcing, how much to delegate

When getting outside help with AI strategy development, I believe how you define the scope of what you delegate determines the outcome.

The five decisions, especially the purpose and the responsible person, are not things to have outsiders decide. A purpose set by an external party belongs to no one inside the company, and a strategy without an owner does not move. What you can outsource is building the materials for making decisions, and implementation after decisions are made.

Scope of delegationWhat you can outsourceWhat to keep in-house
Before decidingWork inventory, confirming data locations, researching competitors' moves, organizing optionsThe five decisions
After decidingBreaking down target work, AI design and implementation, building evaluation frameworks, embedding into frontline operationsBusiness judgments, decisions to stop or expand
After operations beginPreparing materials for quarterly reviews, expansion to the next taskDecisions on revisions

In sales meetings, it is worth confirming "how far into implementation will the person who created the strategy materials go?" As the white paper results show, Japanese companies tend to stall at the "who, what, and by when" part after stating the purpose. Support that does not reach that part tends to just add one more document. What we take on is described in Services.
