Team Topologies for FDEs: enabling teams explained

In brief

  • The book by Matthew Skelton and Manuel Pais is not about FDEs, but it gives you the vocabulary to describe where an FDE team stands relative to product and platform
  • Cognitive load is the main reason to split teams: overloaded teams make poor decisions and move slowly
  • Every collaboration between FDEs and platform should be time-boxed, after which platform provides the result as a service for FDEs to consume

Docker has written about reorganizing its engineering teams along Team Topologies lines, and the account contains a sentence rarely seen on a corporate blog: the first attempt did not reduce cognitive load. They had to do it again.

That detail carries more weight than any endorsement. The book by Matthew Skelton and Manuel Pais is no recipe that works once applied. It is a vocabulary for describing where each team stands and how teams touch one another.

For a forward deployed engineer, that vocabulary is what matters most. You sit between the customer, sales and the product team, so every time a request from the field gets stuck between the three, you need to say clearly who does what, for how long, and when it stops.

Which edition to read, and in what order?

The first edition, Team Topologies: Organizing Business and Technology Teams for Fast Flow, was published by IT Revolution Press on September 7, 2019, and runs to 240 pages. The whole approach revolves around four fundamental team types and three modes of interaction between teams.

The second edition came out on September 23, 2025, at 304 pages, with a new subtitle: Organizing Business and Technology for Fast Flow of Value. The publisher treats the team as the fundamental unit for delivering value, and still calls the book a major step forward in organizational design for IT and knowledge work.

Read in this order. Start with the Key Concepts page on teamtopologies.com, which is short and contains all the definitions. Then read the second edition. Finally, set the book beside PostHog’s public FDE handbook to see where a real company places its FDE team.

First idea: overloaded teams make bad decisions

The main reason to split teams, according to the book, is cognitive load. The Key Concepts page puts it neatly: an overloaded CPU slows a computer down, and an overloaded team makes poor decisions and moves slowly.

Take a hypothetical FDE team of four serving six customers, each on its own stack. Every quick fix in the field becomes something the team has to keep in its head indefinitely. By the seventh customer, the team is not short of skills. It has simply run out of headroom.

The first thing to do at a customer site: list everything the team is maintaining on its own, such as connectors, scripts and custom configuration. That list shows what needs to be pushed to another team.

Second idea: name the team type correctly

Of the book’s four team types, three bear directly on everyday FDE work; you will meet the fourth when you read the Key Concepts page.

A stream-aligned team follows one stream of work, usually tied to a business domain, and the product team typically sits here. A platform team builds services that help stream-aligned teams move faster by absorbing some of the complexity.

The type that matters most for FDEs is the enabling team: a team that helps stream-aligned teams overcome obstacles while spotting missing capabilities. That role is very close to how PostHog describes its FDE team.

That team sits between customers, sales, CS and product engineering, does not own product direction, and carries real lessons from customers back to the product through the product engineering teams.

Rocketlane describes a different model: placing the FDE function inside product and R&D rather than in go-to-market. No single placement suits every company. Your job is to identify which model your team follows, because that determines where customer requests end up and who decides.

Third idea: pick the right interaction mode, and set an end date

The book sets out three interaction modes: collaboration, X-as-a-Service and facilitation. Collaboration is defined as two teams working together for a defined period of time to discover something new, such as an API, a practice or a technology. X-as-a-Service is when one team provides something and the other consumes it as a service.

For an FDE, facilitation is when you help the product team understand a problem from the field so they can build the solution themselves, rather than writing the feature for them.

Say a banking customer needs SSO through an unfamiliar identity provider. For the first three weeks, the FDE and the platform team collaborate to find the right API. After that, platform provides it as a service, and the FDE simply consumes it.

If that collaboration has no end date, platform gets dragged into every customer, and the cognitive load spreads to both teams.

To decide when a piece of work should become a service, use the test Rocketlane proposes. A solution that helps only one customer is customization. A solution that helps every future customer is product development.

Applied to the example above, a connector written specifically for the bank’s identity provider is still customization; it becomes product development only once it is built into something every future customer can use.

What does Docker teach about redrawing the team chart?

A chart that looks right on paper does not necessarily reduce load in practice, and Docker is the clearest example. The company used the Reverse Conway Maneuver to align teams with its architecture, aiming to reduce cognitive load, cut dependencies and match the direction of the product.

The first time, they got it wrong. It is a reminder that placing a new team, such as an FDE team, next to existing ones means checking whether cognitive load has actually fallen; redrawing the chart is not enough.

Who should read it, and how to use it in your career?

If you are an engineer with two to eight years of experience, working as an FDE or hoping to move into the role, this is one of the rare books on organization you can put to use the following week. Anyone leading a newly formed FDE team has even more reason to read it.

In interviews, ask directly: does the FDE team report to product or to sales, and by what route do customer requests reach the platform team? A vague answer signals plenty of friction ahead. On your CV, instead of writing “customer integrations,” describe how you turned a piece of customization into a feature shared by many customers.

Every org chart has an FDE box. The book helps you draw the arrows connecting that box to other teams, and write an expiry date on each one.
