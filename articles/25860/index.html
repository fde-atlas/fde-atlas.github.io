How Implementation Teams Should Evaluate an Onboarding Platform: A Scorecard and Pilot Plan

Quivly · 2026-10-28 · 7 min read

Summary

This Quivly guide argues that implementation teams evaluate onboarding platforms poorly because they buy infrequently, defaulting to feature checklists and polished demos. It proposes instead: derive requirements from a review of your last three rollouts (one smooth, one slipped, one complex); assign a small cross-functional group with a clear decision-maker; score candidates on six to eight weighted criteria such as reusable rollout templates, customer input collection, readiness gates, technical signal visibility, and shared FDE/CS views; then run a two-to-four-week pilot on a live implementation, triggering real events like slipped dates. It lists demo questions, five red flags predicting abandonment, and advice on planning the switch. The author discloses how Quivly maps to its own scorecard.

Key points

  • Build requirements from your last three rollouts, not vendor feature pages.
  • Use a small cross-functional evaluation group with an agreed decision-maker.
  • Weight six to eight criteria; weights matter more than scores.
  • Pilot on a live implementation for two to four weeks and log workarounds.
  • Red flags include manual-only status and no technical health visibility.

Most implementation teams do not buy an onboarding platform often, and it shows. The evaluation usually starts with a feature checklist, moves to a round of polished demos and ends with a decision based on which vendor told the most convincing story. Months later, the forward deployed engineers (FDEs) and implementation leads who live in the tool every day discover that it handles the happy path well and the messy parts of a real rollout poorly.

A better evaluation starts from your own rollouts, not from a vendor's feature page. This guide lays out a practical process for implementation and FDE teams: how to build requirements from recent projects, how to score candidates with a weighted scorecard, how to run a pilot on a live implementation and which warning signs to watch for along the way. If you want a primer on the capabilities themselves first, see our guide to customer onboarding tools.

Start From Your Last Three Rollouts, Not a Feature List

Generic requirement lists produce generic tools. The fastest way to get specific is to pull up the last three implementations your team ran, ideally one that went well, one that slipped and one that was unusually complex, and walk through each from kickoff to cutover.

For each project, write down where time was lost and where information went missing. Useful questions include:

  • Which tasks waited on the customer, and how did the team chase them?
  • Where did status live, and how many places did someone have to update when a date moved?
  • When an integration or data migration had problems, how quickly did the FDE find out, and how did CS hear about it?
  • What did the account look like at go-live, and how much context survived the handoff to ongoing adoption?
  • Which steps were repeated on every project and could have come from a template?

The answers become your requirements. "Supports task dependencies" is a feature. "When the SSO step slips, dependent milestones and the customer's view update without anyone editing three spreadsheets" is a requirement you can actually test. If your team has not yet written down its rollout phases, our guide to the implementation timeline from kickoff to go-live is a useful starting point.

Decide Who Owns the Evaluation

An onboarding platform sits between several teams, so the evaluation should too. A small group works well:

  • An implementation lead or senior FDE who owns the scorecard and runs the pilot day to day.
  • A CS representative, because the account moves to CS after go-live and the platform should carry context across that boundary.
  • Someone from engineering or solutions who can judge integration depth and how technical signals are handled.
  • An operations or RevOps owner who cares about data quality, reporting and how the tool connects to the CRM.

Agree up front on who makes the final call and what "good enough" means. Without that, evaluations tend to drift into a search for a tool that does everything. If the pilot depends on one senior leader’s time, see how to run a post-sales platform pilot without becoming the bottleneck.

Build a Weighted Scorecard

A scorecard turns opinions into something you can compare. List six to eight criteria drawn from your rollout review, give each a weight that reflects how much it matters to your team and score every candidate from one to five on each. The weights matter more than the scores: they force the group to admit what it actually cares about.

Criteria most FDE teams should include

  • Repeatable rollout plans. Can you build templates with milestones, owners, dependencies and dates, and do slipped dates roll forward to dependent work automatically?
  • Customer input collection. Can the team request files, screenshots, SSO metadata and answers from the customer in one place, and see what is still outstanding?
  • Readiness gates. Can cutover be tied to explicit technical checkpoints, such as SSO and provisioning or a migration dry run, rather than a date on a calendar?
  • Technical signal visibility. Does the platform see integration health, sync gaps and API errors, or only task status? Our comparison of onboarding platform integrations shows which CRM, support and project connectors each vendor lists.
  • Shared view for engineering and CS. Do FDEs and CSMs work from the same timeline, or does someone translate between two tools?
  • Continuity after go-live. Do the same signals carry into adoption tracking, so the post-launch picture does not reset?
  • Admin load. How much manual upkeep does the tool itself require to stay accurate?

Run a Pilot on a Live Implementation

Demos run on clean sample data. Your rollouts do not. The most reliable signal you can get is a short pilot on a real, in-flight implementation with an actual customer.

  • Pick the right project. Choose a rollout that is far enough along to have real tasks and dependencies, but early enough that the pilot can shape it. Avoid your most politically sensitive account.
  • Rebuild the plan in the tool. Recreate the project from a template, including milestones, owners and outstanding customer requests. Note how long this takes and what you had to work around.
  • Run it in parallel for two to four weeks. Keep your existing process as a fallback, but make the pilot tool the place where status changes first.
  • Trigger real events. Let a date slip, add a blocker, request an input from the customer and watch how the tool and the team respond.
  • Debrief against the scorecard. Rescore each criterion based on what happened, not what the demo promised.

Ask the FDE running the pilot to keep a short log of every time they had to leave the tool to get something done. That log is often the most honest output of the whole evaluation.

Questions to Ask in Every Demo

Demos still have a place, as long as you drive them. Bring your rollout review and ask vendors to show, not tell:

  • Show me what happens to downstream milestones and the customer's view when this integration task slips by a week.
  • Show me how an FDE and a CSM each see the same technical issue, and how the customer gets told.
  • Show me how a blocker raised in a chat thread becomes a tracked task without someone retyping it.
  • Show me what the account looks like the day after go-live, and what carries over from the implementation.
  • Show me how much of this works without a dedicated admin keeping it up to date.

Answers that start with "we are working on that" or require a separate add-on are worth recording on the scorecard rather than taking on trust. That pairs with a related guide on what implementation teams need from an AI customer success platform.

Red Flags That Usually Mean Trouble Later

A few patterns tend to predict a tool that will be abandoned within a year:

  • Status lives only in manual fields. If every update depends on someone remembering to change a dropdown, the plan will drift from reality within weeks.
  • No view of technical health. A platform that only knows about tasks cannot warn you when an integration is quietly failing, which is where many enterprise rollouts stall. Our guide to implementation risk signals covers what to watch.
  • Separate worlds for FDEs and CS. If the implementation tool and the CS tool do not share context, the handoff becomes a document nobody reads.
  • A heavy rollout of its own. If adopting the onboarding platform looks like an enterprise implementation in itself, weigh that cost honestly.
  • Reporting that cannot answer simple questions. You should be able to see overdue projects, blocked milestones and planned versus actual effort without exporting to a spreadsheet.

Make the Decision and Plan the Switch

Once the pilot is done, total the weighted scores, read the FDE's log and make the call with the group you set up at the start. A close score is usually broken by admin load and by how well the tool handled the messy events you triggered during the pilot. We cover onboarding went well but the customer still churned: proving value after implementation in more detail in a related post.

Then plan the switch the way you would plan a customer rollout. Build your standard templates first, move new projects onto the platform before migrating in-flight ones and set a date after which status updates only happen in the new tool. Define what success looks like after the first quarter, such as fewer status meetings, fewer missed customer inputs or a shorter path from kickoff to first value, and check it.
