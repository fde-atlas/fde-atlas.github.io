How to Run an FDE Workshop: Design and Facilitation Steps for Drawing Out Customer Challenges

Summary

This FDE Soken article explains how forward deployed engineers should design and run workshops with customers: jointly breaking down frontline workflows to agree on which problems to solve and what to validate next. It contrasts workshops with regular meetings and requirements definition, stresses working from real screens and data, and lays out a five-stage process, a half-day timetable, facilitation techniques, and how to connect outcomes to a PoC via a problem description document and agreed success and exit criteria.

Key points

  • Workshops aim at validating problem hypotheses and securing consensus, unlike status meetings or requirements definition.
  • Preparation is 80% of success: agree upfront on goals, participants, real artifacts, initial hypotheses, and deliverable templates.
  • Facilitation hinges on asking about facts and recent actions, not opinions, and having both frontline staff and decision-makers present.
  • Outputs are a problem description document and a PoC scope that explicitly states what will not be done.
  • Success criteria and exit criteria must be quantified and agreed before a PoC starts.

An FDE workshop is a working session where you break down frontline business processes together with the customer and get stakeholders to agree on "what to solve" and "what to validate next." Its goal is clearly different from ordinary meetings aimed at reporting or finalizing specifications. This article organizes the practical steps from design and facilitation through to connecting to a PoC (proof of concept).

What Is an FDE Workshop? Its Purpose and How It Differs from Ordinary Meetings

An FDE workshop is a working session for breaking down the customer's frontline business processes together and agreeing on the challenges to solve and the scope of validation.

An FDE (Forward Deployed Engineer) is a role that embeds with the customer on-site and handles everything end to end, from discovering challenges to implementation. The first major hurdle is the workshop. If the challenges don't gain resolution here, downstream PoCs and implementations tend to end up as "built but never used."

The Workshop's Purpose: "Identifying Challenges" and "Gaining Agreement"

The purpose is to identify business challenges that even the customer hasn't yet been able to articulate, and to agree with stakeholders on what to validate next.

The customer's initial requests often take the form of means, such as "we want to streamline operations with AI" or "we want a dashboard." The FDE's job is to find the business bottlenecks behind those requests. You dig concretely into who is spending how much time on which decisions. From hands-on experience, simply reframing the request with "once that screen exists, whose work, exactly, will be reduced?" often dramatically changes the quality of the discussion.

How It Differs from Regular Meetings and Requirements Definition

Regular meetings aim at information sharing; requirements definition aims at finalizing specifications. What an FDE workshop aims at is validating hypotheses about the challenges.

PerspectiveRegular meetingsRequirements definitionFDE workshop
Main purposeSharing progress and informationFinalizing specificationsValidating challenge hypotheses and agreeing on priorities
ParticipantsProject stakeholdersClient-side and developer-side staffFrontline staff + decision-makers + FDE
Materials usedReportsRequirements lists and screen mockupsReal data, actual screens, business flows
DeliverablesMeeting minutesRequirements specificationChallenge description document and PoC scope proposal
ApproachReport → Q&AExhaustive listing and confirmation of itemsObserve → break down → hypothesize → agree

The biggest difference is "talking in front of the real thing." You put reports, business system screens, Excel summary sheets, and so on on display, and discuss while watching how frontline staff actually work with them.

The Prototype Seen in Palantir's "AIP Bootcamp"

Palantir runs short, intensive, hands-on sessions called "Bootcamps" for rolling out its AIP (Artificial Intelligence Platform). It's a representative example of the FDE-style workshop.

Since 2023, Palantir has introduced AIP Bootcamps—using customers' real data to shape use cases in a short period—as a primary means of customer acquisition in earnings briefings and official announcements. The idea of "showing something that works on the spot and gaining agreement," rather than requirements work on paper, is a useful reference for designing FDE workshops at any scale.

How Do You Run a Workshop? Steps from Preparation to Building Agreement

Designing the process in five stages—"preparation → understanding the frontline → surfacing challenges → prioritization → agreement and next actions"—keeps things stable.

The quality of preparation, more than facilitation skill on the day, determines the outcome. It's no exaggeration to say preparation is 80% of the work.

Five Things to Decide in Advance

In preparation, agree with the customer beforehand on five items: the goal, participants, the real materials to bring, hypotheses, and the deliverable format.

ItemWhat to decideCommon failure
GoalWhat must be decided by the end for the session to be a successEnds as mere "opinion exchange" with nothing decided
ParticipantsWhether both frontline staff and decision-makers can attendDecision-maker absent, agreement gets deferred
Real materialsScreens, reports, and sample data of the target businessOnly verbal explanations, so the actual work remains invisible
Initial hypothesesTwo or three challenge hypotheses the FDE forms in advanceGoing in with a blank slate, discussion diverges
Deliverable formatTemplates such as a challenge description documentTranscription afterward balloons the workload

Initial hypotheses don't have to be right. Having hypotheses makes it easier for the customer to respond with "no, it's not that—actually, this is the problem." Far more information comes out than asking with a blank slate, "Is there anything you're struggling with?"

Standard Agenda for the Day (Example: Half-Day Version)

Aim for a half day (about 3 hours), structured so the first half builds understanding of the frontline and the second half narrows down the challenges.

  • Introduction (15 min): Share the goal, the process, and the day's deliverables
  • Recreating the work (45 min): Have frontline staff operate the actual screens and reports, and follow the flow of the work
  • Surfacing challenges (45 min): For each step of the business flow, write out on sticky notes or a whiteboard where things "take time," "involve uncertain judgment," or "require rework"
  • Break (10 min)
  • Prioritization (40 min): Arrange the challenges on two axes—impact and ease of implementation
  • Agreement and next actions (25 min): Decide PoC candidates, owners, and deadlines, and read them aloud

The time allocation is just one example. If the business is complex, it can work better to spend an entire session on "recreating the work" and hold the workshop across two sessions.

Building Agreement and How to Record It

As a rule, agreements should not be left verbal; write them on the screen on the spot, read them aloud to confirm, and share them within the same day.

It is not uncommon for a perception gap to arise after a workshop, with someone saying "that's not what I meant." The countermeasure is for the FDE or a scribe to write the discussion in real time on a shared screen, and at the end to read out the decisions, pending items, and next actions. For the summary sent the same day, placing the decisions at the top and moving the detailed minutes to the back makes it easier to read.

What are the facilitation tips for drawing out customers' business challenges?

There are three tips: ask for facts rather than opinions, touch the actual materials together, and bring the decision-makers and frontline voices into the same room.

Ask "what did you actually do last time" rather than "what do you think"

To draw out challenges, asking about recent concrete work activities in chronological order is more accurate than asking for opinions or wishes.

If you ask "what is inconvenient?", you tend to get generalities or complaints about other departments. If you ask "when you did that aggregation last week, what did you open first?", the actual steps and sticking points become visible.

Questions to avoidQuestions to replace them with
Is there anything you are struggling with?What was the most time-consuming task recently?
What do you want to do with AI?What information do you look at when making that decision?
Do you want this feature?When did you last do this task, and how many minutes did it take?
Please tell us about your current challengesAt which step did rework or confirmation send-backs occur?

Exact figures do not need to come out on the spot. Even rough estimates like "about half a day" or "two days at month-end" are sufficient material for prioritization.

"Touch it together" with screen sharing and real data

Discussing while having them actually operate business systems or Excel reveals exception handling and implicit rules that never come up verbally.

Frontline staff tend to see their own work as "just the normal thing," and steps often get left out of explanations. Watching the operations surfaces steps like "here I open another file and visually cross-check," which sometimes becomes the prime candidate for automation. The FDE notes observations on the spot and confirms, for example, "does that cross-checking happen every time?"

Preventing skewed discussion and the "absence of decision-makers"

To keep the discussion from being swayed by senior members' remarks, it is effective to have everyone write down ideas individually first before sharing.

Simply setting aside about five minutes for everyone to silently write on sticky notes or a shared board makes it easier for younger staff and frontline members' perspectives to emerge. On the other hand, deciding final priorities requires someone with decision-making authority. From practical experience, if both frontline staff and decision-makers cannot attend, rescheduling the session to get both together tends to shorten the time to PoC overall.

How do you connect workshop outcomes to a PoC or implementation?

The key to connecting outcomes to a PoC is consolidating the challenges into a single problem statement and agreeing on success criteria and exit criteria before starting.

Distilling the outcomes into a "problem statement" and "PoC scope"

Workshop outcomes are consolidated into two documents, a problem statement and a PoC scope, and approved by the customer's decision-makers.

The problem statement should include, at minimum, the following items.

  • Target business and personnel: whose work, in which department, and which tasks
  • Current state: steps, time required (estimate), frequency of occurrence
  • Challenges: whether the problem lies in time, quality, or judgment
  • Desired state: whose work will change, and how, once solved
  • Available data: location, format, and whether access permissions exist

In the PoC scope, clearly state "what will not be done" as much as "what will be done." If the scope is not narrowed, the PoC can turn into a redo of requirements definition midway through.

Decide success criteria and exit criteria first

Before starting the PoC, agree in advance, using numbers or conditions, on "what must be confirmed to proceed to production" and "what would trigger a stop."

CriterionExample setting (item template)Key point
Success criterionTime required for the target work is reduced by a certain percentage or more versus the current stateGrasp current values during the workshop
Quality criterionOutput accuracy and review effort stay within acceptable rangesFrontline staff serve as the judges
Exit criterionRequired data cannot be obtained by the deadlineInclude non-technical factors in the conditions
DurationBounded in units of a few weeksPrevents the effort from dragging on

Since concrete target values differ by business, decide them with the customer based on the current values gathered in the workshop. Setting target values without grounds becomes a source of disputes over the PoC evaluation itself.

What companies considering adoption should prepare

For companies receiving FDE support, it is effective to prepare three things in advance: securing frontline staff's time, data access permissions, and decision-makers' participation.

Workshop quality is not determined by the FDE's individual skill alone. Can frontline staff participate without interrupting their work? Can sample data be provided in advance? Is there someone who can decide priorities on the spot? These receiving-side preparations greatly affect whether results appear in a short period. Even for individual engineers aiming to become FDEs, this "ability to orchestrate the customer's preparations" is considered a capability valued alongside technical skill.
