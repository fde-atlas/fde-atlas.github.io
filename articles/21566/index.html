Summary

This article by Shohei Tokumitsu, CEO of DeFactory, explains SES (System Engineering Service) contracts for Japanese companies. Under SES, firms dispatch engineers to client sites on quasi-mandate (junyin) contracts, paying for work performed rather than deliverables, unlike fixed-price contracting with SIers. Citing METI data, it notes Japan's IT talent shortage could reach roughly 790,000 by 2030. The piece lists four benefits of using SES firms (filling engineer gaps, avoiding training costs, staffing flexibility, easier handling of spec changes) and two cautions (engineers may leave when contracts end, and direct authority over them rests with the SES firm). It closes with promotion of DeFactory's offshore development services, citing 600+ corporate clients.

Key points

  • SES contracts are quasi-mandate agreements paying for work, not deliverables.
  • METI projects an IT talent shortage of up to ~790,000 by 2030.
  • Benefits: fill gaps, cut training costs, flexible staffing, easier spec changes.
  • Cautions: engineers may depart at contract end; direction authority stays with the SES firm.

First, let's take a detailed look at the characteristics of SES as a service and how it differs from other development approaches.

Limited to engineers with 5+ years of professional experience

High-rate, direct-contract project information

SES stands for "System Engineering Service." Therefore, an SES company is a company that enters into "SES contracts" with freelance engineers or engineers belonging to SES partner companies and dispatches them to external companies.

Also, an SES contract is equivalent to a "quasi-mandate contract" (junin keiyaku); since it is a contract for the performance of work, a key characteristic is that the provider bears no responsibility for deliverables. In contrast, under a "fixed-price contract" (ukeoi keiyaku), the provider is responsible for deliverables, making it a different contract type from SES.

Upon request from end companies such as large enterprises or SMEs, SES companies station engineers on-site at system development projects and provide human resources. As a result, the end company does not pay the SES company "compensation for deliverables," but rather pays for the "work performed by the stationed engineers."

In-house development refers to a state in which a company develops and operates its own services using its own resources. The difference with SES is that contracted engineers are dispatched on a fixed-term basis and temporarily added to the company's resources.

Examples of services handled by companies doing in-house development include websites, smartphone apps, e-commerce sites, and business systems for corporations. Note that in some cases, in-house development companies may also request engineers from SES companies.

A SIer is a company that provides contracted software development or information processing services, also known as system integration. Most SIer companies enter into fixed-price contracts with clients, so they are compensated based on deliverables.

On the other hand, SES companies operate under SES contracts, so they differ in that they do not bear responsibility for deliverables. Also, SIer companies are broadly divided into the following three types.

Manufacturer-affiliatedAffiliated with major computer manufacturers; handles system development for the parent company and group companies
User-affiliatedSpun off from the information systems departments of major general companies; handles system development for the parent company and external companies
IndependentHas no parent company; handles system development for external companies

As shown in the table above, when an external company wants to request contracted software development from a SIer company, it would contact a user-affiliated or independent company.

Next, let's look at the 4 benefits of using an SES company.

While the IT industry's market size continues to expand, many companies struggle with a shortage of engineering talent. Using an SES company offers the benefit of addressing this challenge.

According to materials from the Ministry of Economy, Trade and Industry, the gap between the supply of IT talent, including engineers, and IT demand may widen as follows.

- "Low-growth scenario" with a small supply-demand gap: shortage of approximately 160,000 IT professionals

- "Medium-growth scenario" with a certain level of IT demand: shortage of approximately 450,000 IT professionals

- "High-growth scenario" with a large supply-demand gap: shortage of approximately 790,000 IT professionals

※ Reference: Ministry of Economy, Trade and Industry, "Survey on IT Talent Supply and Demand - Survey Report"

Given these survey results, the engineer shortage may widen further in the future. Using an SES company, which allows you to secure resources for your company even temporarily, offers clear benefits.

When you request an SES company and have dispatched engineers stationed at your company, you can reduce training costs. This is because the SES model is premised on being provided with the "skills the engineers possess."

For example, if you clearly communicate the skills and knowledge you require in engineering talent at the consultation stage with the SES company, you increase the likelihood of being assigned engineers at the level you need.

Another benefit of using SES companies is that they allow you to increase workforce flexibility according to your project. For example, even if you need extra hands while a project is underway but want to adjust staffing as soon as the project ends, this is no problem with engineers assigned from an SES company.

Also, you cannot suddenly assign tasks requiring skills your in-house full-time engineers have not yet acquired to them. However, by using an SES company, you can request engineers with the skills your company needs to be assigned to you.

When using an SES company, you enter into an SES contract in which you bear no responsibility for deliverables, so even if specifications change during the project period, you have the advantage of being able to respond flexibly under your company's direction.

On the other hand, with a fixed-price (contract-for-work) agreement, if major specification changes occur, it may be necessary to revise the "requirements definition document" that formalizes the client's requests, so an immediate response is not always possible.

However, under an SES contract, if the skills required after a specification change fall outside the engineer's scope of work, you will need to consult the SES company about replacing them with new personnel.

In this section, we will introduce two points to keep in mind when using an SES company.

One thing to be aware of when using an SES company is that an SES contract is a "period-based contract" in which skills are provided by engineers, so engineers may leave once the contract period ends, even if development is still in progress.

If the project schedule has fallen behind and you want the engineers stationed under an SES contract to continue, you need to consult the SES company before the contract period ends. However, there is no guarantee that the same engineers will be assigned.

This is because SES companies manage engineers' project assignments, and in anticipation of the end of the contract period with your company, some engineers may already be scheduled to work on-site at another client.

Another point to note is that the authority to give instructions to engineers ultimately rests with the SES company. Generally, under staffing dispatch contracts, the end client holds the authority to instruct engineers, so even if you suddenly want to change the work content on the day, you can respond flexibly.

However, for engineers stationed via an SES company, the authority to give instructions lies with the SES company in the first place, so if you want to change the work content, you need to consult them in advance. Also, if the SES company that employs the engineer rejects the requested work content, you cannot assign that work to the engineer.
