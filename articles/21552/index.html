What's the Difference Between VPoE and CTO? Defining the Required Roles and How Freelancers Can Land High-Paying Projects | Career Support Media Specializing in FDE, Engineers, and IT Freelancers

Original title: VPoEとCTOの違いとは？求められる役割の定義とフリーランスにおける高単価案件獲得術 | FDE/エンジニア/ITフリーランス特化のキャリア支援メディア

Summary

This Japanese article distinguishes the CTO from the VPoE (VP of Engineering): the CTO owns outward-facing technology strategy, architecture decisions, and technical vision, while the VPoE owns inward-facing people management—hiring, evaluation, culture, and engineering managers. The author argues that when engineering headcount reaches 30–50 people, a CTO handling both roles typically breaks down, so a VPoE should be introduced. It covers common failure patterns (undefined authority, double reporting, VPoEs lacking technical context), remedies such as RACI matrices, and freelance market rates in Japan as of 2025: former CTOs can command roughly ¥1–1.8M per month as fractional CTOs or advisors, with dual CTO/VPoE experience reaching ¥1.5–2M or more across multiple clients.

Key points

  • CTO decides what and how to build; VPoE decides who builds and how the team is organized.
  • At 30–50 engineers, CTO dual-hatting fails and a dedicated VPoE is recommended.
  • Common failures: undefined authority, double reporting, and token VPoEs undermined by CTO interference.
  • RACI matrices clarifying hiring, promotion, and tech-stack decision rights resolve most conflicts.
  • Freelance fractional CTO/VPoE roles pay ¥1M–2M+ monthly, often across several clients.

"What's the difference between a CTO and a VPoE?" "Which one does my organization need?" — We often hear these questions from executives looking to scale their engineering organizations and from senior engineers exploring their career direction.

In Japanese startups and growth-stage companies, the CTO (Chief Technology Officer) is a relatively familiar role, but the VPoE (VP of Engineering) is still not widely recognized, and in many cases organizational design proceeds with the distinction between the CTO and VPoE remaining unclear. This confusion is one of the main causes of the "30-50 person wall," where both technology and the organization break down.

In this article, we thoroughly explain the differences in roles, missions, and required skills between the CTO and VPoE, along with real-world failure examples and solutions, and even how engineers who have held these positions can achieve high-paying independence as freelancers.

The CTO, as the "highest authority on technology," is responsible for technology selection, architecture design, and technical commitment to business growth, while the VPoE, as the "highest authority on the engineering organization," commits to people management such as hiring, evaluation, and culture building. The biggest difference is whether you scale the technology or scale the organization.

In a nutshell, the CTO is the technical decision-maker for "what to build and how to build it," while the VPoE is the organizational decision-maker for "who builds it and how to organize it." The two are like the two wheels of a car; without either one, an engineering organization cannot scale healthily.

The CTO (Chief Technology Officer) is a role that formulates and drives the company-wide technology strategy and leads business growth through the power of technology. The CTO's main areas of responsibility are as follows.

Main areas of CTO responsibility:

  • Technology strategy and vision formulation: Building mid- to long-term technology roadmaps and making decisions on the technology stack to adopt
  • Final decisions on architecture: Determining the technical direction of product system design
  • Technical risk management: Assessing and addressing security, scalability, and technical debt
  • External technical presence: Communicating the technology vision to investors, customers, and job candidates (through talks, blogs, interviews, etc.)
  • R&D and innovation promotion: Researching new technologies, running PoCs, and analyzing competing technologies
  • Executive participation as CTO: Contributing a technical perspective to board and executive meetings

The CTO is characterized by a strongly outward-facing role. They often appear at investor briefings (IR) as the face of the company's technology, promote the company's technical appeal in recruiting, and handle technical negotiations with external partners.

The VPoE (VP of Engineering) is a role that maximizes the productivity, quality, and culture of the entire engineering organization. The VPoE's main areas of responsibility are as follows.

Main areas of VPoE responsibility:

  • Engineer hiring strategy and execution: Developing hiring plans, designing interview processes, and making offer decisions
  • Engineer development and evaluation system design: Establishing grade systems, performance reviews, and 1-on-1 frameworks
  • Engineering manager (EM) management: Managing multiple EMs in the reporting line and improving management quality
  • Development process and productivity optimization: Improving delivery speed, velocity, and sprint completion rates
  • Building organizational culture and engineer experience: Ensuring psychological safety and creating an environment where engineers can work comfortably
  • Budget management: Managing the engineering department's headcount, tools, and training budgets

The VPoE is characterized by a strongly inward-facing role. They focus on building an organization where engineers can perform at their best, prioritizing the optimization of internal people, processes, and culture over external communication.

As this table shows, CTO and VPoE are not in a superior-subordinate relationship; both are executive-level roles with different areas of expertise. Some organizations have the VPoE report to the CTO, but in larger organizations the two often stand on equal footing.

In the early startup stage, the CTO often handles organizational management as well, but management tends to break down when the engineering organization crosses the "30-50 person wall." Establishing a VPoE at this point and dividing the roles is the optimal solution for scaling the organization.

In the first few years after founding, when the team has roughly 5-15 engineers, it is not uncommon for the CTO to single-handedly take on the roles of tech lead, EM, and VPoE. At this stage, speed and flexibility of decision-making take priority over division of labor.

However, even in this phase, once the following signs begin to appear, it is a signal that "it's time to start thinking about separating the roles."

Typical signs of CTO overload:

  • Interview time exceeds 10 hours per week, delaying technical decision-making
  • Time for architecture reviews and technical research disappears because of 1-on-1s and performance reviews
  • Engineer attrition starts to rise, but there aren't enough resources for root-cause analysis or countermeasures
  • Technical debt keeps accumulating while there is no bandwidth to create an organization-wide remediation plan
  • The CTO personally feels frustrated: "I want to talk about technology, but my calendar is filled with hiring, evaluations, and 1-on-1s"

A CTO in this state tends to produce mediocre output on both the technical and organizational fronts.

Once the team exceeds 30-50 engineers in the growth phase, the CTO's dual role effectively stops functioning. By establishing a VPoE at this point, the CTO can focus purely on technical challenges (technology strategy, architecture, R&D).

Expected benefits after establishing a VPoE:

  • Faster hiring: With the VPoE dedicated to managing the hiring process, engineer staffing rates improve
  • Lower engineer attrition: The quality of 1-on-1s, development, and evaluations rises, improving engineer retention
  • Improved development velocity: Having a dedicated leader for process optimization increases delivery speed
  • Greater CTO output: The CTO can focus on setting the technical vision, eliminating technical debt, and researching new technologies

Guidelines for when to establish a VPoE:

Number of engineersRecommended structure
Up to 15The CTO also serves as an interim EM (temporary)
15-30A few EMs are placed under the CTO; it often functions without a VPoE
30-50The phase where establishing a VPoE should be seriously considered
50+Establishing a VPoE is strongly recommended. Without one, the risk of organizational breakdown rises

The most common failure is when the boundaries of authority between the VPoE and CTO (especially around technology selection and personnel evaluation) become ambiguous, confusing engineers on the ground. In professional settings, the reporting lines and final decision rights of both parties are put in writing, and a structure of mutual respect is built.

It is very common that even after hiring and establishing a VPoE, the CTO continues to intervene in individual engineer evaluations, hiring, and development.

Main causes of this failure:

  • Lack of trust: Confidence in the VPoE has not yet been fully established, and the CTO feels "it's faster if I do it myself"
  • Undefined roles: The scope of authority between the CTO and VPoE is not documented and is run on implicit rules
  • Emotional difficulty letting go: The CTO has long played the role of the organization's "mom and dad" and feels psychological resistance to letting go

Problems this failure causes:

  • Engineers end up in a "double reporting" state, consulting both the CTO and the VPoE, delaying decision-making
  • The VPoE becomes a "VPoE in name only" that doesn't actually function, and the hired person loses motivation and resigns
  • The CTO remains under heavy personal workload and cannot focus on technical challenges

Solution: The most effective approach is to document the areas of responsibility of the CTO and VPoE using a RACI (Responsible, Accountable, Consulted, Informed) matrix. Simply clarifying three points—"final hiring decision rights," "decision rights on raises and promotions," and "decision rights on technology stack changes"—resolves most of the confusion.

When a VPoE specialized in people management is hired externally, engineers may push back, saying "I can't trust decisions from a VPoE who doesn't understand technical matters."

Common examples of this friction:

  • The VPoE introduces a policy of "code review turnaround within 24 hours" from a process-efficiency standpoint, but because technical complexity is not taken into account, it meets fierce resistance from the field
  • In promotion evaluations, the VPoE cannot correctly judge technical achievement, and a tech lead appeals directly to the CTO that "this evaluation is wrong"
  • In hiring interviews, the VPoE cannot properly assess technical skills, increasing hiring mismatches

Solution: If the VPoE lacks a technical background, the following complementary measures are effective.

  • Position tech leads and principal engineers as the VPoE's "technical advisors" and involve them in technical judgments for evaluations and hiring
  • Have the VPoE hold regular pair sessions with field engineers (time spent looking at code) to create opportunities to learn technical context
  • Set up weekly sync meetings between the CTO and VPoE so the VPoE can always stay aware of technical developments

The CTO and VPoE create the greatest synergy when they work together on "eliminating technical debt" and "solving organizational challenges."

Examples of excellent collaboration patterns:

Eliminating technical debt requires engineers' understanding, motivation, and skill development. Working alone, the CTO can design the technical solution of "what to fix and how," but the organizational challenges of "who does it, how to prioritize it, and how to get engineers' buy-in" are the VPoE's domain.

A practical collaboration framework:

  • The CTO creates a technical debt map: Technical debt across the entire system is visualized, with priorities, risks, and remediation costs organized
  • VPoE creates an organizational execution plan: Develop a plan specifying which team, who, and when will handle each item, in coordination with hiring and skill development plans
  • Jointly persuade stakeholders: The CTO explains "technical risks and ROI" to management, while the VPoE explains "human resources and timelines"
  • Joint progress monitoring: Both parties regularly review progress and immediately share technical and organizational obstacles

Professionals with advanced management and technical expertise who have led both technology and organization are extremely rare in the Japanese market. Engineers with this level of experience have overwhelmingly high market value, and by going independent as freelancers (technical advisors, external CTOs, external VPoEs), they can serve multiple companies concurrently as advisors and reliably secure high-rate engagements exceeding 1 to 1.5 million yen per month.

In recent years, freelance demand has surged for roles such as "Fractional CTO," "external VPoE," and "technical advisor."

Main reasons for the growing demand:

  • Startups in the seed to Series A stages lack the funds and competitiveness to hire full-time talent with CTO-level expertise
  • In corporate DX initiatives, demand is rising for external professionals who can "transform both technology and organization simultaneously," which companies lack internally
  • The "fractional" work style, committing 2-3 days per week to multiple companies, which is common in the West, is beginning to spread to Japan

Key points for securing high-rate engagements:

  • Show concrete results with numbers: Organize quantitative achievements such as "grew the engineering team from X to Y in Z years," "reduced hiring costs by X%," or "reduced incident count by X% by resolving technical debt"
  • Clarify your specialty: Position yourself with sharp expertise, such as "SaaS x engineering organization launch" or "technology architecture renewal during large-scale scaling"
  • Continue external communication: Increase industry recognition and credibility through technical blogs, conference talks, and social media
  • Register with advisor agents: Registering with freelance advisor agents specializing in IT and engineering, or tech-focused executive talent agents, is effective
  • Build a track record in communities: Participate in engineer and executive communities and build trust within them

A: While an EM is a "manager of people and processes for a single team," a VPoE is the "executive responsible for the entire engineering organization." The VPoE is a position above the EM and manages multiple EMs.

Promotion from EM to VPoE usually follows the stages of "Senior EM → Engineering Director → VPoE." Experience managing multiple EMs and experience in organizational design overall, including hiring, evaluation, and culture building, are prerequisites for promotion.

Situations where freelance CTOs and VPoEs are especially valuable:

A: You can, but a minimum understanding of technical context is essential to earn the trust of the engineering team. "You don't need to write code, but you must understand technical discussions and be able to ask questions" is the realistic minimum bar.

The realistic bar for the technical skills required of a VPoE is as follows.

  • Required: Being able to understand the background and trade-offs behind technical decisions (e.g., "in a discussion about choosing between microservices and a monolith, understanding the characteristics of each architecture and its impact on the team")
  • Required: Understanding what engineers are struggling with and being able to ask the right questions
  • Not required: Current-level coding skills or knowledge of the latest frameworks

Becoming a VPoE with no programming experience is extremely difficult, but with 3-5 or more years of hands-on engineering experience, it is entirely realistic to then shift your focus to EM and management roles and aim for VPoE while maintaining your technical context.

A: Holding both roles is unavoidable in the early stages, but the biggest point of caution is to consciously decide "which role to prioritize" and position the dual role as a "temporary measure."

Common pitfalls when holding both roles, and countermeasures:

  • Both technology and organization end up "half-done": It is effective to consciously switch focus, such as "this week I'll focus on organization (hiring, 1-on-1s)" and "next week I'll focus on technology (architecture, debt reduction)" on a weekly basis
  • Hiring gets deprioritized: When urgent technical issues arise, hiring interviews tend to be postponed. We recommend fixing hiring interviews on your calendar as "inviolable time blocks"
  • Burnout risk: Holding both roles involves high cognitive load. Once the number of engineers exceeds 20, start seriously planning to hire a dedicated VPoE or CTO

There is a clear distinction: the CTO "drives the business through technology," while the VPoE "scales development through organizational strength." Neither is superior or inferior to the other; each provides indispensable value to the organization along a different axis of expertise.

Let's summarize the key points of this article.

  • Essential difference in roles: The CTO is the final owner of technology strategy and architecture (outward-facing), while the VPoE is the final owner of hiring, evaluation, and organizational culture (inward-facing). Their success metrics and authority are fundamentally different
  • Need by phase: Seriously considering establishing a VPoE once the engineering team exceeds 30-50 members is the optimal solution for organizational growth
  • Common failure patterns: Double reporting due to undefined authority, continued micromanagement by the CTO, and the VPoE's lack of technical context are the main causes of failure
  • Importance of collaboration: A structure in which the CTO and VPoE respect each other and collaborate with authority clearly documented via RACI is the key to scaling the organization healthily
  • Outstanding market value: People with CTO and VPoE experience are extremely rare in the freelance market. As external CTOs, technical advisors, or external VPoEs, they can hold multiple high-rate engagements exceeding ¥1-1.5 million per month across several companies

The experience of "scaling technology and organization simultaneously" that you built as a CTO or VPoE can be maximized by going independent as a freelancer.
