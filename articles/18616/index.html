What is AX Consulting? Comparison of 4 Types and How to Choose and Design Contracts

Original title: AXコンサルとは？4タイプ比較と選び方・契約設計

FDX株式会社 · 2026-07-13 · 20 min read

Summary

This article, aimed at executives considering external consultants for company-wide AI transformation (AX), divides Japan's AX consulting market into four types: strategy firms, integrated firms, AI startups, and implementation-partner firms based on the FDE model popularized by Palantir and adopted by OpenAI. It compares their strengths, fee levels, and what remains after contracts end. Citing IDC's forecast that Japan's AI market will grow to about ¥6.9 trillion by 2029 and MIT research that only ~5% of generative AI deployments show measurable P&L impact, it argues failures stem mostly from contract structure. It proposes four contract principles: keeping strategy and implementation unified, defining outcomes via business KPIs, setting deadlines for knowledge transfer, and combining quasi-delegation fees with success-based compensation. It recommends comparing vendors on 18–24 month total cost rather than monthly rates.

Key points

  • Four AX consulting types: strategy, integrated, AI startup, and FDE-style implementation partners.
  • IDC forecasts Japan's AI market reaching ~¥6.9 trillion by 2029 (36% CAGR).
  • MIT research: only ~5% of GenAI deployments achieve measurable P&L impact.
  • Four contract principles: unified strategy-implementation, KPI-based outcomes, deadline-bound knowledge transfer, success fees.
  • Compare vendors on 18–24 month TCO, not monthly rates; knowledge transfer success determines later costs.

What is AI transformation consulting? (3 key points)

  • AI transformation consulting (AX consulting) is a specialized service that externally supports transformation (AX)—redesigning operations, organizations, and decision-making with AI as the premise. It goes beyond support for introducing individual tools, covering everything from envisioning company-wide transformation to implementation and adoption.
  • Market players fall into four types: strategy firms, comprehensive firms, AI ventures, and implementation-support providers, which differ fundamentally in their strengths across project phases and in "what remains in your hands after the contract ends."
  • Most selection failures come down to two things: "ending with a proposal" and "implementing but failing to sustain it"—both of which can be structurally avoided through type selection and contract design. This article provides decision-making material in the form of comparison tables and checklists.

AX consulting refers to AI consulting that supports company-wide transformation—rebuilding operations, organizations, and decision-making with AI as the premise. For guidance on choosing AI consulting in general, including AI adoption for individual operations, see "What is AI consulting?".

In addition, how to choose AI consulting in general (including AI adoption support for individual operations) is organized in "How to choose AI consulting" as 3 categories and 5 questions. As a companion piece, this article focuses on the consulting market that promotes "AX," i.e., company-wide transformation, delving into market structure, type-by-type comparison, contract design, and cost ranges. For the definition of the concept of AX itself, see "What is AX (AI transformation)?".

Three reasons the AX consulting market is expanding

Companies advertising "AX consulting" and "AI transformation support" have increased rapidly since 2024. Three structural changes lie behind this.

First, the rapid expansion of AI investment itself. IDC predicts that Japan's AI market will grow rapidly from 2.3725 trillion yen in 2025 to 6.8897 trillion yen in 2029—roughly 2.9 times—and that the compound annual growth rate (CAGR) for 2024–2029 will reach 36.0%. In a phase where investment amounts increase by an order of magnitude, demand for external services supporting investment decisions and implementation also rises proportionally.

Second, the main battleground of the consulting market has shifted to AI. According to IDC's analysis, the overall domestic consulting market—combining business consulting and IT consulting—will expand from about 1.4554 trillion yen in 2025 to about 2.2897 trillion yen in 2030, with the business consulting market expected to reach about 1.4064 trillion yen in 2030, exceeding 1.4 trillion yen, driven by corporate transformation centered on AI. Both strategy firms and comprehensive firms now operate in a market environment where they cannot win engagements without featuring AX.

Third, the mass occurrence of "PoC dead ends" created demand for implementation support. The MIT NANDA survey "The GenAI Divide: State of AI in Business 2025" reported that only about 5% of corporate generative AI adoptions achieved measurable profit-and-loss impact. In other words, about 95% failed to produce results commensurate with the investment. This mountain of failures has boosted demand for "support that takes you all the way to something that works, not just strategic proposals" (the structural causes of PoC dead ends are detailed in "5 structural reasons AI PoCs fail").

In short, the expansion of the AX consulting market reflects the AI boom, but is also a reaction to the mass failure of the first wave of AI adoption. That is precisely why, in selection, you need to determine not "can they talk about AX" but "can they implement AX all the way to the end."

Structure of the AX consulting market | Four types

Companies claiming to support AX can be organized into four types based on their origins and revenue structures. Every type claims to cover "strategy through implementation," but their actual centers of gravity differ greatly.

Type 1: Strategy firms | Drawing the picture of transformation

Origin: Management strategy consulting. Center of gravity: Formulating AX strategy, prioritizing investment areas, building executive consensus, transformation roadmaps. Strengths: The ability to define AX as a management agenda; materials and logic that can pass a board of directors; cross-industry benchmarks. Structural weakness: Implementation is not their main battlefield, and the implementation phase tends to become a separate contract and separate team (or partner vendors). Even if the strategy's premises collapse at the implementation stage, responsibility falls outside the scope of the strategy contract.

Strategy firms deliver value when executive alignment is lacking and the company has not yet made the company-wide decision of "which businesses and operations to start AX with." Conversely, if you hire a strategy firm when the target operations are already decided, you end up investing in the work of turning what you already know into beautiful materials.

Type 2: Comprehensive firms | Covering the board with staffing

Origin: A composite of general consulting and SI. Focus: Takes on everything from strategy through requirements definition, implementation, and operations/maintenance in bulk with large-scale teams. Strengths: Ability to build teams of dozens to hundreds, core system integration, global reach, project governance. Structural weakness: The larger the team, the bigger the total cost, and the revenue structure creates an incentive to stay involved "longer and wider." Knowledge transfer and insourcing are on the menu, but weak as contractual incentives.

For general firms, in large-scale transformations involving thousands of people across a group, they are often the only viable option in terms of team capacity. The question to ask is "Can the contract explicitly state a deadline for insourcing and transfer criteria?" If you proceed while leaving this vague, external dependency becomes permanent.

Type 3: AI Ventures | Striking with Technology

Origin: Technology startups in generative AI and machine learning. Focus: Implementation of cutting-edge technologies such as LLMs, AI agents, and RAG; applying AI to specific business processes; PoC and prototype development. Strengths: Technical discernment and implementation speed. Ability to keep up with the latest models and frameworks. Small elite teams with high output per unit price. Structural weakness: Business process redesign, organizational change, and company-wide adoption are usually out of scope. Even if they deliver results at individual points, they may not reach the "transformation" of rebuilding business processes and operational structures.

AI ventures can be the best choice for areas where the target business and requirements are clear and the technical difficulty is high. On the other hand, if you leave the question of "how to advance AX company-wide" to a venture, technology-driven partial optimization tends to accumulate.

Type 4: Implementation-Companion Type | Delivering Results in the Field

Origin: The Forward Deployed Engineer (FDE) model. This is the "engineers embed themselves at the customer's site" approach established by Palantir and also adopted by OpenAI. Focus: An end-to-end process from on-site embedded business understanding → business redesign → implementation → production deployment → transfer of operations to insourcing. Strengths: Strategy and implementation continue within the same team, so there is no gap between recommendations and the actual product. Knowledge transfer is at the core of the contract, and the deliverable is an in-house team that can operate independently at the end. Structural weakness: Smaller team size compared to strategy and general firms, not suited to mega-projects requiring hundreds of people. Market recognition is still developing, which can add explanation costs in internal approvals.

The implementation-companion type directly addresses the most common challenge in today's AX market: "getting past the PoC stage to production deployment and insourcing." Details of the FDE model and a structural comparison with SES, SI, and consulting are covered in "FDE vs SES vs SI vs Strategy Consulting".

Contract Design That "Doesn't End with Consulting"

Just as important as type selection is contract design. AX consulting failures are more often caused by a contract structure that allows the engagement to "end with recommendations" or "end with implementation" than by lack of capability. Here are four design principles for carrying a transformation through to the end.

1. Do Not Separate Recommendations from Implementation

If strategy formulation and implementation are split into separate contracts and separate companies, a responsibility gap emerges when the premises of the strategy collapse during implementation. In AX, generative AI technology evolves so quickly that strategic premises from six months ago easily become obsolete, making this separation risk greater than in conventional IT investments.

Design: At the strategy contract stage, confirm that the same team (or an integrated accountability structure) will carry the work through implementation. If separation is unavoidable, include a clause requiring the strategy side to "remain involved in the implementation phase and take responsibility for revising premises."

2. Define Outcomes in Business KPIs

If deliverables are defined as "reports" or "systems," the contract is complete upon delivery, and whether the business actually changed is never questioned. AX outcomes can only be measured by changes in business KPIs (processing time, cases handled, quality metrics, sales contribution).

Design: Specify the KPIs of the target business processes and baseline measurements in the contract's definition of outcomes. For example, "reduce the man-hours from meeting minutes creation to CRM reflection by X%": agree on the measured baseline and target values before starting work.

3. Hold knowledge transfer to a deadline

A verbal promise of "internal capability building support included" will not be executed unless the contract specifies a deadline and criteria. If the transfer remains ambiguous for two years, a structure becomes entrenched in which not a single improvement can be made without the external team.

Design: Write milestones into the contract, such as "after 6 months, the client leads improvements to prompts and tool definitions" and "after 12 months, the client completes the entire operation in-house." Ideally, also define completion criteria for the transfer (e.g., the internal team can run one full improvement cycle on their own). For concrete steps on internal capability building, see "How to Internalize AI Capabilities".

4. Align incentives with a quasi-mandate contract plus success fees

AX is a transformation in which requirements change as you go, making it a poor fit for fixed-scope contracts that assume finalized specifications and acceptance testing. On the other hand, a pure quasi-mandate (time-and-materials) arrangement leaves a dynamic where the vendor's revenue grows the longer the engagement drags on.

Design: Use a quasi-mandate as the base and combine it with success fees tied to achieving business KPIs. Furthermore, if payment conditions are linked to achieving knowledge transfer milestones, the external partner's incentives align toward "deliver results quickly and hand over quickly."

These four principles only work when the client holds the initiative. Conversely, whether a candidate company can accept these terms itself serves as a litmus test of their seriousness. The more a company is optimized for proposal-based or outsourced revenue models, the more likely it is to resist explicitly documenting outcome definitions and transfer deadlines.

Breaking down the implementation-embedded type one level further, FDE talent rates by role fall roughly in the following ranges: senior at ¥3–5 million per month, mid-level at ¥2–3.5 million, and junior at ¥1.5–2.5 million. For a team of 3–5 members, the practical market rate is around ¥10–20 million per month. Details on role-based ranges and budgeting are covered in "FDE Cost and Pricing Guide".

What I want to emphasize here is that comparing single-month costs is nearly meaningless. What should be compared is the total cost over 18–24 months (TCO). Once you include separately contracted implementation after strategic proposals, PoC redos, accumulating maintenance fees, and even structures where "internal capability building never happens and external costs persist," it is not uncommon for the most expensive option and the cheapest option to reverse. As a rule of thumb, companies that succeed at knowledge transfer can dramatically compress external costs from year two onward, while companies that fail at transfer keep paying external costs at the same level as the first year.

Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ)

Q1. How does AX consulting differ from AI consulting?

A. The scope of transformation differs. AI consulting is a broad concept that includes introducing and applying AI to individual business processes, while AX consulting refers to the specialized area of supporting company-wide transformation (AI transformation) that redesigns business processes, organizations, and decision-making with AI as a given. If the challenge can be solved by introducing individual tools, AI consulting suffices; if it requires rebuilding business processes and the organization itself, candidates should be evaluated using the selection criteria for AX consulting.

Q2. What are typical fees for AX consulting?

A. As a rough guide, strategy firms typically charge 3–6 million yen per person per month, while implementation-accompaniment types (FDE type) typically charge 2–4 million yen per person per month combined with success fees. General firms tend to have the largest total cost as lump-sum projects, while AI vendors often come in lower than strategy firms on a per-project basis. However, comparing by monthly unit price alone is inappropriate; you should compare total cost over 18–24 months, including separate implementation contracts, redone PoCs, and the perpetuation of external costs due to failed insourcing.

Q3. Isn't AX consulting just a rehash of DX consulting?

A. It is true that some companies are merely rebranding, and that skepticism is a healthy part of selection. However, AX differs from DX in the agent of transformation: it restructures business on the premise that AI will take on the judgment and generation work that people have performed, so the required capabilities shift to the twin pillars of business redesign and AI implementation. The way to tell is simple: ask whether the company can show examples of rewriting business workflows and KPI changes after production deployment. A company that cannot, whatever its name, lacks the ability to implement transformation.

Q4. Can a major general firm and an implementation-accompaniment type be used together?

A. Yes, and for large enterprises this is a realistic option. A typical division of labor has the general firm handle areas decided by organizational scale, such as core system overhauls or global expansion, while the implementation-accompaniment type handles business redesign with generative AI and agents and launching insourcing. The key caution when combining them is clarifying the boundary of responsibility: unless the division of target processes, data, and success KPIs is documented upfront, attribution of results and failures becomes ambiguous and the two parties undermine each other.

Q5. Are success-fee-based contracts really feasible?

A. Yes, but full success fees are unrealistic; the practical solution is a hybrid combining a base fee under a quasi-delegate contract with success fees linked to business KPIs. As a prerequisite, the KPI baseline of the target processes must be measured before work begins, and the criteria for judging success must be agreed by both parties. Whether a candidate company will accept this design reflects its confidence and revenue structure, so simply asking about success fees is itself an effective selection filter.

Q6. Is there value in mid-sized companies using AX consulting?

A. Yes. In fact, mid-sized companies, which make decisions quickly and can narrow target processes easily, tend to reach results faster than large enterprises. However, the scale advantages of a general firm with hundreds of staff are hard to realize, so a better return on investment comes from focusing on one or two areas with an implementation-accompaniment type or AI vendor, achieving production deployment and a success story in 3–6 months, then expanding horizontally. Rather than finalizing a company-wide vision first, starting with a proof in one area is the realistic approach.

Q7. What does it mean for AX to be "complete"?

A. FDX defines it by five conditions: the business processes have changed; AI is continuously handling part of the work; employees can improve things with AI; results in time, quality, revenue, cost, and so on are being achieved; and improvement can continue in-house even without external vendors.
