How to prepare a customer Quarterly Business Review (QBR)

Original title: 客户季度业务回顾（QBR）怎么准备

FDE Times · 2026-10-24 · 5 min read

Summary

This guide teaches forward deployed engineers (FDEs) how to prepare customer quarterly business reviews (QBRs). Its core argument: a QBR is not a status update—prepare backwards from the decision you need the customer to make, present only 2-3 quantifiable business outcomes using the customer's own metrics, align metric definitions with the customer beforehand, surface risks early with mitigation plans, and end with a joint 90-day action plan. Using an insurance claims AI agent example, it shows how mismatched numbers (25 vs 29 minutes) can derail a meeting, and how a strong QBR doubles as a resume line for FDEs.

Key points

  • Prepare backwards from the decision you need, not a Jira list.
  • Show 2-3 quantifiable outcomes; keep internal metrics in the appendix.
  • Reconcile metric definitions with the customer before the meeting.
  • Raise every risk early, each paired with a mitigation.
  • Close with a joint 90-day plan with named owners and dates.

Key takeaways

  • A business review is not a status report: pick 2-3 quantifiable business outcomes, don't read through tickets one by one.
  • Your numbers must match the numbers the customer has, or the whole meeting will turn into an argument about figures.
  • Raise risks early, each with a mitigation; leave the meeting with at least one decision, one commitment, or one next step.

Three weeks after the system went to production, the account manager messages you: there's a quarterly review with the customer next Thursday, the VP of Operations will attend, and can you prepare the technical part?

Many engineers' first instinct is to open Jira and list everything they've delivered. This is the fastest way to waste an hour of the most important person in the room.

It's at the business review that the customer decides whether to expand the engagement, renew, or quietly let the project sink. For a Forward Deployed Engineer (FDE), it's also the moment your work gets measured: whether the pilot converts to production, whether the customer renews or expands scope, or whether everything stops at the trial.

So preparing the business review is a real FDE skill, not a chore to hand off to the account manager. You know the system better than anyone, and you're the one who can translate it into the language of the person who signs the contract.

A status report is not a business review

The review is not a status sync, nor a technical troubleshooting session. The agenda should be built around the outcomes the customer has achieved, not a list of updates.

You don't need to cover everything either. Pick only 2-3 outcomes that demonstrate real, quantifiable business value; don't lay out every activity the team has done.

The simplest test: the review must produce at least one decision, one commitment, or one clear next step. If none of the three, it's just a slide show.

So the starting point for preparation is completely different. Before opening a spreadsheet, write one sentence: what do you want the customer to agree to by the end of the meeting? Every number and every risk on the slides should serve that sentence.

An example: an agent for processing insurance claims

Suppose you deployed an agent for an insurance company that reads and classifies claim documents. The decision you need: the customer agrees to expand next quarter from the pilot processing team to the remaining teams.

Your first draft has p95 latency, token consumption per claim, and extraction accuracy on the eval set. Those are your numbers, not theirs. The slides should show the metrics that matter most to the customer's business, so you switch to average handling time per claim and rework rate.

Next, compare against last quarter's baseline to show the trend. Suppose your dashboard shows handling time dropped from 32 minutes last quarter to 25 minutes this quarter: the story is clear.

Then you go ask the customer's operations lead and discover their internal report says 29 minutes, because they also count the time claims spend waiting in the queue.

If your slide says 25 and the VP's sheet says 29, the whole meeting will revolve around who's right and who's wrong. Once one number doesn't match, the customer doubts all the others, and the main storyline gets thrown aside. So before walking into the room, you must reconcile the numbers across sources.

The fix is to align on definitions before the meeting, then state clearly on the slides where each metric starts and ends. You can list both numbers and add a line explaining that the gap is queue time. That gap can even become a proposal for next quarter.

Every risk needs a mitigation

In the example above, suppose there are two problems you know about: only part of the pilot team uses the agent daily; and the agent performs poorly on handwritten claim forms. The instinct is to hide them to make the meeting look better. That's wrong.

Risks should be raised early, before they threaten the renewal, and each risk should come with a proposed mitigation. For the usage risk, pair it with a proposal: schedule two on-site training sessions.

For the handwritten claims risk, pair it with another proposal: route those claims to a manual review process while collecting samples to improve the model.

Presented this way, the customer sees that everything is under control. If they discover it themselves, they'll wonder what else you're hiding.

A one-page pre-read

Send the pre-read about 48 hours in advance so the meeting isn't spent re-explaining background. Time together should be used for discussion and decisions. Here's a template you can use for the claims example:

SectionContentExample (hypothetical)
Decision neededOne sentence, at the topExpand to the remaining processing teams next quarter
2-3 outcomesCustomer metrics, compared to last quarter, with definitions statedHandling time per claim, rework rate
Time to valueFrom engagement start to a system running and actually being usedWeeks from project kickoff to daily use by the pilot team
RisksOne line per risk, with mitigationLow usage, paired with on-site training
Next 90 daysPriorities, owners, and dates for both sidesCustomer designates one point of contact per new team

The time-to-value line is a metric for evaluating an FDE: from engagement start to a system that runs and is being used. "Being used" is the key phrase. A deployed system nobody uses hasn't created value yet.

End the meeting with commitments from both sides

Use the last part of the meeting to agree on the next 90 days: priorities, owners, and dates, for both sides, not just yours. Ideally, both sides bring a joint success plan documenting shared goals, milestones to measure, and risks.

Close with a joint action plan, and schedule the next review before anyone stands up to leave.

If the expansion requires the customer to grant data access for new teams, that must be its own line with a specific owner on the customer side and a specific date.

Mistakes that sink a review

One of the most common mistakes is putting internal metrics like latency and eval scores on the main slides, when the audience cares about their own team's cost and time. Those numbers belong in the appendix, brought out only if someone asks.

The second is not reconciling numbers with the customer before the meeting (like the 25 and 29 minutes above), hiding risks, or raising risks without mitigations. The worst is ending with "let's discuss this further," which means the meeting decided nothing.

Turn the review into a line on your resume

The claims example is also a template for a strong resume line. Instead of "communicated well with customers," write: prepared and led quarterly business reviews for customer leadership, aligned metric definitions with the operations team, and the review led to expansion to the remaining teams.

In interviews, the 25 vs. 29 minutes story is worth telling in detail. It shows you understand both the data and the person across the table who owns that data.

Exercise: build a 90-day table for your project

Take the project you're working on and draw a four-column table: task, owner, which side, completion date. Write at least three rows for your side and at least three for the customer side, each with a real name and a specific date.

If you can't fill in a customer-side name for a row, that's the first risk to raise at the next review, along with a proposed mitigation.

The next time the account manager asks you to "prepare the technical part," don't open Jira first. Ask them instead: what decision do we need the customer to make?
