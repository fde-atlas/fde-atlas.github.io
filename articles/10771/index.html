FDE: The Hottest Role in AI Implementation, and Possibly the Shortest-Lived One

Original title: FDE：AI 落地最火的岗位，也可能是最短暂的岗位

53AI · 2026-08-31 · 9 min read

Summary

Drawing on his IBM consulting-era experience, the author argues that today's hot FDE role—OpenAI and Anthropic are hiring many—exists because large models create a capability gap with messy enterprise realities. But like consultants two decades ago, FDEs face knowledge diffusion from three directions: smarter models, increasingly flexible software, and enterprises internalizing their own know-how. Companies must first clarify their own data, workflows, and rules (one map, one table, one process), which cannot be outsourced. The best FDEs should solve unknown problems and convert patterns into products, so the same problem never needs an FDE again.

Key points

  • OpenAI and Anthropic are hiring FDEs at scale; the role is real and expensive.
  • FDE value comes from the gap between models and enterprise reality.
  • Like consultants, FDEs will lose premium as knowledge diffuses.
  • Models, flexible software, and enterprise learning squeeze FDE value.
  • Great FDEs should turn one-off problems into product capabilities.

Full text

At a recent CIO forum, we discussed a role that is extremely hot in Silicon Valley right now: the FDE, Forward Deployed Engineer.

FDEs have indeed been on fire these past two years. OpenAI has built a dedicated Forward Deployed Engineering team, and its careers page lists a large number of FDE positions in healthcare, legal, government, and various regions; within Anthropic's Applied AI team, a series of similar roles such as FDE and Technical Deployment Lead have also emerged. OpenAI's definition of the role is quite typical: go deep into the customer, understand the problem, work all the way from technical solutions and system design to production deployment, and then feed the issues discovered on-site back to the product and model teams. Anthropic is also continuously expanding similar teams.

So I don't think the FDE is a "fake need." On the contrary, today it is a real need, and an expensive one.

But the more I see FDEs taking off, the more it reminds me of another profession from more than twenty years ago: the consultant.

And I happened to live through that era.

When I worked at IBM early in my career, consultants were a highly valuable profession.

At that time, enterprises were rapidly informatizing, but few people had real experience with global IT builds. How to plan a large conglomerate's ERP, how to design a global IT architecture, how to run project management, how to build data systems, how to restructure business processes—this kind of know-how was extremely scarce in every country.

So back then, enterprises were willing to pay high prices to bring in companies like IBM and Accenture.

On the surface, enterprises were buying a Consultant; in reality, they were buying a huge Know-how Gap.

You know it and I don't; you've seen it at dozens of companies worldwide, and I'm doing it for the first time. That knowledge gap is itself enormous business value.

But what happened later?

Enterprises' own CIOs, CTOs, and IT teams became more and more mature. People had done SAP, done Oracle, built data warehouses, moved to the cloud, and stepped in countless digital transformation pitfalls. The methodologies once held only by a few international consulting firms gradually became common knowledge across the industry.

Consulting, of course, didn't disappear, but the words "consultant" themselves became less and less valuable. A lot of work gradually shifted from Consulting to Implementation, and then from Implementation to Outsourcing.

Behind this lies a very simple rule:

Any profession that earns a high premium through knowledge gaps and capability gaps will eventually face the diffusion of knowledge.

When I look at FDEs now, I often get that familiar feeling.

Why do we suddenly need so many FDEs today?

It's not because enterprises suddenly forgot how to use software over the past few decades, but because the emergence of large models has created another huge capability chasm.

Large model demos are impressive, but once you actually enter an enterprise, you immediately run into completely different problems: Where is the data? How do you get permissions? How does the business process actually run? What does this field mean? How do systems call each other? How do you verify when results are wrong? When finance, production, or procurement is involved, who approves? Who is responsible when something goes wrong?

So on one side there is an extremely capable but highly probabilistic large model, and on the other side an enterprise that has been running for twenty years, with hundreds of systems and a large number of legacy processes and organizational rules.

In between lies a huge Gap.

The FDE stands right in that Gap.

During the CIO forum discussion, one line stuck with me: "When model capability falls short, engineering makes up the difference." Some people on-site did directly raise the question: is the FDE just a rebranding of the existing delivery and consulting market with a new name and new tools? The discussion kept circling back to Workflow, well-defined inputs and outputs, deterministic execution, and how to abstract one-off engineering experience into reusable Building Blocks.

This is exactly why FDEs are so valuable today.

You throw an excellent engineer into a customer site—someone who understands models, can write code, and can communicate with business people. They help the customer connect data, connect APIs, tune models, write Skills, build Workflows, debug Agents, and finally push an AI demo that originally couldn't run into the production environment.

Of course that's valuable.

But the question is:

How long can this value last?

Today, many CIOs facing difficulties in AI adoption easily arrive at a conclusion: do I not have enough AI engineers? Should I bring in some even stronger FDEs?

But anyone who has done real enterprise projects knows that many problems are not technical problems at all. Suppose tomorrow you send ten of the world's best FDEs to an enterprise—the first thing they still have to ask is:

Which system's inventory data is actually accurate? Above what purchase amount does approval become required? Why are overseas subsidiaries' processes different? After a customer complaint, who exactly should it be routed to? What kind of result counts as the Agent having done the job right?

If the enterprise itself can't answer these questions clearly, no large model will help, and even the most expensive FDEs can only guess alongside the enterprise.

So lately I increasingly like to use a very simple formulation to describe what enterprises should actually prepare for AI:

One diagram, one table, one process.

"One diagram" means drawing out the enterprise's real business world: what the actual relationships are between customers, products, orders, equipment, and the organization. Today it's popular to talk about Ontology—essentially, it's about telling the AI: what your enterprise world actually looks like.

"One table" is not simply listing a few databases, but truly explaining the data clearly: how metrics are defined, what fields mean, which system is the Source of Truth, which data is trustworthy, and which data can be used by Agents. Behind this is really Data Governance and the Semantic Layer.

"One process" means explaining clearly how things actually get done: from input, to judgment, to decision, to execution, to approval, to exception handling, and then to result verification. Only when this Workflow is truly mapped out can an Agent move from "being able to chat" to "being able to do work."

Here comes the question:

Can these things be outsourced to FDEs?

Of course you can hire consultants to help, have FDEs help you sort things out, or engage service providers to build systems for you.

But ultimately, it cannot be outsourced.

Because what an enterprise truly cannot outsource is not Python, not Prompts, not even Agents.

What truly cannot be outsourced is: what your data means, why your processes run the way they do, what your rules are, and ultimately what counts as "getting the job done right."

These things inherently belong to the enterprise itself.

This is actually the thing many enterprises today get most easily backwards.

Now, as soon as people see Agents, they start looking for models, hiring engineers, buying platforms, and running PoCs. But once an Agent truly enters production, what it faces is not a clean problem, but an entire enterprise.

An enterprise has its own language.

What counts as a "core customer," what counts as a "valid order," under what circumstances a refund can be given, under what circumstances manual approval is mandatory, which data can be shown to whom—none of these are generic knowledge from the internet.

They exist in ERPs, in Excel files, in SOPs, and in large part in the minds of veteran employees.

So the real difficulty of the Agentic Enterprise is not just having a stronger brain, but building an understandable Enterprise World Model for that brain.

Data, Ontology, Workflow, Governance—these all sound like old terms from the previous generation of enterprise software.

But in the Agent era, they matter more than ever.

In the past, when a human operated the software, much of the semantics lived in a person's head; today, if you want Agents to autonomously operate enterprise systems, you have to make explicit the Context that used to be hidden in people's minds.

From this perspective, I would even say:

The real AI infrastructure of the future enterprise is not just models, but the degree to which the enterprise itself can be understood by machines.

And no single FDE can accomplish this for a company.

Because today's FDE is caught right in the middle of three converging forces.

The first force comes from models.

Today, much of an FDE's value exists because large models aren't smart enough yet, so engineers are needed to write code, tune Prompts, build RAG, write Tools, and Debug Agents.

But over the past few years, these are exactly the areas where large models have improved fastest.

A Workflow/Loop that an engineer spent days writing yesterday can now be largely handled by Coding Agents like Codex and Claude Code; where Prompts once needed constant human adjustment, models are now starting to manage Context themselves; today engineers still need to observe why an Agent fails, but in the future, Agents will increasingly do their own Evaluation, Reflection, and Debugging.

In other words:

Every time a model upgrades, it eats away a piece of the FDE's "cleverness."

The second force comes from software itself.

Why does it take a person on-site at the customer to change so many things today?

A big reason is: today's software is too rigid.

The logic of traditional SaaS is that I design the product first, and customers adapt to my product. If your processes don't match my design, you either change your business, do Customization, or bring in Professional Services.

The FDE hasn't fundamentally changed this logic.

It has merely upgraded the ordinary implementation consultant of the past into a smarter engineer who understands AI better and can write code.

In other words:

The FDE uses a "flexible human" to compensate for "rigid software."

But I believe the most important change in next-generation software is precisely that the software itself is starting to become flexible.

I've long been thinking about the concept of Flexible Software. Future software will likely be "harder at the core, softer at the edges."

The underlying Security, Governance, Permission, Data Model, Audit, and Runtime must become increasingly deterministic; but the outer Workflow, UI, Integration, Automation, and Business Logic can increasingly be dynamically generated by Agents based on the enterprise's Context.

So the old path was:

Business raises a requirement → FDE understands it → writes code → tests → deploys.

In the future, it will increasingly become:

Business expresses a requirement → Agent understands the Context → calls the enterprise Ontology → generates a Workflow → Harness validates → executes → feeds back.

As the Agent Stack matures, the large amount of Glue Code, Integration, and Workflow Adaptation done manually by FDEs today will gradually become capabilities of the software product itself.

So:

Every layer of the Agent Stack that matures also eats away a piece of the FDE's "engineering."

The third force comes from the enterprises themselves.

As enterprises gradually complete their own Data Governance, Ontology, and Workflow organization, the Know-how once held by external FDEs will gradually settle back inside the enterprise.

This is very similar to what consultants experienced twenty years ago.

At first, I knew nothing, so you were expensive.

Later, I learned it myself, and your price naturally came down.

So the FDE today stands in a very delicate position:

Above, large models are getting smarter.

Below, Flexible Software and the Agent Stack are maturing.

And on the other side, enterprises themselves understand AI, data, and processes better and better.

Three forces are simultaneously squeezing the value space of today's FDE.

So I don't believe enterprises will stop needing FDEs entirely in the future.

On the contrary, I believe the best FDEs will remain extremely important.

It's just that their job should not be integrating Salesforce for Customer A, modifying approval workflows for Customer B, and generating a new set of Apache SeaTunnel Code for Customer C.

If a SaaS company has to add dozens of FDEs for every hundred new customers, then no matter how it packages itself, the business model will ultimately look more and more like a technical services company.

What truly excellent FDEs should solve are unknown problems.

Enter an industry that hasn't yet been transformed by AI, discover a new scenario the product doesn't yet understand, help the first batch of customers get it running, and then find Patterns from that on-site experience.

The most important next step is not to keep hiring more FDEs, but:

FDE → Pattern → Product → Platform.

Abstract one-off customer problems into product capabilities, and distill one person's Know-how into Building Blocks that all customers can use.

Then the FDE goes off to solve the next problem that has never been solved before.

In this sense:

The goal of an excellent FDE should not be to prove that customers will always need FDEs, but to ensure that the same problem never needs an FDE again the next time.

This is actually something that has happened repeatedly in the software industry for decades.

Consulting solves the first problem; products solve the thousandth.

So when I see Silicon Valley hiring FDEs at scale today, I don't think it's a mistake.

It precisely shows that AI is moving from Demos into real enterprises, and whenever a major technology enters the early stage of an industry, it takes a lot of people to fill the gap between the technology and reality.

Twenty years ago, these people were called Consultants.

Ten years ago, they might have been called Solution Architects, Customer Engineers, or Professional Services.

Today, they're called FDEs.

The names keep changing, but the laws governing how the tech industry develops have not.

What is truly worth discussing is not which name sounds sexier, but:

Can the problems that today must be solved by humans become products tomorrow?

If not, then the FDE will ultimately just be more expensive tech outsourcing.

If so, then every pitfall the FDE stumbles into today should become part of tomorrow's Flexible Software and Agent Stack.

I increasingly believe that the real software revolution in the Agent era is not simply putting a Copilot on top of SaaS, nor assigning each customer a few more expensive engineers.

It should move in another direction:

In the past, people adapted to software; today, FDEs help enterprises adapt to AI; in the future, software will actively adapt to enterprises.

But before that, there is one thing that no model, no software, and no FDE can replace:

Enterprises must first be able to articulate themselves clearly.

Your data, your processes, your rules, your organization, your experience.

Only when these things truly settle into an enterprise world that AI can understand will models have a brain, agents have context, and software truly possess flexibility.

By that time, the FDE we chase so eagerly today may still exist.

It just will no longer stand between every customer and the software.

Because truly mature technology will ultimately productize human experience and softwareize human repetitive labor.

That is the mission the FDE should ultimately fulfill.
