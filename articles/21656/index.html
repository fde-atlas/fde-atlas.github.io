Summary

This long Japanese article by Shohei Tokumitsu, CEO of DeFactory, explains what an IT architect is, how the role differs from SE, tech lead, and PM positions, and how senior engineers can move into high-rate freelance work. It outlines four specializations (application, infrastructure/cloud, data, enterprise) with monthly rate ranges of roughly ¥800k to ¥2.5M+, and argues that AI code generation lowers implementation costs while raising the market value of architects who make structural decisions. It closes with career roadmaps, certifications, and networking tactics, plus promotion of DeFactory's freelance platform.

Key points

  • IT architects translate business requirements into system-wide design, unlike SEs or PMs.
  • Four specializations: application, infrastructure/cloud, data, and enterprise architecture.
  • Freelance rates range from ¥800k to ¥2.5M+ monthly; enterprise architects earn the most.
  • AI coding tools raise, rather than reduce, the value of architectural judgment.
  • Top-rate deals flow through networks and personal branding, not public job listings.

Conclusion: An IT Architect is "an engineer responsible for interpreting business requirements and designing the overall structure (architecture) of a system." While an SE "translates specified requirements into an implementable form," an IT Architect decides "what should be built in the first place and how it should be structured" between management and the field. The scope of decision-making and the weight of responsibility are the biggest differences.

Conclusion: The essence of an IT Architect is that of a "translator." They translate ambiguous, dynamic business challenges into an implementable and operable system blueprint through comparing technical options and making trade-off judgments. They are the ones responsible for "the highest-value decisions" made before any code is written.

The primary responsibilities of an IT Architect are as follows:

  • Interpreting business requirements: Extracting system requirements from corporate strategy and business processes
  • Designing the architecture: Defining the overall system structure, component division, and data flows
  • Technology selection: Choosing programming languages, frameworks, cloud services, and middleware
  • Defining non-functional requirements: Setting target values for availability, performance, scalability, maintainability, and security
  • Making trade-off judgments: Balancing cost, speed, quality, and future scalability
  • Establishing design guidelines: Developing design principles and coding standards for the development team to follow
  • Architecture reviews: Continuously verifying whether the code being implemented aligns with the design intent

In particular, "defining non-functional requirements" and "making trade-off judgments" are areas of human judgment that AI cannot replace. Even as AI code generation evolves, decisions like "Does this system require 99.99% availability, or is 99.9% sufficient?" will remain with human IT Architects because they require an understanding of business context and accountability.

Conclusion: IT Architects are responsible for "the quality of the system itself," PMs for "project progress management," and SEs for "implementing individual features." While they work side by side on the same project, the metrics (KPIs) by which they are evaluated are completely different.

The scarcity of talent capable of simultaneously overseeing multiple SaaS platforms, legacy core systems, generative AI, data infrastructure, and security requirements creates a structure that drives up rates.

Conclusion: IT architects fall into four major specialty areas: "Application," "Infrastructure/Cloud," "Data," and "Enterprise." Each differs in required skills, layers involved, and typical rates. It is important to choose your specialty based on your background and the career you want to pursue.

Conclusion: An application architect is a specialist who "designs the software structure of business applications." With responsibilities such as microservice decomposition, domain-driven design (DDD), and API gateway design, this is the architect role closest to the code level. Monthly rates are mostly in the range of 900,000 to 1.3 million yen.

The main design targets of an application architect are as follows.

  • Component decomposition: Defining domain boundaries, choosing between microservices vs. modular monolith
  • Applying design patterns: Selecting CQRS, event sourcing, Saga pattern, etc.
  • API design: Choosing between REST, GraphQL, and gRPC; API Gateway architecture
  • Frontend structure: Design choices among BFF, SPA, SSR, and PWA
  • Test strategy: Allocation of responsibilities for unit, integration, and E2E tests; test pyramid design

This position is an "architect closely involved with code" and retains the character of a working engineer. It is a highly popular next step for tech leads.

Conclusion: An infrastructure/cloud architect is a specialist who "designs the foundation that keeps systems running stably and able to scale when needed." Centered on cloud design for AWS, Azure, GCP, and the like, they handle networking, security, and cost optimization end to end. Monthly rates are mostly 1 to 1.5 million yen.

Conclusion: A data architect is a specialist who "integrates data scattered across an enterprise and designs a foundation capable of supporting analytics and AI use." Expertise in data lakehouses, ETL/ELT, data governance, and master data management (MDM) is required. Monthly rates are mostly in the range of 1 to 1.6 million yen, and this is one of the fields gaining the most attention in the generative AI era.

The typical design responsibilities of a data architect are as follows.

  • Data platform architecture: Designing the composition of data lakes, DWHs, and data marts
  • Data pipelines: Orchestration design for ETL/ELT/Reverse ETL
  • Data modeling: Selecting 3NF, Dimensional Modeling, or Data Vault
  • Data governance: Maintaining data catalogs, lineage, and quality rules
  • Master data management: Designing integration and synchronization of customer and product master data
  • Access control: Designing row- and column-level security and PII protection
  • AI/ML integration: Integrating feature stores, vector databases, and data platforms for RAG

This position is closely linked to production operation needs for generative AI (RAG), and its market value rose sharply in 2025-2026. Advancing from data engineer is the standard route, but the key differentiator is whether you can go as far as data "design," "quality," and "governance."

Conclusion: An enterprise architect (EA) is the highest-level architect who "integrates business strategy with IT strategy and designs the optimization of an enterprise's overall IT investment." The scope is not individual systems but the company's entire IT portfolio. Monthly rates are in the range of 1.5 to over 2.5 million yen, and the role often resembles an external CIO aide or IT strategy advisor.

Conclusion: The three core skills common to high-rate IT architects are: (1) "the ability to oversee a broad range of technology stacks," (2) "the ability to define non-functional requirements and judge trade-offs," and (3) "communication skills that bridge management and the front lines." Only when all three are in place can a monthly rate exceeding 1.5 million yen be achieved.

Conclusion: What IT architects need is not "deep expertise in a specific technology" but "the ability to oversee a broad range of technical domains at a certain level of resolution." Not depending on a single language or a single cloud, but being able to compare multiple options and articulate trade-offs, is a prerequisite for trust.

Conclusion: The true value of an IT architect is tested in "trade-off judgments on non-functional requirements." Increasing availability raises costs, improving performance increases complexity, and ensuring scalability slows initial development — the ability to instantly judge this multi-dimensional optimization problem according to business context is the most highly valued capability.

Typical trade-offs in non-functional requirements are summarized below.

Trade-off axisExample 1Example 2
Availability vs. cost99.99% uptime + 1 million yen/month99.9% uptime + 300,000 yen/month
Performance vs. complexityMulti-tier caching + complex invalidation processingSimple configuration + somewhat slower responses
Scalability vs. development speedMicroservices + 3-month delayMonolith + production launch in 1 month
Security vs. UXMandatory MFA + increased drop-off ratePassword only + convenience
Consistency vs. availability (CAP theorem)Strong consistency + reduced availabilityEventual consistency + always-on operation

These trade-offs are inherently "without a single correct answer." Even for the same "web service," the right design choices are completely opposite for a financial service versus an internal tool. Professional IT architects have the judgment framework to immediately say, "For your company's business, you should choose this option, for the following reasons..."

Conclusion: The third essential skill for IT architects is communication ability that "translates bidirectionally between the languages of management and front-line engineers." Explaining technology selection rationale to management in terms of "return on investment," and conveying business requirements to the front lines as "design constraints" — this translation ability is the essential value of an architect who can move an organization.

The specific behavioral traits that make up this skill are summarized below.

  • Dialogue with management: Able to discuss technical risks along three axes: "business continuity risk, compliance risk, and lost opportunities"
  • Instructions to engineers: Able to translate abstract requirements into design constraints (e.g., "API responses must be within 200ms")
  • Negotiations with vendors: Able to break down quote justifications technically and spot unreasonably high estimates
  • Stakeholder coordination: Able to convert conflicting demands from multiple departments into discussions about priorities
  • Accountability in failure: Able to calmly explain the situation, impact, and countermeasures to management when trouble occurs
  • Documentation skills: Visualization ability to drive decision-making with a single architecture diagram

In particular, the last item — "an architecture diagram that drives decisions on a single page" — is where an IT architect can show their skill. The ability to compress complex system configurations into a level of detail management can understand in 10 minutes, while clearly indicating the decision points, becomes an intangible asset cultivated over years of career.

Conclusion: With the spread of generative AI in 2025–2026, the design target of IT architects has undergone a paradigm shift from "applications used by humans" to "systems where AI agents and humans collaborate." New domains such as RAG, agents, evaluation harnesses, and governance are being incorporated as essential knowledge for IT architects.

Conclusion: Architecture trends have evolved from "monolith → microservices → AI agent-integrated." As of 2026, design premised on orchestration by AI agents, in addition to human API calls, is becoming standardized.

In AI agent-integrated architectures, traditional "deterministic API calls" are combined with "non-deterministic processing where the LLM assesses the situation and selects tools." This introduces new design concerns such as "fallbacks on failure," "hallucination countermeasures," and "cost management." Conclusion: When incorporating RAG or external LLM APIs into business systems, security and data governance design different from the past is required. Three new design principles—"don't send sensitive data to external APIs," "ensure the reliability of LLM responses," and "maintain access control consistency"—are now demanded of IT architects.

Let's organize the new design principles of the LLM era.

  • Data minimization principle: Minimize data sent to LLMs and mask PII and sensitive information in advance
  • Access control consistency: Design RAG search results so they never exceed the user's original access rights
  • Model provider separation: Avoid dependence on a single LLM vendor and design fallbacks across multiple models
  • Prompt injection countermeasures: Clearly separate system prompts from user input
  • Response evaluation harness: A mechanism to continuously evaluate the quality of LLM responses in production
  • Cost cap controls: Request and token limit design to prevent runaway billing incidents
  • Logging and auditing: Tracking who invoked the LLM, when, and with which prompts

These new principles are distinct from best practices of traditional web application design. IT architects who can design LLM architectures are extremely rare as of 2026, and projects close even in the range of 1.8 to 2.5 million yen per month. Detailed skill sets for LLM engineering are covered individually in the Complete Guide for LLM Engineers.

Conclusion: With the spread of AI code generation tools such as GitHub Copilot, Claude Code, and Cursor, the marginal cost of "implementation" is dropping rapidly. Meanwhile, the IT architect's judgment on "what to build" and "how to structure it" is hard to replace with AI, and its market value is actually rising.

What cannot be delegated to AI is "judgment that carries responsibility," "interpreting industry context," "building consensus with management," and "accountability when things fail." All of these are core duties of IT architects, and their importance is actually increasing with the spread of AI. For engineers who want to boost their career's "AI resilience," shifting to IT architect is one strategic option.

Conclusion: IT architect is one of the roles where "market value is hardest to lose and rates keep rising even in the AI era" throughout an engineering career. Multiple career paths are available: stepping up from senior engineer, going independent as a freelancer, expanding into technical advisory or external CIO support, and leveraging global certifications.

Conclusion: Stepping up from tech lead to IT architect requires deliberately accumulating, over 3 to 5 years, experience that expands your perspective from "designing individual systems" to "company-wide design." The standard path is to broaden the scope of your design judgment through internal side projects, leading PoCs, sharing knowledge at external meetups, and publishing books.

You need to make a conscious effort to create touchpoints with management (CTO candidate programs, requesting transfers to internal DX promotion offices, publishing on technical blogs, etc.).

For freelancing as an IT architect, what matters most is "choosing your projects during the first six months to a year." If you can't command an appropriate rate on your first project, your subsequent rate tends to be anchored lower. Conclusion: IT architect projects paying over 1.5 to 2 million yen per month circulate almost exclusively through "personal networks" or "specific agents." They don't appear in publicly listed job openings, and "nominatability" and "personal brand" are the deciding factors for rates. Building a personal brand over the medium to long term is the key to securing ultra-high rates.

Here is a summary of sales strategies for winning ultra-high-rate projects.

  • Building a personal brand: Gaining recognition through technical blogs, books, conference talks, and OSS contributions
  • Participating in industry communities: Ongoing involvement in JAWS-UG, Cloud Native Days, and similar events
  • Maintaining relationships with past clients: In many cases, rates rise when clients re-engage you 3 to 5 years later
  • Selecting agents: Narrow your choice down to 2 or 3 agents that are strong in the high-rate segment
  • Flexibility in working arrangements: Being able to propose working 2-3 days a week plus juggling multiple clients
  • Ability to propose advisory contracts: Being able to present contract formats such as "advisory (1-2 days per week)"
  • Clarifying your specialty: Expertise defined along two axes, such as "finance × AI" or "manufacturing × IoT"

Positions such as "external CIO assistant" or "technical advisor" often take the form of 500,000 to 1 million yen per month across multiple companies, and total annual income can reach the 30 to 50 million yen range. In reality, "multiple medium- to long-term advisory contracts" makes it easier to achieve both income stability and high rates than "a single project at 2 million yen per month."

An IT architect is a "designer who translates business requirements into the optimal solution for the entire system." While adjacent to SEs, PMs, and tech leads, they play a clearly distinct role. There are four specialized domains—"application," "infrastructure/cloud," "data," and "enterprise"—each with different rate ranges, required skills, and career paths.

What high-rate IT architects have in common are the following three core skills.

  • The ability to survey a broad range of technology stacks: Not dependence on a specific technology, but the ability to compare multiple options
  • The ability to judge trade-offs among non-functional requirements: Multi-dimensional optimization of availability, performance, scalability, and maintainability
  • Communication skills that bridge the languages of management and the field: Two-way translation between return on investment and design constraints

In the era of generative AI, while the "marginal cost of implementation" is falling, the market value of IT architects—who decide "what should be built"—is instead soaring. If you go independent as a freelancer, this can be considered one of the most economically rational choices in an engineer's career, with ultra-high-rate projects of ¥1.5–2.5 million per month and, by working with multiple companies, annual incomes of ¥30–50 million within reach.
