How Forward Deployed Engineers Handle Customer Expansion Requests and Upsell Signals

Original title: FDE 如何处理客户扩展需求与增购信号

FDE Times · 2026-11-07 · 5 min read

Summary

This guide addresses how forward deployed engineers should respond when customers ask about new use cases mid-deployment, using a customs-form question during a demo as its running example. The core advice: recognize the expansion signal, respond without committing, and route it to whoever owns expansion—which varies by company. Firecrawl's job posting makes expansion an explicit FDE duty, while Chad Horenfeldt assigns it mostly to CSMs, and Underdog.io defines FDEs as post-sale technical roles without revenue targets. The article offers a three-step response script, a five-line signal log template posted in shared channels, and routing rules (usage growth to CSMs, new buyers to AEs). It warns against three failure modes: hoarding signals, becoming a de facto salesperson, and leaving no handoff records before departing, and suggests interview questions to clarify reporting lines and expansion ownership.

Key points

  • Expansion ownership differs by company; ask directly in interviews.
  • FDEs should acknowledge customer questions without technical or pricing commitments.
  • Write a five-line signal log in a shared channel the same day.
  • Route usage-growth signals to CSMs, new buyers to AEs, new use cases to both.
  • Avoid hoarding signals, acting as sales, and skipping handoff documentation.

The key is not to sell, but to respond without committing, then hand the signal to whoever owns expansion—and that person differs from company to company.

Key Takeaways

  • At companies like Firecrawl, the FDE is seen as the person closest to the customer and is expected to proactively surface expansion opportunities
  • The FDE's job is to document technical evidence; pricing belongs to whoever carries the revenue quota
  • Every company assigns expansion to a different person—ask directly in the interview

Imagine you are in week three of a deployment. The invoice-extraction data pipeline is finally running reliably on real data. The customer's operations lead watches the demo, nods, and asks: "Can this read customs declarations?"

That question could lead to a bigger contract—or to a commitment no one at your company has actually agreed to. Neither the Account Executive (AE) nor the Customer Success Manager (CSM) is on this demo, so the Forward Deployed Engineer (FDE) has to speak first.

The skill required here is not selling. You need to recognize the signal, respond without committing, and route the signal to the right path. Handle it poorly, and the AE loses the opportunity, the CSM feels their work is being poached, and the customer hears three different stories from three people.

Who actually owns expansion signals?

Honestly, it depends on the company. Firecrawl's FDE job posting states explicitly that customer expansion is the FDE's responsibility: identifying opportunities to grow usage, add use cases, and expand contracts. The posting also says the FDE doesn't need to wait for a CSM to flag upsell opportunities, because there, the FDE is viewed as the person closest to the customer.

Chad Horenfeldt, a leader in the customer success field, describes the opposite model: most of the work on renewals and uncovering expansion opportunities belongs to the CSM.

In his view, the deeper someone goes into building and designing solutions, the further they drift from revenue. He also offers a hypothesis about the friction involved: perhaps sales feels that customer success is encroaching on its territory.

Underdog.io defines the FDE as a post-sale technical role—not responsible for closing deals and not carrying a revenue quota. The FDE is measured on three things: whether the customer is actually using the product, whether the product solves a business problem, and whether the customer renews.

The last two overlap almost entirely with customer success work. As a result, all three roles encounter the same signal, but each is measured differently.

How should you answer the customer in the meeting?

Back to the customs declaration question. A good answer has three steps: acknowledge the question, learn more about the problem itself, then make clear who will follow up on scope. Sample script:

"That's a great question. Can I ask a few follow-ups: roughly how many customs declarations do you process per month? Who handles them today? Where do errors usually occur? We'll send you a technical feasibility note this week. For scope and contract, Lan from our customer team will reach out to you."

This script deliberately avoids three sentences that slip out easily: "Sure, that's easy," "That shouldn't cost extra," and "I'll try it over the weekend." The first is a technical commitment made before seeing any data.

The second is a pricing commitment, which is not the FDE's to make. The third turns a business opportunity into free out-of-scope work.

Writing the signal record: five lines is enough

Write a short note the same day and post it in the shared channel for this customer, not in a DM to one person. Using the customs declaration example, a five-line template might look like this:

谁提出的：运营负责人，X 日演示时
问题：报关单目前靠人工处理；数量由客户自行估算
现有证据：发票数据管道在真实数据上运行，运营团队每天都在用
技术可行性：需要报关单样本才能评估，尚未承诺任何事
已告知客户：AE 会就范围联系对方

The "customer has been informed" line matters most. It tells the AE and CSM what the customer is waiting for, so no one accidentally contradicts you on the next call.

Hand it to the AE or the CSM?

If the company has no explicit rule, classify by the nature of the signal, as shown in the chart at the top. If the customer is using more within the same use case, or shows signs of not renewing, the CSM leads.

If a new department or new buyer appears, the AE leads. For a new use case from the same buyer, the two decide together. This is a reasonable division of labor, not an iron rule—confirm it with the AE and CSM for that account.

The new-buyer case matches Firecrawl's description: the FDE works with the growth team to show technical buyers how the product solves their problems. The FDE appears in commercial conversations, but as the person providing evidence, not the one setting the price.

Mistakes that leave the FDE squeezed from both sides

The first mistake is sitting on the signal. Imagine an FDE spots additional customer needs, quietly builds a trial version on the side, and the AE finds out three weeks later. By then, the customer is used to getting results for free, and the contract negotiation becomes awkward.

The second mistake is the opposite: turning yourself into a salesperson. Perspective AI's playbook warns that when FDEs report to the Chief Revenue Officer (CRO), compensation, OKRs, and politics inside the go-to-market organization push them to pursue billable hours at the customer rather than building reusable parts of the product.

Every new request becomes a one-off project, and the product learns nothing.

The third mistake is forgetting that you will eventually leave. The same playbook recommends that most FDE projects be handed off to customer success and product engineering within 120 days of launch. Signals you didn't document disappear with you when you leave the account, and the CSM who takes over starts from zero.

Read the job description to figure out which model you're entering

KORE1 notes that some companies place FDEs under the CRO's organization, so reporting lines vary by company. It also observes that job postings mixing the titles Sales Engineer, Solutions Engineer, and FDE are almost always FDE roles whose responsibilities are written by people in the sales organization.

So ask three questions in the interview: Who do you report to? Who owns the expansion metric? Which channel do FDE-discovered signals go through? If the job description contains language like Firecrawl's "drive customer expansion," prepare a specific story: a time you spotted a new customer need and handed it to the commercial side.

Don't write "supported sales" on your resume. Write it in order: which use case you identified, what evidence you had, who you handed it to, and what the outcome was. People hiring FDEs want to see that you understand where the boundaries are, not just that you're enthusiastic.

The next time a customer asks "Can this do X?", you have only a few seconds to respond. Use those seconds to learn more about the problem, not to make a commitment.
