What's the Difference Between FDE and SES? Comparing Contract Types, Chain of Command, and Accountability for Results

Original title: FDEとSESの違いとは？契約類型・指揮命令・成果責任で比較

FDE総研 · 2026-07-23 · 11 min read

Summary

This article from FDE Soken compares Forward Deployed Engineers (FDEs) with SES (System Engineer Services) across contract type, command authority, and outcome responsibility. Its core argument: FDEs sell solved problems as outcomes, while SES sell billable hours. Citing Palantir's blog framing (regular engineers deliver one capability to many customers; FDEs deliver many capabilities to one customer), the piece maps four contract types—quasi-delegation, contractor, worker dispatch, and in-house employment—along two axes of command authority and completion obligation. It also outlines a six-stage engagement model, VACAN Technologies' "accompanying firm" model, career paths from SES to FDE, and legal risks like disguised contracting.

Key points

  • FDEs are paid for outcomes; SES are paid for hours worked.
  • Palantir contrasts FDEs as serving one customer with many capabilities.
  • Four Japanese contract types are compared on command authority and completion duty.
  • FDEs engage across six stages from problem discovery to production and product feedback.
  • SES-to-FDE transitions hinge on problem redefinition and outcome accountability; disguised contracting is a legal risk.

What's the difference between FDE and SES?

FDE is a role that sells outcomes in the form of problem-solving, while SES is a role that sells man-hours. Palantir's official blog defines FDE as "many capabilities for one company."

Whether they sell "outcomes" or "man-hours"

The difference between FDE and SES comes down to what the contract treats as consideration. SES provides working hours, while FDE provides a state in which the problem has been solved.

An FDE (Forward Deployed Engineer) is a role that works on-site with customers and handles everything in one continuous flow, from discovering problems through implementation to adoption. What they sell is the result that "the problem was solved with a working output," so the length of working hours is not the essence. If a problem is solved with half the estimated man-hours, the value an FDE provides is actually rated higher. SES, by contrast, centers on contracts that provide pre-agreed utilization, and the outcomes themselves are not necessarily the subject of the contract.

Palantir's definition of FDE

Palantir explains that the difference between a regular engineer and an FDE is that "the direction of engagement with the customer" is reversed, and this contrast can also be applied when comparing with SES.

Palantir contrasts regular engineers as "one capability, many customers" with FDEs as "one customer, many capabilities" (Palantir's official blog). SES is close to a way of working that "lends one skill to multiple sites," which is the opposite direction from FDE. This difference also shows up in assignments: FDEs typically stay engaged with one customer for a long time, while SES engineers move between sites based on their skills.

Even when based at the client's site, the purpose differs

Even in the same on-site arrangement, for SES engineers it is a place to provide man-hours, while for FDEs it is a place to solve problems and drive adoption.

SES engineers sit at the client's site to carry out the requested scope of work. FDEs go on-site to understand the customer's business from the inside and uncover the real problems. For an FDE, being on-site is both a place for implementation and a means of gathering information. The two are distinguished not by the form of employment, but by what they are accountable for.

How to distinguish FDE from SES by contract structure?

SES is generally a quasi-mandate contract under Article 656 of the Civil Code, and if the client issues direct orders, it falls under dispatching under the Worker Dispatch Law. FDE is distinguished by whether there is accountability for outcomes.

The difference between person-month contracts and outcome-based contracts

Person-month contracts use the hours worked as the standard of performance, while outcome-based contracts use how much the business has advanced. This difference determines how people work day to day.

Under a man-month contract, issues found outside the scope of work are, in principle, handled in the next estimate or the next contract. This is not good or bad; the contract is simply designed that way. Under a deliverables-based contract, the FDE is responsible for identifying the issues themselves and, if necessary, goes as far as how the product should be built. When an out-of-scope issue is found, SES pushes it to "the next estimate," while the FDE "solves it as part of the current deliverables" — this difference directly affects career growth.

How does the depth of involvement on-site differ?

FDEs are involved across six stages, from project initiation to production deployment and feeding back into the company's own product, while SES handles the work scope that has been carved out (Palantir official blog).

The difference in decision-makers, seen across six stages

According to FDE Soken's analysis, the difference between FDE and SES is greatest at the requirements definition stage. This is because who decides the issues changes at that point.

FDEs redefine the issues before implementing

An FDE understands the client's operations, structures the real issues, and then implements. The starting point is re-examining "what should be solved" before "building what was asked."

The client's initial request is not necessarily the real bottleneck. Behind a request like "we want this screen" there may be a hidden structure where manual data entry is slowing down operations. The FDE observes the flow of operations to reframe the issues and translates them into a form that can be solved with the product. Whether or not one takes on this upstream work is the major dividing line from SES.

SES handles the carved-out work scope

SES is mainly a contract that provides labor for a carved-out work scope; redefining issues and deliverables are, in most cases, not included in its area of responsibility.

The clearer the work scope and the more fixed the specifications, the more stable the value SES provides. Whether to step into issues outside that scope depends on the contract. If, while remaining under a quasi-delegation contract, one acts like an FDE by taking direct instructions from the client, the contract and reality diverge, creating the risk of disguised subcontracting.

VACAN's "accompanying firm" model

VACAN Technologies promotes a "technology accompanying firm" that handles both consulting and contract development. It does not lean toward either consulting or implementation alone.

VACAN Technologies defines itself as "a technology accompanying firm that solves on-site issues with working output" (official website). Regarding launching the business, it states that it aimed to be "a company that can accompany clients' businesses hands-on — not merely offering advice, and not merely building systems" (Wantedly interview). Because the person who proposes and the person who builds are not separated, understanding of the issues flows directly into implementation, and insights gained during implementation feed back into the next issue definition.

Which suits you better: FDE or SES?

According to FDE Soken's criteria, SES suits development with fixed specifications, while FDE suits environments where issues are ambiguous. VACAN Technologies adopts the FDE model.

People suited to an SES-style way of working

People who feel fulfillment in building things to high quality in environments with clear requirements can leverage their strengths in SES. It is also a good fit for those who want to deepen a specific technology.

Because the work scope is clear, it is easier to focus on the technologies you handle, and the ability to build to spec on schedule translates directly into positive evaluation.

People suited to FDE

FDE suits people who want to define issues themselves in ambiguous situations and deliver results through the product. The dividing line is whether you can enjoy the chaos before specifications are fixed.

FDE requires both "the ability to understand the field" and "the ability to build with your own hands." With only one of the two, you may see the issues but be unable to solve them, or build something that misses the mark. FDE Soken organizes salary and rate benchmarks with dates and sources in FDE cost benchmarks and rates.

Career path from SES to FDE

For those from SES backgrounds who have experienced both client-facing work and implementation, there is significant overlap with FDE requirements, making a move to FDE a realistic option.

The two points where differences tend to emerge when transitioning are "redefining issues" and "responsibility for deliverables." Even in your current role, you can start by practicing articulating the issues that lie behind the requested work and proposing them. The required skills are covered in Skills required for FDE, and an overview of the role in What is FDE.

Looking at FDE Soken's four prerequisite axes (years of experience, current role, English ability, and company size), how easily one can move to FDE depends greatly on experience in client-facing work (What is FDE).

How the judgment changes with English ability and company size

Because the required level of English ability and company size varies by FDE job posting, judge based on the job duties in the posting rather than the job title.

For overseas AI companies and foreign-affiliated FDE positions, check the job posting to see whether it includes customer interaction and document creation in English. Regarding company size, if your customers are large enterprises, there are many stakeholders and redefining problems takes time. With smaller customers, the distance to production is shorter. Which suits you depends on which of the six stages covered so far you want to excel at.

Who FDE is not suited for, and commonly overlooked risks

FDE may not suit people who want to focus on work with a clear scope, or who want to clearly delineate their contractual scope of responsibility.

Since FDEs are accountable for customer outcomes, the scope they take on expands every time a new problem is found, making it difficult to draw boundaries around their work. Also, when joining as an FDE under a service/contractor agreement, if the contract remains a quasi-delegation (junim) while the client gives direct instructions, there is a risk of disguised contracting. Before changing jobs or taking on contract work, verify three points in writing: whether it is employment or a service contract, who gives directions, and what counts as the deliverable.
