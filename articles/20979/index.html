FDE Interview Full Process Breakdown: Customer Scenario Questions, System Decomposition Questions, and Follow-up Questions on Delivery Evidence

Summary

Drawing on 676 open positions tracked on the site and public interview guides from Palantir, OpenAI, Anthropic and Amazon, this article breaks down the four common dimensions of Forward Deployed Engineer interviews: resume and delivery evidence screening, hands-on coding and debugging, customer scenario problem decomposition, and incident review with handover. It offers a three-step, 45-minute method for decomposition questions, contrasts weak versus engineering-grade answers on a sample contract-review scenario integrating with ERP, and lists four frequent follow-up probes covering eval construction, idempotency, offline deployment and maintainability handover.

Key points

  • Among 676 tracked openings, Lead and Senior roles make up 74% of positions with stated levels; only 26 are Graduate roles.
  • Four interview dimensions: delivery evidence, coding resilience, problem decomposition, and incident handover; red-line mistakes are disqualifying.
  • Decomposition method: clarify users and bottlenecks, split deterministic rules from model duties, then define a Week-1 MVP with fallbacks.
  • Sample scenario stresses idempotency keys, a rules engine for compliance thresholds, shadow-mode rollout and quantified acceptance metrics.
  • Follow-up probes target eval denominators, duplicate-click handling, air-gapped deployment and customer-run maintenance handover.

As of 2026-11-15, among the 676 open positions indexed on this site (see Mapping of FDE Hiring Structures at Leading Overseas AI Companies), roles explicitly labeled Lead (154) and Senior (104) account for 74% of positions with stated seniority, while Graduate roles number only 26. Job summaries and skill requirements heavily involve customer collaboration (Customer, 385), production delivery (338), and agents (Agent, 144). This structure means the FDE (Forward Deployed Engineer) interview differs fundamentally from traditional backend or pure algorithm roles: interviewers are not satisfied with isolated algorithm problems or idealized architectures for millions of concurrent users. Instead, they repeatedly probe how you would take a customer's vague business pain point and, under the constraints of dirty data, legacy systems, and compliance red lines, break it down into a production loop that can go live for validation within 1-2 weeks, with idempotency safeguards against duplicates and manual fallback (we recommend preparing system design cases alongside Hands-on Integration with WeCom, DingTalk, and ERP and Multi-Agent Ticket Permission Governance).

Note on Evidence Boundaries Interview round names and order vary across companies (for example, Palantir's official hiring guide publicly describes a Problem Decomposition stage and a technical hands-on stage, while some companies emphasize take-home engineering assignments or live customer role-play). This article distills common evaluation dimensions across companies; all questions are labeled as illustrative exercises.

Based on the responsibilities in open JDs from leading companies worldwide and publicly available interview guidance, FDE selection typically revolves around four progressive dimensions. Even if a candidate successfully solves the algorithm problem, hitting a red line in "business boundary judgment" or "production fault-tolerance design" results in an outright rejection:

Deep Dive on the Core Stage: The 45-Minute Method for Customer Scenario and Problem Decomposition Questions

Taking the Decomposition stage publicly described by Palantir and similar companies as an example, this type of interview typically presents an extremely broad customer background, sometimes deliberately ambiguous (e.g., "A large manufacturer has severe after-sales ticket backlog and wants to introduce AI to improve efficiency" or "A multinational organization has fragmented HR and permission systems across multiple platforms, making approvals extremely slow").

What the interviewer observes is not whether you can recite a standard architecture template, but how, like a truly on-site FDE, you converge from clarification and scoping to implementation within 45 minutes:

  • Step 1 (0-10 minutes): Clarify frontline roles and the real bottleneck. Do not rush to bring up LLMs. First ask the interviewer: Who exactly sits in front of the screen operating this system every day (customer service agents, approval managers, or warehouse dispatchers)? Which systems do they need to consult to make one decision? Is the current slowness due to "manually reading unstructured attachments" or "waiting on named approvals across departments"? Which operations, if wrong, would cause compliance or financial-loss incidents? At the end of the 10 minutes, restate and confirm the problem boundary to the interviewer in one sentence.
  • Step 2 (10-28 minutes): Take stock of the data landscape and split "deterministic rules vs. model responsibilities". Ask the interviewer where the data lives (legacy ERP databases, ticket system APIs, or scanned PDFs) and how high the field missing rate is. Then draw clear engineering red lines: anything involving employee identity verification, permission matrix comparison, or amount threshold judgment must be implemented with deterministic code and database rules; the LLM is only responsible for extracting structured fields from unstructured text with citations to the source; any action written back to production systems must be bound to an approval receipt and a unique business idempotency key (biz_request_id).
  • Step 3 (28-45 minutes): Define the Week-1 MVP, eval set, and degradation fallback. Never propose "spending three months rebuilding the company-wide data platform first." Pick the single sub-scenario with the highest volume share and clearest rules as the first-week pilot, and launch it in "Shadow Mode" or "Human-in-the-Loop" mode: the model provides pre-filled suggestions with rationale, and frontline staff confirm with one click before writing to the system. Also explain how you would collect a golden dataset of 100+ samples covering common cases, edge cases, and unauthorized-access counterexamples, and how the system automatically falls back to the existing manual queue when the model times out (e.g., over 8 seconds) or confidence is insufficient.

Illustrative Scenario Exercise: Non-Standard Procurement Contract Pre-Review Coordinated with ERP Project Initiation

The following is an illustrative exercise question designed by this site (not an actual interview question from any company), used to demonstrate how to give a persuasive, structured answer in an interview:

[Illustrative Question]: The legal and procurement teams of a large retail enterprise receive about 400 non-standard supplementary agreements submitted by vendors every day (a mix of PDF and Word files). Procurement specialists need to manually verify whether the payment terms, penalty rates, and jurisdiction courts in the agreements comply with company red lines, and after passing review, manually enter contract elements into a legacy ERP system to create a procurement initiation order. Currently, each order takes an average of 45 minutes, and duplicate initiation orders are often created in the ERP due to missed supplementary clauses or repeated clicks caused by network timeouts. Suppose you are an on-site FDE; please provide a delivery plan for the first two weeks.

Engineering practice and delivery evidence follow-ups: the 4 types of follow-up questions interviewers love to dig into

In the resume deep dive and hands-on coding stages, interviewers will typically drill down three levels in a row on a single number on your resume. Before the interview, prepare STAR-E (Situation-Metrics-Architecture-Resilience-Evidence) details for the following four high-frequency follow-up categories:

  • Follow-up 1: "You say accuracy improved from 72% to 94%—what's the denominator? How was the eval set constructed?"—Key points to answer: state the eval set's sample size (e.g., 150 anonymized historical tickets), category distribution (70% regular samples, 20% boundary samples with missing fields, 10% malicious prompt-injection or privilege-escalation counterexamples), and explain how every change to the Prompt or retrieval chunking strategy is validated by running full regression via automated scripts, rather than eyeballing a few examples and feeling it got better.
  • Follow-up 2: "If a user clicks 'approve activation' twice in a row in WeCom/DingTalk, and the downstream system's first request happens to time out over the network, what happens to your system?"—Key points to answer: clearly explain event deduplication at the callback entry (deduplicating by platform MsgId or an action unique key), the atomic lock of the approval order state machine (CAS state transition from pending to executing), and the downstream execution API's "check-then-remediate" recovery flow based on a unique business key.
  • Follow-up 3: "If the client site is in a virtual private cloud (VPC) or a fully air-gapped isolated data center, which components of your previous solution would fail?"—Key points to answer: point out that components relying on public-cloud SaaS vector stores, public commercial model APIs, or online dependency fetching would fail; provide alternatives such as offline container image packaging, local open-source weights with a vLLM/SGLang inference service, and connecting to an intranet read-only replica database.
  • Follow-up 4: "When you finish a 6-week on-site engagement and prepare to leave, how do you prove the client team can maintain this system on their own?"—Key points to answer: present the four handover essentials—a standard operations manual (Runbook, including common alert handling steps), a one-click degradation/rollback switch (falling back to pure rules/manual when the model misbehaves), an observability tracing dashboard (logging each order's input payload, matched rules, and latency), and a local regression test suite that runs without external network keys.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do you prepare for an FDE interview without job experience carrying the FDE title?

Interviewers care about the substance of responsibilities, not past titles. As long as you have personally delivered a technical system into a real business flow (e.g., deploying internal enterprise tools, ToB private-cloud integration, or overhauling complex ticket or data pipelines), you can directly map to FDE requirements by restructuring your experience along the lines of "business bottleneck → rules and model boundaries → exception and idempotency fallbacks → quantified acceptance results."

Does the Problem Decomposition stage require writing code?

Usually you are not required to write full production code; instead, the assessment examines your ability to verbally clarify requirements, map out data flows and state boundaries on a whiteboard or document, and prioritize the minimal first-week pilot. However, interviewers may at any time drill into the specific data structures behind a node you drew (such as idempotent retries or permission filtering).

When preparing for an FDE interview, which matters more: grinding LeetCode algorithm problems or building an engineering portfolio?

Coding fundamentals (string/JSON parsing, state graph traversal, concurrency and exception handling) must be solid, but grinding high-difficulty competitive algorithms alone yields limited returns. An engineering portfolio featuring state-machine guardrails, offline eval sets, and failure-recovery drills often provides a decisive advantage in the screening interview and the hiring-manager interview.
