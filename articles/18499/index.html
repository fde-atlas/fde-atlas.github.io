What Is an AI Consultant? Differences Between 4 Types, How to Choose, and Pre-Engagement Checks

Original title: AIコンサルとは？4タイプの違いと選び方・発注前チェック

FDX株式会社 · 2026-06-19 · 16 min read

Summary

This article, aimed at executives selecting AI consulting firms, divides the market into three types: strategy firms (McKinsey, BCG), implementation/SI vendors, and embedded co-building consultants modeled on Palantir's and OpenAI's Forward Deployed Engineer approach. The author argues strategy-only engagements stall before production and full outsourcing leaves no internal knowledge. It recommends evaluating vendors on implementation capability, business understanding, and exit conditions, offering five vetting questions, a scoring table, a ten-item pre-contract checklist, and contract designs that gradually shift work in-house over 6–12 months.

Key points

  • AI consulting splits into strategy, SI implementation, and FDE-style embedded co-building types.
  • Strategy firms deliver roadmaps but rarely reach production; SI handoffs leave no internal knowledge.
  • Selection should rest on three axes: implementation ability, business understanding, and exit conditions.
  • Contracts should specify knowledge-transfer milestones and exit criteria to prevent permanent dependency.
  • Common failures: strategy-implementation split, full outsourcing, and vendor lock-in via proprietary frameworks.

Key points on selecting AI consulting (3 lines)

  • AI consulting comes in three categories (strategy-focused / implementation-focused / in-house enablement), with fundamentally different value propositions. Both "expecting a strategy consultant to implement" and "asking an implementation vendor for strategy" lead to mismatches.
  • The selection axes for large enterprise adoption are three: implementation capability / business understanding / exit conditions. In particular, if exit conditions are not decided upfront, dependence on consultants becomes permanent.
  • Moving beyond the binary choice of "strategy only" or "fully outsourced implementation" and choosing the in-house enablement type that lands on in-house capability is the optimal solution as of 2026. The Forward Deployed Engineer concept embodies this.

The three categories of AI consulting

"AI consulting" is often discussed as a single category, but it splits into three types with different value structures.

Category 1: Strategy-focused consulting

Value provided: Strategy formulation for executives, prioritization of business areas, investment decisions, organizational transformation plans. Main providers: Major strategy firms (BCG, McKinsey, Bain, Accenture's strategy division, etc.), think tanks. Strengths: Ability to persuade executives, cross-industry benchmarks, organizational transformation experience. Weaknesses: Does not go as far as implementation (or does so at extremely high cost), thin on operational design after PoC.

The deliverables of strategy firms are, in most cases, PowerPoint strategy maps and prioritized business lists. A strategy map shows what to build, but does not go into how to implement or operate it.

Category 2: Implementation-focused consulting (SI type)

Value provided: End-to-end implementation from requirements definition → design → implementation → delivery → maintenance. Main providers: Major SIs (NTT Data, Fujitsu, Hitachi, NEC, etc.), AI implementation specialist vendors. Strengths: Builds something that reliably works when requirements are clear. Project management and control capabilities. Weaknesses: Low flexibility for requirement changes. Post-delivery improvements require contract changes. Knowledge does not remain in-house.

The typical failure of implementation-focused firms is that the characteristics of generative AI and agents (adaptation to changing assumptions, improvement loops) are not anticipated at the requirements definition stage, and the system becomes obsolete within six months of going to production.

Category 3: In-house enablement (Forward Deployed Engineer type)

Value provided: Embedded on-site at the client company, starting from business understanding, building side by side with in-house engineers. At completion, hands over operational capability as well. Main providers: Palantir (the pioneer), OpenAI's Forward Deployed Engineering, some AX implementation partners. Strengths: Deeply embedded in the business, knowledge stays in-house, can support continuously from PoC to production. Weaknesses: Smaller than strategy or SI firms, with limited options. May be less persuasive to executives than strategy consultants.

The in-house enablement type has been attracting attention since 2024 as a third option beyond the binary choice of strategy consultants and implementation vendors (see: What is an FDE? / Palantir and OpenAI case studies).

Five questions to ask when large enterprises adopt AI

In selecting AI consulting, pose the following five questions to candidate firms. How they answer reveals the essence of the value they provide.

Question 1: "Will you take responsibility through implementation, or only up to strategy formulation?"

Strategy-focused firms often stop short of implementation. Implementation-focused firms often have shallow strategy formulation. Ask upfront exactly where their responsibility begins and ends.

A reassuring answer is "We take end-to-end responsibility, from business understanding and strategy formulation through PoC implementation, productionization, and operational handover." On the other hand, if they reply "For implementation after strategy formulation, we will introduce a separate implementation partner," that is a sign that boundaries of responsibility are likely to become ambiguous.

Question 2: "What mechanism do you have for leaving knowledge inside our company?"

A typical weakness of implementation-focused firms is the "deliver and hand off" model. If knowledge is not left in-house, you cannot run operations or improvements yourself, and external dependency becomes prolonged.

Good answer example: "We work on-site, building side by side with your engineers. We document every process, and at operational handover your team will be fully capable of running it." Answer to watch out for: "We will carefully deliver the deliverables (code and documentation)." (Deliverables alone do not transfer operational capability)

Question 3: "Can exit conditions be decided upfront?"

Long-term contracts carry the risk of perpetuating consulting dependency. Ask whether they can decide upfront when and under what conditions they will exit.

The desirable counterpart is one who can commit concretely: "We can state in the contract milestones such as passing PoC in 6 months and handing production operations to your team in 12 months." Conversely, an answer like "We will provide continuous support until results are achieved" lacks exit conditions and tends to breed dependency.

Question 4: "Can you set up a structure to work directly with business owners?"

Technical skill alone cannot penetrate the business. Ask whether they can establish a structure for daily collaboration with business owners.

Good answer example: "We will set up a structure where the business owner, your engineers, and our FDEs discuss together in the same room weekly." Answer to watch out for: "Your project manager will act as the point of contact and consolidate requirements." (A structure where the business owner is not directly involved fails)

Question 5: "Can you share cases of failure and withdrawal?"

Be wary of consultants who only talk about success stories. Ask whether they can candidly share their own cases of failure and withdrawal.

A trustworthy counterpart can speak concretely: "In the past, at Company XX we failed to narrow the business scope and redesigned the project after 3 months. That learning is now reflected in our standard process." An answer like "All of our cases have been successes" should raise suspicion that they either do not understand their own limitations or are not disclosing them.

Score each criterion on a 5-point scale, and weight the three criteria of "implementation capability," "in-house capability building support," and "exit conditions" to make a selection consistent with the decision axes for enterprise adoption (implementation capability, business understanding, exit conditions).

Three selection patterns that fail

A structural breakdown of common failures in enterprise AI consultant selection.

1. Expecting a strategy consultant to implement

You engage a strategy-focused consultant and receive a strategy map, but vendor selection for implementation becomes a separate contract. As a result, the premises of the strategy collapse during the implementation phase, or responsibility becomes ambiguous between implementation vendors.

How to avoid it: From the strategy formulation stage, verify the structure for taking responsibility through implementation (or close collaboration with an implementation partner).

2. Handing everything to an implementation vendor with no path to in-house capability

If you sign a "leave it all to you" contract with an SI-type vendor, five years later AI-related operations remain fully outsourced and no expertise is left in-house. Only maintenance costs balloon, and improvements require contract changes.

How to avoid it: Explicitly state in the contract a condition such as "operations will be brought in-house after XX months." For details, see the separate article "How to Approach AI In-House Capability Building".

3. Failing to notice the structure of vendor lock-in

If you accept a design dependent on a particular vendor's proprietary framework or proprietary data formats, the later cost of switching to another vendor becomes enormous. On top of knowledge not remaining in-house, the migration itself becomes harder—a double lock-in.

How to avoid it: Choose vendors that adopt open standards (LangGraph, MCP, OpenTelemetry, etc.). For details on framework selection, see "Introduction to LangGraph Implementation".

Designing consultant selection that lands on in-house capability building

Starting with the goal of "engaging consultants, but ultimately internalizing the capability" is a condition for success in enterprise adoption.

The 4 Elements of Contract Design

1. Phase-based role definitions Explicitly state in the contract the ratio of roles between the consultant and the internal team during the PoC phase, the production phase, and the operations phase.

  • PoC phase: consultant 70%, internal 30%
  • Production phase: consultant 50%, internal 50%
  • Operations phase: consultant 20%, internal 80%

Make the structure of gradually increasing the internal share explicit from day one of the contract.

2. Knowledge transfer milestones Set contract milestones for when knowledge moves in-house, such as "the internal team takes over prompt improvement after 3 months," "the internal team takes over tool definition updates after 6 months," and "the internal team completes full operations after 12 months."

3. Specifications for shared documentation Write the format and granularity of the consultant's documentation into the contract. These specifications exist to leave behind not just "something that works" but "a state that can keep working."

4. Handover conditions upon withdrawal Define the state in which handover occurs at contract end or upon early termination. Decide the format in advance, e.g., "code goes to GitHub, documentation to Notion, and operational knowledge to the minutes of weekly meetings."

The 3 Principles of Organizational Design

Principle 1: Choose on-site embedded consultants Choose consultants who are embedded in your actual business operations, not just meeting in an office. Palantir's Forward Deployed Engineer model is an example of this.

Principle 2: Business owners attend decision-making meetings The owner of the target business must always attend meetings with the consultant. "Meetings with only the consultant and IT" are a breeding ground for failure.

Principle 3: Agree on withdrawal conditions before signing Choose a consultant who can define upfront when they can withdraw. By securing withdrawal criteria in advance, structural consultant dependency becomes less likely.

Pre-Order Checklist (10 Items)

Before signing the contract, verify the following 10 items. If any answer is "no," work through that item with the candidate company before proceeding.

  • □ Can they concretely present at least 3 cases that reached production operation?
  • □ Is the scope of responsibility, from strategy formulation through implementation and operational handover, end-to-end in the contract?
  • □ Was on-site interviewing of the target business conducted before the proposal (or was it a proposal based on documents alone)?
  • □ Are knowledge transfer milestones (what the internal team takes over and when) explicitly stated in the contract?
  • □ Have withdrawal conditions and handover criteria (deadlines and target states) been agreed before signing?
  • □ Is there a structure in place for business owners to attend decision-making meetings?
  • □ Does the proposal include non-functional requirements for production operation, such as access control, audit logs, and SLAs?
  • □ Are the evaluation frameworks and data formats open standards, allowing a switch to another vendor?
  • □ Did they disclose failure and withdrawal cases, and how those lessons were reflected into their standard processes?
  • □ Does the contract specify phase-based role ratios (a design where the internal share increases from the PoC phase to the operations phase)?

This checklist also functions as an attachment for internal approval processes. Being able to explain "why this company was chosen" with 10 factual items is the completion condition of the selection process.

Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ)

Q1. What is the typical cost of AI consulting?

A. It varies greatly by type, but as a rough guide: strategy firms charge around 3-6 million yen per person per month; implementation-partner types (FDE types) charge around 3-5 million yen per person per month (2-4 million yen in hybrid configurations); and SIer-type firms typically use lump-sum project contracts. However, comparing unit prices alone is inappropriate; you should compare total cost over 18-24 months, including additional customization and reinvestment caused by failed operational handover. Even if the unit price is low, if knowledge does not remain in-house, total costs balloon as external dependency continues.

Q2. Which contract type is better: quasi-delegation (jun-i) or fixed-price contracting (ukeoi)?

A. For AI adoption involving business process redesign, quasi-delegation is the default, since requirements change as work progresses. Fixed-price contracting assumes finalized specifications and acceptance testing, making it a poor fit for generative AI and agent projects. The practical solution that aligns incentives while preventing dependency is quasi-delegation combined with performance-based compensation tied to business KPIs, with knowledge transfer milestones explicitly stated in the contract.

Q3. What contract duration should be assumed?

A. As a rough guide, 3-6 months to pass the PoC, and 6-12 months including production rollout and handover to internal operations, is one benchmark. Rather than the length of the period, what matters is deciding withdrawal conditions and handover criteria before signing. Open-ended "we'll support you until results appear" contracts tend to perpetuate consultant dependency.

Q4. Which is better: a major consulting firm or a small/mid-size firm or startup?

A. Choose based on fit with your company's goals, not size. If the main objective is building consensus among executives or formulating company-wide strategy, the explanatory power of a major strategy firm is effective; if it is applying cutting-edge AI to a specific business, a startup's technical strength is effective. If you want to reach production rollout and internalization, what is decisive is not size but whether they have an on-site embedded structure and a knowledge transfer mechanism; whether the firm is large or small is not a primary decision axis.

Q5. Can we adopt AI without using AI consultants, on our own?

A. Yes, it is possible. If you have sufficient AI talent in-house and experience in business process redesign, going without external help is a rational choice. However, at many large enterprises, personnel with generative AI and agent implementation experience are in short supply, and going it alone often leads to getting stuck at the PoC stage or rework in technology selection. Even in that case, rather than permanent outsourcing, the high-ROI approach is to launch with a time-boxed partnership contract with transfer conditions and land on internalization.

Q6. What is the most common failure pattern when selecting an AI consultant?

A. The most common is expecting a strategy consulting firm to handle implementation as well, only for the premises to fall apart after the strategy map is delivered and implementation ends up as a separate contract with a separate vendor. The next most common is handing everything over to an SI-type vendor, leaving no knowledge in-house while maintenance costs balloon. Both can be structurally avoided by confirming the scope of responsibility, knowledge transfer, and exit conditions before signing the contract.

Summary

  • AI consulting falls into three categories—strategy-focused, implementation-focused, and in-house capability building support—each with a different structure of value delivered
  • For enterprise adoption, the selection criteria come down to three axes: "implementation capability / business understanding / exit conditions," and five questions reveal a consultant's true nature
  • The three failure patterns (expecting strategy consultants to implement / outsourcing implementation entirely and missing in-house capability building / vendor lock-in) can be structurally eliminated through contract and organizational design
  • By securing exit criteria before signing the contract, dependence on consultants can be prevented from the contract design stage onward
  • Having a design that lands on in-house capability from the start is the condition for success in selecting an AI consultant
