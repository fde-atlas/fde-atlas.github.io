OpenAI's FDE Lead on the AI Deployment Gap

Original title: OpenAI FDE 负责人谈 AI 部署差距

Key Takeaways

  • Colin Jarvis, who leads OpenAI's global FDE team, estimates that models account for only about 20% or less of the deployment gap
  • The rest of the gap is organizational and lies on the customer side: data, permissions, evals, and team structure
  • The goal of FDEs is to make customers self-sufficient and build reusable playbooks, not to earn services revenue

According to Colin Jarvis, model capability accounts for only "about 20% or less" of the deployment gap in enterprise AI. Jarvis leads Global Forward Deployed Engineering at OpenAI. In his view, most of the remaining problems lie not with OpenAI but inside customer organizations: data, permissions, evals, and team structure.

This assessment carries weight because Jarvis has done this work himself. According to his speaker bio for HumanX, he led OpenAI's technical deployments at companies including Morgan Stanley, Klarna, and Coca-Cola. At HumanX Amsterdam, he gave a talk titled "Turning the Promise of Frontier AI into Reality."

For those already working as or aspiring to become Forward Deployed Engineers (FDEs), this estimate is itself a job description: the hard part of the job is not calling APIs.

Models Are No Longer the Bottleneck

Suppose a bank wants to use an AI agent to answer questions for financial advisors. A demo built on sample documents works well. But once the system goes into real use, problems emerge one after another.

The real documents are scattered across three systems. An advisor at one branch has no access to client files at another branch.

No one has defined what a "correct" answer looks like. And no team has agreed to take ownership of the system after launch.

Switching to a stronger model solves none of these problems. They make up the large share of the gap Jarvis describes, and they explain why OpenAI must embed engineers inside enterprises rather than just selling access.

Trust Comes Before ROI

In a podcast episode released by Altimeter Capital in November 2025, Jarvis appeared as OpenAI's head of forward deployed engineering. He discussed his work with Morgan Stanley and a European semiconductor company.

The episode summary emphasized that to deliver real return on investment (ROI), FDEs must stay close to customers, understand their specific needs, and build trust.

The order matters. Only when customers trust the engineers can they gain access to sensitive data, obtain permissions, and change how teams work. Therefore, the ability to work with stakeholders is a prerequisite for the technical work to begin, not an optional bonus item filed under "soft skills."

Success Means the Customer No Longer Needs You

Jarvis is explicit about the goal: "We want to help customers build capability so they can do the next batch of use cases themselves." Another podcast summary notes that the FDE team pursues reusable product playbooks rather than services revenue.

The team's size also reflects this. According to the same summary (published around November 2025), OpenAI's FDE team had only 39 engineers at the time, with a target of 52 by year-end.

A team this small cannot accompany every customer indefinitely. Every deployment must leave behind the capability for the customer to operate on their own, and accumulate experience the team can carry to the next customer.

What This Means for Your Resume

When applying for an FDE role, simply writing "built a RAG application" is too thin. Spell out which customer-side bottleneck you cleared: which data sources you integrated, how permission controls were designed, how many test cases your eval set contained, and which team would maintain the system after you left.

Back to the bank example. Before: "Built a RAG chatbot for a bank." After: "Integrated documents from three systems, built branch-level permission controls, worked with financial advisors to create an eval set of correct answers, and then handed the system over to an internal team to maintain."

When reading job descriptions, watch for words like "enablement," "playbook," and "handover." They signal that the company understands the role the same way Jarvis does.

Jarvis's self-sufficiency goal also offers a simple test for your own work: a deployment is only truly complete when the customer's team can deliver the next use case without calling the FDE.
