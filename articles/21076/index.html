A Complete Guide to FDE Development: How to Use It Alongside Hiring and External Resources, and How to Structure the Development Process

Original title: FDE育成について徹底解説。採用・外部活用との使い分けと、育成の進め方

AI Ops Manager · 2026-09-02 · 27 min read

Summary

This Japanese article, aimed at executives and AI initiative leads, argues that because experienced FDEs are scarce in Japan's hiring market (73 domestic companies were posting FDE roles as of September 2026), companies should consider internal development. It recommends using the company's own operations as the first training ground, progressing through three stages: shadowing, prototyping, and independent ownership. The author suggests hiring the first FDE externally and developing subsequent ones internally, distinguishes product-facing FDEs from the broader in-house AI implementation type, and attributes failed training programs to structural issues—no mentors, no real projects, outdated evaluation systems—rather than poor course content. It also compares hiring, internal development, and external talent as acquisition routes.

Key points

  • Experienced FDEs are scarce; 73 Japanese companies posted FDE roles as of September 2026.
  • Train FDEs on internal operations first, via shadowing, prototyping, then solo ownership.
  • Hire the first FDE externally; develop the second and later ones internally.
  • Training failures stem from structure: no mentors, no projects, unchanged evaluation systems.
  • Distinguish product-facing FDEs from broader in-house AI implementation roles before designing training.

For developing FDEs (Forward Deployed Engineers), a realistic approach is to use your own company's operations as the first field site and proceed through three stages: shadowing, prototyping, and working independently. This article explains the required abilities and aptitudes, rough timelines, why development often fails, and how to balance development with hiring and external resources.

Published date Last updated

You want to secure FDEs (Forward Deployed Engineers), but there are no experienced candidates in the hiring market. In that case, the only option is to develop them in-house. This is the conclusion most companies reach.

However, there is something you should verify before committing to development: whether development is truly the shortest path for your company. Securing FDEs also involves the options of hiring and using external talent, and which one fits depends on your internal situation. And even if you choose development, a flawed approach will end with development costs piling up without a return on investment.

To state the conclusion up front: development should use your own company's operations as the first field site and proceed through three stages—shadowing, prototyping, and working independently. On top of that, a plan becomes realistic if you bring in the first FDE externally and develop the second and subsequent ones in-house.

This article organizes the abilities and aptitudes required for FDE development, the concrete steps and rough timelines for in-house development, and then shows how to balance development with hiring and external resources by condition. It is aimed at executives and AI promotion officers who want to build AI-driving talent within their company.

Why is the need to develop FDEs growing now?

Because hiring, as a means, is structurally unlikely to work. Companies need to systematically develop in-house talent who can work on the front lines of operations and consistently handle everything from identifying issues to implementing AI and embedding it into operations. Opportunities to see this kind of FDE development initiative have become noticeable since the start of 2026.

There are also announcements of significant scale. In October 2026, Dentsu announced that, together with three domestic group companies, it would launch a skills development program for FDEs and FDMs (Forward Deployed Marketers, which the company describes as "practical talent who support business transformation at client sites with a focus on generative AI use") targeting approximately 3,000 people across the group*1. The aim is to strengthen a structure for driving AI-based operational transformation by embedding into client sites. This is also an example where the scope of development expanded beyond engineers to marketing practitioners.

What is an FDE (Forward Deployed Engineer)?

An FDE is an engineer who brings their company's product into a client company's site and, alone or with a small team, handles everything from identifying issues to implementation and embedding the solution into operations. This way of working was established by Palantir Technologies in the US, and as AI adoption spreads, FDE job postings have surged and the title has come into widespread use*2. Its distinguishing feature is that rather than implementing given requirements, the engineer starts by observing the field and defining "what should be solved" on their own.

Note that in Japan, the term "FDE" is increasingly used more broadly than this original definition, referring to talent who implement and drive AI within their own company's operations. The difference between these two will be clarified later in "Which type of FDE does your company want to develop?"

For details on the definition, the five stages of the work, salary levels, and career paths, see "What is an FDE (Forward Deployed Engineer)? Salary, skills, careers, and how companies can secure them". This article focuses specifically on "development."

Why can't companies hire experienced FDEs?

Because the role itself is new and the pool of talent with "hands-on FDE experience" is still thin. Announcements by domestic companies only started piling up from 2026 onward, and competing for candidates in the hiring market does not increase the total pool. In fact, as of September 2026, 73 domestic companies had posted jobs with FDE in the job title*3. Most are companies offering AI products or services, and many companies are simultaneously searching for people with the same experience. We analyzed the details of these postings in depth in our previous article "Why are FDE needs surging? An in-depth analysis of job postings from 73 domestic companies". The judgment that it is more realistic to develop talent on a foundation of adjacent experience comes from this situation.

The nature of the talent shortage also supports the choice of development. The "DX Trends 2026" report published by IPA (Information-technology Promotion Agency) in July 2026, a survey of 1,799 domestic companies, asked about the sufficiency of AI-related talent by talent category. For most categories, "insufficient" accounted for more than half of responses, and in particular, for employees who combine field knowledge with basic AI knowledge and can drive AI adoption within their own company, the proportion of companies reporting a shortage has remained at a high level. Regarding the "quantity" of DX-driving talent as well, 85.5% in total of companies working on DX answered "somewhat insufficient" or "significantly insufficient"*4.

What is lacking is not people who build models, but people who make AI work in the field. And this capability is, by nature, easier for people who know your own company's operations to acquire. This is exactly where development can be effective.

How do FDEs differ from SEs, IT consultants, and SES?

Organized to the extent needed for designing development, the differences are as follows. What counts as a result differs by role, and that difference translates directly into "what to develop."

RoleDefining issuesImplementation responsibilityWhat counts as a result
FDEDefined by themselves through dialogue with the fieldResponsible through to embedding in operationsThe field's operations have changed
IT consultantDefined by themselves through dialogue with the clientBasically up to the proposalReports and roadmaps
SE (systems engineer)Receives requirementsDesign and developmentA system built to specification
SES (on-site at client)Received from the clientWithin instructed scopeProviding working hours

When developing existing SEs or IT consultants into FDEs, foundations such as technical skill and client-facing ability are already in place. What is additionally needed is a way of working that finds issues in the field without waiting for specifications, and a sense of treating post-implementation adoption as part of one's own responsibility. Development becomes more efficient if it concentrates on this gap.

Skills and aptitudes required for FDE development

The abilities required of an FDE can be organized into four. What matters for development design is that these split into those that can be developed through training and practice, and those that should be assessed at the candidate selection stage.

The four abilities required of an FDE

AbilityWhat it means concretely
Break down business processes to identify issuesDecompose business flows into steps and pinpoint where things are getting stuck. Structure the issues that the people involved cannot yet articulate themselves
Build something that works in a short periodDeliver a working prototype quickly rather than pursuing perfect design. Implementation in Python and similar tools, data integration with existing systems, prompt design, RAG (a mechanism that lets AI search and reference internal documents), and AI agent design
Clarify what can and cannot be doneClearly distinguish and communicate what AI can and cannot do. Separate quick wins from results that take time, and never leave the boundaries ambiguous
See it through until it takes holdDon't stop once the system is running; stay involved until the workplace has fully transitioned to the new procedures

When it comes to technical skill, speed creates more value than depth. Being able to show a working prototype right in front of the people on the ground forms the foundation of trust. You don't need to be an expert in every area; what matters more is the willingness to learn deeply when needed.

Which of the four to prioritize depends on what your company is lacking. In companies that have already invested heavily in AI tools, the "ability to decide what to build" is often more lacking than the ability to build things. Conversely, in companies where the issues are visible but nothing gets done, the ability to deliver something quickly is needed first. Before designing a training program, verify which one is missing.

Abilities that can be developed through training versus qualities to assess when selecting candidates

CategoryWhat appliesReason
Developable through trainingAbility to break down business processes, ability to build something quickly, how to align expectationsThese are acquired through repeated real-world projects. In particular, the ability to break down business processes works to the advantage of those who know their company's operations well
Assessed when selecting candidatesAbility to decompose ambiguous situations, integrity in separating facts from speculation, ability to turn failure into next actionsThese rarely change through short-term training, and if they are missing, the training itself will not progress

The most reliable way to evaluate candidates is to give them one problem with no clear answer and have them explain how they would turn it into a plan. What clarifying questions do they ask before jumping to solutions? How do they break down the problem? What do they decide to tackle first? And can they honestly say, "If we take this, we'll have to give up that"? These qualities show up here.

Another thing to watch is their attitude when dealing with unfamiliar areas. People who don't pretend to know what they don't know, who separate facts from hypotheses, and who can go as far as saying "I'll verify this in this way" will not lose trust even when working in the field. Conversely, if this is weak, even strong implementation skills won't sustain relationships with other departments.

In FDE development, half the outcome is decided by "who you select" before you even create the training program. If you distribute uniform training to everyone without assessing aptitude, most of the investment will go unrecovered.

Can FDEs be developed from non-engineers?

Given that AI coding quality has become extremely high, it is possible to develop non-engineers into FDEs. However, there are conditions on how to proceed. The criteria are not current coding skills, but three things: whether they can decompose business processes into data structures, whether they proactively verify unfamiliar technologies themselves, and whether they have experience turning failure into the next improvement. People with these three qualities can learn implementation practices later.

The nature of this work—entering the field and restructuring operations—also applies to areas beyond engineering. The substance remains the same whether the role involves rebuilding GTM processes spanning sales, marketing, and customer success through data integration and automation, or working in back-office functions like accounting or customer support: reading the business and redrawing the procedures. This role is covered in a separate article, "What is a GTM Engineer? Job Description, Differences from Sales Planning, and the Reality of 4 Domestic Job Openings."

The condition is not letting them handle implementation alone. When developing non-engineers, it's faster to get started by pairing them with someone who can implement, with the business-savvy side deciding "what to build" and the implementation side handling "how to make it work."

The first field for training

For the first practical experience, choose your own company's operations rather than a client project. Training is best done on real projects, but you cannot immediately assign a new trainee to a client project. This starting point holds whether you are developing FDEs who work at client sites or developing in-house talent to implement AI in your own operations.

There are three reasons: maximum risk mitigation by containing the impact of failure within the company, the fact that the trainee already understands the business context, and the results directly improving your own company.

There is another, often overlooked reason: it is unrealistic to expect engineers who have only worked within development teams to suddenly behave appropriately in external client settings. Drawing out the client's business needs, aligning expectations, reporting progress. Practicing these behaviors with other internal departments keeps the cost of failure low. The approach of incorporating AI into the development team's own work is covered in a separate article, "What is AI-Driven Development (AIDD)? Benefits, Challenges, Implementation Methods, and an Adoption Roadmap."

There are three criteria for choosing the first business process:

  • It occurs repeatedly, daily or weekly
  • The person in charge is clearly struggling and cooperation can be obtained
  • Results can be measured numerically (number of cases, time, lead time, etc.)

For example, processes like handling inquiries or checking standardized documents, where case counts and processing times are recorded, are relatively easy to work with. Conversely, choosing a large company-wide theme first will consume time in stakeholder coordination, exhausting the trainee before they can even get hands-on.

The three stages of training

Training proceeds in three stages: shadowing, prototyping, and independent ownership. The principle is not to aim for the finished form all at once. Move through the following three stages, advancing only after the graduation criteria are met.

StageWhat the trainee doesCriteria for moving to the next stage
① ShadowingAttend meetings, take notes, and diagram the flow of the target processCan explain the business in the field's own words
② PrototypingA senior colleague or external practitioner handles requirements definition; the trainee implements and presents to the fieldDelivered a working product in a short time and can explain the causes of rework themselves
③ Independent ownershipOwns one small process alone, from requirements definition through adoptionThe field has transitioned to the new procedures and metrics have moved

The goal is not merely "a state where they can operate without support." Organizational FDE capability is only established when you reach the point of reproducibility, where multiple people can stand in the field using the same methods. If only one person becomes independent, everything reverts when that person leaves.

Most stumbles happen at stage ②. This is the stage where the trainee is first expected to deliver results at a level that can be shown to the field. Failure here tends to erode confidence, so an effective allocation is for those around them to take responsibility for requirements selection, letting the trainee focus on implementation.

Time required to develop FDEs

Some guides suggest six months to a year as a benchmark, but it varies greatly depending on the trainee's background and the volume of projects you can prepare. In practice, it works better to decide the graduation criteria for each stage first, rather than deciding the duration first. If no projects are available, no amount of allotted time will produce progress.

Check progress every three months and decide each time whether to move to the next stage. If you promote people automatically based on elapsed time, someone who cannot yet work independently is placed in a position of working alone, leaving a burden on both the field and the person themselves.

What to decide before starting

The four things to decide are: how to measure results, the graduation criteria for each stage, the career path after development, and how many people to develop.

Tie the measurement of results to business-side metrics. If the only goal is "development," it becomes ambiguous whether the activities are good or bad. Capture baseline numbers before starting, such as hours spent, number of items processed, and lead time for the selected business processes.

There is another reason to tie results to business-side metrics. Without deliberate attention, AI adoption results tend to stop at efficiency gains. According to IPA's "DX Trends 2026," 91.6% of companies cited "business became more efficient or faster" as the effect of AI adoption, while only 3.9% cited "sales or profits improved" and 4.5% cited "customer satisfaction improved"*5. If the goal of development is simply "becoming able to use AI," results will stop at business efficiency and never reach changes in sales or customers.

As for headcount, do not leave it to a single person. If there is only one person who knows information systems, DX, or generative AI, the entire company's efforts stop the moment that person takes a day off or transfers. Choosing three or more people from different positions—information systems, HR, field departments, sales—also makes cross-departmental discussions easier to get through.

If you establish it as an organizational unit, it functions better placed under development/product rather than sales. Starting small with two or three people is realistic. Placing it under sales optimizes it for short-term deal closing, diluting the original value of feeding insights gained in the field back into products and internal systems.

Why FDE development fails

When development plans fall apart, the cause is almost never the quality of the training. The structure needed for people to grow is not in place on the organizational side. Here are common patterns seen in the field.

① No one in-house to teach

This is the most common. Development guides usually assume shadowing a senior, but companies trying to develop their first FDE have no such senior. Classroom learning alone does not teach the judgments made in the field. How to break the cycle of "no teachers means no growth, no growth means no teachers emerge" is the biggest issue in building from zero to one.

② No projects for hands-on practice

FDEs only grow through projects. However, handing over customer projects right away is risky, and a look around the company finds no suitable material. As a result, only training accumulates. Classroom learning can only fill knowledge gaps; the structural problem of having no projects will not move no matter how many training sessions you add.

③ Evaluation system still based on traditional job frameworks

If the old boundary of "that's not my job" remains, FDE-style behavior is not rewarded. In organizations where engineers stepping forward to other departments, or business-side staff getting into implementation, do not count positively in evaluations, the very people being developed find it hardest to act. Before building a development program, you need to touch the evaluation system and job definitions.

④ Training ends when the course ends

Training and certification help establish a common language, but they are not results in themselves. People try a little, find it interesting, but it never takes hold in the business, and they eventually return to the usual way of working. This pattern is called "PoC fatigue." It happens when using AI itself becomes the goal, with no design for reaching actual results.

Meanwhile, entering 2026, efforts to build certification systems for visualizing FDE skills have begun. In June 2026, the IoT Certification Committee announced a plan for an FDE certification system. It is designed around stages: analyzing business issues and redesigning operations with AI in mind (DESIGN), building systems that leverage AI and digital technologies (BUILD), and embedding them in the field for continuous use (EMBED), with exams planned to start sequentially from Level 1*6. In October 2026, it was also announced that an FDE training school for engineers with at least one year of practical experience would open in November, and that consultations for corporate training programs had begun*7. A realistic division of roles is to position certifications and training as tools for establishing a common entry-level language, and to measure results by actual changes in business. If you set the number of training completions as the KPI for development, you will easily fall into the aforementioned state.

⑤ Leaving the company after being developed

FDEs are a highly marketable profession. The more successful the development, the stronger the demand for that talent in the job market. Rather than seeking a way to make attrition zero, it is more realistic to design the organization assuming a certain percentage will leave. Use fixed-term assignments, keep backup talent in reserve, and prepare a replenishment plan before it is needed. The more the system depends on one person, the greater the blow when they leave.

It also helps to show the career path after independence in advance. If trainees cannot see what lies ahead after development, offers from outside will look more concrete.

Choosing between hiring, developing, and external talent

We have focused on development so far, but development is not the only way to secure FDEs. The three options are not mutually exclusive; in practice, they are combined.

Benefits and remaining challenges of the three approaches

MethodBenefitsRemaining challenges
HireBring in ready-to-work talent from outsideThe pool of experienced candidates is small and selection takes months. Compensation and onboarding systems must be in place, and post-hire retention is also a challenge
Develop in-houseDevelops people familiar with your own business, and know-how stays in-houseTakes time before results appear. Requires a teaching structure and projects for hands-on practice
Use external talentBrings in specialized expertise immediatelyUnless there are efforts to systematize their practices and knowledge and accumulate it in-house, no know-how remains when that person leaves

On the third point, you need to distinguish between outsourcing and bringing in an external practitioner as a member of your organization. The former is a relationship where you receive deliverables, while the latter is one where you advance real projects together and gradually transfer the working methods themselves to your internal staff. What remains behind differs. For outsourcing, how the way of giving instructions changes under a contract, and what to decide before making a request, are covered in a separate article, "What to Know Before Outsourcing FDEs | Criteria and Preparation."

How to Choose Based on Your Company's Situation

Your company's situationRealistic choice
There is someone internally who can teach, and time is availableMake in-house development the main focus. Invest in preparing projects for hands-on practice rather than training programs
No one internally can teach, and results are needed within six monthsBring in an external practitioner as a member of the organization and have the trainee work alongside them
The product to implement is already decided, and you want it usable in the fieldLeave it to that vendor's FDE. Decide in advance who will handle work outside the scope, such as cross-product business design and rollout to other departments
Over the medium to long term, you want to increase promoters who know your company's business wellDevelop them in-house. However, this requires an evaluation system and career paths as prerequisites

As a prerequisite, if requirements are already fixed, you can proceed with ordinary development outsourcing. The FDE role is effective at the stage when the field cannot yet articulate what should be automated with AI. If you are already at the point where things can be written into specifications, there is no need to rush FDE development.

Types of FDEs to Develop

Before putting the above into practice, there is one more thing to decide. Even within "FDE development," the type of talent to develop splits into two.

プロダクト型FDE（本来の定義） 社内実装型FDE（広義）
Who develops themCompanies with their own productCompanies that want to promote internal AI adoption
Where they workBusiness sites of client companiesEach department within the company
What is centralTheir own productTheir own business processes
How results are measuredChanges in client operations and feature contributions back to the productMan-hours, quality, and lead time of internal operations
Top priority skill to developCombining product knowledge with client relationsCombining business decomposition with AI implementation

Among published explanations of FDE development, many are written for SIers and contract development firms, i.e., assuming something close to the former. However, many companies searching for "FDE development" likely fall into the latter category: the need to have in-house talent capable of implementing AI into their own operations. If you design training without distinguishing between the two, both the content taught and the evaluation criteria will drift.

Another point to note is that a structural distortion arises if a client-side company tries to keep an FDE in the original sense in-house. The FDE role is built on the premise of license revenue from one's own product, and the same structure does not hold for companies without a product to sell. Since most people calling themselves FDEs have achieved results with a specific product, if your company does not use that product, only part of their experience transfers. Even so, their compensation will be pulled toward FDE market rates.

In other words, what client-side companies need is not a person with the FDE title. It is deciding who will handle the execution phase that lies between conception and adoption. Once that is decided, the skills to develop and the criteria for who to bring in from outside naturally become clear.

How to Bring in the First Person

Bringing in the first person from outside and developing the second and subsequent ones internally makes development a realistic plan. Development usually stumbles not on the training content itself, but on how to launch the first person and how to prepare projects for them to practice on.

In-Housing and External Talent Are Not in Conflict

The more a company aims for in-housing, the more cautious it tends to be about using external talent, viewing outsourcing as the opposite of in-housing. However, trying to do everything yourself without a first FDE means the trial-and-error time becomes a direct cost. In a field where available tools change on a monthly basis, self-study without anyone internally to teach you is far too inefficient.

What matters is not whether to bring in external talent, but whether you define the handover goal from the start. Which operations, handled by whom, by when, should be in a state where your own team can run them? In addition, align three points before starting: the scope of access to operations and data, how much decision-making authority to delegate, and how results will be measured. If you bring someone in while these remain vague, even an excellent performer ends up in a state where "things only work while that person is there."

Conversely, if these are decided, bringing in external talent becomes a shortcut to in-housing. They are not in conflict.

Working Inside as a Member of the Organization and Leaving the Methods Behind

When bringing in an external practitioner, the realistic approach is to have them work inside as a member of the organization, not as an external consultant or vendor. Rather than a relationship of receiving advice or deliverables, it is one where, with in-housing as the premise, you work together on everything from inventorying operations and identifying issues to tool implementation, operational design, and internal training.

The talent you bring in does not need to be limited to engineers. Whether in sales, marketing, back-office functions like accounting, or customer support, the nature of the work—reading operations and redrawing procedures—does not change. Choosing someone with practical experience in a field close to the internal operations selected first will speed up the ramp-up.

If you have a trainee work alongside them, the internal operations selected first become the teaching material itself. Running real projects while showing the reasoning behind decisions is development that classroom training cannot replace.

Conclusion

The means of securing FDEs are three: hiring, development, and external utilization. Given the current situation where the pool of experienced people is thin, development attracts attention, but the right choice depends on whether you have someone internally who can teach, when results are needed, and how far requirements are fixed.

If you choose development, the causes of failure lie in structure, not training quality. No one to teach, no projects for practice, an unchanged evaluation system, training that ends without application, or trainees who change jobs after developing. None of these are solved by improving training content.

The realistic sequence is to choose internal operations as the first site, bring in the first person from outside to run real projects together, and transfer the working methods themselves to the company. Do not try to assemble even the first person entirely in-house. That is the most reliable shortcut to keeping FDE development from falling through.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q. How long does it take to develop an FDE?

Some explanations suggest a timeframe of six months to one year, but this varies greatly depending on the trainee's background and the volume of projects you can prepare. Rather than deciding the duration first, it is more practical to first define the graduation criteria for each stage: shadowing, prototyping, and handling work independently. If no projects are available, no amount of time will move things forward.

Q. If there is no one in-house who can teach, where should we start?

Do not try to develop your first FDE entirely on your own. First, select one target from your company's operations, bring in someone with hands-on experience from outside as a member of the organization, and have the trainee work alongside them. At that point, define the handover goal upfront: which operations, handled by whom, will be run independently by when.

Q. Can a non-engineer become an FDE?

Yes, in the broad sense of an FDE who implements and drives AI in their company's operations. The criteria for judgment are not current coding skills, but three points: whether they can break down business operations into data structures, whether they proactively verify unfamiliar technologies themselves, and whether they have experience turning failures into subsequent improvements. However, do not let them shoulder implementation alone; pair them with someone who can implement, starting with a structure where the business-savvy side decides "what should be built." This makes the ramp-up faster.

Q. Can FDEs be developed through training and certification alone?

No. Training and certifications serve as a common language to align stakeholders' terminology, but the judgments made in the field can only be learned through real projects. If you set the number of training graduates as the KPI for development, you risk falling into "PoC fatigue," where initiatives are tried and then rolled back. Evaluate based on actual changes in business operations.

Q. How can we mitigate the risk of trained FDEs leaving the company?

It cannot be reduced to zero, so design on the assumption that a certain percentage will leave. Specifically: develop three or more people from different positions rather than concentrating on one, prepare personnel for fixed-term assignments and backups, and present career paths after independence in advance. Design development and retention as a single package, not as separate efforts.

Q. Should FDEs be secured through hiring or development?

If you need ready-to-work talent immediately, hiring or external talent is the way to go; if you want to increase people who deeply understand your company over the medium to long term, development is better. In practice, combining both is realistic: transferring know-how from external practitioners to internal talent as you go. The criteria for judgment are three points: whether there is someone in-house who can teach, by when results are needed, and how far the requirements have been defined.
