Does FDE require an in-house product? Differences and boundaries compared to contract development

Original title: FDEに自社プロダクトは必要か？受託開発との違いと境界

Inovie株式会社（イノビー） · 2026-08-17 · 10 min read

Summary

This Inovie article asks whether FDEs need their own product. For FDE in the original sense, as practiced by OpenAI and Palantir, a company product or platform is central, so the answer is essentially yes. But Japanese-style FDE or FDE-type support can start without a finished product, provided learnings from each engagement are converted into reusable shared assets. The author proposes a five-stage maturity model from bespoke work to external products, contrasts FDE with contracted development, and argues the real boundary is whether project learnings flow back into the provider's own assets. It also classifies what should stay customer-specific, become shared components, or become standard processes, and lists five questions to decide whether to generalize a feature.

  • The FDE/contracted-development boundary is where learnings flow back.
  • Five questions decide whether a feature should be generalized.

If you call yourself an FDE, do you need your own product?

In its original sense, a Forward Deployed Engineer is a role that deploys a company's own product or platform into customer environments. In this sense, the answer is, in principle, "yes." However, if we apply the same criteria to the broader FDE-style support that is spreading in Japan, we would close off the entry point for AI consultancies, SIers, and contract development firms looking to move into FDE businesses.

The conclusion is: you don't need a finished SaaS product at the start, but you do need a mechanism for turning project knowledge into reusable shared assets. If you share nothing and keep building from scratch for each customer, the reality is closer to bespoke contract development than FDE.

The question doesn't end with a single yes-or-no about "having a product." From the very first project, you must decide what to keep customer-specific and what to shape into something the next customer can use. What's being tested is whether you can design that boundary.

The basic definition of FDE is covered in "What is an FDE? The Original Definition and How Japanese-Style FDEs Differ", and the overall business design process is explained in "How to Launch an FDE Business."

For differences in roles, contracts, and deliverables compared to AI consulting, SIers, contract development, and SES, see "Differences Between FDEs and AI Consultants, SIers, Contract Developers, and SES."

The Original FDE Sits Between the Product and the Customer Site

OpenAI positions its FDE team at the intersection of customer delivery and core platform development. FDEs are responsible not only for everything from Discovery to production deployment, but also leave behind the methods that worked on projects as tools, playbooks, and shared components, feeding evaluation results from the field back into product and model roadmaps.

OpenAI's FDE job description also explicitly states that methods that worked on projects are preserved as tools, playbooks, and shared components, and that field evaluation results are fed back into product and model roadmaps. Completing individual projects and creating shared assets are placed within the same role.

Palantir likewise describes Forward Deployed Engineering as a methodology in which engineers close to the field work with core development teams to turn customer feedback into new features. With a shared platform of AIP, Foundry, and Apollo, it's a structure that creates a back-and-forth between customer-specific implementations and platform evolution.

Therefore, the original FDE is not simply "an engineer who develops at the customer's site." It is a role that makes customer-specific value and the evolution of the provider's product happen within the same project.

Sources: OpenAI "Forward Deployed Engineer (FDE) - NYC", Palantir "Architecture Center Overview" (accessed August 17, 2026)

Japanese-Style FDE Can Start Without a Finished Product

So, can AI consultancies, SIers, and contract development firms not start FDE businesses? If we mean the broader sense of FDE used in Japan—Japanese-style FDE or FDE-style support—then starting is possible.

In fact, public materials from Japanese companies show examples of FDE businesses built not solely around their own AI products, but by combining existing customer bases, development and operations structures, SI and SES expertise, and third-party AI services. Hmcomm integrates its own AI technology with system development and operations capabilities, showing a path from an FDE business toward a future AI implementation platform. Seraku, under its "Japanese-style FDE model," has stated a policy of handling both its own AI platform and third-party AI.

These two companies alone cannot be generalized to the entire market. Still, it is clear that in Japan, approaches are being conceived that go beyond starting from a single finished product—instead bundling assets from existing businesses and cultivating a shared platform through projects.

Sources: Hmcomm "Notice Regarding the Launch of the Next-Generation AI Implementation Business 'FDE'", Seraku "FY August 2026 Second Quarter Earnings Presentation" (accessed August 17, 2026)

However, you cannot stop the conversation at "we don't have a product yet." Without the will and process to build shared assets from individual projects, calling it FDE-style support will not create any structural difference from contract development.

A Product Is Not Just SaaS

Even companies that think they "have no product" may already have things they can reuse across projects. If you don't limit "product" to finished goods that customers sign contracts for, but instead view it gradually as a reusable execution foundation, you can see where you currently stand in your launch.

StageState of Shared AssetsExamplesState as an FDE Business
0. Individual handlingBuilt from scratch for each projectCustomer-specific code, individual staff experienceClose to contract development
1. Standard processesReusing decisions and methodsDiscovery procedures, SOWs, evaluation sheets, runbooksEntry point for FDE-style support
2. Shared componentsUsing technical elements across multiple projectsAuthentication, logging, evaluation infrastructure, connectors, RAG componentsReproducibility emerges
3. Internal platformAssembling components into a configurable foundationIndustry templates, admin screens, deployment infrastructureCan be rolled out to multiple teams
4. External productBecomes a product customers can use continuouslySaaS, AI agent platforms, data productsApproaches the original FDE structure

Stages 1 and 2 may not generally be called "products." But if speed and quality improve as projects accumulate, they are important business assets. At inovie, we view this gradual accumulation of shared assets as the "minimum execution foundation" of an FDE business. This is inovie's own framing, not an industry-standard definition.

You don't need to build the finished product first. What you need is a mechanism that ensures the same work is never done twice when the same problem appears twice.

Where Is the Boundary Between FDE and Contract Development?

Contract development and FDE are not opposites. FDE projects still involve customer-specific development, and quasi-mandate or fixed-price contracts may be used as contract forms. The boundary lies not in the contract name, but in the project's purpose and the flow of knowledge.

Comparison itemTypical contract developmentFDE / FDE-style support
Starting pointAgreed requirements or specificationsCustomer outcomes, business challenges, value hypotheses
Primary responsibilityDesign, development, deliveryFrom problem discovery through production use and improvement
Handling of changesSubject to change management and additional estimatesScope continuously adjusted based on evaluation results
What remains after a projectCustomer-specific systems and materialsIn addition to customer outcomes, reusable shared assets
Where learnings flow backThe project and the responsible teamProducts, platform, standard processes, roadmap
How success is judgedAcceptance, deadlines, budget, qualityProduction use, business outcomes, reusability, profitability

This table does not uniformly classify all custom development firms and FDE providers. Excellent custom development firms may also handle Discovery, continuous improvement, and shared componentization. In such cases, the two practices overlap.

If we narrow the difference down to one thing, it is how the project's learnings are designed to become someone's assets and where they flow back. Do you finish by leaving deliverables with the customer only, or do you abstract them within the limits permitted by contract to improve your own next delivery and products? This is where the business structures diverge.

Separate what stays customer-specific from what should be shared

If you try to share everything, you end up with a hard-to-use product that ignores differences in customer operations. Conversely, handling everything individually prevents the business from scaling. At the design stage of each project, divide things into three categories.

What should remain customer-specific

  • Customer-specific business rules, organizations, and approval flows
  • Customer data, permissions, and confidential information
  • Settings and exceptions specific to existing systems
  • Logic directly tied to competitive advantage
  • Some screens and reports created for the customer

Forcing these into generalizations not only creates information management and rights issues, but also produces abstractions that cannot be used by other companies.

What is easy to turn into shared components

  • Authentication, permissions, audit logs, error handling
  • Interfaces for data connections and model connections
  • Methods for evaluating AI output, structures for test cases
  • Methods for managing prompt and model changes
  • Monitoring, incident response, and operational handover procedures

Candidates are things that play the same role across multiple projects without containing customer data or proprietary logic.

What should remain as standard processes

  • Interview items and methods for documenting business flows
  • Prioritization of use cases
  • Criteria for deciding whether to move from PoC to production
  • Security, legal, and operational checklists
  • Templates for proposals, estimates, SOWs, and responsibility boundaries

Knowledge that never becomes code is also an asset. Sometimes it is easier to start by standardizing decision-making methods rather than technical components, as this is more reusable.

Five questions for deciding whether to share

If you put a feature you used once straight into the product, the convenience of a specific customer gets mixed into the platform. When a sharing candidate emerges, check it with the following questions.

  • Does the same problem occur across multiple customers or projects?
  • Does value remain even if customer-specific data and business rules are removed?
  • Can it be handled through configuration differences, without increasing code branching?
  • Can you take responsibility for maintenance, security, and compatibility after sharing?
  • Will it actually lead to improvements in time, quality, and risk on the next project?

Some questions cannot be answered from a single project. In that case, do not make it a product feature; record it as a sharing candidate and re-examine it on the second project. Rather than building, it is important to maintain the discipline of being able to flow things back.

A realistic sequence for companies starting without their own product

You do not need to build a finished SaaS first and then look for customers. If you are starting from existing customers' projects, the following sequence is realistic.

  • Narrow down target customers and business domains that share the same challenges
  • Build standard processes for Discovery, production rollout, and operational handover
  • Validate evaluation methods and responsibility boundaries on the first project
  • Move components and decision methods used twice into shared assets
  • Consolidate into a configurable internal platform from the third project onward
  • Consider external productization if customers continuously pay for shared functionality

What matters here is not the number of projects itself, but whether the same challenges recur and whether you have confirmed willingness to pay for shared functionality and operational responsibility. It is not a criterion like "three projects means you can definitely productize."

The first finished artifact does not have to be a SaaS. If you have an evaluation suite, common connectors, security standards, and runbooks in place, and can proceed with the second project safely and quickly, it is functioning as the foundation of an FDE business.

Do not make "building a product" the goal itself

An FDE business needs shared assets. However, not everything should become a SaaS. If you productize features with large per-customer operational differences that cannot be absorbed by configuration and are rarely used, you only increase your maintenance burden.

It is better not to rush productization in cases like the following.

  • Heavily dependent on one company's legal and business requirements
  • The definition of success differs by customer
  • Individual data preparation accounts for a larger share than shared functionality
  • Value is complete in a one-time migration or build rather than continuous use
  • Post-sharing maintenance responsibility and revenue are out of balance

Even in these cases, you do not need to abandon the project. You can treat it as individual custom work and decide not to force it into the shared foundation of the FDE business. The goal is not to eliminate individual customization, but to manage the boundary between individual customization and shared assets.

What FDE needs is a feedback loop, more than having a product

Returning to the question at the beginning: does FDE require your own product?

If you claim to be a true FDE, your own product or platform deployed to customers is at the center of the business. On the other hand, at the stage of starting as Japanese-style FDE or FDE-style support, a finished product is not required.

However, a state where shared assets do not grow even as projects accumulate and the same design and implementation are repeated is not the finished form. What is needed is a feedback loop that solves on-the-ground challenges and flows the knowledge gained back into standard processes, shared components, an internal platform, and ultimately a product.

What you should judge is not just "what you built." What can you now bring to the next customer? If you have a concrete answer to that, you can move an FDE business forward even from a stage without your own product.
