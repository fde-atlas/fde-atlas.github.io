Why It's Blowing Up Now · OpenFDE

Original title: 为什么现在爆火 · OpenFDE

Full text

FDE is not a new term—Palantir has used it for twenty years. Its sudden rise in 2025–2026 as Silicon Valley's hottest job is not because the title got sexier, but because the capability curve of large models has pushed enterprises up against a new chasm: models are getting stronger every month, yet the pace at which enterprises integrate them into core workflows lags far behind. The people who bridge that chasm are FDEs.

Different media outlets use different counting methods—some count FDEs, others also include FDSWEs, Applied AI Engineers, and Solutions Engineers—but the direction is highly consistent. Business Insider↗Fast Company↗WSJ↗ These numbers should not be taken as precise market size, but they are enough to make one point: the FDE has gone from a Palantir-style niche role to a key position in the commercialization organizations of AI companies.

Three driving forces

Building a demo is fast; getting into production is hard. Enterprise data is scattered across CRMs, ERPs, data warehouses, ticketing systems, email, and permission systems; workflows involve multi-step, multi-role, multi-system coordination; and you still need SSO, RBAC, auditing, compliance, evals, rollback, and cost and latency management. Real ROI comes from restructuring workflows, not from single-point model calls—which is exactly why engineers must be deployed on-site. a16z↗

Hiring has spread from Palantir to OpenAI, Anthropic, Stripe, and Google Cloud, and on to 100+ YC startups. Three years ago, zero companies were hiring FDEs on YC; today, the FDE has become the "standard opening move" for AI startups. YC Library↗

In May 2026, OpenAI founded The OpenAI Deployment Company, embedding FDEs to connect customer data, tools, and workflows, with over $4 billion in initial investment commitments. OpenAI↗ The same month, Anthropic partnered with Blackstone, Hellman & Friedman, and Goldman Sachs to launch an enterprise AI services company, having Applied AI Engineers bring Claude into customers' core operations. Anthropic↗

The moves by these three leading companies point to the same judgment—AI model companies are no longer just selling APIs or SaaS seats, but are re-embracing a commercialization model of "deep deployment + engineering services + product feedback." Deployment itself is being upgraded from after-sales service to a core business capability.

The real scarcity: adoption speed, not model capability

Why "now"? Because the scissors gap between capability and adoption is being stretched to its widest. Bob McGrew, former Chief Research Officer at OpenAI and the second engineer at Palantir, put it bluntly:

Over the next 5 years, the world's AI capabilities will keep advancing rapidly, yet in the real world we may increasingly feel mediocre. It's like getting into a Waymo today: you won't marvel that "self-driving has arrived"—you'll just complain about "why is there so much traffic." The opportunity for FDEs is precisely the best moment to fill the gap between "what AI can do" and "what customers can adopt." YC Library↗

The stronger the models, the more enterprises want to integrate them into core workflows; and the more critical those workflows are, the more they demand permissions, auditing, data governance, workflow restructuring, and clear accountability—none of which models can solve on their own. So a counterintuitive conclusion emerges: the stronger AI gets, the more FDEs are needed, at least in the short term.

OpenAI summarizes this approach as build, prove, generalize: first solve a specific high-impact problem for a customer, validate the business impact, then generalize the reusable patterns into product capabilities.
