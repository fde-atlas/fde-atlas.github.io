Frontline Co-Creation, Two-Way Empowerment: Industry Observations and Practice Report on the FDE Model

Original title: 前线共创，双向赋能：FDE 模式行业观察与实践报告

腾讯云 · 2026-09-24 · 7 min read

Summary

A Tencent Research Institute report examines why the Forward Deployed Engineer (FDE) model is surging. Over 60% of enterprise AI pilots stall at the experiment stage, not because models are weak but because of the gap between demo and production: system integration, compliance, and unclear use cases. The report argues FDE is a delivery-model overhaul, not a new job title—its defining test is whether field experience is converted into reusable assets like skills, templates, and product capabilities. AI makes execution cheap but makes judgment scarce. It also covers China-specific constraints (smaller contract sizes) and Tencent Cloud's practices in education and media.

Key points

  • US Indeed FDE postings jumped over 700% in a year, from 643 to 5,330
  • FDE differs from on-site outsourcing by leaving reusable assets after projects
  • AI lowers execution costs while making scenario judgment more valuable
  • China's low contract values require reuse via skills, connectors, and partners
  • Tencent Cloud model: vendor pilots, partners replicate, customers self-serve

Large model capabilities are leaping forward every quarter, yet over 60% of enterprise AI pilots remain stuck in the experimental stage. It's not that the models aren't strong enough—it's that there's a wide gap between Demo and production: system integration, permission mapping, process redesign, compliance audits, organizational adaptation, and most importantly, customers need to figure out "what exactly should AI do".

This is why the FDE (Forward Deployed Engineer) is being repriced. Over the past year, FDE job postings on the US Indeed platform surged from 643 to 5,330, an increase of over 700%; OpenAI and Anthropic each announced the formation of dedicated deployment teams on the same day; hundreds of YC-backed startups began hiring FDEs—a number that was nearly zero three years ago.

The latest report released by Tencent Research Institute, "Co-Creation on the Frontline, Two-Way Empowerment: Industry Observations and Practices of the FDE Model", combines cutting-edge overseas practices with frontline domestic experience to systematically analyze, for the first time, why this model is exploding now, what problems it solves, whether it can scale, and the special path for the Chinese market. Below are the key findings.

Report's core conclusions:

First, the core contradiction of AI adoption is shifting from "can the model do it" to "how does the organization use it". Model capabilities are leaping forward quarter by quarter, but over 60% of enterprise AI attempts remain stuck in the pilot stage. The bottleneck isn't the Demo—it's how to integrate systems, embed processes, meet compliance requirements, and produce measurable business results.

Second, the essence of FDE is not a new job title, but a paradigm shift in the delivery model. It recombines the capabilities of pre-sales, implementation, product, and customer success into a single frontline squad, allowing delivery and learning to happen simultaneously. The driving force is that AI has simultaneously lowered three cost barriers—knowledge distillation, custom development, and the supply of hybrid talent—making the heavy-delivery model commercially viable on a broader scale.

Third, the essential difference between FDE and traditional on-site staffing lies in whether the project leaves behind reusable assets. Leaving the customer with just a system is outsourcing; bringing back experience that cannot be reused is project-based delivery; converting field experience into Skills, templates, test sets, or product capabilities after strictly removing customer information—that is FDE; and when that accumulation significantly reduces the cost of the next similar delivery, that is scalable FDE.

Fourth, AI makes engineering execution cheaper and judgment scarcer. AI coding and agent tools have lowered the costs of prototyping and system adaptation, but the ability to identify high-value scenarios, understand industries, and drive organizational adoption has become more valuable. Frontline roles are evolving from "people who write code" to "people who direct AI to accomplish business goals".

Fifth, the sustainable path is accumulation first, reuse first, and gradual customer self-service. Building an ontology entirely from scratch is extremely costly; a more realistic path is to first accumulate high-frequency Skills and system connectors, then gradually form industry templates and knowledge bases, so that every frontline engagement leaves behind reusable assets.

Sixth, three-way collaboration among platform, partners, and customers is the consensus forming across the industry. Platforms accumulate capabilities and benchmarks, partners scale replication and provide localized services, and customers gradually develop self-service capabilities through accompanied engagement. This collaboration gives top customers deep service while allowing mid-tier customers to access mature capabilities at lower cost.

Seventh, the future belongs to those who understand business better, understand organizations better, and are better at directing AI. Low-end implementation will continue to be squeezed, while scenario definition, customer trust, organizational drive, and industry knowledge accumulation will keep appreciating. Customers just starting out need not worry about "standard answers"—FDE was born for differentiated scenarios; teams already on the journey should focus more on how to turn frontline experience back into their own capabilities.

Not necessarily on the frontline, and not necessarily deploying in person:

FDE is not "a higher-level form of on-site outsourcing"

The concept of Forward Deployed Engineer can be somewhat misleading. The most common misconception is equating FDE with "an implementation engineer who knows AI better". This understanding only sees the shell.

The real difference lies not in whether one is on-site, but in what is left behind after the project ends. If the project only leaves the customer with a system, that's outsourcing; bringing back some experience that cannot be reused is project-based delivery; strictly removing customer information and accumulating industry-generic experience into Skills, templates, and product capabilities—that is FDE; and when that accumulation significantly reduces delivery costs for the next similar customer, that is scalable FDE.

Behind this is a "two-way accumulation" mechanism. Facing the customer, FDEs convert business experience and process rules scattered in employees' minds into AI-invocable knowledge bases, workflows, and application capabilities. Facing the platform, FDEs accumulate industry scenarios, system interfaces, testing methods, and failure cases into reusable product components, continuously iterating the product. The shared vehicle for both layers of accumulation is the ontology layer—a structured business knowledge graph that allows different customers in the same industry to share a common way of understanding business objects.

There is a clear economic logic behind this. The first project costs more than pure project-based delivery, because in addition to delivering the customer's system, knowledge abstraction and validation must also be completed. But after seven or eight similar customers, the ontology is basically stable, and subsequent delivery costs drop significantly. This is exactly why former Palantir executive Bob McGrew emphasized a core measurement principle: if the first customer requires 10 person-months and the tenth similar customer still requires 10 person-months, the model hasn't worked.

AI makes execution cheaper and judgment scarcer

The report uses a concise framework to analyze the FDE capability structure: Echo is responsible for "what to do", and Delta is responsible for "how to build it".

AI coding, low-code platforms, and agent tools are dramatically compressing the cost of Delta—prototyping, system integration, and test validation no longer require large engineering teams. But Echo has not become cheaper; it has become scarcer. The customer's biggest bottleneck is shifting from "can it be built" to "do you know what to build": business departments have pain points but struggle to translate them into AI tasks, IT departments understand technology but are too far from the business, and decision-makers want to push AI but don't know how the organization should adjust.

This is why the lower the barrier to "building applications with natural language" becomes, the more the value of FDE rises—shifting from builder to scenario discoverer, requirement translator, and method coach. The report predicts that FDE's future will clearly bifurcate: low-end implementation and repetitive building will be continuously squeezed by agents and platforms, while scenario definition, customer trust, organizational drive, and industry knowledge accumulation will continue to appreciate.

Chinese enterprises can't copy Silicon Valley, but they have a chance to skip ahead

The Chinese market has its own uniqueness. In many large US enterprises, processes, data, and job divisions have been refined over many years, with relatively complete ERP and CRM systems—AI is more like installing an intelligent brain onto a precision machine. In contrast, many domestic enterprises are more like "conversation-driven": requirements are raised in unstructured ways, and processes are often written not in systems but in tacit understanding and personal relationships.

This increases implementation difficulty, but it also brings a chance to skip ahead. Traditional software required filling out forms, drawing processes, and configuring rules first; AI's entry point is natural language—and natural language is precisely the collaboration method such organizations are most familiar with. Many customers may not be able to map out their processes clearly before adopting a system, and are instead better suited to "running while building the road" under FDE accompaniment.

The core challenge is economics. High-value Silicon Valley customers can support six-to-seven-figure USD annual contracts, while many domestic projects remain in the range of 100,000 to 1 million RMB. Chinese FDEs must solve two problems: use AI and platforms to lower delivery costs, and use Skills, connectors, industry templates, and partner ecosystems to increase reuse rates.

Tencent Cloud's practice: from accompaniment to empowerment

Tencent Research Institute has conducted extensive research on frontline samples of Tencent Cloud's FDE practice. The core logic is "the vendor builds the prototype, partners replicate it, customers self-serve":

In education, the team explored a "bootcamp accompaniment" model, designing closed loops around teachers' real tasks—from scenario selection and process breakdown to application building and classroom validation. What was delivered was not training, but runnable applications and reusable Skills. The education-specific capabilities accumulated in this process eventually incubated the industry product LearnBuddy.

In media, collaboration with an ISV validated the efficiency of the Echo+Delta two-person combo—one person presents industry cases and customer benchmarks, while the other quickly builds prototypes on-site using platform tools, resulting in faster response and lower cost. Media scenarios also confirmed a trend: the technical barrier to building AI agents is being "flattened," while the demand for business understanding is being "raised."

The CodeBuddy/WorkBuddy customer success team is forming an end-to-end practice that spans aligning decision-makers' understanding, tool-driven engineering execution, and validation of business outcomes. The Echo layer conveys organizational transformation experience to enterprise leadership, while the Delta layer uses AI tools to reduce engineering execution costs; the synergy between the two layers is spreading from benchmark customers to a broader range.

Whoever first solves

the "from Demo to production" problem

will hold a structural advantage

Large models will not walk into customers' sites on their own. The stronger the model capabilities, the wider the scissors gap between "technology can do it" and "the organization can use it." The rise of the FDE is not a naming game for a new job title, but the result of shifting contradictions in enterprise AI adoption.

It reveals a repeatedly verified rule: for large models to truly enter industry settings, it takes not only stronger models, but also people who connect model capabilities, business processes, organizational mechanisms, and reusable assets. This work is not glamorous, but it is the necessary path for AI to move from "looking impressive" to "actually making money."

*Co-Creating on the Frontline, Two-Way Empowerment: Industry Observations and Practice of the FDE Model* spans 12 chapters, covering the essential mechanics of the FDE, a systematic review of Palantir's twenty years of frontline practice, the cost structure changes brought by AI, frontline tactics for going from Demo to production, talent organization and assessment mechanisms, a full-process operations manual, future trend analysis, and in-depth analysis of domestic and international recruiting data.

The report was produced by Tencent Research Institute. The research involved in-depth interviews with Tencent Cloud regional architects, industry teams in education/media/culture-tourism/government-enterprise/finance, the ADP platform, the CodeBuddy/WorkBuddy product team, the HR team, as well as institutional partners including Quest, Cresta, VentusAI, Hangfeng Huizhi, Juzi Interactive, BISHENG, Zhipu AI, Baidu Cloud, Volcano Cloud, and Alibaba Cloud.
