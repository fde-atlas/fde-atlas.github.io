How to Build an FDE Team

Fourth Age's Substack · 2025-11-03 · 11 min read

Summary

In the second part of its Forward Deployed Engineering series, Fourth Age examines why companies struggle to build FDE teams at scale. It identifies two common traps: hiring freelance experts (specialization without scale) and relying on traditional consultancies (scale without specialization). Using Palantir as the main case study, the authors propose a pagoda-shaped growth model anchored by a core of senior FDEs who deliver impact, develop themselves, and mentor junior hires. They argue that headcount quality should drive sales, not the reverse, and outline nonnegotiable steps: clear vision, high hiring bars, mentorship-based development, and rewarding talent with autonomy and hard problems.

Key points

  • FDE talent supply badly lags demand, making experienced hires scarce and costly.
  • Freelance models lack sustainability, scalability, and well-rounded capability.
  • Traditional consulting pyramids rarely produce high-impact FDE work.
  • Palantir grew to a few thousand FDEs over ~20 years with strict hiring standards.
  • The proposed pagoda model uses senior FDEs as the foundation for scaling quality.

In the second installment of our series on Forward Deployed Engineering, we explore some of the key difficulties in building a differentiated FDE function at scale. While there are a few common pitfalls, we believe that an unrelenting focus on a few key principles can overcome these challenges and lead to massive long-term value creation for both employees and customers.

Previously we covered the key elements of what it means to be a Forward Deployed Engineer and how it differs from traditional IT consulting. The result is a role that is unique and still nascent: there aren’t too many proven engineers out there to hire as FDEs, nor are there many firms with proven results hiring and deploying those engineers. Even as the AI wave continues to swell, most of the world is still oriented around traditional ways of purchasing software, traditional ways of utilizing consultants, and traditional expectations for how all those investments might (or might not) lead to a clear return for the business.

All the characteristics that make the FDE unique therefore create a challenge: how can a company begin to build a successful team of Forward Deployed Engineers who are capable of delivering the desired high value outcomes for their business or their customers? The challenge often requires unsatisfying and expensive trial and error, leaving clients and stakeholders wondering why transformative value remains elusive beyond a few pilot projects or isolated successes.

Most commonly, we see that firms fail to deliver against this model because they fall into the trap of trying to grow their FDE function through one of two ways:

1. Specialization without scale

The first route is through specialization without scale via freelance or “freelance plus” models:

There is a huge supply-demand imbalance in the Forward Deployed Engineering market, where the amount of specialized support that companies are looking for is far greater than the set of people out there to hire who have meaningful, proven experience using software to help customers achieve their most ambitious goals. While more expertise will filter into the market over time, we believe this imbalance is unlikely to meaningfully change any time soon (even with Palantir’s AI FDEs in the mix).

As a result, companies naturally look to grow by hiring one-off freelancer talent to meet existing demand. However, there are several key problems with this path that ultimately limit success:

  • Lack of sustainability: Proven FDEs come at high cost and can become liabilities as budgets change or as projects end. Each individual also presents a constant flight risk if another opportunity comes along that could pay them more. As a result, they don’t tend to add much institutional capacity over time.
  • Lack of scalability: Even the best individual contributor only has so many hours in the day and days in the year. It might be fine to start out with one or two experts, but what happens when bigger projects require a team of five, or need certain strengths and specializations? We find that the most ambitious customers reach a point sooner or later where they need a more predictable, scalable talent pool.
  • Lack of completeness: Even the most senior FDEs come with a range of strengths, technical skills, and experiences, and work best when they are able to leverage their unique strengths. For example, some contributors can be technically brilliant but lack experience leading teams or high-stakes projects and would not necessarily be set up for success if placed in those roles. Additionally, some of those who hit the market with a freelance mindset are looking to “cash in” on skills they’ve already learned, and lack one of the most important differentiators of an FDE: the almost magnetic attraction to continuing to learn, grow and own as much responsibility as possible. As a result, imbalances may linger unaddressed and be more pronounced in this cohort.

This model can be thought of like popcorn, with many individual kernels that are just fine individually (or by the handful) but only providing a temporary surge of energy. At the end of the day, they don’t create a solid foundation for growth.

In order to find a solution that is more scalable than one-by-one contracting, the other natural way to scale is to look more traditional consultancies or large systems integrators. This brings us to the second common scaling trap:

2. Scale without specialization

Scaling without specialization is often seen when engaging with more traditional IT service providers. Maybe a company already has an enterprise-wide MSA with one such service provider, and they have been a solid partner based on factors like geography, industry, cost, and scale. Everyone’s life would be easier if this partner could simply take on more specialized FDE projects in addition to other work they’re already doing, but the risk is that they don’t have the structure and unique types of talent necessary to succeed. In other scenarios, this model might be observed with newer entrants trying to build Forward Deployed Engineering teams who scale too rapidly without a sufficient core of in-house capability.

Some of the problems with this model are:

  • There is little incentive to upend existing consulting engagement models to discover a different Forward Deployed Engineering model based on outcome-oriented, software-based, builder-driven solutions. Traditional consulting models rely on a base of junior analysts who lack the experience necessary to build high-impact, production-ready solutions, paired with managers who may deeply understand the client context and high-level strategy but lack the hands-on expertise necessary to ensure the junior engineers are building effectively.
  • Even if there is a will, there’s often not a “way”, as existing companies lack the critical mass of experience with the FDE model to ensure they are consistently deploying teams against high value, fast-paced projects.
  • In cases where the service provider in question is not a well-known, global consultancy, but hasn’t yet developed a deep bench and specialized practice, they will find it very difficult to compete based solely on scale and affordability. After all, when it comes to Palantir and other major software platforms, the Accentures, Deloittes, and PwCs of the world (and more) are all already in the mix — and we are still in the early innings.

The traditional way of representing this consulting model is with a pyramid, with a base of high-margin junior talent alongside increasingly senior managers and partners who oversee the work. This is very scalable in terms of hiring talent and billing hours, but its hierarchical approach does not lead to the highest impact FDE deployments.

The Risks in Scaling

While each of these models has downsides, competitive pressures and supply-demand imbalance in the market often lends itself to a fragile equilibrium, oscillating between some of the main weaknesses of each model. A path to building a Forward Deployed Engineering team sometimes looks like the following:

  • A company begins to hire FDEs due to clear market opportunities or an urgent need to see results from existing software/AI investments. They start with relatively few, high-cost senior FDEs who are hired based on their prior experience to staff initial projects.
  • The first project sees some success and requires more staff, and/or subsequent projects are sold; sales are made and budgets allocated to continue staffing FDEs.
  • Because of high cost and low availability of experienced FDEs, more junior or less proven talent is hired to fill the gaps and keep the wheels turning on active projects.
  • Bigger (perhaps external) sources of funding are tapped to ensure that headcount continues to grow to meet the needs, while investing more in tangential functions like sales, management, training, and recruiting.
  • As projects scale, quality is observed to be highly variable and some unsatisfied customers ask for different teams. A few more experienced FDEs are hired to reinforce these projects already sold or budgeted.
  • The relatively small number of experienced FDEs are asked to pick up the slack as well as manage junior talent and burn out; costs go up for everyone; ROI is reduced or turns negative and projects fail.

A company might try various things to patch up these issues, all of which could be worthwhile in their own right: they could invest more in product-specific training. They could invest in marketing to highlight successes and double down on attracting talent interested in the FDE model. They could raise salaries to try to attract more of the experienced FDEs on the market. In the end, however, a missing foundation cannot easily be filled in retroactively, and the challenge is only greater as headcount and project count increases.

The Middle Way: Differentiation at Scale

The good news is that it is indeed possible to build a Forward Deployed Engineering team that is highly differentiated at scale. We believe that it simply requires consistent, focused effort to grow by hiring and developing the right types of employees in the right numbers, with few cheat codes or shortcuts along the way.

As we work to build the best possible Forward Deployed Engineering team at Fourth Age, we model this growth as looking more like a pagoda than a traditional consulting pyramid. This model begins with investing initially and carefully in a critical mass of experienced, hands-on, senior and mid-level FDEs with a range of strengths and an aligned vision. These relatively senior engineers will do three things to drive growth: they will work with customers to drive impact from day one, they will be actively pursuing ways to grow their own skills and responsibilities, and they will serve as the key scaffolding to support and develop more junior talent. The more junior talent, in turn, should be held to an equally high bar and hired in the right numbers to ensure they are able to learn by doing and build their intuition with the products as fast as possible, before they too can help build up the next generations of FDEs.

The key differentiator behind this model is that its core foundation relies on carefully selected senior FDEs supporting an appropriate number of equally impressive junior FDEs. The end result is a consistent level of execution and professional development that looks very different than models that rely on juniors supporting the rest of the pyramid, or one-off freelancer talent working to drive profits through their own billable hours alone.

The Palantir Case Study

Palantir has provided the primary case study that we see as supporting this unique model of growth. They proved that the Forward Deployed Engineer was uniquely valuable as they grew from a handful of FDEs helping deploy and develop their initial products nearly 20 years ago to something on the order of a couple thousand FDEs today (based off total headcount of 4,000 by the end of 2024) and a position among the most successful software companies in the world. While they have been able to recruit the best and brightest engineers and provide differentiated results to their customers over many years, their FDE headcount growth seems to average out to roughly around 100 people a year. This is a rate that, if continued, might get to Accenture’s headcount somewhere around the year 10,000 or so!

Throughout this growth, Palantir maintained a reputation for having some of the hardest interviews in tech and hiring FDEs only when they had extremely high conviction that the person had both the technical skills and the motivations to hit the ground running and grow into an all-star engineer over time. This in turn fed a culture where no matter how new or how tenured an FDE was, the assumption was that they could work side-by-side to simultaneously utilize their strengths and expose themselves to the fastest possible pace of learning and development.

As a high-growth, high-margin product company that has never wanted to be a consulting firm, Palantir’s growth has been limited more by their appetite than any hard constraints on the FDE model. Regardless, they demonstrated disciplined growth against an uncompromising vision over a long period of time, and have maintained the ability to deliver consistent, differentiated outcomes for their customers as they grew.

How to Replicate the Model

To scale this model to the services world, our view is that it’s not the rate of headcount growth or the rate of sales that presents the primary constraint to building differentiated teams. It is instead growth in the right quantity and quality of headcount that should ultimately drive sales, rather than the more typical case where sales drives headcount growth.

Our model ensures that a core foundation exists to allow every FDE to demonstrate and grow their impact, while maintaining consistently excellent delivery quality across the group as a whole. We believe a few nonnegotiable steps are required to achieve this type of sustainable growth:

  • Have a clear vision of what differentiates your team.
  • Hire talent that meets a high bar for technical skills and uncompromising alignment with the vision.
  • Train and develop talent through osmosis and mentorship rather than formal training and hierarchical career progressions.
  • Reward talent with access to the most challenging problems possible and increasing autonomy and ownership over time.

We will dive deeper into each of these steps in subsequent installments in the series.

Many companies will not go out of their way to invest the time and energy necessary to build a sustainable foundation around these steps. But whether they are newer niche consultancies, larger SIs, centers of excellence at large companies or FDE teams at other software companies, we believe that those who do follow this model will provide something in very high demand: a differentiated team of FDEs that consistently deliver what’s needed to thrive in today’s high-stakes world of software and AI.
