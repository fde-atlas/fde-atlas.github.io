Getting to Yes for FDEs: Negotiate Scope, Deadlines

FDE Times · 2026-10-26 · 6 min read

Summary

This article applies the 1981 negotiation classic Getting to Yes (Fisher, Ury, Patton; Penguin 2011, 240 pages) to FDE work. It argues that negotiating over positions—like haggling over a two-week deadline—produces poor outcomes, and that FDEs should instead uncover the interests behind client demands, know their BATNA before negotiating offers or scope, and follow PostHog-style practices like measurable outcomes and small scope. It also lists common mistakes, such as guessing client interests or asking "why" like an interrogation.

Key points

  • Positions are decisions; interests are the reasons behind them—negotiate at the interest level.
  • Asking why a deadline is two weeks often reshapes the whole project.
  • A BATNA tells you when to decline an offer or refuse vague scope.
  • PostHog requires measurable outcomes, timelines, and a shared definition of done.
  • Common mistakes: guessing interests, interrogating clients, and overcommitting after understanding them.

In brief

  • Getting to Yes (Fisher, Ury, Patton; Penguin edition 2011, 240 pages) argues that bargaining over positions produces unwise outcomes.
  • A position is what the client has decided; an interest is why they decided it. Good FDEs negotiate at the level of interests.
  • A BATNA sets the point at which you walk away, from an offer or from an overly broad project scope.

A client says: “We need this dashboard in two weeks.” An inexperienced FDE argues about the two weeks. An FDE who has read Getting to Yes asks why it is two weeks, and the answer often changes the whole project.

The book by Roger Fisher, William L. Ury and Bruce Patton was not written for engineers. Yet an FDE’s job is constant negotiation: over scope, deadlines, the definition of “done” and, before any of that, your own offer.

An old book that is still in print

The first edition appeared in 1981 from Houghton Mifflin, with only Fisher and Ury named as authors. Patton joined for later editions, including the 1991 edition and the 2011 one published by Penguin. The one to look for is the updated Penguin Books edition released on May 3, 2011, ISBN 9780143118756, at 240 pages.

The publisher pitches it as a step-by-step method of negotiation that works in any situation and reaches agreements that satisfy both sides. The blurb sounds grand, but the core of the book is compact: the three ideas below are enough to change how you work next week.

Idea one: don’t bargain over positions

The opening chapter, “Don’t Bargain Over Positions”, argues that positional bargaining produces unwise and inefficient outcomes. When each side holds on to a number, the conversation becomes a tug of war: whoever concedes less wins, and nobody touches the real problem.

Take the two-week tug of war. You propose four weeks, the client pushes for three, and you settle on three and a half. Nobody asked what the dashboard was for, so there is a fair chance you deliver, on time, something nobody opens. That is the “unwise outcome” the book warns about.

Idea two: interests sit beneath the demand

The book’s central line draws a sharp distinction: your position is something you have decided upon, while your interests are what caused you to decide.

Focusing on interests rather than positions is one of the four principles of what the authors call principled negotiation. Harvard’s Program on Negotiation adds a gloss: interests are the needs, motives and concerns that lie beneath surface demands.

Back to the example. You ask why two weeks, and suppose the client answers that the CEO needs a churn number for the board meeting. The interest is a reliable number, which a full dashboard is only one way to supply. A clean query plus one chart could be done in three days, with the complete dashboard moved to a later phase.

PostHog’s FDE handbook says the same thing in engineering language: the strongest move is often to correct the client’s mental model rather than build exactly what they asked for. You can only correct that model once you understand the interest behind it.

Idea three: know your alternative before you sit down

The book introduces the BATNA, the best alternative to a negotiated agreement. The logic is simple: people negotiate to produce something better than what they could get without negotiating. So any offer worse than your alternative should be turned down.

For an FDE offer, that means knowing exactly what you already have: your current job, another offer, or a chance to stay on your old team in a role closer to customers.

Consider a hypothetical case. You are a backend engineer at a stable company and receive an FDE offer that requires you to be on site with clients for most of the month. Your BATNA is staying in your current job, which is fine but keeps you away from customers.

The position that comes out most easily is “I don’t want to travel much.” Once you write down your real interests, you may find two: you need a few fixed days at home each week, and after a while you want to bring lessons from clients back into the product.

Those two interests open up things to trade. “I won’t travel” opens up only a tug of war.

So you might offer to accept the on-site schedule as described while fixing set remote days, and ask for a clear path into the product team. If the company cannot meet either request and the final offer is still worse than your current job, your BATNA gives you a clear reason to decline without guilt.

For project scope, an FDE’s BATNA is usually to refuse vague work. PostHog requires every engagement to have a measurable outcome, a rough timeline and a shared definition of “done” before implementation starts.

The company also advises keeping scope small, because expanding a tight scope is cheaper than unwinding a loose one, and sending a short brief for the client to confirm before committing.

What does misuse look like?

The first mistake is guessing the client’s interests for them. You decide “they probably just need the churn number” and cut the scope without checking. An interest the client has not confirmed is still only your hypothesis, and it easily hardens into a new position, this time yours.

The second mistake is asking “why” like an interrogation. The question should invite the client to talk about the pressure they are under, something like “Where will this number be used?”, instead of forcing them to defend their request.

The third mistake is understanding the interests and then taking on more work. Knowing what worries a client does not mean solving every worry in one engagement. PostHog’s advice on small scope is the brake for this. The last mistake is going into an offer negotiation without writing down your BATNA, then conceding bit by bit to avoid giving offense.

Who should read it, and in what order

If you are an engineer with two to eight years of experience about to move into an FDE role, or you have just taken on your first project, read this before any sales book. Start with “Don’t Bargain Over Positions” to see the trap, then the section on interests, then the section on the BATNA. At 240 pages, the whole book fits into a weekend.

Don’t read it just to underline, though. After each section, pull up a real client request and rewrite it in two columns: position and interest. Most scope arguments disappear once the second column is filled in correctly.

For your career, make this skill visible on your CV and in interviews. Instead of “worked with clients”, write one specific sentence: you narrowed an initial request to a smaller scope that still met the need, with a measurable result.

When reading FDE job descriptions, look for lines about settling scope or aligning expectations with clients, and have exactly one such story ready for when you are asked.

More than forty years after the first printing, the question Fisher and Ury posed is still the most valuable one in a first client meeting: “Why?”
