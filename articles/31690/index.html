What Goes Wrong in FDE Engagements

Reveneau · 2026-09-08 · 12 min read

Summary

Reveneau's editorial catalogs six recurring failure modes in forward deployed engineering engagements: the endless engagement, the demo that never reaches production, the engineer who drifts into account management, the unupgradable bespoke fork, the pilot never destined for production, and handover delivered as a slide deck. Each has a warning sign visible by roughly week three, and most stem from reasonable small decisions rather than bad actors. The common fix is assigning a named owner and a written date, since these are structural problems that harder work cannot solve.

Key points

  • Six failure modes, each detectable in the first month via warning signs, not status reports.
  • The endless engagement is most damaging; a production date moved twice is the signal.
  • The bespoke fork is worst because early signs look like success; costs surface two years later.
  • A pilot with no named production owner and no budget line was never going to production.
  • Every intervention shares a name and a date; failures are unassigned jobs.

Forward deployed engagements fail in a small number of recognisable ways, and most of them are visible by week three if you know the warning sign. The endless engagement. The demo that never becomes ready for production. The engineer who became an account manager. The bespoke fork nobody can upgrade. The pilot that was never going to production. And the handover that was a slide deck. Each has an early signal and a specific intervention, and none of them get fixed by the engineer working harder.

Published September 8, 2026. Editorial.

Key takeaways

  • Six failure modes, each with a warning sign visible in the first month. Watch the warning signs rather than the status report.
  • The endless engagement is the most damaging and the most comfortable. Its warning sign is a production date that has moved twice.
  • The bespoke fork is the one that causes damage only later: a16z describes the end state as deployments impossible to maintain or upgrade.
  • A pilot with no named production owner and no budget line beyond the pilot was never going to production, and that is knowable on day one.

These are the ways embedded engagements fail. Each one is recoverable when named early and expensive when discovered late.

One: the endless engagement

What it is. The engagement has no end. Scope grows by one reasonable request a week and the production date keeps moving later.

The warning sign. The production date has moved twice. The customer's own engineers have not touched the system. Both sides have started calling it a partnership rather than a deployment.

Why it happens. It is comfortable. The vendor has predictable revenue and a happy customer; the customer has capable people handling something they never staffed. Nobody is behaving badly, and the outcome is still a dependency.

The intervention. A handover date in writing, and a named inheriting engineer. Structural rather than cultural, because goodwill does not produce endings. Handover and exit covers it.

What it looks like from inside. Six months into an engagement, everyone involved would say it is going well. The vendor's engineer is trusted, responsive, and clearly good at the job. The system does more than it did at the start. Nobody would describe this as a problem in a status meeting, because nothing about it feels like a problem in a status meeting. The only way to see it is to ask a question the status meeting never asks: if this person left tomorrow, who on our side could keep this running. When the honest answer is nobody, the engagement has quietly become permanent, regardless of what the contract says.

Two: the demo that never becomes ready for production

What it is. An impressive prototype that cannot keep working in production, usually because the rest of the system came to depend on its shortcuts.

The warning sign. The demo gets better every month and the production date does not get closer. Nobody can say what the prototype fakes.

Why it happens. Week-one shortcuts are correct decisions: hardcode the credential, skip the pagination, query production directly. Then somebody senior sees the demo, the demo becomes the plan, and the shortcuts are in the critical path.

The intervention. Write down what the prototype fakes, in the same week you build it. Keep it small enough to discard. What a forward deployed engineer releases in week one covers the discipline.

A common version. Week one, the engineer connects directly to a production database because setting up a proper read replica would take three days they do not have, and the demo works. Week six, three other features have been built on top of that same connection because it was already there and worked. Week twelve, someone asks why the system cannot handle the customer's real data volume, and the answer is that the direct connection was never meant to survive contact with real load. Rebuilding the data access layer now means touching four features instead of one, which is the exact cost the written note in week one would have made visible before it compounded.

Three: the engineer who became an account manager

What it is. The embedded engineer's week fills with meetings, status updates and asking again and again for approvals, and the building stops.

The warning sign. The time split has moved away from the roughly 20 percent customer-facing and 80 percent building that First Round Review describes, toward half and half or worse.

Why it happens. Three causes, and it is worth knowing which. The scope is unclear so the engineer is establishing it in meetings. The customer's organization is blocking and the engineer became the person asking again and again for signatures. Or the role was presales all along and nobody said so, which the three jobs page shows is 30 percent of the market.

The intervention. Name an account owner on the vendor side and a person on the customer side who removes obstacles, so neither job goes to the builder.

How to tell the three causes apart. Look at the calendar rather than the complaint. If most meetings are the engineer asking clarifying questions about what to build, the scope was never set and someone needs to set it. If most meetings are the engineer chasing a signature, an approval, or an account, the customer's organization is the obstacle and needs a named person to clear it. If most meetings involve the customer's business stakeholders rather than its engineers, and there was never much code being written to begin with, the role was presales from the start and calling it something else earlier would have saved both sides a difficult conversation later.

Four: the bespoke fork nobody can upgrade

What it is. The deployment is its own codebase. It works, and it cannot take a platform upgrade without a project.

The warning sign. Nobody can say where shared product ends and custom code begins. The custom share grows with each account rather than shrinking.

Why it happens. No reusable platform underneath, and no owner for generalising. a16z's Marc Andrusko describes the end state as a large number of bespoke deployments that are impossible to maintain or upgrade, where revenue grows but no reusable work builds up.

The intervention. A regular productization process with a named owner, and a tracked ratio of shared to custom code per account. The productization loop and revenue per engineer are the mechanisms.

Why it is the worst one. The early signs look like success. Contracts are signed and customers are delighted, because each got software built exactly for them. The cost appears two years later.

Five: the pilot that was never going to production

What it is. A pilot runs, works, impresses people, and stops. Nobody cancelled it and nobody released it.

The warning sign, available on day one. There is no named production owner, no budget line beyond the pilot, and no answer to what has to be true for this to reach production.

Why it happens. Pilots are easy to fund and production is hard to fund, so a pilot becomes the thing that gets approved. MIT NANDA's July 2025 study reported that 95 percent of generative AI pilots delivered no measurable profit-and-loss impact, drawing on 52 executive interviews, 153 leader surveys and 300 public deployments. That number describes this failure across many companies.

The intervention. Ask the three questions before starting. If any answer is missing, the honest move is to fix that first, because a pilot with no route to production is a demo with a budget.

Why this one is so common. A pilot needs a smaller decision than a production rollout. It usually needs one manager's sign-off rather than a change advisory board, a security review and a budget committee, so it gets approved quickly and the production step gets skipped over in the excitement of a working demo. The fix is to require the same three answers, a named owner, a budget line, and a stated path to production, at the moment the pilot itself is approved, rather than waiting until the pilot succeeds and discovering that nobody thought about what happens next.

Six: the handover that was a slide deck

What it is. The vendor left, documents were delivered, and the system was handed back as an emergency within a quarter.

The warning sign. No engineer on the customer's side has ever changed the system alone. The handover artefact contains no runnable tests.

Why it happens. Handover gets treated as a documentation task at the end rather than a capability transfer that starts at production.

The intervention. The customer's engineer releases a real change unaided, through the real release path, before the vendor leaves, and the vendor watches rather than helps. Schedule it a few weeks before the handover date so there is time to fix what it exposes.

How to use this list

Read it at week three and again at the halfway point. Check the warning signs rather than the status report, because every one of these failure modes can exist alongside a status report that says everything is fine for a while.

And notice what the interventions have in common: a name and a date. Almost every failure here is somebody's job that nobody was assigned.

None of these six require a difficult vendor or a difficult customer to occur. Every example above involves reasonable people making reasonable decisions in the moment: skip the read replica this week, let the pilot's manager approve it alone, let the demo become the plan since it is working. The failures accumulate from small, sensible choices rather than from anyone acting in bad faith, which is exactly why checking a status report is not enough. A status report asks whether things are going well this week. These six questions ask whether the structure underneath is quietly building a different outcome than the one anyone intended.

Best for

  • A week-three or mid-engagement health check
  • Diagnosing an engagement that feels wrong while the status report says everything is fine
  • Deciding what to put in a contract to prevent the predictable failures

Avoid if

  • You have not started and want the qualifying questions instead

Check before you decide

  • Has the production date moved more than once
  • Can anyone state what the prototype fakes, in writing
  • Is the engineer's week still roughly 80 percent building
  • Can anyone say where shared product ends and custom code begins
  • Is there a named production owner and a budget line beyond the pilot
  • Has a customer engineer changed the system unaided

Common questions

What is the most common way a forward deployed engagement fails?

The endless engagement. Scope grows by one reasonable request a week, the production date keeps moving later, and the customer's own engineers never touch the system. It is the most damaging failure and the most comfortable one, because neither side is behaving badly and both are getting something they value.

How early can you spot these failures?

Most are visible by week three if you watch the warning signs rather than the status report. Every failure mode here can exist alongside a status report that says everything is fine for a while, which is exactly why the warning signs matter: a moved production date, an undocumented prototype, a time split moving away from 20/80, an unclear custom-versus-shared boundary.

Why is the bespoke fork the worst failure mode?

Because the early signs look like success. Contracts are signed and customers are delighted, since each got software built exactly for them. The problem appears two years later when a platform upgrade has to be applied across many forks and nobody can do it. a16z describes the end state as deployments impossible to maintain or upgrade.

How do you know a pilot will never reach production?

Three signs available on day one: no named production owner, no budget line beyond the pilot, and no answer to what has to be true for this to reach production. If any of those is missing, fix it before starting, because a pilot with no route to production is a demo with a budget.

What do all the fixes have in common?

A name and a date. An account owner, a person who removes obstacles, an inheriting engineer, a productization owner, a production date, a handover date. Almost every failure in this list is somebody's job that nobody was assigned, which is why the interventions are structural rather than motivational.

How do you tell if an engineer has become an account manager?

Check the time split against the roughly 20 percent customer-facing and 80 percent building that First Round Review describes. When it moves to half and half, diagnose which of three causes applies: unclear scope, an organization that is blocking, or a role that was presales from the start.

How a forward deployed engagement runs

A forward deployed engagement runs in five phases: discovery inside the environment, a prototype against real data, deployment to production, productizing what turned out to be reusable, and scaling back so the customer's own team runs it. The first three are what everyone plans. The fourth is what turns one customer's work into a product. The fifth is the one that gets skipped, and skipping it is the difference between a deployment and a dependency. AWS describes a similar three-phase structure for its partner programme, ending with the partner working independently.

The first two weeks of a forward deployed engagement

The first two weeks decide the pace of everything after them. By day ten a working engagement has released something small against real data, written down what correct means, and mapped the path a change takes to production. The most common way to lose those two weeks is access: an engineer waiting on repository credentials is the most expensive unused resource in the company. Three things have to exist before day one, and all three are the customer's to provide.

Discovery inside the customer environment

A discovery call gives you what the customer can describe. Being inside the environment gives you what nobody thought to mention, and that second category is where deployments fail. Marty Cagan's argument for the embedded model is that it is a product discovery technique rather than a delivery technique: engineers who visit multiple customer sites see the similarities and differences across a market, which is the essence of customer discovery. The practical version is that the environment is the source of the information, and you cannot study it from outside.

What a forward deployed engineer ships in week one

A forward deployed engineer should ship (release into real use) something small and working against real data in the first week. Not a mockup and not a demo on a small, selected set of data: one transform, one screen reading live records, one integration returning a real response. The point is diagnostic rather than promotional. What breaks when you try to release something tiny tells you more about the engagement than any planning document, because it makes you work with the data, the permissions and the release path all at once.

Handover and exit from a forward deployed engagement

The ending of a forward deployed engagement has to be designed at the start, with a date in the contract at signature. An engagement whose conclusion is decided later does not have one: it keeps going, because there is always one more improvement and both sides find the arrangement comfortable. AWS named customer self-sufficiency after the engagement as a design goal of its own programme, and its partner model ends with the partner team working independently. The test of a handover is not whether documents were delivered. It is whether the customer's own engineer has changed the system, alone, before the vendor left.
