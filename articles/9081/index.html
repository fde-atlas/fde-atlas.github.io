Week 2: Connecting Strategic Goals with Frontline Reality

Original title: 第 2 周：连接战略目标与一线真实工作

FDE 成长手册 · 2026-08-25 · 22 min read

Summary

Week 2 of this FDE handbook teaches the first loop of customer discovery through a fictional B2B company, “北辰协作”. The author argues FDEs must neither accept a sponsor's strategy at face value nor merely collect frontline complaints: identify the business decision the boss must make, reconstruct a real recent task with a frontline employee, then link both layers on one evidence chain. All notes use six tags—quote, observation, interpretation, hypothesis, decision, unknown—to keep claims from masquerading as facts. Deliverables are a current-state workflow v0, a business problem tree, and a week-3 research plan, not a technical solution. Sample dialogues show how to separate management targets (e.g., a 30% cost reduction) from measured facts, and why employee workarounds may be rational adaptations rather than errors to automate.

Key points

  • Week 2 is only the first discovery loop; outputs are hypotheses and workflow v0, not solutions.
  • Six evidence tags (Q/O/I/H/D/U) separate quotes, observations, interpretations, hypotheses, decisions, and unknowns.
  • Sponsor interviews should surface the pending business decision and split targets from verified facts.
  • Frontline interviews replay a recent real task; workarounds may serve as risk controls.
  • An evidence translation board links sponsor assumptions to frontline observations while keeping competing explanations.

Direct answer: When doing customer discovery, an FDE should neither only listen to the boss's strategy nor only collect frontline employees' complaints. You must first find out what business decision the boss is preparing to make, then look at real tasks frontline employees recently completed, and finally put both sides' accounts onto the same chain of evidence. The outcome of Week 2 is not a technical solution, but a first version of the workflow, business hypotheses, and the next round of research plan.

First, be clear: Week 2 is only the first iteration of Discovery

Heading "First, be clear: Week 2 is only the first iteration of Discovery"

The 24-week standard roadmap schedules customer discovery and problem definition in Weeks 2–4; the 20-week and 28-week versions are just different paces. The three-week progression is:

WeekKey questionsWhat can be formed this weekWhat this week cannot pretend to be
Week 2Why does the boss want change? How does the frontline work now?First batch of verbatim quotes, observations, workflow v0, hypotheses and unknownsComplete Discovery, final problem definition
Week 3Do the initial signals hold across more roles and data?More interviews, log sampling, anomaly and baseline evidenceProven ROI, final technical solution
Week 4Which problem is worth moving to the next phase?Discovery Brief, problem definition, draft metrics, scope and risksFinished software or production commitments

The GOV.UK Discovery guide notes that Discovery has no fixed length; typical public service projects commonly take 4–8 weeks. This course's three weeks is a constrained teaching arrangement and does not mean real enterprise Discovery can always be completed in three weeks.

This week's case: two realities behind one phrase "add AI"

Heading "This week's case: two realities behind one phrase 'add AI'"

We continue using the fictional B2B software company "Beichen Collaboration". All companies, people, conversations, tickets and numbers below are teaching simulation materials and do not represent real customers or project results.

The business owner says:

"Customer service costs keep rising. We must add AI this quarter, ideally automatically answering most tickets."

The frontline support agent says:

"The current system is too hard to use; for every question I have to hunt for information everywhere."

Beginners tend to stitch the two statements into:

Build an AI auto-reply system that solves hard-to-find information and reduces costs.

This is not problem definition; it ties two unverified statements and a preset solution together. What Week 2 must do is take them apart:

Use only six evidence tags throughout the week

Heading "Use only six evidence tags throughout the week"

To avoid "feels like fact", all notes are uniformly tagged:

TagMeaningExampleWhat it cannot prove
[Q]Someone's verbatim quote or statement[Q] The support lead says tickets keep increasingCannot prove ticket volume is actually growing
[O]An observable, verifiable event in observations, logs or approved materials[O] Three documents opened during one taskCannot prove all tasks are like this
[I]The researcher's interpretation of evidence[I] Version checking may be a source of time lossCannot directly become a requirement
[H]A prediction that later decisions depend on but is not yet verified[H] Improving version identification will shorten handling timeCannot be written as expected benefit
[D]A decision or constraint confirmed by an authorized role[D] No auto-sending in phase oneDoes not mean this decision will never change
[U]A question still unknown that may change direction[U] What proportion of tickets are specialCannot be filled in with guesses

The most important sentence is:

"An employee said X" can be a [Q]; "X holds across the whole organization" is still not a fact.

When there are no real users: use the role-play material pack

Heading "When there are no real users: use the role-play material pack"

Reading a completed example only counts as "observing an expert demonstration" and cannot pass as having done customer research. When there are no real users, find two peers to play the business owner and the support agent respectively; have the facilitator privately distribute role cards, and learners can only obtain information by asking questions. Mark all artifacts as "Beichen Collaboration teaching simulation".

Facilitator only: business owner role card

Opening line only: "Customer service costs keep rising. We must add AI this quarter, ideally automatically answering most tickets."

Only reveal the following when the learner asks about the relevant topic:

  • What this phase really supports is the next decision at the end of Week 4, not immediate approval of a production system;
  • "Reduce by 30%" is a management target with no workflow-based calculation;
  • Customer growth comes from sales forecasts; ticket growth needs to be verified against the support system;
  • "Slow responses hurt renewals" has no direct evidence; the customer success team may have complaints and renewal reasons;
  • Hopes value shows up as delayed hiring or controlling backlog, not laying off existing staff;
  • Does not accept auto-sending, expired policies, or ordinary agents seeing supervisor clauses;
  • Ticket data is owned by the support lead, budget by finance, permissions by IT;
  • If evidence does not support AI, can accept improving processes and data first, continuing to gather evidence, or stopping.

If the learner directly pitches RAG, agents, or promises ROI, follow up: "What is your basis for judging it can affect costs?" Do not proactively fill in evidence for them.

Facilitator only: frontline support agent role card

Opening line only: "The current system is too hard to use; for every question I have to hunt for information everywhere."

Only reveal the following when the learner asks about recent tasks, asks to walk through steps, or asks about exceptions:

  • The most recent ordinary ticket was a price-difference issue after a plan upgrade;
  • First check the plan, region, purchase time and tags, then open the customer record and knowledge base;
  • The search terms were "plan upgrade, price difference, refund", which opened two or three documents with similar titles;
  • Will check effective dates and the responsible person; for some special tasks also checks the supervisor group's pinned messages;
  • "Six or seven minutes" is just recollection; there was a phone call in between, so it can't be treated as actual timing;
  • For high-amount refunds, the supervisor policy isn't visible, so you can only ask the supervisor;
  • Personal notes store frequently used links and escalation rules; the advantage is speed, the risk is staleness;
  • Before sending, check the date, region, and source; escalate when encountering high amounts, special customers, or conflicts;
  • Two weeks ago, before sending, I noticed an old reply no longer applied and rewrote it myself.

If the learner asks "What AI feature do you want?", answer "Just make search faster", but do not proactively explain the real process; have the observer record this leading question.

Day 2: Talking to the Boss About Decisions, Value, and Trade-offs

Title "Day 2: Talking to the Boss About Decisions, Value, and Trade-offs"

A boss interview is not about getting them to approve your technical solution. It aims to obtain three things:

  • A business decision that is about to be made;
  • A tree of business questions that has not yet been validated;
  • Assumptions that need further verification with frontline, finance, data, and risk roles.

First, watch a failed interview

Title "First, Watch a Failed Interview"

Boss: We want an AI customer service agent within four weeks, ideally cutting customer service costs by 30%. FDE: Sure, we'll use RAG plus an agent and connect the knowledge base first. Boss: It also needs to be secure. FDE: We'll add guardrails and access controls; efficiency should improve by at least 40%. Boss: The supervisor says agents spend most of their time looking up documents. FDE: Then a vector database is exactly the fix for that bottleneck. Boss: Give me a proposal next week. FDE: Okay, I'll start designing the architecture and budget.

It has five obvious problems:

  • Treating the boss's proposed solution directly as the problem definition;
  • Treating "cut 30%" as an existing calculation;
  • Fabricating "improve 40%" without a baseline or adoption mechanism;
  • Not asking what decision actually needs to be made in four weeks;
  • Treating what the boss heard about the frontline as working fact.

Full example: how to respectfully deconstruct a strategic mandate

Title "Full Example: How to Respectfully Deconstruct a Strategic Mandate"

FDE: You mentioned "build an AI customer service agent within four weeks." Before discussing solutions, what business decision do you need to make by the end of this phase? Boss: I need to decide whether to keep investing in the pilot, and I also want to judge whether customer service can avoid expanding headcount in proportion to business growth next year. FDE: Those are two different decisions. Which is the higher priority right now: whether to invest in a controlled pilot, or freezing the hiring plan? Boss: First, decide whether to invest in the pilot. The hiring plan can't be frozen directly yet. FDE: Understood. Not growing headcount is a strategic intent, not a commitment for this phase. What happened recently that made this decision urgent? Boss: Enterprise customers are expected to keep growing; agents report more and more tickets, and response times are slowing. FDE: What data sources back "customer growth," "more tickets," and "slower responses" respectively? Boss: Customer growth comes from sales forecasts; ticket volume is in the customer service system; slower responses was mentioned by the supervisor at the weekly meeting. FDE: I'll list the first two as data to be retrieved and record the third as a management statement. What business consequence are you most worried it will cause? Boss: Enterprise customers waiting too long could affect renewals; continued agent growth will squeeze gross margin. FDE: Is there currently any evidence linking response time to renewals? Boss: No direct evidence; the customer success team may have records of complaints and renewal reasons. FDE: Then "affects renewals" is recorded as a hypothesis for now. Does the 30% cost reduction come from an existing calculation, or is it a management target? Boss: It's a management target; there's no separate calculation for the customer service process. FDE: Even if employees spend a few minutes less per ticket, if wages, overtime, or hiring plans don't change, it doesn't equal a cash cost reduction. How do you want the value to ultimately be realized? Boss: Mainly by deferring planned hires while controlling backlog, not by laying off existing staff. FDE: We'll need to verify hiring budget and timing with finance, and confirm capacity and backlog with the supervisor. Which step of the process do you think wastes the most time? Boss: I've heard it's looking up policies, but I haven't observed it myself. FDE: I'll record "policy lookup is the main bottleneck" as a boss hypothesis, to be validated through frontline interviews and process observation. Besides speed, what must not get worse? Boss: We can't cite outdated policies, regular agents can't see supervisor clauses, and no auto-sending in phase one. FDE: If later evidence doesn't support the current assumptions, what changes, alternatives, or stop outcomes would you accept? Boss: Yes. I need the problem solved; it doesn't have to be AI. FDE: Who owns the ticket data, hiring budget, and permission information? Who decides whether to enter the pilot? Boss: The supervisor owns ticket data, finance holds the budget, IT manages permissions; the customer service lead and I decide together. FDE: Let me read back: what we're currently supporting is "whether to invest in a pilot for a narrow customer service process," not immediately freezing hiring. Growth, backlog, policy lookup, and renewal impact need to be verified separately; 30% is a management target, not a projected benefit. The confirmed boundaries are: no auto-sending, no exposing restricted policies, and not trading speed for errors. Accurate? Boss: Basically accurate. Enterprise customer service risk should come before the cost target.

This conversation did five things: found the decision, separated facts from hypotheses, traced how value is realized, confirmed outcomes that cannot be sacrificed, and identified the next evidence provider.

The Five Business Questions

Title "The Five Business Questions"

  • Decision: By the end of this phase, who must decide between which options?
  • Why now: What event made it a current priority? What is the opportunity cost of not doing it?
  • Value mechanism: Will the outcome be realized through capacity, revenue, risk, or real spending changes?
  • Trade-offs: Which quality, customer, safety, or organizational outcomes must not get worse?
  • Evidence: Which are sponsor judgments, and which need verification by finance, data, or frontline roles?

Objection branch: the boss insists AI must be adopted

Title "Objection Branch: The Boss Insists AI Must Be Adopted"

Have the person playing the boss pick any one objection, then press for three consecutive rounds:

Boss: The board has already said we must adopt AI; stop questioning the direction. FDE: I'll record "must demonstrate an AI direction" as a confirmed constraint, while not treating business impact as proven. To make the decision in four weeks credible, which change do you most need to see, and what kind of failure would make you stop? Boss: First give me an ROI showing 30% savings. FDE: The 30% can remain a management target. We're still missing task volume, time that can be freed up, adoption rate, and how value is realized; I can't write the target as a projected benefit. Can we confirm today the owners of these data and the permitted definitions? Boss: I have to report next week; there's no time to wait for complete data. FDE: I can submit "knowns, unknowns, risks, and minimal evidence-gathering actions" on time, but I won't fill in ROI with guesses. We can define next week's decision as whether to continue gathering evidence, not whether to fully commit. Do you accept this decision boundary?

Pass criteria: neither positively rejecting the boss's strategic constraints nor escalating goals, deadlines, or numbers into facts; and still ending with decision boundaries and evidence owners.

Day 3: Walk through the most recent task with a frontline employee

Title "Day 3: Walk through the most recent task with a frontline employee"

The goal of frontline interviews is not to collect feature wish lists, but to reconstruct real behavior. When an employee says "this is how it usually goes," go back to the most recent specific task.

Ethical hard gates before the interview

Title "Ethical hard gates before the interview"

  • Explain that the research is not a performance review, and the direct supervisor does not sit in;
  • Obtain authorization from the organization or data owner before observing; when there is no right to view real data, only sandboxes, anonymized replays, or synthetic materials may be used;
  • Obtain consent for recording, allowing the employee to skip, correct, or withdraw content;
  • Do not secretly record, screenshot, or copy personal information, customer data, keys, or restricted text;
  • Do not ask employees to bypass permissions, display restricted content, or open unauthorized personal notes;
  • Have employees use anonymized cases, and if necessary only review the steps;
  • Explain how the record will be used and who can see it.

If any item is not met, this record cannot be used as course evidence.

Full example: Reviewing a billing ticket

Title "Full example: Reviewing a billing ticket"

FDE: Today I'd like to understand how you actually handle tickets. This is not a performance review, nor validation of an already-decided solution. The record will only keep approved work steps, no customer information; only the research team will see it. You can skip questions, stop at any time, and withdraw the record after the session. Is that okay? Customer service rep Zhou Ning: Okay. FDE: Please pick an account or billing ticket from yesterday or today that you remember clearly and have already finished handling, and walk me through it starting from when it entered the queue. Zhou Ning: Yesterday there was a price-difference issue after a plan upgrade. I first looked at the customer's plan and payment records, then went to the knowledge base to find the upgrade and refund policies. FDE: Which fields did you look at first? Which system did you open after that? Zhou Ning: Plan, region, purchase time, and ticket tags. Then I opened the customer record and the knowledge base. FDE: Do you remember your first set of search terms? Could you demonstrate with an anonymized case? Zhou Ning: "Plan upgrade, price difference, refund." A lot of documents with very similar titles come up. FDE: After the search results appeared, what did you do? Zhou Ning: I opened two or three and checked the effective dates and owners. Sometimes the pinned group message is updated faster than the knowledge base, so I check that too. FDE: Yesterday, what did you ultimately base your judgment on for which document to use? Zhou Ning: One was from last year, and the other said it took effect this month. I also checked the supervisor's pinned message to confirm the policy switch. FDE: Roughly how long from the first search to selecting the policy? Is that from system records or from memory? Zhou Ning: I'd estimate six or seven minutes; that's from memory. I also took a phone call in between. FDE: Then let's record it as "interviewee's estimate," not as a measured duration. What about the most recent ticket that didn't go smoothly following this process? Zhou Ning: A large special refund. I found the policy but didn't have permission, so I had to ask my supervisor, who was in a meeting at the time. FDE: How did you know you should go to your supervisor? Where is this rule written? Zhou Ning: A senior colleague told me during training, and I noted it in my personal notes. I'm not sure whether it's written in the official process. FDE: What problem does that note solve? When does it become outdated? Zhou Ning: It's much faster, but if the policy changes and the note isn't updated, it might still be the old one. FDE: Before clicking send, what makes you confident? What situations make you stop and ask someone? Zhou Ning: I need to see the effective date, applicable region, and source. For high amounts, special customers, or policy conflicts, I ask my supervisor. FDE: When was the most recent time a reply was blocked or sent back for rework before sending? Who caught it? Zhou Ning: About two weeks ago, I myself noticed before sending that a copied old reply was inconsistent with the current policy and rewrote it; if it had already been sent, the supervisor usually coordinates the correction and customer communication. FDE: Both of these handling methods still need to be verified with the supervisor, so I'll keep them as your statements for now. FDE: Let me read back: the reply text is not the only time-consuming part; you confirm versions across the customer record, the knowledge base, and some group notifications. Special refunds go into the supervisor path, and some rules rely on training and personal notes. We don't yet know the overall frequency of these situations, and we haven't measured the time for each step. Is that right? Zhou Ning: Basically right, but not all tickets involve checking three places; only some special tasks add extra sources. FDE: I'll correct that scope. Next, I'd like to walk through at least one anonymized task and check whether the tags support frequency analysis; if there's capacity, we'll add one exception task.

Note the correction in the last sentence. Without the read-back, the FDE would likely have exaggerated "some special tasks" into "all tickets."

Watch once, ask once, verify once

Title "Watch once, ask once, verify once"

  • Watch once: In the first pass, interrupt as little as possible; only record triggers, actions, systems, waits, and visible outcomes.
  • Ask once: At safe pauses, probe for judgment criteria, alternative paths, exceptions, and responsible parties.
  • Verify once: Read back along the timeline, letting the employee correct omissions, parallel tasks, and misinterpretations.

Day 4: Put the boss's assumptions and frontline evidence together

Title "Day 4: Put the Boss's Assumptions and Frontline Evidence Together"

How to handle contradictions

Title "How to Handle Contradictions"

The boss may say "employees resist the new system," while frontline employees may say "the new system doesn't have current prices and requires repeated data entry." An FDE should not take sides or frame the contradiction as a political issue, but should record:

- 管理层解释：[Q] 采用问题来自员工抗拒改变- 一线解释：[Q] 缺少当前数据且需要重复录入- 已观察证据：无- [U] 尚未观察一次完整任务- 竞争假设：[H] 培训 / 数据完整性 / 重复工作 / 激励与责任- 下一步：观察一次完整任务，核对采用日志和字段缺失，分别访谈流程与数据所有者

Keeping different explanations on record is more professional than quickly writing up a single "root cause."

Day 5: Produce a v0, don't pretend Discovery is done

Title "Day 5: Produce a v0, Don't Pretend Discovery Is Done"

Draw the current process, not the future system

Title "Draw the Current Process, Not the Future System"

current-workflow-v0.md

Include at least:

触发 → 输入 → 一线动作 → 使用的系统/材料 → 人工判断     → 交接/等待 → 结果 → 错误如何被发现 → 谁负责修复

Based on this week's materials, an acceptable v0 could be expressed like this:

[O] 工单进入队列，客服查看套餐、地区、购买时间和标签  → [O] 打开客户记录与知识库，输入搜索词  → [O] 打开多篇文档，比较生效日期与负责人  → [O] 本次任务又查看群置顶，随后选定一篇作为依据  → [O] 撰写草稿，对照日期与地区并记录来源  → [O] 发送回复，进入等待客户状态
异常分支（目前只来自受访者陈述）：[Q] 高金额或政策冲突  → [Q] 普通客服没有主管政策权限  → [Q] 联系主管，等待期间处理其他工单  → [U] 主管怎样批准、受限内容怎样安全传递、错误怎样统一修复
个人工具与返工分支（目前只来自受访者陈述）：[Q] 客服用个人笔记保存常用链接和升级规则  → [Q] 笔记可能因政策更新而过期  → [Q] 两周前曾在发送前发现旧回复不适用并自行重写  → [U] 个人工具的使用范围、正式更新责任，以及发送后错误由谁统一处理

The normal path comes from one synthetic observation, and the exception branch comes from one interview recollection, so they cannot be expressed with the same level of confidence.

Requirements:

  • Draw only the current actual process; no AI assistant or future features;
  • Mark waiting, rework, human judgment, permissions, and personal tools;
  • Connect each step to [Q], [O], or [U];
  • Title clearly states v0 / to be validated;
  • Attach the revision record after employee read-back.

Schedule Week 3 evidence-gathering actions

week-03-research-plan.md

# 第 3 周研究计划
| 优先级 | `[U]` 未知问题 | 为什么会改变方向 | 需要的角色或材料 | 最小核验动作 | 负责人 / 日期 || --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- || P0 |  |  |  |  |  |

Write at least 5 items, which must cover business value, normal process, exception frequency, data definitions, and permissions or risk. Actions must be specific, e.g., "extract two weeks of ticket labels and stratify by type"; don't just write "continue research."

Write a 90-second business read-back without exaggeration

Title "Write a 90-Second Business Read-Back Without Exaggeration"

Completed example:

This week we completed one business owner interview, one customer service interview, and observed one anonymized task. In this billing case, customer service cross-checked versions between the knowledge base and group messages, taking 5 minutes 52 seconds from first query to selecting a basis; the interviewee also described the permission and supervisor confirmation path for high-amount refunds. Content versions, permissions, and handoffs have become competing explanations that must be checked in parallel with "auto-writing replies," but one person and one observation cannot represent all tickets. In Week 3 we will sample ticket timestamps, follow up with supervisors and knowledge owners, and confirm the frequency and impact of exception paths. Until this evidence is complete, we do not recommend freezing an AI solution or committing to cost-benefit figures.

This paragraph simultaneously tells the boss: what was found, how strong the evidence is, what remains unknown, and why the next step is worth doing.

Independent transfer exercise: supply chain exception handling

Heading "Independent transfer exercise: supply chain exception handling"

A supply chain team lead says:

"We need AI to predict delays in advance."

A planner says:

"The ERP data is never accurate, so I have to redo everything myself every day."

An anonymized teaching record was observed:

14:02 计划员收到延误提醒14:04 从 ERP 导出订单 CSV14:07 打开承运商门户，发现本案例的更新时间比 ERP 晚两小时14:10 使用个人 Excel 关联两份数据14:14 询问仓库实际装车状态14:19 根据仓库回复修改风险等级14:22 把结果发给区域经理

Your task:

  • Break the material into [Q/O/I/H/D/U];
  • Write five business questions to ask the team lead;
  • Write six non-leading questions to ask the planner;
  • Draw the current process v0, and identify two workarounds and their current value;
  • Propose only three verification actions for week 3; do not propose a system solution.

View the answer checkpoints

  • "The ERP data is never accurate" is only a [Q], not an overall fact.
  • This case has four information sources: ERP, the carrier portal, personal Excel, and warehouse messages; this is [O].
  • "Sync delays may affect judgment" is [I]; "real-time integration will shorten processing" is [H].
  • No [D] yet: the material does not give any decision or constraint confirmed by an authorized role; do not invent one just to fill the category.
  • [U] includes the overall frequency of ERP delays, the business consequences of wrong judgments, the data owners, and who has the authority to change the process.
  • CSV plus personal Excel and sending messages to the warehouse are both workarounds, but they may also serve data verification and responsibility confirmation.
  • Reasonable next steps include sampling and comparing order timestamps, interviewing warehouse roles, and checking delay and escalation logs.
  • The five business questions should each cover: the decision to be supported, why now, how value will be delivered, what cannot be sacrificed, and who will provide the evidence.
  • If your business questions already mention "prediction models, real-time integration, or automatic notifications," you are still pitching features and need to rewrite them.
  • Directly suggesting a prediction model, eliminating Excel, or automatic notifications all fail this week's requirements.
