What an FDE Should Do in Their First Week in the Field

Original title: FDEが現場で最初の1週間にやること

日本Fde協会 · 2026-09-05 · 5 min read

Summary

This article from the Japan FDE Association argues that a Forward Deployed Engineer's first week on-site largely determines a project's success. Rather than jumping into development, the first week should build trust and gather evidence for what to build—and what not to build. It lays out a day-by-day approach: observe work firsthand before interviewing, quantify problems in hours and costs, consider governance and access control early, and end the week by showing a small working prototype to test priorities through real reactions.

Key points

  • The first week's goal is earning trust, not solving problems immediately.
  • Days 1–2: shadow workers to uncover undocumented manual steps interviews miss.
  • Days 3–4: quantify tasks in monthly hours and decide what not to build.
  • Include governance, permissions, and audit trails from the start to avoid rework.
  • Day 5: show one small quick-win prototype and let reactions set priorities.

How an FDE spends their first week largely determines the success or failure of the entire project. As the Japan FDE Association, speaking from the position of practicing both technical and business aspects in the field, we have organized how to approach this kickoff phase. By reading this article, you will understand what to observe, who to talk to, and what to build immediately after arriving on-site, and you will be able to apply it directly to your own project. Here, FDE refers to Forward Deployed Engineer (an engineer who embeds themselves in the customer's site), not the abbreviation for disk encryption. Let's think through this together.

Goals for an FDE's First Week

The goal of the first week is not to jump straight into solving problems. It is to earn the trust that "this person understands our operations" and to gather the information needed for decisions later. If you skip this and start developing, you will end up building features the field doesn't actually need.

It's Also a Week for Deciding What NOT to Build

We sometimes hear from clients, "It was a shock when they first asked me, 'Do you really need that?'" To stop unnecessary development, you need time to see the actual state of operations with your own eyes. We view the first week as a period for gathering both the reasons to build and the reasons not to build.

Days 1-2: Observe the Work with Your Own Eyes

Starting on day one by filling out an interview sheet is too early. First, begin by having the field show you how things normally run, right beside them. People struggle to put their difficulties into words, and manual tasks their hands perform automatically are precisely the ones they are least aware of.

Track Who Touches What, in What Order

At one manufacturing client's site, we spent a day watching a shipping and receiving clerk's screen. We discovered an unofficial step where they would first transcribe data into Excel to check it before entering it into the core system. The person considered it "just routine work," and it never came up in interviews. Observation first, questions second—this order works.

Note Down Terminology and Key People

Every field has its own jargon. It's not uncommon for the same report to be called by different names in different departments. Writing down a glossary and the roles of stakeholders at this stage prevents misunderstandings later in design. For deepening your interviews, FDE Interview Techniques is also a helpful reference.

Days 3-4: Organize Problems with Numbers

Measure the tasks observed during observation in terms of man-hours and cost. We value the principle of backing proposals with ROI, cost, and man-hours. Without numbers, the field's vague sense of "it's kind of hard" cannot be translated into priorities.

Start with Monthly Work Hours

Methodically stack up answers to "How many minutes does that task take per instance, and how many times per month?" In the manufacturing example above, transcription and checking alone were consuming over ten hours a month. Once you have the numbers, conversations align instantly, whether reporting to management or reaching agreement with the field. Speaking with numbers speeds up decisions.

Decide What Not to Do

PerspectiveWhat to Look At
FrequencyDoes it happen daily, or only occasionally?
Man-hoursTime per instance × number of instances
DependencyCan only specific people do it?
GovernanceDoes it require organizing permissions or logs?

Include Governance from the First Week

How to handle permissions, logs, and audit trails becomes rework if added later. We heard from a SaaS company executive that outsourcing governance compliance after growth cost them many times the original estimate. Confirming "who is allowed to touch this task" during the first week's organization phase makes design much easier.

Day 5: Show Something Small That Works

Close out the week not with a perfect requirements document, but with a working prototype. Showing something that works before aiming for perfection and getting feedback as fast as possible accelerates the kickoff.

Pick One Quick Win

Limit what you build in the first week to a single item with small impact but visible results. From our startup experience, if you start designing a big system first, time passes without earning the field's trust. Even a small script that semi-automates transcription work, if the field feels "this helps," will dramatically deepen subsequent interviews. For how to build prototypes, see FDE Prototyping Techniques for details.

Show It and Confirm Priorities from the Reactions

When you show the prototype, the field will tell you honestly, "Actually, this first." These reactions are the best material for setting priorities from week two onward. Building is not the goal; you build to verify the right order. We will dig into day-by-day approaches in future articles.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q. Should an FDE start development right away in the first week? It's better to avoid jumping into full-scale development immediately. We recommend spending the first week on observing operations and quantifying problems, then showing a small working prototype on day five to confirm priorities.

Q. The field can't articulate their problems well. Observing the work beside them is more effective than interviews. Unconscious manual tasks in particular never get mentioned verbally, so tracking who touches what, in what order, reveals hidden processes.

Q. Do we need to consider governance and security from the first week? Yes. Permissions and log organization tend to cause rework if added later, so confirming "who is allowed to touch this task" during the first week's organization phase makes subsequent design easier.
