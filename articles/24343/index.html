Summary

Written by Shohei Tokumitsu, CEO of DeFactory, this article examines seven failure patterns behind Scrum adoption problems in Japanese IT teams: scope creep, skill gaps within teams, dysfunctional Product Owners, meeting overload, knowledge silos from missing documentation, lack of organizational buy-in, and botched migrations from waterfall. It argues that with adoption above 20% yet nearly half of projects failing, the root cause is ceremonial adoption without understanding Scrum's rules. Each pattern gets concrete countermeasures such as release planning, strict timeboxes, embedding documentation in the Definition of Done, and pilot-team rollouts. The article also contrasts Scrum with waterfall, lists project types suited to each, and closes with freelance market rates for Scrum-related roles in Japan.

Key points

  • Nearly half of Scrum adoptions fail, mostly due to superficial, form-only implementation.
  • Seven failure patterns include scope creep, weak Product Owners, and skill gaps.
  • Fixes: release planning, timeboxes, documentation in DoD, gradual pilot rollouts.
  • Fixed-requirement, large-scale, or embedded projects fit waterfall better.
  • Experienced Scrum freelancers in Japan command 600k–1.3M yen monthly rates.

"We introduced Scrum, but the project isn't running smoothly." "We claim to be agile, but the field is in chaos."—These voices are still common in Japan's IT workplaces.

The adoption rate of Scrum development has exceeded 20%, and it is becoming established as the epitome of agile methodologies. However, on the other hand, data has also been reported showing that nearly half of the projects that adopt it fail.

Why does a theoretically excellent framework malfunction in so many workplaces?

The answer is simple: because they adopt it in form only, without correctly understanding Scrum's "disadvantages" and the "structure of failure."

In this article, we dive deep into the disadvantages of Scrum development as 7 failure patterns, explaining the root causes and practical solutions for each. Whether you are "considering introducing Scrum" or "feeling challenges with your current operations," we provide knowledge you can put into practice immediately.

Most organizations that fail at Scrum development hold the following misconceptions before adoption.

Common misconceptionsActual characteristics of Scrum
"Documentation is no longer needed"Managing minimal deliverables (backlog, etc.) is essential
"If speed increases, planning is unnecessary"Sprint-level planning must be strictly conducted
"If you leave it to the team, it will naturally work out"Self-organization requires high skills and experience
"Agile = Scrum"Scrum is merely one of the agile methodologies
"It can be used for any project"There are projects it suits and those it doesn't

Scrum is a "flexible development methodology," but to support that flexibility, there are strict rules and role definitions. If these rules are not followed, it can sometimes cause more confusion than the waterfall model.

One of the most typical disadvantages of Scrum development is that the overall project goal and completion date are difficult to foresee.

Requirements are added and changed with every sprint iteration, resulting in a state where the project never reaches "completion"—this is called "scope creep."

Why It Happens (Root Causes)

Scrum emphasizes "responding to change," so its structure naturally accumulates new items in the product backlog. If the Product Owner fails to properly manage priorities based on ROI (return on investment), the project tends to drift toward "just adding more features."

A Concrete Failure Scenario

A case from a contract development company. The project started with 10 sprints (about 5 months), but the Product Owner (the client's representative) kept requesting "let's add this feature too" every single sprint. As a result, the sprint count ballooned to 18, causing a timeline 1.8 times the original and a budget overrun.

Key Countermeasures

  • Create a release plan separate from sprint planning to visualize the overall roadmap
  • Regularly calculate the projected completion date based on the number of backlog items and remaining velocity
  • Establish a mechanism that allows the PO to make trade-off decisions: "If we add something to this sprint, what do we remove?"

Scrum development assumes that all team members possess a certain level of technical skill. A division of labor like the waterfall approach—"seniors design, juniors implement"—does not work well.

A sprint can be as short as one week. Within that short period, the team must complete "design → implementation → testing." There is no time to carefully train less skilled members, and they end up unable to contribute, disrupting the development rhythm.

The Chain of Problems Caused by Skill Gaps

  • Tasks concentrate on specific members (creating bottlenecks)
  • Sprint goals go unmet, lowering team motivation
  • Even when "blockers" are shared in the daily scrum, no solutions emerge
  • Velocity (the measure of work completed within a sprint) becomes unstable
  • Define the skill set required for the scrum team at the hiring and assignment stage
  • Incorporate mob programming and pair programming into sprints to deliberately transfer skills
  • Split backlog items into fine-grained pieces so tasks can be assigned according to skill level

In scrum development, the Product Owner is not merely "the person who provides requirements." They are a key figure responsible for the product's ROI (return on investment) and the only person with the authority to decide backlog priorities. If this role does not function, scrum will break down no matter how skilled the development team is.

Three Patterns of a Dysfunctional Product Owner

PatternDescriptionResult
No authorityDecision-making power over priorities remains with management, leaving the PO effectively unable to actDecision-making is delayed and sprints spin their wheels
Too busy to engageServing concurrently with their main duties, they cannot devote time to backlog refinement or reviewsDevelopment proceeds with a vague backlog, causing rework
Lack of understanding of scrumNot understanding how scrum works, they interrupt developers directly with tasks mid-sprintSprint goals go unmet and the team burns out

At startup Company A, the busy CEO himself served as PO. Repeatedly unable to attend sprint reviews, the development team kept adding features without knowing "what was actually right." Three sprints later, it turned out the product's direction had drifted far from market needs, requiring a complete overhaul.

  • Appoint as PO someone with the authority to make business decisions, in a dedicated or equivalent position
  • Agree across the organization on the PO's responsibilities (backlog management, prioritization, review participation) before the sprint begins
  • To prevent task interruptions, strictly enforce the rule: "No direct requests to developers. Everything goes through the backlog."

Scrum defines multiple recurring events: the daily scrum, sprint planning, sprint review, and retrospective. If these become mere formalities or time management becomes lax, you end up in a situation of "nothing but meetings, no development."

Appropriate Time Allocation for Scrum Events (for a two-week sprint)

EventRecommended TimeCommon Problems
Sprint planningUp to 4 hoursSometimes drags on for half a day or more
Daily scrumUp to 15 minutesSmall talk and detailed discussions creep in, ballooning to 30–60 minutes
Sprint reviewUp to 2 hoursPoor preparation makes demos ineffective, causing overtime
RetrospectiveUp to 1.5 hoursBecomes a formality, turning into a "feelings-sharing session"

The daily scrum is not a "reporting meeting for information sharing"; it is a short coordination session for the team to align. The iron rule is that detailed discussions should be held "after the daily scrum, only with the members who need to be involved," but this is often not observed in practice.

  • Strictly enforce timeboxes, with a rule to forcibly end meetings that run over
  • Limit the daily scrum to just three points—"what was done, what will be done, and blockers"—and hold detailed discussions separately
  • Have the scrum master regularly evaluate meeting quality to prevent them from becoming empty formalities

Due to the misconception that "agile doesn't need documentation," there are cases where maintaining design documents and specifications is neglected after adopting scrum. As a result, the team falls into the "trap of knowledge silos," where no one understands the specifications the moment a member leaves.

The Correct Interpretation of the Agile Manifesto

The Agile Manifesto states "working software over comprehensive documentation," but this does not mean documentation is unnecessary. It expresses a priority of values: prioritize working software over spending time on excessive documentation.

Concrete Risks Created by Knowledge Silos

  • Development halts when a key member resigns or takes leave
  • Six months later, you can no longer understand the intent of code you wrote yourself
  • New members take far too long to get up to speed
  • Fatal problems emerge during the maintenance and operations phase
  • Define "the minimum necessary documentation" as a team and incorporate it into the backlog
  • Include code comments, ADRs (Architecture Decision Records), and README maintenance in the "Definition of Done (DoD)"
  • Use knowledge base tools such as Confluence and Notion, and build a culture of updating them regularly

Scrum does not work if it is introduced by the development team alone. The entire organization needs to understand and respect the Scrum process.

In reality, however, cases of pressure from management and other departments—such as "We don't know what the Scrum team is doing," "Report every week when it will be finished," or "Add this feature right now"—occur frequently.

Three problems caused by lack of organizational understanding

  • Frequent ad-hoc task insertions: Urgent requests that ignore the sprint goal reach developers directly, bypassing the PO
  • Neutralization of the Scrum Master: Even when the SM tries to remove impediments, they are overridden by a single word from management
  • Scrum becoming a hollow formality: Out of resignation—"It will be overridden from above anyway"—the team starts cutting corners on Scrum practices
  • Hold Scrum briefing sessions for management and related departments before introduction
  • Invite management and stakeholders to sprint reviews and regularly show them the results
  • Agree on the rule "No direct requests to developers; everything goes through the PO" as an organization-wide policy

A "transitional introduction" that adds Scrum to an existing development process is far more difficult than introducing it on a new project.

Problems that commonly occur during transition

ProblemDescription
Cultural resistanceResistance from team members and managers who "don't want to change long-standing practices"
Confusion in a hybrid stateWaterfall management structures (Gantt charts, phase gates) remain in place, and trying to layer Scrum on top causes both to fail
Mixed rolesExisting project managers double as Scrum Masters and continue command-and-control management
Rushed transitionMany cases fail from trying to switch from waterfall to Scrum within a few weeks
  • Transition gradually. Start with a pilot introduction in one team, create a success story, then roll it out horizontally
  • Bring in an external Scrum coach and receive support from a neutral perspective
  • Set the transition period to "at least 6 months" and steadily drive organizational culture change without rushing

To successfully introduce Scrum, preparation "before" introduction is the most important factor. Introducing it without the following three conditions in place carries a high risk of failure.

① Team readiness

  • Are people available who can fill the three Scrum roles (PO, SM, and development team)?
  • Is the development team's skill set at a level where they can complete "design → implementation → testing" within a sprint?
  • Do all team members understand the basics of Scrum (terminology, events, artifacts)?

② Organizational readiness

  • Do management and stakeholders understand Scrum values (responding to change, short-cycle feedback)?
  • Does the PO have real decision-making authority?
  • Is there an organization-wide agreement on rules to prevent direct instructions to developers?

③ Product readiness

  • Is the product goal (the business objective to achieve) clearly defined?
  • Has an initial product backlog been created, and is the PO in a position to prioritize it?
  • Has the "Definition of Done" been agreed upon by the team?

Only when all three of these are in place can Scrum deliver its full performance. The "let's just start and see" approach is the pattern most likely to fail.

One of the most common concerns heard about Scrum is "there is leftover work even after the sprint ends." In most cases, this is due to problems with task granularity and velocity management.

Optimizing task granularity

Ideally, tasks in the sprint backlog should be sized so that each task can be completed within 1–2 days. Vague, large tasks like "implement XX feature" make progress within the sprint invisible and lead to situations where, near the end of the sprint, it suddenly turns out "it actually wasn't finished."

Examples of well-grained tasks:

  • "Implement validation for the user registration form (email format check)" → about 4 hours
  • "Define and test the response format of the product list API using JSON Schema" → about 6 hours

Examples of poorly-grained tasks:

  • "Implement user management functionality" → impossible to estimate
  • "Improve performance" → unclear completion criteria

Practicing velocity management

Velocity is a metric of the amount of work (story points) a team can complete in one sprint. Knowing your stable velocity improves the accuracy of predictions about "what can be done in the next sprint."

  • For the first 2–3 sprints, treat velocity purely as a "measurement period"
  • Determine the next sprint's planned workload based on the average velocity of the past 3 sprints
  • If velocity drops sharply, analyze the cause in the retrospective

The sprint retrospective is the most important event for realizing Scrum's "continuous improvement." Yet in many workplaces, it has devolved into a "grievance session" or a "perfunctory impressions meeting."

How to run an effective retrospective (an enhanced KPT method)

Adding "root cause analysis" to the standard KPT (Keep, Problem, Try) dramatically improves the quality of improvements.

STEP 1. Keep (what went well): List good practices that should continue into the next sprint

STEP 2. Problem: List things that caused trouble during the current sprint

STEP 3. Root cause analysis: For each Problem, repeat "Why did that happen?" five times (5 Whys)

STEP 4. Try (improvement actions): Decide on concrete, verifiable improvement actions for the root causes (down to "who will do what by when")

STEP 5. Evaluate the previous Try: Check whether the Trys decided in the previous sprint were executed and whether they had an effect

What matters most for preventing Scrum from becoming a mere formality is "STEP 4 and STEP 5." By not leaving improvement measures as "done and forgotten" and by always reviewing them at the next retrospective, the team grows continuously.

An effective practical approach to development is combining certification with hands-on experience. Certifications such as Scrum Alliance's CSM (Certified ScrumMaster) and Professional Scrum Master (PSM) are useful for acquiring systematic knowledge. However, a certification is only an "entry point"; accumulating real experience on actual teams is what leads to genuine growth.

Projects that meet more of the following conditions will benefit the most from adopting Scrum.

✅ Characteristics of Projects Suited to Scrum

  • Requirements change frequently: User needs and market conditions shift easily, and course corrections during development are expected
  • Early feedback is important: Users need to try the product early, with continuous improvement thereafter
  • New product development: Products built by repeatedly testing hypotheses from a state where the right answer is unknown
  • Startups and internal DX projects: Speed and flexibility are paramount, and a working product matters more than perfect specifications
  • The team can work in the same location (or always online): An environment that allows close communication

Concrete Examples of Application

  • New feature development for SaaS products
  • MVP development for mobile apps
  • Improvement and operation of internal tools
  • Ongoing feature additions to e-commerce sites

Professionals know best that Scrum is not a universal solution. For projects like the following, waterfall is the more appropriate choice.

❌ Characteristics of Projects Not Suited to Scrum

  • Requirements are completely fixed: Specifications that cannot be changed later, such as deliverables for government agencies or requirements based on laws and regulations
  • Large-scale, long-term projects: Large systems involving 100 or more developers, where managing overall consistency is the top priority
  • Hardware and embedded systems: Physical constraints make the cost of design changes extremely high
  • High predictability is required: Contracted projects where fixed budgets and schedules are contractually mandatory
  • Frequent team member turnover: Fluid membership makes it impossible to maintain continuity as a Scrum team

Typical Scenarios Where Waterfall Excels

In a municipal core system renewal project, requirements are fixed by laws and ordinances, and both deadlines and budgets are strictly defined by contract. Adopting Scrum for such a project offers no opportunity to leverage the benefit of "flexible change management," while losing waterfall's strength of "schedule visibility."

In real-world practice, there are many cases where neither pure Scrum nor pure waterfall is used, but rather a hybrid approach combining the two is effective.

Representative Patterns of Hybrid Approaches

PatternDescriptionWhen to Apply
Waterfall → ScrumRequirements definition and high-level design are finalized with waterfall, and development from detailed design onward is done with ScrumProjects with fixed requirements but expected changes during implementation
Scrum → WaterfallThe MVP is developed and validated with Scrum, and large-scale rollout after stabilization is done with waterfallTransitioning from startup phase to full-scale expansion
Parallel operationPart of the project (core features) is developed with Scrum, while peripheral systems are developed with waterfallWhen new feature development and existing system maintenance proceed simultaneously

What matters is not thinking in the binary terms of "Scrum or waterfall," but having the judgment to select and combine the optimal methods according to the nature of the project, the team's maturity, and the organization's culture. This is precisely one of the "architecture selection skills" expected of excellent PMs and lead engineers.

Why Do Scrum Skills Lead to Higher Rates?

While the number of people with Scrum knowledge has grown, professionals who can actually make it work in the field remain scarce. This gap between "knowing" and "doing" is what raises the market value of experienced practitioners.

  • Not just coding, but "process improvement skills" that boost team productivity are highly valued
  • Engineers with both technical skills and Scrum operational expertise can easily move into upstream processes as "Tech Leads"
  • PMO and Agile Transformation projects are commissioned at high rates by major SIers and operating companies

Engineers and PMs who can handle Scrum development are in a position to easily land high-rate projects when going independent as freelancers. This article explains the reasons why, along with concrete strategies.

Why Scrum Projects Are Easy to Win as a Freelancer

  • Expected to be an immediate contributor: When companies hire freelancers, they look for "someone who can lead the team right away." Having hands-on Scrum experience shows you can contribute within one or two sprints.
  • Easy to get involved in upstream processes: If you can take on roles supporting the Scrum Master or Product Owner, you can engage not merely as an "implementer" but as a "development process designer." This significantly raises your rate.
  • Contracts tend to be long-term: Since Scrum team stability directly affects performance, clients are reluctant to let go of good freelancers. It is common for 3-6 month contracts to be renewed continuously.

Concrete Actions to Win High-Rate Projects

  • Obtain Scrum-related certifications: Certifications such as CSM (Certified ScrumMaster), PSM (Professional Scrum Master), and CSPO (Certified Scrum Product Owner) are useful when explaining your profile to agents and matching with projects.
  • Add "Scrum adoption support" to your portfolio: Rather than simply saying "developed using Scrum," being able to state concrete, quantified results such as "supported Scrum adoption from scratch" or "improved team velocity by XX%" is a key differentiator.
  • Combine Agile with a specific domain: Adding domain knowledge such as "finance," "healthcare," or "e-commerce" on top of Scrum experience enables differentiation from competitors and higher rates.

Most of Scrum's disadvantages can be overcome with proper understanding and countermeasures. Conversely, a situation where "we adopted Scrum but it isn't working" is a sign that some prerequisite or rule is not being followed.

Professionals who do not merely "use" Scrum but deeply understand "why each rule exists" and can tune it to fit their workplace hold extremely high market value in the IT industry.

And it is precisely such professionals who are positioned to win high-rate PM, Scrum Master, and Agile Coach projects when going independent as freelancers, and to take control of their own careers.

Thoroughly understanding Scrum's disadvantages is the first step toward "truly mastering" Scrum. And that knowledge and experience opens the door to a freelance career that combines higher compensation than full-time employment with a flexible way of working.

What is Scrum development: One of the Agile development methodologies. A framework in which a small team of 3-9 members repeatedly works through "sprints (1-4 week iterative cycles)" to progressively complete a product. It consists of three roles: Product Owner, Scrum Master, and development team.

Main disadvantages of Scrum development: (1) The overall schedule is hard to see (2) Gaps in technical skills have a large impact (3) Depends on the Product Owner's skills (4) High communication costs (5) Documentation tends to be lacking (6) Requires organizational understanding (7) Transition from existing processes is difficult

Conditions of projects suited to Scrum: Frequently changing requirements, importance of early feedback, new product development, startup projects

Conditions of projects not suited to Scrum: Completely fixed requirements, large-scale long-term projects, embedded/hardware systems, contracted projects where high predictability is contractually required
