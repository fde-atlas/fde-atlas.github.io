How to Choose an Implementer: First Figure Out Who Can Change What

Original title: 实施方怎么选：先分清谁能改变什么

pafa.github.io · 2026-02-23 · 6 min read

Summary

This article advises AI buyers on choosing implementation partners, arguing the key question is who has both the ability and authority to change a process when it breaks. It offers a capability map organized by the type of stuck work: platform engineering, business redesign, systems integration, on-site exception handling, and operational handover. Citing 2026 events—OpenAI's Frontier Alliances with BCG, McKinsey, Accenture and Capgemini; Accordion's acquisition of Stride with roughly 50 FDEs; AWS's partner FDE program; and a Gartner caution on agentic AI costs—the author stresses that announcements prove division of labor, not customer outcomes. ERP integration skill does not imply model-changing ability. The real purchase is an improvement path: buyers should verify handover by having their own engineers independently change a business rule during a pilot, and write delivery as checkable actions.

Key points

  • Ask who can actually change the process when it breaks.
  • Map vendors to six types of stuck work, not logos.
  • ERP integration skill is not model-modification ability.
  • Partner-owned knowledge differs from customer self-sufficiency.
  • Test handover in pilots: client engineers change a rule independently.

The previous chapter looked at what FDEs need to practice from the perspective of personal growth; switching to the procurement side, the question becomes how to judge whether a team truly possesses these capabilities, and who has the authority to make changes.

An AI engagement proposal may simultaneously list models, consulting, integration, agents, and FDEs. With more and more names, buyers are actually more likely to miss one question: when a process needs to change, who exactly has the ability and authority to change it?

The second part of this book helped judge whether it is worth organizing an FDE team; this part looks further at external implementers. Rather than ranking companies by size, it observes along the lines of real division of work: who provides the platform, who redesigns business processes, who connects existing systems, who handles exceptions on site, and who bears long-term operation. Several capabilities can come from the same company or be jointly provided by several parties; the overall responsible party on the contract still needs to explain how they connect.

As the collaboration network expands, responsibility must not become blurred

On February 23, 2026, OpenAI's Frontier Alliances placed BCG, McKinsey, Accenture, and Capgemini in the same collaboration network as its FDE team. The announcement distinguished between research and product experience, business and organizational transformation, systems integration, and global delivery. It proves that a division-of-labor arrangement is taking shape; it does not prove that the four firms have already delivered the same results for every client. 1

Another combination comes from financial consulting. On October 13, 2026, Accordion announced the acquisition of software engineering firm Stride, saying that about 50 FDEs would join, and that it plans to pair the engineering team with its finance and operations experts; Stride's team in Latin America will also join its delivery network. 2 This shows that companies can combine domain and engineering capabilities through acquisition, but it does not prove that team integration has produced client benefits, nor should the staffing plans in an acquisition announcement be taken as the actual configuration of any particular project.

For buyers, what needs to be verified is not whether two titles appear together on an org chart, but whether finance experts can confirm business rules, whether engineers can modify systems, and how the two share responsibility for the same client outcome.

When selecting an implementer, you can use this to build a capability map. The table below is this book's procurement analysis framework, not an exclusive capability or commitment of the companies mentioned above.

Stuck workCapability to check firstWhat to ask candidate teams to demonstrate
Insufficient capability within the model or platformVendor product engineering and upgrade channelsWho can change the product, and how on-site issues enter the product roadmap
Conflicting goals across departmentsBusiness redesign and change managementWho decides the new process, and which old steps and metrics change with it
Data, identity, and order systems cannot be connectedIntegration and data engineeringWhich system is authoritative, and how cross-system failures are recovered
Local exceptions cannot be handled with headquarters templatesOn-site domain knowledge and engineering modification rightsWho interprets which exception, and how remote teams receive actionable decisions
The software runs but the team cannot take it overOperations and capability transferWhether the client can independently change a rule or locate an error
The problem has been standardizedOff-the-shelf products or conventional implementationWhether a simpler solution can meet the same acceptance criteria

The easiest mistake to make here is confusing the third case with the first. Being skilled at connecting ERP—ERP is the foundational system for managing procurement, production, inventory, finance, and other business functions—does not mean the team can modify the underlying model; having a partnership with a model vendor does not mean it has change rights over the client's financial systems. Before procurement, ask both parties to point out specific interfaces and the people responsible, rather than just displaying partner logos.

What you are really buying is a path of improvement

In its partner FDE announcement of June 30, 2026, AWS proposed that initially AWS engineers would co-build deeply with partners, later shifting to helping partners scale their own practices; knowledge such as delivery tools, domain models, and evals would form a reusable system held by the partners. The announcement covered only a limited set of partners at the time. 3

This helps explain why large-scale delivery cannot rely merely on adding headcount: the judgment and tools left by a previous project can reduce duplicated work in the next one. But "the partner retains knowledge" and "the client gains self-sufficient capability" are two different propositions. Buyers should ask separately: are the source code, configurations, tests, permission documentation, and troubleshooting procedures they receive sufficient to support the next modification? It can be reasonable for the implementer to retain generic assets, but the client also needs to know where operational dependencies lie.

There is no need to require that every underlying tool be owned by the client. What needs to be written clearly is: when the current team withdraws, a third-party model updates, or the contract expires, who can continue to operate the client-specific systems, which capabilities still require subscriptions or licenses, and how much work alternative paths would cost. These questions should be evidenced during the pilot: have the client's engineers independently change a business condition, then have the original team check the result. If even a small change cannot be completed, "handover training completed" is not sufficient proof of takeover capability.

Use objections to check your own procurement rationale

On September 29, Gartner raised an objection to Agentic AI built by vendors' FDEs: costs may rise, and clients may struggle to evolve independently. The public page did not provide samples and methods sufficient to reproduce the projections. 4

These risks are worth using to examine proposals: if scope and interfaces are already clear, would conventional implementation be more appropriate? If on-site teams cannot detect shared product problems, what did the FDE premium buy? If the client can never independently change rules, has the time saved by fast deployment been exchanged for long-term waiting?

The opposite judgment also has merit: complex platforms still require long-term vendor involvement, and building everything in-house would forfeit professional support. The key is not to force the vendor out, but whether the client can make an informed choice. Renewal should happen because the vendor keeps solving problems worth paying for, not because only the vendor knows how the system works.

Make the first engagement a comparable experiment

Below is a teaching example: a wholesale company wants to improve order reconciliation. Candidate option A buys an off-the-shelf document extraction tool; B has an integrator connect the order systems; C involves the vendor and the client jointly engineering solutions to unresolved cross-system semantic problems. The example does not fabricate any company's pricing or results.

First, have all three parties use the same batch of authorized orders, including short shipments, returns, cross-currency transactions, and customer abbreviations. The goal of comparison is not whose demo looks most human, but which requests can be completed, which require human decisions, how errors are discovered, and whether internal staff can modify rules. If the tool can solve the main problems, narrow the scope of joint engineering; if interfaces are the obstacle, fix integration first; if standard products and conventional integration still cannot resolve uncertainties in key business meaning, operating conditions, or product capability, then purchase FDE effort with a corresponding scope.

Once the engagement is decided, write the delivery as several checkable actions: the data owner confirms fields and access boundaries; the business owner confirms how conflicts are handled; the implementer modifies the system and submits evidence; the client approves go-live and arranges operators. Once an exception crosses the boundaries of several parties, designate one owner to see it through to recovery; "waiting for the vendor" must not be treated as the endpoint of a ticket.

The next chapter first reads Palantir's evolution from on-site engineering to AI FDE, then compares the practices of large implementation partners. Readers should carry the same question into it: in this case, which segment of work did the company actually change, and what was still left to the client?
