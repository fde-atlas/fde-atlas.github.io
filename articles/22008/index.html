Summary

This Japanese guide by Shohei Tokumitsu, CEO of DeFactory, explains eight channels freelance engineers can use to win projects: agents, crowdsourcing, referrals from former employers and clients, SNS and tech blogs, personal sites, direct sales, and events. It stresses building a system for continuous work rather than one-off wins, citing a government survey where about 60% of freelancers report unstable income. Using data from 4,234 listings on its BizDev Tech job site (August 2026), it shows Java (30.9%) and AWS (25.7%) dominate demand, with 9.1% of listings paying over ¥1 million monthly. It covers rate negotiation tactics, contract types (mostly quasi-mandate contracts), and channel priorities by experience level, with case studies showing specialization lifting monthly rates from ¥600k to ¥1.1 million.

Key points

  • Eight acquisition channels fall into referral, platform, and outbound categories.
  • BizDev Tech data: 83.5% of 4,234 listings pay ¥300k+ monthly; 9.1% exceed ¥1 million.
  • Common mistakes: panic-accepting low rates, relying on one channel, poor skill sheets.
  • Channel choice should shift with experience: crowdsourcing early, agents plus content later.
  • Case study: AWS specialization and quantified results doubled monthly rate in one year.

One of the most important themes when working as a freelance engineer is "securing projects." No matter how strong your technical skills are, without projects you cannot earn income or build a stable career. This article summarizes the current landscape, now that more project platforms targeting freelancers are emerging.

Recently, many engineers have gone freelance, drawn by the "freedom of working style" and the "potential for high income." Crowdsourcing and agents have made it easier to find work, but the biggest accompanying challenge is "securing projects." According to the FY2022 Freelance Actual Conditions Survey conducted by the Cabinet Secretariat, about 60% of respondents cited "low or unstable income" as a challenge in working as a freelancer, showing that success in securing projects greatly affects income stability.

If you cannot secure projects, you not only face financial instability but also risk mental stress and anxiety. Conversely, freelancers who properly secure projects and build a system for steady work enjoy high income while benefiting from the freedom of freelancing.

While the diversification of project platforms has increased options, it has also created a new concern: "I don't know where to start." In the following chapters, we will look concretely at the characteristics of each channel and how to choose the one that suits you.

The key to securing projects is not simply finding work, but "building a system that continuously brings in work." Rather than settling for one-off jobs, aiming for repeat business and long-term contracts is essential for securing stable income.

Therefore, beyond immediate project acquisition, "building trust-based relationships," "personal branding," and "creating a system for continuously securing projects" are also essential.

The most common stumbling block in securing projects is not a lack of technical skill, but poor decisions driven by "anxiety about projects drying up."

  • Rushing into low-rate projects out of anxiety: Some people, worried about not landing projects, take on projects at rates far below market value and then find themselves stuck at that rate level afterward.
  • Depending on a single channel or a single agent: With only one option, you cannot compare alternatives and may be forced to accept unfavorable terms.
  • Starting to apply without a polished skill sheet: Moving forward unprepared lowers your document screening pass rate, ultimately prolonging the time it takes to secure a project.

These pitfalls can be avoided with advance preparation and by securing multiple channels in parallel.

Understanding the types and characteristics of projects is the first step toward choosing work that suits you and securing projects efficiently. Below, we explain the main project types and their characteristics.

The required pace and communication style vary depending on project scale. Choosing a scale that matches your preferred pace is one perspective for working long-term and stably.

  • Large enterprise projects: Often well-funded, large-scale, and highly specialized. Deadlines and requirements tend to be strict, and these projects often come with high rates and long-term contracts. They make it easy to build trust, and once you secure one, it tends to become a stable source of income.
  • Small to mid-sized projects: Rates are relatively low, so you often need to juggle multiple projects to supplement your income. The work is highly varied and easy for beginners to enter, making these projects well suited for building a track record and gaining experience. If you are not yet confident in your skills as an engineer, consider starting with these small to mid-sized projects.

Related article: What are the rate benchmarks and payout rates for SES engineers?

  • Short-term projects: Mainly projects lasting from a few days to a few weeks. Easy to move on to the next job quickly, and you can handle multiple projects in a short period to build skills. However, be aware that your income may become unstable between projects.
  • Long-term projects: Continuous projects lasting from several months to several years. They lead to stable income and trust-based relationships, but committing for the long term may require turning down other projects. The benefit of building long-term relationships with clients is also significant.

High-rate projects are attractive because a single project can provide substantial income, but on the other hand, they are harder to secure and tend to be limited in number. For this reason, many freelancers aim to balance "high-rate projects with a regular income stream."

  • Build "medium- to long-term partnerships" and create relationships that allow you to continuously win stable projects.
  • "Juggle multiple projects" to diversify risk and stabilize income.
  • Acquire projects from multiple angles by combining "crowdsourcing and agent-based projects" with "your own sales efforts and referrals."

Identifying the actual conditions that project listings alone won't reveal, before applying, is key to avoiding failure.

  • Check the stated work arrangement: Even if a listing says "remote work allowed," some cases actually require several days of on-site work per week. Be sure to confirm the actual working conditions during the interview.
  • Check the depth of the contracting chain: Depending on how many layers of subcontracting a project has, the effective rate (the level after intermediate margins are deducted) changes. When going through an agent, check the depth of the contracting chain as much as possible.
  • Distinguish required skills from nice-to-have skills: Even when a wide range of technologies is listed as "required," in many cases only one or two are actually used primarily, so it is important to confirm the actual work content specifically during the interview.

Not only the type of project, but also the work arrangement changes how you work and how easy it is to find projects.

  • Fully remote: You can work from anywhere, but the number of projects itself is limited (about 5.0% of the total, according to our job site BizDev Tech, as of August 2026). Since prioritizing fully remote work narrows your options, it is more realistic to search by combining it with other conditions such as flexibility in working days.
  • On-site: This arrangement involves working at the client's site. There are many projects available, and because face-to-face communication is easy, they tend to lead to repeat orders.
  • Hybrid: This arrangement involves going to the office only a few days a week, sitting between fully remote and on-site work. Some people start with a hybrid arrangement to build trust, then later shift to primarily remote work.

When comparing agents, it becomes easier to choose if you clarify your priorities from the following perspectives.

  • Whether to prioritize the number of public projects or access to non-public, high-rate projects: General-purpose agents excel at the former, while the specialized BizDev Tech excels at the latter.
  • Whether to prioritize speed to starting work: The pace of meetings and proposals differs by agent, so if you need to secure a project urgently, consult multiple companies simultaneously and use response speed as a comparison factor.
  • Whether to prioritize the level of support from your representative: The scope of rate negotiation and contract procedure support varies by agent, so confirming specifics in the initial meeting helps prevent mismatches later.

By combining the eight routes above in the following four steps, you can efficiently narrow down projects.

  • Put your work history and skills into words: Before starting your project search, writing out your scope of responsibility, technologies used, and achievements will make later registration and applications go more smoothly.
  • Register with multiple agents: Register with at least one general-purpose and one specialized agent each to secure access to non-public projects.
  • Also check job sites and crowdsourcing in parallel: In addition to waiting for agent introductions, searching public projects yourself increases your comparison material.
  • Align rates and working conditions and proceed to contract: Once candidates are narrowed down, clearly state your desired rate and number of working days before entering negotiations.

As a way to build a "pull-type" acquisition structure where clients find you and reach out without relying on sales, there is the use of GitHub portfolios and technical blogs (Qiita, Zenn).

  • GitHub portfolio: Functions as a "living resume." Publish code close to real-world work (IaC samples, API servers, automation scripts, etc.) and clearly describe the overview, technologies used, and points of ingenuity in the README. Including the link when registering with agents or applying tends to increase pass rates.
  • Technical blogs (Qiita, Zenn): Function as a "second resume." Articles about solving errors encountered at work or introducing self-made tools convey your thought process well and build trust. When articles rank highly in Google searches, pull-type project acquisition emerges where clients proactively contact you.

By linking GitHub and technical blogs (linking to code within articles, posting article links in GitHub READMEs), you create a state where clients scout you because they judge "I like how this person writes code and approaches technology." The immediate effect is low and it takes 3–6 months or more to see results, but by continuing, you can aim for a state where sales costs are nearly zero.

If you have publishable work, organizing it on GitHub along with URLs (see 3-5) becomes the foundation for all your sales activities. If it's still too difficult, one option is to start out on the assumption that agents will handle your sales activities.

Success in winning projects doesn't come from blindly applying. Choosing projects that suit you and negotiating appropriately leads to long-term stability and higher income. This section explains the specific key points.

When you're unsure how to choose a project, using the question "Does this project fill what I currently lack (track record, rate, or specialization)?" as your axis makes it easier to prioritize. When you want to build a track record, prioritize continuity over rate; when you want to raise your rate, prioritize highly specialized projects. It's important to change your criteria according to your stage.

Choosing projects is a crucial step that forms the foundation of success. Keep the following in mind when selecting and considering projects.

  • Does it match your skills?: Confirm that what you're proposing matches your experience and technologies. Forcing yourself into difficult projects risks missed deadlines and lower quality.
  • Project scale and duration: If you want long-term stability, prioritize long-term contracts or repeat projects. Short-term projects are good for skill improvement, but note their low continuity.
  • Client reliability and reputation: Judge the client's reliability from past transaction history, reviews, and the content of their responses. Avoid anything suspicious.
  • Compensation and conditions: Check whether the rate matches the budget, as well as payment timing and terms.

Negotiation doesn't need to be perfect the first time. It's easier on your mind to see it as something you refine with each project you take on.

Negotiating fair compensation requires experience and preparation. It's important to objectively understand the value of your skills and expertise.

  • Advance research: Research average rates for comparable projects and industry standards to understand your own value.
  • Have clear grounds: Propose a fair price based on grounds such as "past track record," "specific skills," and "project scale."
  • Propose in stages: Don't start with a high figure; first convey the market rate, then adjust the conditions.
  • Understand the other party's position: Be flexible, taking into account budget constraints and urgency.
  • Key points to avoid failure:

Decide your compromise point in advance: Set the minimum acceptable rate beforehand.

  • Avoid unreasonable negotiation: Insisting only on price can cost you trust.

Specific examples of negotiation phrases:

  • "In my recent work, I contributed to improving XX and achieved the result of YY. Based on this experience, I believe a monthly rate of XX yen is a fair level." (Conveying based on track record)
  • "I understand that similar projects at other companies run in the range of XX yen per month, but since your project requires specialization in XX, I believe there is room to go above that." (Linking market rates to the project's characteristics)
  • "Could we discuss a rate review based on results about three months after I start working?" (An alternative if the first negotiation doesn't reach agreement)

To succeed with projects and prevent disputes, it's important to understand the differences between contract types, accurately grasp the scope and specifications, and firmly execute a written contract. The contracts freelance engineers enter into fall mainly into three types: "contract for work (ukeoi)," "mandate (inin)," and "quasi-mandate (jun-inin)," each with different compensation standards and scopes of responsibility. Quasi-mandate contracts are the mainstay for freelance engineers, but for contract-for-work projects that require deliverables, you need to check the requirements definition and contract points described below with particular rigor.

  • Detailed requirements definition: Put the scope of work, deliverables, deadlines, budget, and change procedures in writing.
  • Key contract points:

Clarify the scope of work: Make clear what you are responsible for and what is not included.

  • Payment terms: Advance payment, milestone payments, lump sum after delivery, etc.
  • Confidentiality and rights: Clearly specify ownership of source code and deliverables.
  • Rules for termination and additional work: Provisions for early termination and additional fees.

Raise your rates gradually

  • Rather than demanding a high rate right away, build trust and propose increases little by little. Use that opportunity to showcase your results and capabilities, so the client understands that "the value justifies the price."

Understand market needs and negotiate accordingly

  • Understand the latest technologies and industry trends, and use them to substantiate your value. For example, saying "this skill will be in higher demand going forward" makes it easier to propose an appropriate rate.

Clearly present the benefits for the client

  • For high-rate projects, emphasize benefits to the client such as "high quality," "fast response," and "added-value proposals," and convince them that "it's worth the investment."

Have the courage to say no:

  • It's also important to have the courage to firmly decline projects that don't meet your desired rate or whose conditions don't suit you. Avoid wasting time and effort, and aim for high-rate, long-term contracts.

Beyond one-off negotiation tactics, raising your market value itself over the medium to long term leads to sustained rate increases.

  • Narrow down your specialty: Even among projects listed on BizDev Tech, projects in specific areas such as AWS, SAP, and generative AI tend to skew toward the higher end of the rate distribution. Rather than covering technologies broadly and shallowly, focusing deeply on one or two areas makes it easier for companies to reach out to you by name with offers.
  • Gain experience in upstream processes: Having a track record of involvement in requirements definition and design phases gives you more leverage in rate negotiations. If your current project offers opportunities to participate in upstream processes, proactively raise your hand alongside your implementation work.
  • Show results in numbers: Instead of "I handled database design," say something like "I reduced batch processing time from 6 hours to 40 minutes through query optimization." Communicating your scope of responsibility and results tied to numbers makes them easier to use as material in rate negotiations.

Projects listed on our job site BizDev Tech with monthly rates of 1 million yen or more (384 projects as of August 2026) include many that require specialization in specific areas and experience in upstream processes, such as SAP data integration and ETL design/development, and PdM support for SaaS/AI products (based on BizDev Tech research).

To keep earning a stable income as a freelance engineer, it's highly effective not just to land a single project, but to build "long-term, repeat-business relationships." Here, we explain specific points and techniques for doing so.

To be entrusted with work over the long term, "credibility and trust" are the foundation.

  • Regular progress reports and communication: Frequently report on work progress, and respond immediately to questions or issues. Always keep in mind the goal of giving the client peace of mind.
  • Managing expectations and making appropriate proposals: Communicate in advance about specification changes and delay risks, and commit to realistic schedules and deliverable quality.
  • Sincere and courteous responses: Handle even small requests and questions with care, building trust as a "reliable partner."
  • Meeting deadlines: The most basic yet most important point. Meeting deadlines dramatically improves client trust.
  • Quality and quality control: Be meticulous about the quality of deliverables, avoiding bugs and mistakes. Conduct thorough reviews and testing as needed.
  • Exceeding expectations: Subtly offer additional proposals and improvement suggestions to add value, making the client think "I want to work with you again next time." This is difficult when pressed for time, but the payoff is correspondingly high.

Specific actions that lead to repeat requests:

  • One or two months before a contract ends, proactively ask, "Is there a possibility of continuing next term?" Since clients may find it hard to bring up themselves, asking proactively makes confirming their intent to continue smoother.
  • Share improvement points you notice during your engagement as simple notes, even if they're outside your scope. There's no need to address them immediately; the very act of "noticing and sharing" builds trust.
  • Regular follow-up: Continue solving system operation issues and proposing improvements even after delivery, increasing opportunities for long-term contracts as a "dependable presence."
  • Actively collect reviews and word-of-mouth: Ask clients for feedback and testimonials, and use them for future projects and your portfolio.
  • Reminders and newsletters: Stay in touch and share information even outside of work, conveying that you "care."
  • Use project management tools: Use tools like Trello, Asana, and Notion to visualize progress and prevent missed tasks and delays.
  • Time management and efficiency: Keep detailed records of your working hours and tasks to identify areas for improving how you work.
  • Standardize workflows: Turn your process from proposal and estimate to delivery into templates to work efficiently.

Once your projects get on track and your income stabilizes, preparing for tax filing and taxes, which tend to be postponed in the early days of independence, becomes important. For details, also see The Complete Guide to Freelance Tax Filing | Tax Savings, Penalties, and How to File.

The more dependent you are on a single client or channel, the greater the risk when a contract ends.

  • A combination like "a main project of 3-4 days a week plus a side project of 1 day a week" is a realistic form that balances income stability with channel diversification.
  • During periods when your main project's continuation isn't certain, keep consulting with agents and checking job listings in parallel while working, so you can secure the next project before the current one ends.
  • Making a habit of monthly simulations of "if the current project suddenly ended, when could I start the next one?" helps you objectively assess your channel diversification.

When handling multiple clients in parallel, if you don't clearly allocate your working hours, you risk giving half-hearted attention to both projects.

  • Allocate working hours per client weekly in advance, and consult with clients early if you're likely to exceed them.
  • Manage the progress of multiple projects centrally in a single dashboard such as Notion to prevent oversights.
  • Making a habit of recording your working hours can also serve as evidence when negotiating rates at contract renewal.

The insights gained through practice are a valuable asset that cannot be obtained from books or seminars alone. Here, let's learn the keys to success and points to note from actual case studies of those who succeeded in winning projects and maintaining long-term engagements, as well as those who failed.

Background Mr. A is an engineer specializing in web application development. Initially, he handled multiple projects through crowdsourcing, but in the process he met one company and developed the relationship into one where he continued to receive work from them on an ongoing basis.

Keys to Success

  • High-quality deliverables and attentive communication: Even after delivery, he regularly followed up with inquiries and offered improvements and new proposals.
  • Building trust: Client satisfaction was high, and more clients said they "want to work with him again."
  • Proposing added value: He provided not just coding, but also system improvement proposals and documentation maintenance.

Result

  • His project rates were gradually raised from half of the initial amount, and he ultimately succeeded in exceeding an annual income of 8 million yen.

In the previous chapters, we explained in detail the keys to winning projects, examples of success and failure, and the secrets to securing ongoing and repeat work. Finally, we'll wrap up with a summary of how to succeed as a freelance engineer and a look at what lies ahead.

If you've read this far and feel that your skills could hold up, that intuition is probably correct. With practical experience and the right choice among the paths introduced in this article, you can steadily find projects. Freelancing is a continuous exercise in self-management and self-responsibility. To earn a stable, high income, the following mindsets are essential.

  • Continuous willingness to learn: Technology is constantly evolving. Keep actively learning about new technologies and market trends, and never neglect the effort to improve your skills.
  • Communication skills: Building good relationships with clients directly leads to ongoing projects and repeat business. Be sincere and attentive in your interactions.
  • A long-term perspective: Don't limit yourself to short-term projects; choose work strategically with an eye on your own brand and career, and keep building on it.
  • A spirit of challenge: Continuously taking on high-rate projects and new technologies is key to raising your market value.

Winning projects is not a one-time success; it is a process of continuously reviewing your channels, negotiating rates, and choosing your area of specialization. Using the eight paths introduced in this article and how to choose among them by years of experience as a reference, start with the path that best matches where you are now.

  • Sharpen your expertise: Establish a competitive advantage through deep specialization and differentiation in a specific field. This allows you to win high-value projects.
  • Side businesses and business expansion: Leveraging your skills for education, consulting, or developing your own services is another way to increase future income sources.
  • Global expansion: Expand into overseas projects and transactions with foreign-affiliated clients to access global markets.
  • Expanding your network: Broaden your connections through industry communities, study groups, and social media, creating opportunities for new projects and partnerships.

Winning projects is not a one-time goal but a theme you will face repeatedly as your career progresses. Take your time and start step by step with the path that suits your situation.

The greatest appeal of being a freelance engineer is the continuous cycle of "freedom and challenge." By honing your skills, building trust, and developing your own unique strengths, you can achieve a stable, high income and a fulfilling way of working.
