Interview Skills That Make an FDE Successful | How to Listen to Draw Out On-the-Ground Insights

Original title: FDEを成立させるインタビュースキル｜現場のインサイトを引き出す聴き方

TIMEWELL · 2026-09-15 · 17 min read

Summary

In this TIMEWELL article, Hamamoto argues that an FDE's value lies not in coding speed but in extracting real requirements on site, since specification documents mislead: people describe idealized procedures rather than actual work. He distinguishes FDE interviews from traditional client requirements gathering, aiming to surface corrections quickly by placing working software in front of users, while separating reusable patterns from customer-specific branches. The piece details three interview types (discovery, deep-dive, validation), questioning principles drawn from The Mom Test and the Critical Incident Technique, and listening techniques like silence, parroting, and pairing. AI handles transcription and terminology extraction, but not summaries, which drop the rare exceptions that matter most.

Key points

  • Specifications mislead; requirements must be dug out through on-site interviews.
  • FDE interviews differ from contracted development hearings in purpose, scope, and cost logic.
  • Three interview types: discovery (timeline), deep-dive (incidents), validation (reactions to working software).
  • Ask about past concrete actions and exceptions, not hypothetical opinions.
  • AI handles transcription; silence, hesitation, and screens require human attention.

Hello, this is Hamamoto from TIMEWELL Inc.

We are a company of engineers. Everyone is hungry for development and itching to build things that work. That is exactly why, when we enter a customer's site as an FDE (Forward Deployed Engineer), the thing we are most careful about is not "don't build right away" but "what to ask before building." In a previous article, I wrote that the value of an FDE lies not in the speed of writing code, but in the skill of drawing out the real requirements from the field. This time, focusing on interviews among those skills, we will organize what our engineers learned in external training and use in the field, including the parts that did not go well.

This article is the first in a series that digs deeper into FDE practice. The FDE role itself and why we take the FDE style are covered in What is an FDE, and the difference from AI-assisted accompaniment is covered in another article. Here we go beyond that, talking about how to open your mouth after you have sat down at the customer's site. If you want to first check how well your team can handle field requirements using AI, please use the AI literacy diagnostic.

Why engineers should learn interviewing. Specifications lie

First, I will explain why engineers go out of their way to learn the skill of asking. There is one reason: specifications lie. Not out of malice. The person who wrote the specification simply cannot describe their own work as a specification.

Most of the work in the field is not put into words. Exception rules written on sticky notes, hidden columns in Excel, a separate screen opened to check with a supervisor. The person in charge does this every day, but when asked "please explain your operations," they describe the procedures as they should be, not the procedures as they actually are. This is not a matter of the person's ability; it is a property of human memory. As is often pointed out in user interview practice, people cannot accurately recall their own behavior and end up answering with opinions and generalities 1.

At Toyota's sites there is the phrase "genchi genbutsu" (go and see for yourself), and Taiichi Ohno said, "Ask why five times." According to Toyota's own explanation, the point is that the nature of a problem and its solution become clear as you repeat asking why 2. I believe this is precisely the reason engineers should learn interviewing. Requirements are not something you are given; they are something you dig for in the field. The tool for digging is the question, and the place to dig is the field.

This is also what Palantir's FDEs have actually done. According to the recollections of a former employee who worked as an FDE for eight years, among the books distributed at onboarding were 'Interviewing Users,' a practical guide to user interviews, and 'Impro,' a book on the theory of improvisational theater. The reasoning: to earn trust at a customer's site, you need the skill of listening and the skill of reading the room 3. An engineering company was handing new hires books on interviewing, not books on code.

And what distinguishes FDEs is how they use what they dig up. Consultants turn what they dig up into a report. FDEs turn it into something that works within the day and put it in front of the person in charge. Seeing something that works, the person in charge starts saying things they could not say in front of a blank page. So an FDE's interview is not "ask and be done"; it is a back-and-forth of "ask, build, and ask again." Half of the basis for our claim that we can implement the fastest lies in the speed of this back-and-forth. I will write about the other half in a separate article.

FDE interviews differ from custom development requirements gathering in the purpose of asking

Here, let me make one thing clear. FDE interviews differ in purpose from requirements gathering in custom (contracted) development. Looking only at the action of entering the field and asking, they appear the same, so I will lay out the differences in a table.

PerspectiveCustom development requirements gatheringFDE interview
Purpose of askingSolidify requirements and use them as the basis for estimates and acceptanceFind the shortest "working thing" that reaches outcomes
ScopeFixed by contract; changes incur additional costExpansion aligned with the customer's mission is welcome; branches that violate product principles are declined with reasons shown
Ownership of deliverablesThe customer's assetsPatterns are returned to the standard features of our own product; customer-specific branches are managed as branches
HoursRevenue. The longer it continues, the betterInvestment. The fewer hours per customer, the better
How it endsAcceptance and withdrawalThe customer becomes self-sufficient, and it is rolled out horizontally to the next customer

Custom development requirements gathering is done to avoid disputes later. So what was heard is solidified into a specification, and changes incur additional cost. In this structure, when the person in charge sees something that works and says "actually, that's not right," it is not welcomed. FDE interviews are the opposite: they are done to draw out "actually, that's not right" as early as possible. The person who led Palantir's commercial business called the FDE a "CEO with zero authority," writing that an FDE is not a role for shrinking customer requests to fit the product's current state, but a person responsible for the customer's business outcomes. He also wrote that if the customer's mission demands it, scope expansion is welcome, as long as it does not deviate from product principles 4.

That said, we do not accept everything we hear. a16z's January 2026 analysis of "Palantirization" lists three pressure tests for distinguishing genuine FDE patterns: whether hours are decreasing at mature customers, whether you can say "No" to customization, and whether the model still works beyond 50 customers 5. In our words, this is the weekly decision of taking things home on Friday and sorting them into "pattern or branch." If it is a pattern, return it to the product; if it is a branch, watch it so it does not multiply too much, and decline branches that violate product principles with reasons shown. Neglecting this judgment leads to the "services trap" a16z warned about in 2025: high-billable contracting that sends people in without a product backbone 6.

In 2026, this pattern has spread to major companies as well. In May, OpenAI launched a specialized implementation company with about 150 FDEs, describing the typical approach as "diagnosing value, selecting a small number of priority workflows with management and the field, and designing, building, and deploying within the organization" 7. In June, AWS invested one billion dollars to build an FDE organization, placing at the center of its design the state in which the customer can become self-sufficient at the end—that is, leaving behind something that works, documentation, and trained internal staff 8. In Japan as well, starting with Accenture establishing a specialized FDE organization in April 9, major consulting firms and cloud providers have set up FDE organizations one after another, and the name has spread to the point that a chaos map listing companies offering FDEs has been published. The more the name spreads, the more the responsibility for explaining the difference from contracting falls on those who call themselves FDEs. That is why this article goes into the details of how to ask.

There are three types of FDE interviews. Change how you ask depending on the purpose

In the first week at a customer's site, we use three types of interviews, each for a different purpose. Mixing them leads to failure.

TypePurposeMain questionsDeliverable
DiscoveryMap the work"What did you do yesterday afternoon?" "What came next?"A map of the workflow, a list of nouns and verbs
Deep diveCollect exceptions and pain points"What was the biggest problem last month?" "How did you handle it last time?"An exceptions notebook, candidates for the first feature to build
ValidationCapture reactions to something working"What's different?"A list of fixes, the next version

Discovery interviews take place on day one and day two. The goal is to map the work, prioritizing coverage over accuracy. What matters here is not saying "please explain your work." Instead, you ask, "What did you do yesterday afternoon?" Yesterday is something they can recall. Then you trace through the day in chronological order: "What came next?" "Where is that screen located?" As you trace, you write down the company's nouns and verbs without translating them into system terminology. It is at this stage that you discover "allocation" and "reservation" mean different things. One more thing you must always ask during discovery is who owns the data. "Which system does that number come from?" "Who administers that system?" A former Palantir FDE gives examples where most of an 8-12 week pilot was spent negotiating access to data, writing that the obstacle was not technology but organizational politics 3. Identifying the data owners on day one keeps you from stalling in week two.

Deep dive interviews start on day three and beyond. The goal is to collect exceptions. Routine work already runs on existing systems, so the room for an FDE lies in the exceptions. The tool used here is the critical incident method, explained later. "What was the biggest problem you faced last month?" "How did you handle it last time?" "Who else knows how to handle it?" The answers are rules that exist only in that person's head.

Validation interviews begin the moment you have something that works. Sometimes as early as the evening of day one. You put down something incomplete and ask only, "What's different?" If you ask "What do you think?", you'll get praise and that's it. If you ask "What's different?", they start looking for differences. I ported this technique to customer interviews for new ventures and documented the procedure in the live mock article.

The reason for separating the three types is that the nature of the questions differs. Discovery is chronological, deep dives are event-based, and validation is diff-based. If you mix them, the person you're interviewing no longer knows what to answer and retreats into generalities.

Principles of question design: past specifics, actions, exceptions

There are learnable principles for constructing questions, taught in training programs. I'll summarize the patterns our engineers learned in external training into three points, in my own words.

First, ask about past actions, not future opinions. To the question "Would you use a feature like this?", nearly everyone answers "yes"—as a matter of politeness. What The Mom Test, the standard book on startup customer interviews, repeats over and over is a single point: ask about concrete facts from the other person's life; don't ask for opinions about your idea 10. In FDE practice, this takes the form of "When was the last time you did that task? What happened then?"

Second, ask about events. The critical incident method, systematized by John Flanagan in Psychological Bulletin in 1954, is a procedure for collecting human behavior in a directly observable form, focusing on specific, significant incidents and asking what happened, how it was handled, and what the outcome was 11. It originated in aviation psychology research during World War II, and it is still used today in user experience research 12. This method works for FDEs because exceptions are stored in memory as events. "Please tell me your exception rules" yields nothing, but "the biggest problem last month" does.

Third, layer the whys. However, repeating "why" five times can corner the other person. "Why do you follow that procedure?" "Why?" "Why?" By around the third time, they feel they're being blamed. Toyota's five whys are directed at problems, not at people 2. At our company, we replace "why" with "what." "What is getting in the way of changing that procedure?" You can reach the same depth without blaming anyone.

Here is a table of substitution examples. The left column shows questions people tend to ask; the right column shows questions that worked in the field.

Questions to avoidReplacement questions
Would this feature be convenient?When was the last time you did that task, and how many minutes did it take?
Please explain your workflow.What was the first screen you opened yesterday afternoon?
Are you facing any difficulties?What was the most time-consuming issue last month?
Why do you follow that procedure?If that procedure changed, what problems would it cause?
Is the data clean?Which fields are often left blank?

What the right column has in common is that it points to a specific time, a specific screen, a specific field. These are questions where the thing the person needs to recall in order to answer is fixed. The left column leaves it undefined what the person should recall.

Techniques for listening: silence, parroting, working in pairs

Even good questions can be ruined by how you listen. This is something I learned in training and failed at repeatedly in the field.

Wait in silence. After asking a question, stay silent for three seconds; after the person answers, stay silent for three more. Engineers are bad at this. They want to fill the gap and end up offering candidate answers themselves. "So, you mean approvals are slow?" The moment you say it, the other person latches onto that candidate. An answer that latches on is not their answer—it's your hypothesis. If you stay silent, they add more. What they add contains the exceptions.

Parroting. Return the person's words exactly as they said them. "So the allocation gets duplicated." Do not rephrase. If you rephrase it as "So it's duplicate inventory," their words disappear. The work of building a dictionary of the company's nouns and verbs starts with parroting. If the words you repeat are wrong, they will correct you. The corrected words are the company's proper terminology.

Go in as a pair of two. One person asks, one records. The person asking does not take notes. Taking notes lowers your gaze, and you lose sight of the person's expressions and the screen in front of them. The recorder keeps a verbatim record of statements, and also writes down actions: the person pointed at the screen, looked at a sticky note, checked with the person at the next desk. This record of actions is the entry point to the observation method covered in the next article. When going alone, get consent to record and focus on listening.

Saying "Please show me." When the explanation gets long, switch to "Could you show me that screen?" The screen contains all the steps that never came up in the explanation. This is where the boundary between interviewing and observation lies; the method called Contextual Inquiry is designed precisely to go to the place where the person works and ask questions while watching them work 13. We consider the interview to be the opening act of observation.

Asking the three layers separately. Management, division heads, and the front line want different things about the same project. If you gather them in the same meeting room, only management's voice survives. We covered this point in a previous article, so we won't repeat it, but to add one more point about how to listen: when asking front-line staff, don't have their supervisor present. With the supervisor there, the staff member talks about the procedures as they should be.

What was learned in training, and what could only be learned in the field

We'll write honestly. Our engineers receive training in interviewing techniques before going into the field. The training was useful. Avoiding leading questions, asking about past events, waiting through silence, separating the roles of note-taking and asking. These are templates, and templates can be done once taught. After the training, the way we crafted questions changed.

On the other hand, there are things the training did not teach: the ability to notice the gap between what the person explains and what the screen actually shows. Right after being told "the section manager always does the approvals," can you look at the screen, see the assistant manager's name in the approval field, and think "huh?" Can you put that "huh" into words and ask back, "Does the assistant manager sometimes approve as well?" This ability only grew through repeated experience of putting something in front of people and capturing their reactions. In an article we wrote about articulating a sense of incongruity, we noted that when AI gets dragged along by context and fails to abstract, human awareness is required; the same is true in interviews. Training gives you the "template for asking," and the field gives you the "eye for noticing gaps."

One more difference between training and the field: time. In training we design a 60-minute interview, but front-line staff are busy and cannot spare a solid 60 minutes. What you can get is 10 minutes between tasks and 5 minutes when showing something that works. So our interviews became short and frequent. Instead of trying to ask everything at once, we ask for 5 minutes each time we put something in front of them. This format surfaced more exceptions than a 60-minute interview, because staff remember things when they see something that works.

Interviewing in the age of AI: records to AI, the room to humans

Finally, how we use AI.

Recording and transcription are left to AI. Get consent, record, produce a verbatim transcript, extract nouns and verbs to draft a dictionary. Up to this point, machines are faster and more accurate. However, we do not let it summarize. AI summaries keep the routine work that happens every time and drop the once-a-month exception. For an FDE, the exception is what has value, so a human re-picks from the verbatim transcript. Summaries are convenient, but precisely because they are convenient, the most important things disappear.

There are three things that cannot be left to AI: silence, hesitation, and the screen that appears when you say "Please show me." A transcript does not preserve the reason the other person was silent for 3 seconds. The "but" that follows "Well, um, basically" is flat in writing, but in the moment it is a signal of an exception. And the screen, in the first place, never makes it into audio. So we divide the work: humans hold the room for asking and seeing, and records and classification go to AI.

This division of labor connects to the observation methods covered in the next article, and beyond that to video recording and AI analysis. We have begun using a method of filming the field and analyzing it later with AI, and the same holds there: consent before filming and what a human watches while filming determine the quality of the analysis.

Summary

What makes an FDE work is not the speed of the code but the skill of listening. Because specifications lie, requirements are dug out in the field. There are three ways to dig: discovery is asked in chronological order, deep-diving is based on events, and verification is asked as deltas against something that works. Questions are framed to ask about past actions rather than future opinions, concrete events rather than generalities, and "what" rather than "why." How to listen: wait through silence, parrot back, go in pairs, and when the explanation gets long, ask to see the screen.

Training gives you the template; the field gives you the eye for noticing gaps. Our engineers enter the field with both, turn what they heard into something that works within the same day, and ask again. This back-and-forth creates half of our implementation speed.
