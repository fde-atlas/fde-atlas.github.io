The difference between an FDE and a Solutions Engineer

Original title: FDE 和解决方案工程师的区别

FDE Times · 2026-10-21 · 5 min read

Summary

This guide distinguishes the Forward Deployed Engineer (FDE) from the Solutions Engineer (SE), two frequently confused roles. The core test is ownership: the SE proves during the sales cycle that a product can work for a customer, often via a POC, while the FDE makes it run reliably in production and is the one on call when it breaks months after deployment. Since timing varies by company—Palantir's FDEs join from the first customer conversation—the author recommends judging roles by reporting lines, whether code reaches production, and whether pay is sales-tied, offering five questions to ask when reading job postings. Common mistakes are covered, including treating FDE as consulting; Anthropic's FDEs, for example, must feed deployment lessons back into product teams, with 25-50% travel.

Key points

  • Ownership after launch is the clearest dividing line between FDE and SE.
  • SEs deliver POCs; FDEs build and maintain production systems long-term.
  • Timing varies by company, so reporting lines and pay structure are better signals.
  • Anthropic FDE postings require 25-50% travel and codifying deployment patterns.
  • Resumes for FDE roles should show production systems, uptime, and incidents handled.

In the same week, you receive two interview invitations. One is for a "Solutions Engineer," the other for a "Forward Deployed Engineer." Both mention customers, both require coding skills, and both promise work that is "highly technical."

You read them several times over and still can't tell whether these are two different jobs or the same job under two names.

In May 2026, Gergely Orosz of The Pragmatic Engineer noted that Google, OpenAI, and Anthropic are all hiring Forward Deployed Engineers (FDEs) at scale. He also observed that the latest form of this role looks a lot like consulting.

High demand also brings risk. You might pick the wrong job and only discover a year later that you're great at giving demos but have never been accountable when a system breaks. This article offers a simple way to tell the two roles apart, and explains how to use it to read job postings and write your resume.

The Real Dividing Line Is After Launch

An article on Scaler comparing the two roles offers the most memorable test: if the system goes down at 2 AM three months after deployment, it's the FDE who restores it, not the Solutions Engineer (SE). All other differences stem from who owns the system.

An SE works within the sales cycle, with the task of proving the product can work for the customer. An FDE dives straight into the customer's problem to ensure the product actually works in production. As a result, SEs tend to prioritize showing customers more features, while FDEs prioritize production-grade robustness.

What each side ultimately delivers also differs. An analysis by Aced.io compares the FDE with the Solutions Architect (SA). The SA and SE are closely related roles, both pre-sales, so the comparison applies to both.

According to Aced.io's analysis, the SA mainly builds proofs of concept (POCs), while the FDE is the one who builds and continuously maintains the system.

Demo code can be thrown away after the presentation, but an FDE's code must withstand months of real data.

Is Timing a Reliable Signal?

The second signal is timing. According to Aced.io's analysis, Solutions Architects join late in the sales process and stay until deployment is complete; FDEs arrive after the contract is signed to make the product deliver real value to the customer.

But the timeline isn't always clear-cut. Palantir, which laid the groundwork for this role, describes the FDE as an engineer who works side by side with customers and owns projects end to end, starting from the very first conversation with the customer. In other words, at some companies, FDEs are on the scene before the contract is signed.

So don't classify a role based on timing alone. Two more reliable signals are organizational structure and compensation: FDEs belong to core engineering teams, while SEs usually sit under sales or pre-sales.

FDEs generally don't carry sales quotas, whereas a Solutions Architect's compensation often includes variable pay tied to sales performance. People work the way they're paid, so this line in the offer says more than the job title.

One Project, Two People, Three Months

Imagine this scenario: a logistics company wants to use an LLM to read supplier invoices and push the data into its accounting system.

During the sales stage, the SE builds a demo that runs on a few dozen clean sample invoices. It extracts the right fields, the interface is polished, and it showcases a few other things the product can do. The question the SE must answer is "Can it be done?" The answer convinces the buyer, and the contract is signed.

Next, the FDE faces a completely different question: "Can it run reliably every day?" The FDE connects the data pipeline to the real accounting system and handles skewed scans, missing fields, and API rate limits.

The FDE also adds logging, retry mechanisms, and an evals set scored against the customer's real data rather than demo samples.

Three months later, a major supplier changes its invoice template, and the data pipeline starts failing at scale. The alert goes to the FDE, because the system was built and is maintained by him. Fixing the bug doesn't mean the FDE's work is done.

Anthropic's FDE job posting explicitly lists a responsibility rarely seen in SE job descriptions: identify recurring deployment patterns, codify them, and share the lessons with the product and engineering teams.

In the scenario above, this is the moment when the FDE writes documentation on how to detect early that a supplier has changed its invoice template, so the next customer doesn't run into the same incident.

Ask Five Questions When Reading a Job Posting

Next time you open a job posting, read it the way an FDE reads a customer's technical documentation: look for actual behavior, not names. Since both SAs and SEs are pre-sales, the following five questions apply to both roles.

  • Does this role report to the engineering team or the sales team?
  • Do you get involved before or after the contract is signed? Do you stay on after launch?
  • Will the code you write go to production, or stop at the POC?
  • When something breaks after launch, who is on call? This is the most important question.
  • Is there a variable component of compensation tied to sales?

If the job posting doesn't answer these, ask directly in the first interview.

Travel is another signal. Anthropic's FDE job posting requires frequent travel, roughly 25-50% of the time, to build directly with customers on-site. That number shows the role requires spending long stretches with the customer, not flying in for a demo and leaving.

Common Misconceptions

The most common misconception is that an FDE is just an SE who writes more code. The difference isn't the amount of code, but who is accountable for it after launch.

The second misconception is judging a job by its title. A role called FDE that reports to sales and carries a sales quota is still pre-sales at its core. A role with a different name that puts you on call for production is actually closer to an FDE.

The third misconception hides in your resume. If you apply for an FDE role but every bullet point says "built demos" or "delivered POCs," recruiters won't see the signals they need.

Instead, write about the systems you pushed to production, how long you maintained them, what incidents you handled, and the processes you distilled and brought back to your team.

The last misconception is treating the FDE as pure consulting. Orosz observed that the latest form of this role looks like consulting. But consultants can walk away when a project ends, while FDEs at companies like Anthropic must bring what they learn from customers back to the product teams.

Job titles may change every hiring season. If you want to become an FDE, build up the experiences where you were the one who got the call when the system went down.
