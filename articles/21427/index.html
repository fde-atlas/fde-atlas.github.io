The Move-Out Clause

FDE Hub · 2026-10-20 · 8 min read

Summary

This piece examines what actually gets handed over when a vendor's forward deployed engineer leaves. Citing a procurement guide and a Gartner prediction that 70% of enterprises will abandon vendor-FDE-built agentic AI by 2028, with fewer than one in five engagements feeding learnings back into product, the author argues that real transfer means business rules are editable by the operators who own the process, not documented in prompts only the engineer understands. Buyers should plan the exit from day one, keep the platform but shed dependency on vendor staff, and staff the internal team with operators rather than IT. The buyer's exit clause and the vendor's rotation discipline measure the same thing: how long the engineer stays after the outcome lands.

Key points

  • Gartner predicts 70% of vendor-FDE agentic AI projects will be abandoned by 2028.
  • Fewer than one in five FDE engagements turn customer learnings into product.
  • Real handover means operators can edit business rules themselves, without tickets.
  • Exit plans should be contractual from day one, in three phases.
  • Independence should target vendor people, not the platform itself.

A procurement newsletter published a guide yesterday on what to do when your vendor’s forward deployed engineer moves in. The advice is specific. Make knowledge transfer a named deliverable with acceptance criteria. Own the code, the configurations, the prompts and the business logic. Write decreasing FDE hours into the contract. Plan the move-out from day one, in three phases: the FDE builds while your people shadow, then your people own the rules while the FDE handles engineering, then your people run it and call the vendor only for new capability.

Two weeks before that, Gartner predicted that 70% of enterprises will abandon agentic AI built through vendor FDEs by 2028, because the cost climbs and nobody inside the company can evolve the system once the engineers leave. The same note estimated that fewer than one in five FDE engagements will turn what they learn at the customer into product, and gave the gap between those two numbers a name: FDE washing.

Both pieces are written from the buyer’s chair, and they mark a shift in what the buyer is asking for.

For the past two years the whole conversation about this role has been about outcomes. First it was deliver the outcome, as opposed to a deliverable or a pilot. Then, as the obvious failure mode showed up, it became define the outcome before you deliver it, so that both sides can tell when the work is done. I have written most of this newsletter inside that frame, and I still think it is the right one. It is just no longer sufficient. The buyers in these two pieces take the delivered outcome as read. They are asking what happens afterwards: who owns what was built, who can change it, how long they will need the vendor, and what leaves the building with the engineer.

I have spent most of this year writing about the same question from the vendor’s side. When is the FDE’s job done, and what has to be true for them to leave. It turns out the buyer and the vendor want the same thing. What neither side has said clearly is what actually gets handed over when the engineer walks out.

The learnings leave with the engineer

Start with the part the contract covers. Code, prompts, configurations, business rules: ownership of these is negotiable and increasingly negotiated. A buyer who insists on owning them is right to, and a vendor who resists has told you something about their business model.

Now the part the contract cannot cover. An FDE who spends three months inside your accounts payable process, or your dispatch planning, or your underwriting queue, leaves knowing how that process really runs. The exceptions, the unwritten thresholds, the step that exists because of something that went wrong in 2019. They cannot sell your rules to anyone. They can, and will, apply what they learned about how a business like yours works the next time they sit down with a business like yours, which is often your competitor.

This is not a loophole. It is the mechanism. Every FDE leader I have interviewed describes the job as learning something at one customer and carrying it back into the product so the next customer gets it faster. One former head of FDE told me the right share of FDE output flowing back into product is around 70%. That number is a measure of how much of what the engineer learned inside you became something the vendor now sells to everyone. The vendor calls it discovery. From where the buyer sits, it is their operational knowledge becoming someone else’s roadmap.

My own work was a concentrated version of this. Engagements running in parallel across logistics, wholesale, manufacturing, insurance and finance, and the value I brought to the sixth client was, in large part, the first five. Nobody signed anything that said so, and every one of them benefited from it, and every one of them would have been right to ask what I was taking with me when I left.

Buyers are catching on. The questions I started hearing late in that period were not about the model. They were about where the learnings go, who else gets them, and how long the company would need me.

Two directions, one destination

Once a buyer realises this, they tend to move in two directions at once.

The first is speed. The pressure to adopt is real and the window is short, so they bring the vendor’s engineers in and build fast. The second runs the other way. They start standing up an internal team to own what gets built, the same way IT went in-house a generation ago. Call it an AI platform team or a centre of excellence or whatever the org chart allows. The point is that the capability stops being rented.

Those two directions look contradictory and are not. Both land on a customer that can run and extend the thing without the vendor in the room. The buyer wants that because dependency is expensive. The vendor, if they are honest about what an FDE team is for, wants it too, because an engineer stuck on one account refining what already works has stopped producing the discovery the team exists to generate. I argued in June that an FDE’s job ends at adoption and maintenance belongs to somebody else. The procurement guide, read from the vendor side, is the operational version of that argument. The buyer has written the exit plan the vendor should have brought to the first meeting.

The place the two sides have to be careful is the platform. A buyer thinking about independence has to decide whether it means independence from the vendor’s people or from the vendor’s platform. The first is achievable and should be the default. The second is a different decision, because the point of a platform is that the internal team can build the next adjacent solution on it without an FDE at all. Walking away from the platform means walking away from that too. In most cases the more useful outcome is to keep the platform and lose the dependency on people, as long as the platform gives the internal team the freedom they need: to see and change the rules, to extend what was built, and to leave if that stops being true. A platform that only works with the vendor’s engineers inside it is the dependency wearing a different name.

What gets handed over

Here is where the move-out clause, as written, is weaker than it looks.

Knowledge transfer as a deliverable assumes there is a body of knowledge to transfer. A manual the vendor is holding. In the engagements I ran, there was no manual on day one. The operational logic was in the operators’ heads. Extracting it was the first half of the job and, on most projects, the half that decided whether the thing shipped in weeks or months. After the build, that logic lived in three places: the heads it came from, my head, and the prompts and rule tables I had written. The acceptance criteria for transferring it back are not obvious, because the thing being transferred was never written down anywhere the buyer could read.

What every buyer I worked with actually asked for was narrower and more useful. They wanted to be able to change the key business rules of each process themselves, through something simpler than a codebase. Which regions ship on which days. Who approves above what threshold. What counts as a matching invoice. They wanted to open something, see the rule, change it, and have the agent behave differently tomorrow, without raising a ticket with me.

That request is the real transfer artefact. A system whose rules are editable by the people who own the process has already been handed over, whether or not the FDE is still around. A system whose rules live in a prompt only the engineer understands has not been handed over no matter how many documents accompany the exit.

It also answers the question the procurement guide gets closest to right. The three-phase plan has the internal team take ownership of what it calls the semantic layer, the definitions, rules and exceptions, while the FDE keeps the engineering. That is the correct split. The part to get right is who sits in that internal team. The instinct will be to staff it from IT, because they can operate the system. The people who can tell whether a rule is still true are the operators who live inside the process. An agent adapts to the context it is given, and a good one will notice when the world has drifted away from its rules. Some of that context will always have to come from the people doing the work. If the move-out plan hands the pen to anyone else, the engineers leave and the rules start ageing the day they go.

The same test from both sides

Gartner’s 70% abandonment figure will turn out to be a prediction about a specific kind of engagement. The ones where the FDE built something bespoke, held the logic in their own head and their own prompts, stayed on the account because leaving was never planned, and left when the budget ran out rather than when the outcome landed. Those deployments will be abandoned, and they will deserve it.

They are also the engagements that fail the vendor’s own test. An FDE who cannot leave is not doing discovery. A team that bills month after month against the same account is a services firm, and there is nothing wrong with being one, as long as the label matches the work.

I have been saying for a while that around three in four teams calling themselves FDE teams are development shops. They build bespoke things for customers, they do it well, and nothing they learn makes it back into a product, because in many cases there is no product for it to make it back into. That is my estimate from the teams I have spoken to and worked alongside, and I have never had a way to check it. Gartner’s figure, fewer than one in five engagements feeding product, puts the same number slightly above 80%. I would not lean on either as a measurement. I would lean on the fact that an analyst firm and a practitioner arrived at roughly the same proportion from opposite directions. FDE washing is a dev shop seen from the buyer’s side of the invoice.

So the buyer’s exit clause and the vendor’s rotation discipline measure the same gap: how long after the outcome lands does the engineer stay. A short gap means the rules have an owner, the owner is an operator, and the next problem is already being scoped. A long gap means somebody is paying discovery rates for maintenance, and it hardly matters which side notices first.

The buyers have started writing the clause. The vendors who are serious about this model should be relieved. It is the first time the customer has asked for the thing the FDE was supposed to be optimising for all along.
