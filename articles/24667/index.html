How FDEs Report to Executives: A Ten-Minute Reporting Script

Original title: FDE 如何向高管汇报：十分钟汇报脚本

FDE Times · 2026-10-09 · 5 min read

Summary

Drawing on Will Larson, Nancy Duarte, and Barbara Minto's pyramid principle, this piece gives forward deployed engineers a minute-by-minute script for a 10-minute executive briefing. The core advice: open with your recommendation and the decision you need, follow with SCQA context and at most three pieces of evidence, and end by restating the ask. Using a hypothetical customs-document agent pilot (82% processed autonomously, per-document time cut from 9 to 2 minutes), it stresses pre-meeting alignment with each stakeholder (nemawashi), a one-page summary with everything else in an appendix, and same-day follow-up email to lock in decisions. Common mistakes include starting with architecture, bringing questions instead of proposals, and piling up metrics.

Key points

  • State your recommendation and needed decision in the first sentence.
  • Executives decide on the spot, so align stakeholders beforehand via nemawashi.
  • Use a one-page summary; push architecture and evals to the appendix.
  • Limit evidence to three points and translate technical metrics into decision language.
  • Send a same-day email confirming the decision, owner, scope, and next checkpoint.

Executives approve recommendations, not results: lead with the conclusion and the ask, then present the evidence—don't start with the architecture.

Key Takeaways

  • Open with the recommendation and the decision you need; save background and architecture for later.
  • Executives often decide on the spot, so you must align with every stakeholder before the meeting.
  • Your deck only needs a one-page summary; put everything else in the appendix for the Q&A.

Picture this scenario: you've just finished a six-week pilot at a logistics company. The document-reading agent is running smoothly, the eval suite passes completely, and the operations team is already comfortable using it. Then you receive a calendar invite: the head of operations has given you 10 minutes on Thursday afternoon.

Those 10 minutes determine where six weeks of work goes. The pilot could move to production, or it could end up buried in a shared folder. Will Larson, author of the blog Irrational Exuberance, has observed that executives often make decisions on the spot in the meeting, and once a decision is made, you rarely get a second chance to discuss it.

So this is not an optional soft skill. Your code only creates value when someone on the customer's side signs off to run it officially. This article breaks down, minute by minute, how to construct those 10 minutes.

Executives approve recommendations, not results

Engineers tend to tell stories chronologically: what the problem was, what approaches were tried, what went wrong, and what numbers came out at the end. The decision request doesn't appear until minute eight, by which point the audience has long since looked down at their phones.

Larson's advice is the opposite. In his view, the clearest structure is always to state the governing thought first, then unfold the supporting arguments beneath it. This is Barbara Minto's Pyramid Principle. The opening follows SCQA: Situation, Complication, Question, Answer.

Presentation expert Nancy Duarte offers similar advice: open with the finding and the recommendation, and state upfront how the meeting time will be allocated.

Larson also makes a point that's easy to overlook: without a recommendation on the table for people to support, consensus is impossible. Walk into the room with only a problem, and people will argue endlessly; walk in with a recommendation, and people will refine it and then sign off.

A 10-minute script, written out sentence by sentence

Back to the hypothetical case above. The pilot processed 2,000 customs documents in total; the agent completed 1,640 of them independently—82%—with the remaining 360 escalated to human review.

Average processing time per document dropped from 9 minutes to 2 minutes. The decision you need: permission for the agent to write to the production enterprise resource planning (ERP) system in one warehouse, and a designated person on the customer's side to own the review process.

Minutes 0–1: Recommendation and agenda. "Today I'd like your approval on two things: first, permission for the agent to write to the ERP in the Binh Duong warehouse; second, having the documentation team lead serve as the review process owner for 8 weeks. I'll speak for 6 minutes, leaving 4 minutes for your questions." Duarte notes that when the audience knows there's time for questions at the end, they're more likely to let you finish your points without interrupting.

Minutes 1–3: SCQA. Situation: the documentation team does everything by manual entry. Complication: peak season is approaching and there's no time to hire. Question: should the agent go into full production before peak season? Answer: yes, provided the two conditions just mentioned are met.

Minutes 3–6: Three pieces of evidence, one sentence each. The agent independently processed 82% of documents. Any document below the confidence threshold must pass human review before being written to the ERP. Every failure mode observed during the pilot now has a corresponding safeguard; details are in the appendix.

Minutes 6–7: Options and the cost of waiting. Option A: deploy in one warehouse now. Option B: pilot for another 4 weeks, which means documents will still be entered manually when peak season arrives. State clearly which option you recommend, and why.

Minutes 7–10: Q&A and close. As your final sentence, repeat the recommendation from minute one word for word. Then stop, and wait for the answer.

The hardest step is translating technical language into decision language:

There's a human-in-the-loop fallbackAny uncertain document is reviewed by a human before it enters the ERP
We need more system permissionsWe need your approval for 8 weeks of write access to one warehouse
What do you think of the results?Can you approve Option A today?

The real work happens before the meeting

Since there's no second chance, most of the work has to be done before you walk into the room. Larson calls the practice of aligning with stakeholders one by one before a presentation nemawashi.

In the example above, you would meet separately with the IT security team to discuss ERP write access, and then with the documentation team lead to confirm they're willing to serve as the process owner.

If anyone in the meeting is hearing the recommendation for the first time, the answer will likely be "let's revisit next week."

Only after all this does the deck come into play. Duarte recommends putting a brief summary of key points at the start, with everything else as an appendix. Architecture diagrams, eval tables, and failure lists all move to the appendix, to be opened only if someone asks.

To test whether your summary page is concise enough, use Duarte's method: imagine your time is cut to 5 minutes—whatever survives is all you need to say.

Writing on LeadDev, Kiger notes that distilling your thinking this way also forces you to understand the problem more deeply. If you can't yet state your recommendation in a single sentence, you probably don't understand the project well enough.

The meeting isn't over when you leave the room

Kiger stresses that key points must be repeated, and everything needs pre-meeting groundwork and post-meeting follow-up. Send a short email the same day confirming the decision, the owner, the scope, and the next checkpoint.

That email turns a nod in the meeting into a written commitment the entire customer team can rely on.

If the answer is "not yet" or "next week," don't just walk away—ask what's needed for approval and who else you should meet. In that case, your follow-up email should record those conditions and the date you'll return with an answer.

Common mistakes that waste those 10 minutes

The most common mistake is starting with the architecture. As Kiger writes, executives have limited time and attention; they only need the information required to make the decision. The second mistake is walking into the room with a problem but no recommendation, which turns the meeting into a fruitless brainstorm.

The next mistake is subtler: piling on numbers. Three pieces of evidence carry more weight than twelve metrics, because people can remember them. The final mistake is being vague when asking for a decision. "What do you think?" is not a decision; "Can you approve Option A?" is.

This week's exercise

Take a piece of technical work you completed this quarter—a migration or a new feature, say—and rewrite it into a 10-minute script using the structure above. The first sentence must answer one question: who needs to approve what, by when?

If your goal is a Forward Deployed Engineer (FDE) role, this script is just as useful in interviews. When asked about past projects, first describe what decisions your work helped others make, then go into the technical details. The same applies to your resume: replace "strong communication skills" with a sentence like this.

Six weeks of pilot results won't change no matter how you present them. What determines whether they reach production is the first sentence you say in the room.
