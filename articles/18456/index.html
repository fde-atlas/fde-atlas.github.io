FDE Job Responsibilities: The Division of Labor Between Palantir Delta and Echo

Original title: FDE 岗位职责：Palantir Delta 与 Echo 分工

FDE Times · 2026-10-08 · 5 min read

Summary

The article compares how the FDE role is split at Palantir versus bundled at AI startups. Palantir pairs Delta, who writes code for one customer, with Echo, a Deployment Strategist handling relationships and adoption, while a Dev team builds shared capabilities across customers. Botminds instead expects one FDE to act as a hands-on CTO across the full deployment cycle, and Arist hires a founding FDE to build the entire function. Palantir's own job posts now blur the lines, asking FDEs to feed field learnings back into AIP. The author warns that merging roles without a path from field work to product turns startups into disguised consultancies, and offers advice on reading job posts and interview questions.

Key points

  • Palantir splits FDE work between Delta and Echo, with a Dev team productizing learnings.
  • Botminds expects one FDE to cover the whole cycle as a hands-on CTO.
  • Arist's founding FDE must build the FDE function, team and operations, from scratch.
  • Palantir's new posts blur boundaries, asking FDEs to return field experience to AIP.
  • Ask in interviews who owns client relations and how field learnings reach the product.

Palantir used to assign two people to a customer. Delta was the Forward Deployed Engineer (FDE) who wrote code; Echo was the Deployment Strategist who handled relationships and process. But the AI startup Botminds says something different in its FDE job posting: it wants someone to serve as a hands-on CTO in every deployment.

One company splits the work between two people; another hands it all to one. Both call the role an FDE, making it easy for developers considering the switch to misread it.

If you're reading an FDE job posting and think strong coding skills plus a willingness to sit next to the customer is enough, read it again. In the postings from the two AI startups Botminds and Arist, the FDE also takes on the work that descriptions of Palantir assign to Echo. At Arist, the FDE even has to build an entire function.

Only by understanding which role you'll be carrying can you prepare effectively.

How does Palantir divide the work?

Many descriptions of the Palantir model rest on a contrast. According to a summary by ResumeAdapter, the Dev team builds one capability and deploys it to many customers, while Delta does the opposite: serving one customer across multiple capabilities.

Echo works alongside Delta. An analysis by Cloud Authority, citing an April 2026 Medium article, says the pairing exists so that solutions are both technically correct and actually adopted by customers. Those two goals require different skills, so Palantir assigns them to two people.

Treat the details with caution. Secondary sources disagree on who occupies which position. FourWeekMBA says Echo goes deep into a customer's operations, looking for bottlenecks and unspoken needs; other accounts say Delta also works side by side with customers.

According to ResumeAdapter, Palantir itself has said the line between Echo and Delta is very blurry.

So don't treat Delta/Echo as a fixed org chart. A more useful framing is that it distinguishes three types of work: writing code for one customer, getting that customer to actually use it, and turning what was learned into a product that serves many customers. Separating these three types of work reveals how much a startup is piling onto one person.

At two startups, the work lands on one person

According to Cloud Authority, the FDE role is effectively three full-time jobs in one: developer, architect, and consultant. The job postings from the two AI startups below show this is no exaggeration.

The FDE Botminds describes covers the entire cycle: understanding the problem, framing it, and then standing beside users when the system goes live. The company emphasizes small teams, high trust, and direct accountability for results. Measured against descriptions of Palantir, this amounts to combining the work of Delta and Echo.

Arist goes even further. It is hiring a founding FDE whose task is to build both the team and the operating model for the FDE function. The posting positions the role at the intersection of engineering, AI, and customer outcomes. In other words, Arist is using the FDE role to build a new function, not to provide after-sales support.

Palantir is rewriting the role too

Even Palantir no longer describes the FDE as one half of a tidy division of labor. In its Forward Deployed AI Engineer job posting, it writes that candidates will work in small teams and be responsible for delivering critical projects for customers.

One notable detail in the posting: the FDE must bring field experience back to the AIP product suite. When the two teams were separate, that feedback loop belonged to the Dev team. The posting suggests the line between Palantir's FDEs and startup FDEs is blurring from both ends at once.

The cost of merging roles

Cloud Authority warns that maintaining a large FDE team is costly and can turn a startup into a consulting firm in disguise. The warning targets the cost of scaling FDEs, but viewed through the three-role framework, it points to a risk specific to merged roles.

The likely mechanism works like this: according to these descriptions, Palantir's model includes a Dev team that turns what is learned at each customer into a general capability. If a startup hands everything to FDEs without such a team, the FDEs have to serve as the bridge to the product themselves.

If no one does this, each deployment tends to become a one-off project, and the company gradually starts to resemble a consulting firm.

For you, this isn't just a matter of company strategy—it shapes what your next two years will look like. Where field feedback genuinely makes it into the product, you'll learn how to design things that work for many customers.

Without that path, you may end up writing the same kind of integration over and over again.

Can job postings tell you which role you'll be carrying?

The workload behind the same title can vary enormously, so when reading a job posting, look for signals about the role rather than just skimming the tech stack list. The table below offers one way to read them, based on the wording in the postings mentioned above.

The second column treats the Dev/Delta/Echo division as a reference framework rather than an official org chart, because the sources describing it are inconsistent.

Wording in the job postingCorresponding Delta/Echo/Dev frameworkWhat to prove in your resume and interviews
"Hands-on CTO in every deployment" (Botminds)Close to Delta and Echo combinedA project where you went from clarifying the problem all the way to launch, including standing beside users
"Founding," building team and operating model (Arist)Delta plus Echo, plus organizational buildingA process or set of documentation you created that others went on to reuse
"Small team, responsible for project delivery" (Palantir)Delta, with a blurry line to EchoA high-stakes project where you bore primary responsibility for the outcome
Bringing field experience back into the product (Palantir)Work that normally belongs to the Dev teamAn experience where you turned a single customer's need into a general feature

The third column matters most. Most developers' resumes only prove the "technically correct" half. The very reason Palantir needs Echo shows that the other half—whether customers actually use the solution—matters just as much.

How developers in Vietnam should prepare

If you're doing outsourcing work, or you're on a project team serving overseas clients, you may already be doing Echo's job without calling it that: running meetings to figure out what the client actually needs, guiding users through their first go-live, or adjusting workflows so the system actually gets used.

Write this work into your resume as concrete outcomes, not compressed into a single line like "responsible for client communication."

The most commonly overlooked role is Dev. Find something generalizable from a recent project—a connector, a data validation suite, or a prompt template—and rework it into a version a second client could use. This proves you won't turn the company into a consulting firm.

In the interview, ask two questions directly: Who owns the client relationship? How does on-site experience flow back to the product team? The answers will tell you how many roles you'll have to carry, and whether the company understands not to leave you carrying them alone for the long term.

According to descriptions of Palantir, this work requires two people, backed by an entire team; Botminds and Arist, by contrast, assign it to a single FDE. People who can do this role well have a big advantage—but only if they choose a company that genuinely has a path for turning field work into product.
