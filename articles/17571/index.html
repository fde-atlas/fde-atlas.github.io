Do FDEs work a lot of overtime? The real pace and where the irregularity comes from

Original title: FDE 加班多吗？真实节奏与不规律性的来源

FDE中国 · 2026-09-07 · 2 min read

Summary

This piece argues that FDE overtime is not uniform but pulse-like: intensity spikes around three moments—launch sprints, acceptance cycles, and production incidents—while steady periods resemble ordinary product development. Whether an offer is intense depends on how dense these spikes are and what buffering mechanisms the team has, not on a generic answer to "do you work overtime." The author offers practical levers to compress the spikes: strict scope management, front-loaded acceptance criteria, and fixed weekly reporting rhythms. Incident response can't be eliminated, but institutionalized compensation or time-off policies signal team maturity. For interviews, ask how the previous project's launch week actually went rather than asking about overtime directly. The irregularity is also framed as part of why FDE pay carries a premium.

Key points

  • FDE workload is pulse-shaped, not uniformly heavy.
  • Launch sprints and daily response pressure can be managed down.
  • Institutionalized incident compensation signals team maturity.
  • In interviews, ask about the last launch week, not overtime.
  • Irregularity partly explains the FDE salary premium.

Full text

This answer is aimed at job seekers concerned about workload and parents asking on their behalf, deliberately avoiding both sugarcoating and fear-mongering. The most valuable takeaway is the workload distribution model: FDE overtime is not uniform—it concentrates on three types of nodes: project kickoff, acceptance, and incidents. During stable periods, the daily rhythm is close to that of ordinary product development. To judge whether an offer is truly high-intensity, look at the density of these three node types on the team and their buffering mechanisms, rather than relying on the vague answer to "do you work overtime?" The most common misreadings are equating irregularity with high intensity, or conversely using "flexibility" to mask structural overtime. Compared with the vague narratives common in China, this article offers actionable ways to push back: scope management, reporting cadence, and front-loading acceptance criteria—all of which correspond to existing playbooks on the site. One additional negotiation perspective: before joining, beyond asking "what does launch week look like," you should also confirm whether incident-response compensation or time-off-in-lieu is institutionalized; having institutionalized buffers is the most direct window into a team's maturity. After reading, do one thing: in the interview, directly ask the target team how launch week went on their last project—the truthfulness of the answer will be very telling. — FDEChina Editorial Team · Practitioner's Perspective

Do FDEs work a lot of overtime?

The direct answer: an FDE's rhythm is more irregular than pure product development, but "pulling all-nighters every day" is not the norm. A common industry observation is that an FDE's workload follows a pulse-like distribution—most of the time it's a normal daytime rhythm, but clear spikes occur at certain types of nodes. So the more valuable question is not "how much," but where the spikes come from, whether they can be pushed back, and whether you're willing to accept this rhythm.

The spikes mainly come from three types of moments. The first is launch sprints: as acceptance or cutover approaches, with the client's business teams watching and leadership waiting, joint debugging late into the night is common. The second is acceptance cycles: the client has final discretion over the results, and the pressure to iterate until it passes is real. The third is production incidents: the system runs in the client's production environment, so when something goes wrong, the response priority is naturally higher than for internal products. Understanding these three sources tells you that the determining factors of intensity are project type and the team's buffering mechanisms, not some universal standard. For a more detailed breakdown of daily work, see A Day in the Life of an FDE.

The good news is that two of the three spike types can be actively pushed back. The intensity of a launch sprint depends largely on scope management: the tighter the scope is locked and the earlier the acceptance criteria are set, the calmer the final two weeks will be—see Expectation Management Handbook and PoC Acceptance Checklist for specifics. Day-to-day response pressure depends on reporting and escalation mechanisms: a client being able to reach you directly at any time versus getting the client used to a fixed weekly reporting cadence are completely different levels of workload—see FDE Reporting Handbook. Incident-driven spikes are hard to avoid entirely, but contingency plans and degradation strategies can significantly reduce late-night firefighting.

How do you probe the real workload in an interview? Don't ask "is there a lot of overtime"—that question only gets you platitudes. Instead, ask directly how launch week went on the last project, what the current client's response expectations are, and how many people on the team backstop a project. How specific the answers are will tell you a lot. Be honest with yourself too: this irregularity is part of the job, and one source of the salary premium—what the client is buying includes "having someone on-site accountable when things go wrong"; see Why FDE Salaries Are High. If you prefer the stable rhythm of deep product development, it's not too late to decide after reading The Difference Between an FDE and a Full-Stack Engineer.
