What Is DX Talent? Five Types and Criteria for Deciding on Hiring, Development, and External Utilization

What is DX talent? (3-line summary)

  • DX talent is a general term for people who drive business and operational transformation using digital technology and data as tools. IPA's "Digital Skill Standard" defines DX-promoting talent as five categories (expanded to six in the ver.2.0 revision of April 2026).
  • 85.1% of Japanese companies report a shortage of DX-promoting talent (IPA "DX Trends 2025"). This is strikingly higher than in the US or Germany, and cannot be structurally solved by competition in the hiring market alone.
  • Securing talent is not a choice among hiring, development, and external resources, but a portfolio. Moreover, now that generative AI is becoming the executor of work, the requirements for DX talent need to be redefined as "AX talent"—people who can delegate work to AI and focus on design and judgment.

This article covers the organizational aspects of DX talent: definition, categories, and securing strategy. For the practical question of how to compare and select training services, see "How to Choose AI Training for Enterprises"; reading it alongside this article connects talent strategy to execution.

Definition and 5 categories of DX talent | Organized by the IPA standard

How do DX talent differ from IT talent?

The term "DX talent" tends to be used ambiguously, but the distinction from the conventional "IT talent" clarifies its outline. While IT talent are specialists in the means—planning, developing, and operating information systems—DX talent are agents of change who use technology to transform business and operations themselves. What is required is not high programming ability, but an understanding of both business and technology, and the ability to drive transformation from goal setting to impact verification.

Therefore, the equation "DX talent = engineer hiring" is the first misconception. As IPA's categories, described below, show, the core of DX talent is rather positioned as business-side talent.

The five talent categories defined by the Digital Skill Standard

The most widely referenced official definition of DX talent is the "Digital Skill Standard (DSS)" developed by METI and IPA (Information-technology Promotion Agency, Japan). The DSS consists of two parts: the "DX Literacy Standard" for all business professionals and the "DX Promotion Skill Standard (DSS-P)" for promotion talent; the latter has defined DX-promoting talent as the following five categories.

Talent categoryRoleTypical mission
Business ArchitectDrives DX initiatives end-to-end, from goal setting through implementation and impact verification, while coordinating stakeholdersNew business development, enhancement of existing businesses, oversight of operational transformation projects
DesignerIntegrates business and customer/user perspectives to design product and service direction and development processesCustomer experience (UX) design, concretizing service concepts
Data ScientistDesigns, implements, and operates mechanisms for data collection and analysis to realize operational transformation and new business using dataDemand forecasting, data platform design, applying AI models to operations
Software EngineerDesigns, implements, and operates systems and software to deliver products and services using digital technologyDevelopment of business systems and applications, API integration
CybersecurityAssesses security risks in the digital environment supporting business processes and takes measures to mitigate their impactSecurity policy formulation, risk assessment, incident response

(Source: IPA "DX Promotion Skill Standard (DSS-P)". Role definitions are based on descriptions in that standard.)

In addition, the corporate survey in IPA's "DX White Paper 2023" uses a seven-job classification: product manager, business designer, tech lead, data scientist, advanced technology engineer, UI/UX designer, and engineer/programmer. The granularity of classification varies by document, but the underlying philosophy is consistent: DX talent consists of two pillars—"talent who lead business transformation" and "talent who implement with technology."

ver.2.0 revision | Review premised on generative AI and expansion to six categories

The Digital Skill Standard has been revised continuously in response to the spread of generative AI; in ver.2.0, released in April 2026, a "Data Management" category for effectively leveraging data and AI to create value was newly established, expanding the framework from five to six categories. Learning items and skills were also reviewed with generative AI as a premise.

This revision carries two implications. First, the recognition that the success of AI adoption is determined by data preparation and governance is now reflected in a public standard. Second, talent categories will continue to be redefined as the technology environment changes. Companies should not treat their skill maps as a one-time exercise, but maintain a cycle of revising them in step with updates to the standard.

How severe is Japan's DX talent shortage?

According to IPA's "DX Trends 2025" (published June 2025), a comparative survey of Japan, the US, and Germany found that a combined 85.1% of Japanese companies reported that personnel to drive DX were "somewhat in short supply" or "severely in short supply." In contrast, the same responses in the US and Germany were far lower, making Japan's sense of shortage stand out. Moreover, while the most scarce role in the US and Germany is the "data scientist," in Japan it is the "business architect," the orchestrator of transformation, whose shortage rate is exceptionally high.

What these numbers mean is clear. As long as every company is competing for the same talent in the hiring market, recruitment alone cannot close this gap. Even in IPA's "DX White Paper 2023," the most common means of securing DX talent among Japanese companies was "developing internal personnel," and a "grow-your-own" approach, including leveraging existing personnel, is being chosen as the realistic solution. The absence of talent is also one of the main reasons DX efforts stall; the overall structure of failure is detailed in "Structural Reasons Why DX Fails."

Hiring, Development, External Use | Comparing the Three Means of Securing Talent

The means of securing DX talent fall broadly into three categories: mid-career hiring, internal development, and external use (engaging partners). First, let's compare their structures.

Decision Criteria × Verification Methods | Which Should Your Company Choose?

The allocation across the three means is determined by asking your company the following five decision criteria. Verify each one together with its verification method.

Decision criterionVerification methodRule of thumb

The onboarding environment is especially often overlooked. Even if you hire scarce DX talent at high salaries, placing them in a "wait-and-see position" with no authority or data ends in early turnover. Before hiring, build the first use case with an external partner and prepare the soil in which DX talent can thrive—this sequence ultimately also raises the success rate of hiring.

The Right Answer Is Allocation | Thinking in Terms of a Talent Portfolio

The three means are not mutually exclusive options. The standard practice is a staggered portfolio: accelerate the launch with external use, develop internal personnel in parallel, and reinforce only the permanently needed core through hiring.

  • Phase 1 (0–6 months): Create the first results with an external partner. Select internal members to work in parallel and prepare the container for OJT
  • Phase 2 (6 months–1.5 years): Fully launch development of the selected members. Centered on business architect candidates (planning and field personnel who know the business), develop them using real work as the material
  • Phase 3 (1.5 years onward): Make the promotion organization permanent. Gradually reduce external dependence and fill only the missing specialist roles (data scientists, etc.) through hiring

The key to this design is contracting external use not as "delegation" but as a "development mechanism." Unless handover and skill transfer are included in the contract terms, Phase 2 and beyond never begins. Criteria for evaluating external partners are organized in "How to Choose an AX Implementation Partner."

A Development Case Study | Six Non-Engineer Departments Achieving AX in Six Months

A "development-centered" approach works even for non-engineer organizations if designed correctly. Here is an example from FDX's training and accompaniment business.

Track Record of the Training Business

FDX's parent training business has surpassed 30,000 cumulative trainees and 150 client companies, with a trainee satisfaction rate of 97% (publicly announced figure when trainees numbered 15,000). The goal is not the numbers themselves; rather, the implementation experience at this scale has accumulated patterns of "where trainees stumble" and "what determines retention," which directly improves the precision of development design.

All Six Departments Achieving AX at a 50-Person, Non-Engineer Company

At a mid-sized creative company (50 employees), all six non-engineer departments—HR, administration, sales, creative, and others—achieved their AX goals after six months of monthly accompaniment (AI-OJT study sessions plus one-on-one sparring sessions with their assigned coach). Five of the departments achieved at an S rating or above.

  • HR: Automated routine tasks around the recruitment ATS, reducing routine work by 720 minutes per month (150% of target, achieving an S rating)
  • Creative: Automation of the submission process delivered a 76-hour monthly reduction (exceeding the 47-hour target by 49%)
  • Design: Production time per project cut by 46 minutes, from 107 minutes to 61 minutes
  • Sales: Preparation time for second meetings reduced by 25 minutes per deal

What is particularly noteworthy is that these implementations were carried out not by external engineers but by the non-engineer staff in each department themselves. Through six months of hands-on accompaniment, the staff learned to use Claude Code, GAS, and the official MCP of an accounting SaaS on their own, reaching the point where they could break down their workflows and build automations. The developer of the accounting SaaS's official MCP evaluated them as "a company that has reached this level has almost no precedent" (case study).

What this case demonstrates is that the success or failure of DX talent development hinges not on participants' science and engineering background, but on an accompaniment design based on real work. Merely "learning about" something in classroom sessions does not change how work is done. It is within a cycle that uses one's own department's work data, sets target KPIs, and verifies results monthly that employees who know the business acquire digital implementation skills.

Redefinition in the Generative AI Era | From DX Talent to AX Talent

How talent requirements change in an era where AI handles "execution"

The conventional discourse on DX talent has assumed that "humans master digital tools." However, the practical deployment of generative AI and AI agents has changed that premise. A division of labor in which AI handles the execution of the work itself, while humans focus on breaking down and designing the work, and on judgment and verification has become a reality (for the full picture of this shift, see "What Is AX (AI Transformation)?").

The capabilities required of DX talent in this environment—that is, AX talent—can be distilled into three.

  • Work decomposition skills: the ability to break down one's own work into processes and distinguish which parts to delegate to AI and which parts humans should judge
  • AI implementation skills: the ability to actually automate the separated processes using prompts, no-code tools, and agents
  • Verification and governance skills: the ability to evaluate AI output quality, manage risks, and keep the system running as an ongoing mechanism

What is notable is that all three of these are grounded in business knowledge rather than programming experience. Since code generation is handled by AI itself, the technical barrier to entry keeps falling. As the six-department case above shows, non-engineers with deep knowledge of the business are the largest candidate pool for AX talent, and we are entering an era in which a substantial portion of the "DX talent shortage" can be filled by redefining and retraining existing employees. IPA has also pointed out the challenges of the talent development environment in the AI era in its discussion paper "Developing Digital Talent in the AI Era," and the updating of the talent profile is progressing publicly as well.

A New Form of External Leverage: FDE and DS

On the external leverage side as well, new role models for the AI era have emerged. One is the FDE (Forward Deployed Engineer), who embeds at customer sites and handles everything end-to-end from business understanding through implementation and handover to operations. The other is the DS (Deployment Strategist), who works alongside executives to drive outcome definition, KPI design, and exit decisions for AI implementation. Born at Palantir and adopted by AI companies such as OpenAI and Anthropic, this model can be described, in IPA's taxonomy, as an entity that provides the functions of a software engineer and a business architect "from the outside, with handover as the premise."

Since business architects are the most scarce talent at Japanese companies, a structure that supplements that function with DS/FDE while developing business architect candidates in-house through accompaniment is rational as a talent portfolio. For details, see "What Is an FDE (Forward Deployed Engineer)?" and "What Is a Deployment Strategist (DS)?"; for how to build an organization with FDE-like functions in-house, see "How to Build an In-House FDE Organization".

Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ)

Q1. What is the difference between DX talent and IT talent?

A. While IT personnel are specialists in the means of information systems planning, development, and operations, DX personnel are drivers of transformation who use digital technology and data to transform the business and operations themselves. What is required is not technical skill alone, but the ability to combine business understanding with technical understanding to drive everything from setting transformation goals to verifying their impact. Even in IPA's taxonomy, the business architect who oversees transformation is positioned at the core.

Q2. What are the five DX personnel types defined by IPA?

A. The Digital Skill Standard (DX Promotion Skill Standard) formulated by IPA and the Ministry of Economy, Trade and Industry defines five types: business architect, designer, data scientist, software engineer, and cybersecurity. In ver.2.0, released in April 2026, data management, which handles the foundation for leveraging data and AI, was newly added, expanding the taxonomy to six types. It is advisable to review your company's skill map in line with revisions to the standard.

Q3. Should hiring or training DX personnel be prioritized?

A. It is not an either-or choice; the correct approach is to allocate resources based on time horizons and the importance of business knowledge. In a market where 85.1% of Japanese companies report talent shortages, relying solely on hiring is structurally disadvantageous, and in fact internal training is the most common means of securing talent among Japanese companies. The standard playbook is a staggered portfolio: accelerate the launch with external resources while training employees who know the business, and reinforce only the core, permanently needed roles through hiring.

Q4. Can non-engineer employees become DX personnel?

A. Yes. With the practical adoption of generative AI, code generation can now be handled by AI itself, and the center of gravity of personnel requirements has shifted to the ability to break down business processes and verify results. These are abilities grounded in business knowledge, so non-engineers who know the business deeply are strong candidates. As a real example, at a company of about 50 employees, staff from six non-engineering departments—HR, administration, sales, and others—reached the point where they could independently implement automation for their own departments after six months of hands-on, accompanied training.

Q5. As generative AI evolves, won't training DX personnel become unnecessary?

A. Quite the opposite. The more AI takes on the execution of work, the more valuable become the people who can design which tasks to delegate to AI, where humans make decisions, and how to verify outputs. Because AI cannot define a company's business context or assign responsibility on its own, in organizations without personnel who can break down business processes and connect them to AI, introducing tools does not translate into results. Only the target of training has changed—from tool operation to designing the division of labor with AI—and the need for training has in fact increased.

Q6. How long does it take to train DX personnel?

A. It depends on the level. The foundational level, where all employees can use generative AI in daily work, takes from a few weeks to a few months; the implementation level, where they can build automation for their own work themselves, takes roughly six months of hands-on, accompanied training using real work as the subject matter. In the actual example as well, six non-engineering departments reached self-sufficiency through six months of monthly accompanied training. However, with classroom-lecture-centered designs, retention does not happen no matter how long the period is extended, so rather than duration, it is the design of real-work connection and impact measurement that determines success or failure.
