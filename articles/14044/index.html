What is a forward deployed engineer?

FDE Interview Prep · 2026-09-25 · 45 min read

Summary

This guide explains the forward deployed engineer (FDE) role for job seekers: a software engineer embedded with one customer who ships production software, rather than offering advice. Drawing on postings and blogs from Palantir, OpenAI, Anthropic, Google Cloud and others, it covers responsibilities, travel expectations and hiring criteria. It distinguishes two FDE types — building agents and evals at AI companies versus platform deployment and data integration at enterprise software firms — traces the role's origin to Palantir's Delta and Echo labels, and documents the 2025-2026 expansion, with OpenAI, AWS and Microsoft committing billions to dedicated FDE organizations.

Key points

  • An FDE's scope is one customer's outcome, not a product component.
  • Palantir calls FDEs Deltas: one customer, many capabilities, versus a Dev's one capability, many customers.
  • Two job types: AI labs build agents and evals; enterprise firms deploy platforms and integrate data.
  • Travel ranges from every 4-8 weeks (Databricks) to 70-90% of time (Palantir NATO role).
  • OpenAI, AWS ($1B) and Microsoft ($2.5B, 6,000 staff) built FDE organizations in 2026.

You keep seeing the title on job boards, often at companies you would love to work for, and the description reads half like software engineering and half like consulting. Before you rewrite your resume or spend a weekend on prep, you want to know which half is the job.

Here is the short answer. A forward deployed engineer (FDE) is a software engineer who works inside one customer’s problem, on the customer’s site or alongside its team, and ships production software there: integrations, data pipelines, applications and, at AI companies, agents and evaluations. The customer’s outcome sets the scope, not a product component. Palantir, which says it pioneered the position, calls it the , calls its holders “Deltas”, and sums up their focus as “one customer, many capabilities”.   • What it is: an engineer whose scope is one customer’s outcome, and who builds the software that gets it there, rather than advising someone else who will.
  • The two kinds: AI labs and AI product companies hire FDEs to build agents and evaluations on their models; enterprise software and data companies hire them to deploy their platform for a customer and integrate the customer’s data into it. The definition, in employers’ words

Every employer words the job a little differently, and the exact line matters when you tailor an answer to one company. These are the definitions employers publish on their postings and blogs:

  • Palantir says Deltas deploy its software platforms to customers, sit in Business Development, and have a mandate to achieve technical outcomes for customers.   • OpenAI’s San Francisco FDE posting says the team works with customers to turn research breakthroughs into production systems, and that FDEs own discovery, technical scoping, system design, build and production rollout.   • Anthropic’s FDE postings put the role on its Applied AI team, embedded with its most strategic customers to build production applications with Claude, including servers, sub-agents and skills.   • Google Cloud’s GenAI FDE postings describe an embedded builder who codes, debugs and jointly ships agentic solutions inside the customer’s environment, as opposed to a traditional advisory role. Read side by side, the definitions have the same shape: an engineer, placed on one customer’s problem, who builds software that reaches production. None of them stops at advice: each ends in software the engineer builds or deploys.

The FDE meaning, in one answer you can say out loud

A recruiter may open with “What do you think this role is?” A vague answer suggests you have not worked out whether the job is to build or to advise. Here is a version built from the definitions above. Change the middle sentence to a line from the posting in front of you:

“An FDE is a software engineer whose scope is one customer’s problem rather than one part of the product. Your posting says the FDE owns everything from discovery to production rollout, so I read that as: I scope it, I build it in their environment, and I’m the person they call when the rollout breaks. It’s judged on whether the customer’s work actually changes, and what I learn in the field goes back to the product team.”

What the job is not

  • Not a consultant. Palantir calls it a common misconception that a Delta is a consultant; a Delta quoted on its blog says consultants typically produce a one-time analysis, whereas Deltas build a long-term solution with the customer.   • Not an advisory architect. The Pragmatic Engineer reported, in August 2025, drawing on input from OpenAI’s Colin Jarvis, that OpenAI’s FDEs write code directly on customer infrastructure using customer tooling, while its solutions architects are more advisory.   • Not one fixed job. At AI Engineer World’s Fair 2026, Sierra’s Head of Agent Engineering, Natalie Meurer, said every forward deployed engineer is accountable to the customer, whether the work is DevOps, enablement, custom solutions or data integration. Where the role came from

The role as employers use it today traces to Palantir, which says it pioneered the position. Source 1Palantir Technologies - Forward Deployed Software Engineer (New York, NY)PublisherPalantir (Lever job board)Source typecompany job posting Palantir’s blog said in April 2019 that Software Engineers and Forward Deployed Software Engineers were its biggest engineering roles by headcount, called “Devs” and “Deltas” internally. Source 2Dev versus Delta: Demystifying engineering roles at PalantirPublisherPalantir BlogSource typecompany blog It draws the difference in two phrases: a Dev’s focus is “one capability, many customers”, and a Delta’s is “one customer, many capabilities”. Source 2Dev versus Delta: Demystifying engineering roles at PalantirPublisherPalantir BlogSource typecompany blogSource 3A Day in the Life of a Palantir Forward Deployed Software EngineerPublisherPalantir BlogSource typecompany blog

That pair of phrases is the most useful thing in this guide to remember. In our reading, it means your week is set by the customer’s problem: a data integration, a permissions fix and a platform bug can all be yours at once, and nobody hands you a ticket that says which one matters most.

Palantir Delta, and Echo

The name “Delta” comes from Palantir’s early days, when each Business Development team was named after a letter of the NATO alphabet. Source 19Beyond Founder Mode: Mission Mode (Palantir Blog)PublisherPalantir BlogSource typecompany blog The labels survive: as of September 2026, Palantir’s New York posting is filed under the team label “Echo” and its FDSE posting under “Delta”. What a week looks like

No employer publishes a measured typical FDE week. What exists are individual accounts, and they agree on one thing: the mix changes with the phase of the project.

  • Dyon, a Delta in Abu Dhabi quoted on Palantir’s 2019 blog, said most weeks included a couple of days at the customer’s premises, split between stakeholder meetings and monitoring, debugging, deploying or configuring the software. Source 2Dev versus Delta: Demystifying engineering roles at PalantirPublisherPalantir BlogSource typecompany blog G.M., a Delta in São Paulo, said some weeks went mostly to developing and reviewing code and others mostly to scoping with a client or working with users. An illustrative week, AI version

This is our composite, invented to show the rhythm. It is not a measured week. Picture an FDE placed with a regional insurer that wants an assistant to route incoming claims:

  • Monday, on site. The head of claims says every claim arrives by email. You sit with the adjusters and learn that the urgent claims arrive through a broker portal nobody mentioned.
  • Tuesday. You ask for read access to the portal’s database. IT says it needs a security review. You write the one-page request that afternoon, naming exactly which tables you need and why.
  • Wednesday. While you wait, you ship the email path end to end to a handful of adjusters: ingestion, a routing model, and a button that says “wrong queue”. It is thin, but it runs on their data.
  • Thursday. The “wrong queue” clicks show the router sending burst-pipe claims to the flood queue and storm-drain claims to escape of water, because both say “water”. You add those claims to your evaluation set, change the prompt, and replay last week’s real claims to check that the misroutes drop and nothing else breaks.
  • Friday, remote. You write the weekly update for the customer’s sponsor, and a separate note to your product team: the platform has no connector for this broker portal, and the next insurer will need one too.

Discovery, build, evaluation, stakeholder writing and product feedback all happened in one week, not in sequence. The same week, enterprise version

Another composite, for the other kind of FDE job. Picture an FDE placed with a manufacturer whose maintenance planners want one screen of overdue work orders per plant:

  • Monday, on site. The planners say the ERP has everything. You ask each of them to show you how they find overdue work today, and learn that the Derby planner keeps a spreadsheet of her own.
  • Tuesday. You pull the work orders and the asset register into the platform and join them, and the Derby plant’s work orders all vanish from the join.

Here is the check that catches it. Before you trust a join, count what did not match, per source:

-- How many work orders per plant found no asset?
SELECT w.plant,
       COUNT(*) AS work_orders,
       SUM(CASE WHEN a.asset_id IS NULL THEN 1 ELSE 0 END) AS unmatched
FROM work_orders w
LEFT JOIN assets a ON CAST(a.asset_id AS TEXT) = w.asset_ref
GROUP BY w.plant;
plant  work_orders  unmatched
Derby  3            3
Leeds  2            0

Every Derby row is unmatched and every Leeds row matches, so the problem is how Derby writes the key, not missing assets. Leeds writes an asset reference as 42; Derby writes it as

P02-00042

. Strip the prefix and compare numbers:

-- SQLite. In Postgres 14 or later: CAST(split_part(w.asset_ref, '-', -1) AS INTEGER)
LEFT JOIN assets a
  ON a.asset_id = CAST(SUBSTR(w.asset_ref, INSTR(w.asset_ref, '-') + 1) AS INTEGER)

Rerun the count and Derby drops to zero unmatched.

  • Wednesday. You ask the Derby planner why the references look different, and learn that the prefix is Derby’s plant code, added because Derby’s system was set up separately, and that the number after it is the same company-wide asset number Leeds uses. You write that down as a rule the pipeline enforces, not a one-off fix.
  • Thursday. The screen ships to the Leeds and Derby planners, and you watch which columns they sort by.
  • Friday, remote. A note to your product team: the connector should normalize plant-prefixed keys, because the next manufacturer will have them too.

This is the enterprise row of the table above, the one with Palantir, Databricks and MongoDB in it: one customer’s data, integrated on the employer’s platform, with an application on top. The sentence to say when an interviewer asks how you know a pipeline is right: “Before I show anyone a number, I count what failed to join, split by source, because a silent join failure looks exactly like a quiet week.” To practice this side of the job, fix a join whose fan-out inflates revenue and design a platform that unifies a retailer’s data sources. What this changes in your interview answers

End your stories on the customer’s outcome, not on the fact that you shipped. Compare:

  • Weak: “I built a document-extraction pipeline for a logistics customer.”
  • Strong: “I built the extraction pipeline, the customer’s billing team stopped keying invoices by hand, and the one field it kept missing became a feature request our product team shipped for everyone.”

The second answer maps onto what OpenAI says it measures: adoption, workflow impact and feedback that changes the roadmap. Source 11Forward Deployed Engineer (FDE) - SFPublisherOpenAI CareersSource typecompany job posting

The other pattern to show is re-scoping. Jarvis described it to The Pragmatic Engineer and on the South Park Commons panel: re-scope when what the customer described does not match the data, and tear up a solution that users do not use. Source 13What are Forward Deployed Engineers, and why are they so in demand? (Gergely Orosz)PublisherThe Pragmatic EngineerSource typenews reportSource 34The Future of Forward Deployed Engineering | OpenAI, Ramp, Nominal, DatalandPublisherSouth Park Commons (YouTube)Source typerecorded talk or interview Show that you did both:

  • Weak: “The spec asked for a dashboard, so I built the dashboard.”
  • Strong: “By the third day the data showed the dashboard’s numbers could not be trusted. I told the sponsor that afternoon, re-scoped to the weekly report the planners already used, and when they ignored my first cut of it, I rebuilt it around the column they actually read.”

Practice the outcome ending on a project you owned end to end and re-scoping on a decision you got wrong and reversed. Both are free, model answers included. In Pro, go on to the customer outcome you are proudest of and leaving a customer able to run what you built.

The first point is the one to be able to show in code. Here is Thursday of the insurer week as a replay check: last week’s claims, each with the label the adjuster confirmed and the label the router chose, and a count of which labels get confused with which.

from collections import Counter

# Last week's claims: (text, label the adjuster confirmed, label the router chose)
replay = [
    ("Burst pipe under the kitchen sink", "escape_of_water", "flood"),
    ("River came over the bank into the ground floor", "flood", "flood"),
    ("Storm drain backed up into the garage", "flood", "escape_of_water"),
    ("Washing machine hose split overnight", "escape_of_water", "escape_of_water"),
    ("Hail cracked roof tiles", "storm", "storm"),
    ("Radiator valve leaking through the ceiling", "escape_of_water", "flood"),
]

right = sum(want == got for _, want, got in replay)
print(f"routed correctly: {right}/{len(replay)}")
confusions = Counter((want, got) for _, want, got in replay if want != got)
for (want, got), n in confusions.most_common():
    print(f"  {want} sent to {got}: {n}")
routed correctly: 3/6
  escape_of_water sent to flood: 2
  flood sent to escape_of_water: 1

The confusion count tells you which way the router is wrong, which an accuracy figure alone hides. Keep the replay set, rerun it after every prompt or model change, and add each new “wrong queue” click to it. The sentence to say to the customer’s sponsor, or to an interviewer: “Before I ship a prompt change, I rerun last week’s real claims against it, so I can show you the fix did not break flood routing.”
