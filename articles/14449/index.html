What level do you think you are? · FDE interview question

FDE Interview Prep · 2026 · 6 min read

Summary

This interview-prep piece explains how to answer the FDE question "what level do you think you are?" It argues the question is technical, not a modesty test: your level sets your pay band and scope. Scale AI postings from September 2026 list Senior and Staff Frontier Agents Engineer roles requiring 5+ and 8+ years, with base ranges of $216,000–$270,000 and $252,000–$315,000 that overlap. How to answer

Where an employer posts pay by level, the level you are hired at sets your band and your scope, so treat this as a technical question with evidence, not a modesty test. Scale AI’s Senior and Staff Frontier Agents Engineer (Forward Deployed Engineering) postings, as of September 2026, ask for 5+ and 8+ years and list base ranges of $216,000 to $270,000 and $252,000 to $315,000. Say it in this order.

  • Ask which levels the req is open at before you name one: “Is this role open at senior, staff or both?” If the posting shows one title and no level, ask what level range the req is budgeted for.
  • Name the level in their words. Read the posting and the company’s titles first, then say “I’d put myself at senior, the level your posting calls Senior FDE.”
  • Give two pieces of scope evidence that match that level’s description: the size and ambiguity of a problem you owned end to end, and who depended on your decisions. Name the system and the outcome.
  • Say what the next level would take. One honest sentence on the gap shows you know the ladder: “Staff would mean setting direction across teams for a year; I’ve done it for a quarter.”
  • Invite their read. “How does your ladder separate senior from staff? I’d like the loop to test me against that.”

What not to do. Don’t argue from years of experience or a title; a senior title at a small company and at a large one describe different scope, and the Google report above came from someone with a decade behind them. Don’t pick the lower level to seem safe; you would be giving the recruiter a lower anchor before anyone has interviewed you. If you sit between two levels, ask to be interviewed at the higher one and say you’d accept a lower result. The recruiter then hears a claim you are willing to have tested, not a demand.

The trap is claiming the higher level with adjectives instead of scope. Hear the difference:

  • Weak: “I operate at a staff level.”
  • Strong: “I set the integration standard every partner team now follows. I’ve done that for a quarter, not a year.”

An adjective gives the recruiter nothing to check, and if the loop comes back lower, you have no evidence left to argue from.

Follow-ups

What the interviewer may ask next, once your first answer is on the table.

  • Your current title is senior. Why should we consider you for staff?
  • What would you need to have done to be at the next level up?
  • If the loop comes back a level lower than you asked for, what would you do?

Where answers go wrong

  • Arguing from years of experience or a title, when the ladder asks about the scope of the problems you owned.
  • Answering “whatever you think” and handing over the anchor before a single interviewer has seen you.

Illustrative answer about a fictional project

Written in the first person to show the structure. Tell yours from your own work.

“Before I answer, is this role open at senior, staff or both?”

The recruiter says both.

“Then I’d put myself at senior, the level your posting calls Senior , with some staff scope. Two things I’d point to.

“First, at a payments software firm I designed and led the reconciliation service for our three largest partner banks. I chose to match on the bank’s own reference before amount and date, and it took the first partner from about 1,100 manual breaks a week to under 90.

“Second, I’m the engineer our account team brings on-site when a partner escalates. When our largest partner’s settlement file broke in March, I was the one there; I decided to roll back the matcher rather than hot-fix it, and they settled the same day.

“Staff, as your posting describes it, means setting technical direction across several teams for a year or more. I’ve done that for one quarter, with the integration standard every partner team now follows.

“So I’m between the two. I’d like to be interviewed against staff, and I’d accept a senior result if that’s where the loop lands. How does your ladder separate them?”

Why this works: the speaker finds out which levels are open before naming one, states the level in the posting’s own title, and backs it with scope: a named system, a design call and a result, then an escalation where the call was the speaker’s. The gap is named before the recruiter has to find it, and the close asks to be tested at the higher level while accepting the lower one, rather than arguing for a title.

If the loop comes back at senior, don’t argue with the result; ask what the panel saw: “Thanks. Before I accept the level, what did the panel see as the gap to staff? If it’s something one more conversation could test, I’d like that chance. If not, I’d like the offer to reflect where I sit in the senior band.” That answers the third follow-up: it asks for evidence and one more chance, and it doesn’t relitigate the interviews.
