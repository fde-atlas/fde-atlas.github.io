What Does a Forward Deployed Engineer Do?

Reveneau · 2026-09-08 · 10 min read

Summary

This Reveneau guide explains what forward deployed engineers actually do: writing production code inside customer systems while learning requirements by sitting with users. Drawing on an analysis of 1,000 job postings, it breaks the role into four repeating activities, explains the 20/80 time-split rule as a diagnostic, and distinguishes the title from sales engineering and consulting roles.

Key points

  • The work cycles through four activities: in-environment discovery, building, integration, and getting changes to production.
  • A healthy split is roughly 20 percent customer-facing and 80 percent building; drift toward 50/50 signals scope, blocking, or mislabeled sales roles.
  • Across 1,000 postings, customer work appeared in 55 percent, AI/ML building in 37 percent, and integrations in 32 percent.
  • Genuinely production-focused roles spend 70 to 90 percent of time coding; sales-engineering variants only 30 to 40 percent.
  • The hardest parts are non-technical: working without a specification and declining customer-specific scope creep.

A forward deployed engineer spends most of the week writing production code inside the customer's systems, and the rest of it finding out what the code should do by sitting with the people who will use it. A useful guideline from teams that run the model well is about 20 percent of the time facing the customer and 80 percent building. When that ratio inverts, the engineer has become an account manager and the deployment has stopped moving. Across 1,000 job postings, the three duties named most often were working directly with customers, building and deploying AI systems, and integrating systems and APIs.

Key takeaways

  • The work splits into four repeating activities: discovery inside the environment, building, integrating, and getting the thing through the customer's path to production.
  • First Round Review's guidance puts the healthy time split at roughly 20 percent customer-facing and 80 percent building. An engineer at 60 percent customer-facing has stopped releasing work.
  • Across 1,000 postings carrying the title, the duties named most often were working directly with customers (55 percent), building and deploying AI and ML systems (37 percent), and integrating systems and APIs (32 percent).
  • The hardest part of the job is usually not the code. It is getting a change through an approval path that was designed for a different kind of software.

The short version: a forward deployed engineer builds software inside somebody else's company, learns what it should do by being there, and stays until it runs.

The longer version is worth having, because the daily pattern of the work is what separates this role from the four or five jobs that look similar in a job description.

Four activities, repeating

Discovery inside the environment. Sitting with the people who will use the software, watching what they actually do, and finding the parts of the process nobody mentioned in the first meeting. This is not a phase that finishes. It runs alongside the building, because building is what raises the next question.

Building. Production code, in a repository, reviewed and merged. This is the bulk of the week and the thing that makes the role engineering rather than advisory. In the 1,000 postings analysed by Henley Wing Chiu in November 2025, the roles that were genuinely production-focused reported 70 to 90 percent of time spent coding.

Integration. Connecting to the systems that already exist, which in a large company means authentication, an identity provider, a data warehouse somebody built in 2014, and an API whose documentation describes a version that was replaced. Integrations were named in 32 percent of postings.

Getting to production. Change advisory boards, security review, a penetration test, a data protection assessment, a release window. This is the part that surprises engineers coming from a product company, and it is where deployments fail.

What a week looks like

Not every week is the same, and this pattern repeats.

Monday tends to be alignment: what was released last week, what the customer's own team is blocked on, what the next thing to prove is. Tuesday to Thursday is where the code gets written, usually with one or two conversations a day pulled in as questions come up. Friday is often for the dull but important work: the review comments, the deployment paperwork, and writing down what was learned so it is not lost when the engagement ends.

Running through all of it is a habit that defines the role: release something small every week, against real data. Not a demo. Something a real user can open. Week-one output is the best scoping tool in the model, because what breaks when you try to release something small tells you more about the engagement than any discovery document. What a forward deployed engineer releases in week one goes into that.

The 20/80 rule, and why it matters

First Round Review's February 2026 guidance on hiring forward deployed engineers puts the time allocation at around 20 percent customer-facing and 80 percent building.

Treat that as a diagnostic rather than a target. When the split moves toward half and half, one of three things has happened: the scope is unclear so the engineer is in meetings trying to establish it, the customer's organization is blocking and the engineer has become the person asking again and again for approvals, or the role was a sales engineering role all along and nobody said so.

All three are fixable and none of them get fixed by the engineer working harder. They get fixed by somebody senior deciding what the deployment is for and who removes the obstacles.

What the job is not

It is not writing reports. A forward deployed engineer who spends the week producing documents has been handed a consulting engagement with a different job title.

It is not running demos. Demos happen before the contract, and that is a sales engineering role. Of the 1,000 postings analysed, none of the forward deployed roles carried a sales quota, which is the clearest single test for telling the two apart.

It is not owning the customer relationship. Somebody has to, and it should not be the person whose week is supposed to be 80 percent building.

The part nobody puts in the job advert

Two things make this job harder than an equivalent product engineering job, and neither is technical.

The first is working without a specification. You are handed a vague problem, a real deadline, and an environment you did not build. Nobody can tell you what correct looks like, so part of the job is establishing that, out loud, in front of people who disagree with each other.

The second is saying no. Every embedded engineer accumulates requests, and each one sounds reasonable on its own. First Round's rule is the useful one: expand the scope when the work creates something reusable, and stop when the next iteration will only ever serve this one customer. Somebody has to make that decision weekly, and if it is not made the engagement never ends. What goes wrong in forward deployed engagements collects the failure modes and their early warning signs.

How this differs by company

The role is not standardised, and the biggest variable is what the engineer is building on.

At a company with a mature platform, the forward deployed engineer configures, extends and integrates, and the custom part is small. At an earlier company, the engineer is writing much more from scratch, and the risk is that the result is a fork nobody can maintain.

The AWS Partner Network's description of its partner-led model names what the mature version looks like: reusable delivery harnesses including domain ontologies, evaluation frameworks and capability registries. That is the tooling that stops each deployment from starting with nothing, and building it is part of the job once more than one customer exists.

Where we sit

We work this way, so the honest version is worth stating. The build side of that 80 percent is AI-written on our engagements, and every change has to pass an eval suite derived from the specification before it reaches your branch. That changes the costs of the model: the discovery, the integration and the path to production still take a person, and writing the code takes less time than it used to. What it does not change is who is accountable when it does not run.

Common questions

What does a forward deployed engineer do day to day?

Four activities repeat: discovery with the people who will use the software, writing production code, integrating with the customer's existing systems, and getting changes through the customer's path to production. The habit that defines the role is releasing something small and real every week rather than waiting to show everything at once at the end.

How much of the job is coding?

In roles that are genuinely production-focused, 70 to 90 percent of the time, according to an analysis of 1,000 job postings published in November 2025. In postings that were really sales engineering roles under the same title, it was 30 to 40 percent. Asking for the number is the fastest way to tell which job you are being offered or sold.

What is the 20/80 rule for forward deployed engineers?

First Round Review's guidance puts the healthy split at around 20 percent of time facing the customer and 80 percent building. Use it as a diagnostic. When an engineer moves toward half and half, either the scope is unclear, the customer's organization is blocking, or the role was a sales engineering role from the start.

Do forward deployed engineers run demos and support sales?

That is a sales engineering role, which is a different job that happens before the contract is signed. Of 1,000 postings carrying the forward deployed title, none were quota-carrying. Some companies do blend the two, so ask directly whether the role carries a sales target.

What is the hardest part of the job?

Two things, and neither is technical. Working without a specification, which means establishing what correct looks like in front of people who disagree. And saying no to requests that only ever serve one customer, which is the discipline that decides whether the engagement ever ends.

Who owns the customer relationship during an engagement?

Somebody other than the embedded engineer should. If the engineer is the relationship owner as well as the builder, the customer-facing share of their week grows until the building stops. A named account owner on the vendor side and a named person on the customer side who removes obstacles keep the engineer building.

What is a forward deployed engineer?

A forward deployed engineer is a software engineer placed inside a customer's organization to build and release production software there, against that customer's real data and real systems, and who stays accountable for the deployment until it is running. The title is often written FDE, and the variant FDSE means forward deployed software engineer. Palantir popularised the role and AWS, OpenAI, Anthropic and Google now use the title. Three tests separate it from the roles that resemble it: production code, the customer's environment, and ownership of the outcome.

The three jobs hiding behind the FDE title

Forward deployed engineer is one title covering at least three different jobs. An analysis of 1,000 postings carrying the title, published by Henley Wing Chiu in November 2025 using Revealera data, split them into a production engineer embedded with customers (60 percent), a sales engineer with implementation duties (30 percent), and an internal tools builder who faces no customer at all (10 percent). The three differ in how much of the week is spent writing code, how pay is structured, and who is accountable for production. Anyone hiring for the title, taking the job, or buying the service needs to know which one is being offered.

Why forward deployed engineering is growing

Forward deployed engineering grew because enterprise AI has an integration problem that nobody can solve from outside the customer's building. Three things happened close together: MIT NANDA reported in July 2025 that 95 percent of generative AI pilots produced no measurable profit-and-loss impact, a16z argued publicly in June 2025 that AI companies should hire teams focused on implementation, and in June 2026 AWS committed $1 billion to a forward deployed engineering organization. Job postings carrying the title grew 1,165 percent year over year. Each of those numbers deserves its caveat, and this page gives them.

Forward deployed engineering explained for executives

Forward deployed engineering means putting engineers inside your organization, or your customer's, to build and run a system rather than to advise on one. It costs more per engineer than any other delivery model and it is the only one that reliably gets an AI pilot to a production system. Three questions decide whether you should buy it: is the contract value large enough to pay for an engineer, do the environments differ from each other, and will anyone change the plan based on what the engineers find. Two noes means buy something else.
