What Happens After an FDE Project Ends: Bench Periods and Labor Law

Original title: FDE 项目结束后怎么办：待岗期与劳动法

FDE Times · 2026-11-04 · 6 min read

Summary

Using TCS's new policy as a case study, this article examines what happens to engineers when client projects end. Since June 2025, TCS caps bench time at 35 working days per year and requires 225 billable days annually; employees exceeding the limit reportedly face disciplinary action, PIPs, or termination. The author notes that even where law does not treat project completion as grounds for layoff (using Vietnam as an example), billable-day targets written into performance standards can become a termination pathway. Practical advice: clarify bench arrangements before signing, and build multi-person support at client sites while documenting project value to reduce dependence on a single champion.

Key points

  • TCS caps bench time at 35 working days per year, with a 225-day billable target.
  • Exceeding the limit can lead to discipline, PIPs, or termination.
  • Vietnam law doesn't count project end as layoff grounds, but performance standards may be exploited.
  • Show project results to at least two or three client stakeholders from month one.
  • Ask interviewers what happened to the last FDE after a project ended.

As of June 12, 2025, TCS has imposed a hard cap on gaps between projects: no employee may spend more than 35 working days per year on the bench. According to Indian media reports, those who exceed the cap without being assigned to a project may face disciplinary action, be placed on a performance improvement plan (PIP), or even have their contracts terminated.

Those holding Forward Deployed Engineer (FDE) offers share the same concern. An anonymous post on Blind claimed that an FDE deployed to a client was let go as soon as the engagement ended; another poster compared this model to consulting, where bench time is factored in by design. Both are just personal opinions, with no data behind them.

Projects will always end. The real questions are: how the company handles you during the gap afterward, what the law in your signing location allows the company to do, and how much weight you've built up for yourself at the client.

At TCS, bench time is a countdown

TCS's policy goes beyond the 35-day cap. It also requires every employee to have at least 225 days of billable client work per year.

The two numbers add up to 260. TCS hasn't published this figure, but 52 weeks a year at 5 days a week is exactly 260 days. By this math, even before deducting public holidays and annual leave, nearly the entire year's working days are already allocated.

Suppose an engineer is pulled off a project in September and waits six weeks for the next one. Six weeks is 30 working days, nearly exhausting the bench allowance, leaving almost no room to wait for anything else for the rest of the year.

Bench time isn't a vacation either. Under the new policy, employees without billable projects can no longer work remotely and must attend mandatory upskilling courses. Those waiting for projects end up under more constraints than those who have them.

The union's allegations are more troubling. The IT industry union NITES has filed a formal complaint with India's Ministry of Labour, claiming that employees not assigned to projects have repeatedly received emails pressuring them to resign, and has asked regulators to investigate.

These are still only union allegations, but they illustrate the point: bench time can be used to push people out without a formal layoff.

Experts interviewed by The Week said that letting bench employees go is a last resort, and that a company of TCS's scale has the capacity to give employees time to transition, but the risk of layoffs going forward is real.

The FDE title tells you nothing about how you'll be measured

This policy targets TCS's IT services employees and doesn't set a standard for the FDE profession. It simply shows, most clearly, how the billable-days logic works once it's written into policy.

Chris Degnan, who has publicly criticized the FDE role, called FDEs "glorified professional services people," placing the role in the professional services category. You don't have to agree with him. The criticism is worth hearing because it hits exactly where this profession is most vulnerable.

The difference lies in the metrics. If the company measures you by billable days, you're working in a billable-days model regardless of your title. TCS's logic could apply to you too—perhaps not spelled out so bluntly, but operating the same way.

So read the job description carefully and listen closely in interviews. If you hear terms like "utilization," "billable target," or "staffing pool," you're most likely being hired into a staffing system.

If the interviewer spends a lot of time discussing how to feed insights from client sites back into the product, that's a different model, and the gap between projects means something different there.

How far can the law protect you?

Labor laws differ by country. Take Vietnam as an example: an employer can unilaterally terminate a contract when an employee repeatedly fails to meet the performance standards set by the employer. The mere end of a project does not count as a legally recognized reason for economic layoff.

To dismiss an employee on grounds of economic layoff, the company must prove business difficulties or changes in technology or organizational structure, and give at least 30 days' notice. Laid-off employees receive one month's salary per year of service, with a minimum of two months. That means one year of work yields two months' salary, and four years yields four months.

The weak point is in the word "standards." If the company's published performance standards include billable days, time exceeding the bench cap could be recharacterized as "failure to complete work."

That would let the company take this route without having to prove grounds for layoff. This is an inference based on the structure of the law; no such cases have been reported so far, but this is exactly the clause you most need to read carefully.

When the terms are too vague, spending an hour consulting a labor lawyer usually costs far less than signing the wrong contract.

Is your project dependent on one person?

The contract can only tell you what happens after the project ends. Whether it ends early or late often depends heavily on whether the client sees its value. If all support rests on one person, once they leave, you'll almost have to rebuild the relationship from scratch.

Suppose you're deploying an AI agent for a bank that processes application materials. Your champion is the head of operations, and only they know how many work hours the system has saved.

If they leave mid-quarter, the successor sees only a running cost with no visible value, and your engagement is easily the first thing to get cut.

The safeguard lies in your daily work. From the first month, show your results to at least two or three people at different levels on the client side.

Document the project's value with numbers so that whoever newly takes over the account can understand it at a glance. That way the project depends less on any one person; and even if it does end, you'll have evidence to bring to the next client.

Bring these questions to the interview

The most useful question isn't on any interview question list: "The last time an engagement ended, what did the FDE in charge do in the following month?" If they give a specific answer—say, moving to another client, returning to the product team, or building internal tools—that's a good sign.

If the interviewer hesitates, that itself is the answer.

On your resume, show that you've worked on multiple client projects and carried something from one project to the next—a library, a process, or a set of evals.

Recruiters will see this as proof that you can create value beyond billable days. When you look for a new job, it also prevents you from being seen as someone who is only valuable when staffed at a client.

Every client project ends eventually. Before you need it, choose an employer that already has a clear answer for this gap.
