Vannevar Labs FDE Role Requirements and TS/SCI Security Clearance

Original title: Vannevar Labs FDE 岗位要求与 TS/SCI 安全许可

FDE Times · 2026-11-03 · 5 min read

Summary

This article examines Vannevar Labs' FDE job postings, where an active TS/SCI security clearance is a hard requirement because engineers must personally deploy Kubernetes microservices onto classified clouds such as IL6 and JWICS. Compliance dominates the work: meeting the Department of Defense's RMF standard, producing System Security Plans, runbooks and CONOPS, and integrating customer infrastructure like IdAM, SIEM and cross-domain solutions. The postings effectively exclude engineers without clearances, pure SaaS backgrounds, or willingness to travel 20-30%. The author argues these skills—deploying into controlled environments and documenting them—transfer directly to FDE work in banking, healthcare and other regulated industries.

Key points

  • An active TS/SCI clearance is a prerequisite, tied to classified servers rather than job titles.
  • Engineers deploy Kubernetes microservices hands-on to IL6 and JWICS classified clouds.
  • RMF compliance documents—System Security Plans, runbooks, CONOPS—are deliverables like code.
  • Integrating customer IdAM, SIEM and CDS systems takes more time than writing code.
  • The same skills apply to FDE roles in banks, hospitals and other regulated environments.

Key takeaways

  • Vannevar Labs' FDE job posting lists a valid TS/SCI security clearance as a hard requirement, because the software must be deployed onto classified clouds such as IL6 and JWICS.
  • A large part of the job is compliance: implementing security controls and writing System Security Plans, runbooks, and CONOPS to meet the US Department of Defense's RMF standards.
  • This posting shuts out engineers without a security clearance and engineers who have only deployed commercial SaaS. However, deploying to controlled environments is a skill needed across all heavily regulated industries.

In Vannevar Labs' Forward Deployed Engineer (FDE) job posting, the hardest requirement to meet has nothing to do with Python or Kubernetes. Candidates must hold a valid US Top Secret security clearance with SCI eligibility. This is a prerequisite for consideration, not a bonus.

At first glance, this looks like the usual administrative hurdle of the defense industry. But reading this FDE posting alongside the company's three other engineering job postings makes the picture much clearer: the security clearance is needed because the software runs on classified clouds, and engineers must personally deploy the code onto them.

You may never apply to a US defense company, but Vannevar illustrates a scenario: what an FDE's job looks like when the customer's environment is controlled to the extreme.

Many other industries also run controlled environments, just less strictly: banks, hospitals, and government agencies in various countries. Anyone who wants to be an FDE in these places should understand this kind of work.

The security clearance follows the servers, not the job title

Vannevar describes itself as a company building AI systems for the US Department of War (the new name for the Department of Defense). As of early 2023, its main product, Decrypt, was running on more than 15 military bases. Simply put, its customer is the military.

The posting for the Full Stack Engineer (FDE/ATO) role describes the mission as: migrating a complex microservices architecture running on Kubernetes into a classified cloud environment.

When deploying software on classified networks, engineers also provide direct "hands on keyboard" support. Judging by the wording, the person responsible for deployment typically works directly on the classified side, not just remotely.

The posting for Senior Software Engineer (ATO) names two environments: IL6 and JWICS. Clearly, the security clearance is tied to where the software runs, not to the title on an employee's badge.

This role goes beyond deployment, too. The FDE posting for Oahu states that the hire will become the technical lead for a mission and its associated customer engagements.

They are responsible for designing and building software, and for owning the software that runs directly in the customer's environment. The company also says that its engineers, product teams, and CTO go directly to where the problems are, including to troops in Ukraine.

Documentation is also a deliverable

Compared with an FDE at a SaaS company, the biggest difference is the compliance workload. The Oahu posting requires experience developing and deploying software under the Risk Management Framework (RMF). RMF is the process software must pass before it is authorized to run on US Department of Defense systems.

The FDE/ATO posting requires implementing key security controls to meet the Department of Defense's certification standards.

The Senior Software Engineer (ATO) posting lists the paperwork very specifically: deployment runbooks, a System Security Plan, and a CONOPS document. Here, documentation is as much a deliverable as code. Without documentation, the software cannot be authorized; without authorization, the software cannot run.

Imagine a requirement that sounds very simple: add a feature that pulls data from a government data platform and displays it to analysts.

At a SaaS company, this might take only a few days: call the API, build the interface, merge, deploy. The Oahu posting lists exactly the systems you would need to deal with: the data platform, the Identity and Access Management (IdAM) service, the Security Information and Event Management (SIEM) system, and a Cross Domain Solution (CDS).

In this hypothetical scenario, users must log in through the government's identity system, not yours. Every access sends logs to a SIEM monitored by the customer.

If data must flow between networks with different classification levels, it must pass through the CDS, and the System Security Plan and runbooks must be updated accordingly. The code itself might still take only a few days; the integration and documentation would likely take up most of the time.

Who this job posting shuts out

The word "active" in the security clearance requirement is the most critical detail. Without a current clearance, no amount of technical skill qualifies you. A security clearance is not something you can obtain after joining, so this role is nearly closed to engineers with purely commercial backgrounds.

The second group excluded is less obvious: engineers who have only deployed SaaS on commercial clouds. In that environment, everything is automated, and documentation is often written after the fact. They might pass a Kubernetes interview, but a role that requires RMF experience, familiarity with IdAM, SIEM, and CDS, and the ability to write a System Security Plan would likely quickly expose the gaps.

The third group is people who cannot travel. This FDE role is marked as fully remote but requires travel, potentially 20-30% of the time, to meet with the people responsible for the mission. "Remote" here does not mean staying at home.

Skills you can take away

Most readers do not qualify for this specific role, and there is no need to. What is worth learning is the capability these postings implicitly demand: getting software into an environment that is not yours, following someone else's rules, and then proving everything is secure with written materials.

An FDE working for a bank or a healthcare organization needs exactly the same capabilities, just with controls that are usually looser.

For people used to SaaS, the hardest part is deploying without internet access or your own infrastructure. Practicing this is far closer to real work than any Helm tutorial.

When writing your resume, do not bury compliance experience in a single line like "familiar with security." Spell out which identity systems you integrated, where you sent audit logs, and which documents you wrote for which audit; before the interview, prepare one example for each row in the last column of the table above.

A US TS/SCI security clearance only applies to this type of role. But writing a runbook on someone else's system that a stranger could follow step by step is a skill every highly regulated customer needs.
