How FDEs Explain System Architecture: Two Approaches Using the C4 Model

Original title: FDE 如何讲解系统架构：C4 模型两种讲法

FDE Times · 2026-11-18 · 5 min read

Summary

This guide argues that FDEs must explain the same system twice: once to an operations director who approves budgets and needs a decision, and once to the customer's principal engineer who will run the system after the FDE leaves. Using the C4 model, it recommends a system context diagram plus a trade-off write-up for executives, and a container diagram with runbooks for engineers. A hypothetical insurance claims AI agent illustrates both versions. Both artifacts must derive from a single model so promises and configurations stay consistent. It also lists common mistakes and suggests documenting both versions as portfolio evidence.

Key points

  • Directors need decisions; principal engineers need operational detail for on-call work.
  • Context diagrams avoid technical names; container diagrams carry them, from one shared model.
  • Trade-off write-ups should end with a question the director can answer, e.g. accepting a few minutes' delay.
  • Worst mistake: handing executive slides to engineers instead of diagrams and runbooks.
  • Side-by-side versions in a portfolio demonstrate both technical depth and communication judgment.

The director needs one paragraph to make a decision; the customer's principal engineer needs enough detail to operate the system independently after you leave.

Key takeaways

  • Choose the audience first, then the framing: directors care about decisions, principal engineers care about operations.
  • A context diagram should be understandable even to non-technical readers; technical names appear only on the container diagram, and both diagrams must come from the same model.
  • The technical version is what the customer's engineers will open after you leave the project, so treat it as a deliverable.

When a system breaks down at 3 a.m., the person who gets called is rarely the one who approved it. A Forward Deployed Engineer (FDE) usually has to explain the same system at least twice. The first time is in a conference room, to the operations director: he approves the budget and answers to management. The second time is to the customer's principal engineer: long after you have left the project, he is the one on call handling incidents in the middle of the night.

These two meetings require two versions of the story. FullStack writes that FDEs must explain technical trade-offs to people who will never read the code. Palantir goes further and splits this work into two roles: according to Oxagile, the Echo role owns the mission, stakeholders, and customer adoption, while the Delta role owns technical execution.

When you work alone, you have to play both roles yourself, and both explanations must come from the same model and never contradict each other.

Each audience arrives with different questions

Lucidchart recommends deciding who the audience is before choosing how to present. In FDE projects, the answer to this question is quite specific.

The director wants to know what the system will change about his work and what decisions he needs to make. The principal engineer wants to know what runs where, what could go wrong, and who fixes it when it does.

The C4 model provides exactly two corresponding levels. According to the C4 documentation, a system context diagram can be shown to non-technical people. Its audience needs no technical knowledge, so the safe approach is to draw only people and software systems.

The container diagram sits one level down and is aimed at developers and operations staff. It shows the high-level architecture, the main technology choices, and how the containers communicate with each other. Both diagrams describe the same system, so what you promise the director stays consistent with the configuration you hand to the engineers.

The diagrams themselves have advantages too. Lucidchart argues that visual content is easier to learn and remember than concepts conveyed only by reading or listening, so a diagram with four boxes often beats three pages of text.

One claims system, explained twice

Consider a hypothetical case. You have deployed an AI agent for an insurance company: it reads claims emails, extracts fields, and places them in an interface for claims adjusters to approve. One decision must be made: process each claim immediately as the email arrives, or put claims into a queue and process them one by one.

The version for the director opens with a four-box context diagram: the customer submitting the claim, the claims adjuster, the company's existing policy management system, and the AI model provider. Not a single arrow on the diagram carries a database name.

Next comes a trade-off explanation, written so the director can answer immediately after reading it:

"There are two options. Option one: read each claim as soon as the email arrives, and the adjuster sees the result almost immediately. The cost is that if the external AI service slows down or goes down, some claims will need to be resubmitted. Option two: claims enter a queue and are read one by one, with results ready in a few minutes. In exchange, even if the AI service has problems, no claim is ever lost. The team recommends option two. There is only one thing we need you to confirm: for the current claims review process, is waiting a few minutes acceptable?"

The version for the principal engineer is a container diagram of the same system. It shows an email ingestion service, a queue, workers that call the model API, a Postgres database storing the extracted results, and a web application for the claims adjusters.

At this point, the conversation becomes: how many retries are allowed, where failed messages are stored, where alerts are set, and who is on call when the queue backs up.

The table below presents the same decision in two framings, side by side:

AspectFor the operations directorFor the principal engineer
DecisionWhether waiting a few minutes is acceptableSwitching from synchronous processing to a queue plus workers
RiskClaims may need to be resubmittedModel API call timeouts, lost messages
MitigationNo claim is ever lostCapped retries, a place to store failed messages
Follow-up taskAnswer yes or noReceive the runbook and on-call schedule

Don't let the two versions become two stories

Start from a single model: list the actors, systems, and containers in one file that the whole team edits together. Draw the context diagram first. If you cannot draw the system without mentioning technology, you may not yet understand who it serves.

Then write the trade-off explanation. The Hustling Engineer suggests opening with why the concept matters to the audience, and always tying technical work to concrete outcomes. So this paragraph should end with a question the audience can answer, such as "is waiting a few minutes acceptable?"

The Hustling Engineer also recommends using everyday objects and experiences as analogies to make complex concepts easier to grasp, and defining any jargon that cannot be avoided.

Try replacing "latency" with "waiting a few minutes" and "retries" with "automatic resending." A queue can be compared to a bank's ticket machine: first come, first served, and when one window temporarily closes, no one gets skipped.

Only then draw the container diagram, copying component names from the same model and adding technical details. To close, ask the director to restate the decision in his own words. If he restates exactly the question you posed, the explanation has succeeded.

Common mistakes come from both ends

With non-technical audiences, the most common mistake is oversimplifying to the point of condescension. a16z describes the FDE's way of working as going deep inside the customer's organization to understand the business context, and that context lives precisely in the minds of the audience in front of you.

The operations director understands the claims review process better than you do, so use the language of that process itself.

The opposite mistake is too much detail. If the name of an open-source library appears on the slides the director is watching, you are probably presenting to yourself.

The most dangerous mistake is on the engineering side: handing the director's slides to the principal engineer and calling it a handover. The person on call at 3 a.m. needs the container diagram, the runbook, and the decisions already made; slides will not help him find which message is stuck.

The final mistake is letting the two versions drift apart, such as telling the director "a few minutes" while the timeout configuration allows up to an hour.

Turn this skill into evidence on your resume

FullStack puts two requirements for an excellent FDE side by side: the technical depth of a senior engineer, and the judgment to work with non-technical people on problems no one has clearly defined. A single line on a resume can hardly show this, but documentation can.

In your portfolio, place the context diagram, container diagram, and trade-off explanation of the same project side by side, each labeled with its target audience. An interviewer can see both requirements at a glance.

When reading job postings, look for items that require you to work with both technical and non-technical stakeholders. They signal that the interview may ask you to explain a system twice. Practice in advance with a system you have built, timed: two minutes for the director, ten minutes for the principal engineer.

On the day you leave the project, the director will remember the decision he made; the first time the queue backs up, the principal engineer will open the documentation you left behind. Every explanation should be written for that moment.
