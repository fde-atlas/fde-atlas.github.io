What to Do in Your First Year as an FDE | Action Plan and Completion Milestones for Months 0–12

Original title: FDE 1年目にやること｜0〜12か月の行動計画と完了の目安

FDE総研 · 2026-11-24 · 9 min read

Summary

This article from FDE总研 lays out a 0–12 month action plan for first-year forward deployed engineers, dividing the year into understanding (0–3 months), implementation (4–6 months), and consolidation (7–12 months), each with a completion criterion. It argues first-year success is measured not by code volume or project count but by picking one business problem and leaving a working system that field staff use weekly. It catalogs three common failure patterns with preventions, adjusts priorities by experience, current role, and English ability, and compares FDEs with SWEs, IT consultants, and PdMs.

Key points

  • First year has three phases: understanding, implementation, consolidation, each with completion criteria.
  • Success means leaving a system the field keeps using, not code volume.
  • Three failure types: building before understanding, excluding the field, person-dependent operations.
  • Priority order for filling weaknesses: implementation, business understanding, English.
  • FDEs sit closer to customers than SWEs and deeper in implementation than consultants.

What Is an FDE's First Year? Key Premises to Understand First

An FDE's first year is the period, as defined by FDE Soken in three stages of understanding, implementation, and establishment, in which one gets accustomed to the customer's site and delivers tangible outputs.

FDE (Forward Deployed Engineer) is a role popularized by Palantir. FDEs work on-site with customers, handling everything from organizing challenges to implementation and establishing operations, either alone or in small teams. Even in Palantir's own official blog introducing a day in the life of an FDE, it is explained that customer dialogue and implementation coexist within the same role. Unlike typical software development, there is no specification document in hand from the start.

What Counts as Results in an FDE's First Year?

The achievement of an FDE's first year is to select one operational challenge of the customer and build it into a working system that people in the field use every week, leaving it in place.

Lines of code or the number of projects are not the primary performance indicators for the first year. The comparison points that VACAN emphasizes, based on FDE Soken interviews and practical experience, are the following three.

  • Can you explain the challenge in the language of the business?
  • Did you build something small, get it moving, and have the customer try it?
  • Is it still being used after deployment?

What characteristics of the role should you know as first-year premises?

Since FDEs are expected to demonstrate technical skill, customer-facing ability, and self-sufficiency simultaneously, identifying your weak areas early is crucial in the first year.

An FDE's work spans a wide range: on-site interviews, connecting and shaping data, implementing screens and analytics, and designing operations. It is rare for anyone to handle all of these at a high level from the start. The first year is realistically a period to deliver results using your strengths while deliberately filling in your weaker areas.

What Should an FDE Do in the First Year, and in What Order? Action Plan for Months 0-3, 4-6, and 7-12

FDE Soken recommends a plan that progresses through the first year in order: understanding in months 0-3, implementation in months 4-6, and establishment in months 7-12.

The table below is a rough action plan organized by FDE Soken and VACAN based on typical field workflows. Because timing varies with the nature of the project and each company's onboarding, it should not be treated as a fixed correct answer.

PeriodPhaseKey ActionsMilestone for Completion
Months 0-3UnderstandGrasping business workflows, locating data, identifying stakeholdersAble to explain the challenge in business terms on a single page
Months 4-6ImplementBuilding a minimal prototype, reviewing it in the field, iterating on improvementsField staff are using the prototype in their actual work
Months 7-12EstablishOrganizing operational rules, designing permissions and monitoring, proposing the next challengeOperations run smoothly even without the person in charge

Months 0-3: What Should You Prioritize Understanding?

In months 0-3, an FDE's top priority is to grasp three things before implementation: business workflows, where the data lives, and who the decision-makers are.

During this period, it is important to resist the urge to write code. Observe the work alongside the people in the field and pick up the implicit rules that live in paper documents and Excel. Based on VACAN's practical experience, code written early tends to be more likely to need rebuilding later as underlying assumptions shift.

Months 4-6: How Do You Deliver a Minimal Output?

In months 4-6, an FDE should not build out every feature, but quickly deliver a minimal output that the field touches daily and gather reactions.

With prototypes, prioritize speed of showing over polish. Customer reactions when they touch it are more accurate information than any specification document. Record feedback, prioritize it, and incorporate only what effectively solves the challenge rather than everything.

Months 7-12: How Do You Connect This to Establishment and Horizontal Expansion?

In months 7-12, an FDE should aim to set up operations that run without them and reach a state where they can propose the next challenge to the customer.

Prepare operational procedure documents, permission design, and contact paths for incidents. Once establishment is confirmed, propose horizontal expansion to similar operations. Only at this point does the role expand from "builder" to "someone who finds and brings in new challenges."

What Failures Commonly Occur in an FDE's First Year? Prevention in a Table

First-year FDE failures fall into three patterns: implementing without sufficient understanding, failing to involve the field, and over-personalization, all of which can be prevented with early action.

These are common failure patterns and preventive measures organized by FDE Soken from field voices. This is a summary of generally common patterns, not a statistical analysis of numbers.

Failure PatternWhen It OccursHow to Prevent It
Implementing without sufficient understandingWriting code first in months 0-3Review the business flow diagram together with the customer before starting
Failing to involve the fieldBuilding alone at your desk in months 4-6Have field staff try the actual system weekly
Over-personalizationOnly you know the operations in months 7-12Decide on procedure documents and a successor before moving on

How Do You Avoid Failure from Over-Focusing on Technology?

FDEs who over-focus on technology tend to put the customer's business challenges on the back burner, so it helps to verbally confirm every week "whose work does this feature make easier, and how?"

When using advanced technology becomes the goal in itself, you end up with systems that go unused in the field. Include whether the technology stays within what the customer can maintain as a criterion for technology selection.

How Do You Avoid Failure from Over-Focusing on Customer Relations?

FDEs who over-focus on customer relations tend to end up merely taking requests, so from the start, build the habit of converting requests into the form of challenges and prioritizing them.

If you keep implementing requests as-is, the specification balloons and the output becomes hard to use. Dig one level deeper into the background of each request and confirm the challenge you truly want to solve.

How Should First-Year Priorities Differ by Years of Experience, Current Role, and English Ability?

FDE Soken believes first-year priorities should be determined by the combination of years of experience, current role, and English ability, and recommends narrowing down the first weakness to fill to just one.

When multiple conditions apply, the basic principle is "fill the most deficient area first." The table below shows general tendencies and will vary with individual differences and company policies.

ConditionFirst-Year PriorityReason
Short practical experienceImplementation fundamentals → customer relationsWithout the ability to build working things, it is hard to earn the field's trust
Extensive practical experienceUnderstanding the customer's business → operational designImplementation skills can be leveraged, but translating them into business language often becomes a challenge
Strong English skills but limited experiencePrioritize implementation fundamentalsEnglish remains a strength, but without deliverables it is hard to be evaluated highly
Weak English skillsBuild a track record on Japanese-language projects → improve English in parallelYou can build a track record while gradually progressing through reading English documentation

If you have limited years of experience, what should you start with?

For aspiring FDEs with limited experience, the realistic approach is to prioritize completing small implementations in the first year, and compensate for customer-facing work by having senior colleagues accompany them.

When conditions conflict, the order of judgment is: implementation skills, business understanding, then English skills. This is because even with strong English, without a working deliverable it is difficult to earn the customer's trust.

How does it change if your current job is SWE, IT consulting, or sales?

If your current job is SWE, customer interaction tends to be the biggest area to strengthen in year one; for IT consulting, it's implementation; for sales, it's technical fundamentals.

SWEs should leverage their design skills while practicing speaking in on-site language. IT consultants should leverage their proposal skills while gaining hands-on implementation experience. Those from sales should leverage their customer understanding and first master the basics of data and APIs.

How does an FDE's first year differ from SWE, IT consulting, or PdM? Who is not suited for it?

The biggest difference in the first year is that an FDE is closer to the customer than an SWE, closer to implementation than an IT consultant, and goes deeper into the field than a PdM.

The FDE Research Institute's comparison axes are three: "distance from the customer," "depth of implementation," and "how results are measured." The table below shows general tendencies by role and varies by company and project.

RoleDistance from customerDepth of implementationHow first-year results are measured
FDEClose (stationed at or accompanying the field)DeepWhether a system that is actually used in the field was left behind
SWEModerate (other roles in between)Very deepQuality and development speed of the features they own
IT consultingCloseShallow (centered on design and proposals)Quality of proposals and consensus building
PdMModerateShallow to moderateProduct metrics and prioritization
SIerClose (based on contracts)Depends on the projectMeeting deadlines and requirements

What kind of person is suited to being an FDE?

People suited to being an FDE are those who are interested in both technology and customers, and who enjoy defining problems themselves from ambiguous situations.

Being able to tolerate business trips and accompanying people to the field, and not minding explaining things in non-engineer language, are also indicators of suitability.

What kind of person is not suited to being an FDE?

People not suited to being an FDE are those who want to focus deeply on technology under clear specifications, or for whom continuous dialogue with customers is a burden.

This is a difference in aptitude, not superiority or inferiority. Those who want to pursue deep specialization may find it easier to achieve results as an SWE. VACAN recommends choosing after understanding both the conditions for suitability and unsuitability.

How are first-year FDE results evaluated?

In the first year, FDEs are often evaluated on three axes—trust from customers, establishment of deliverables, and degree of self-sufficiency—according to the FDE Research Institute's framework, though evaluation criteria differ by company.

Since evaluation methods differ by company, it is recommended to confirm them before joining. Below is a summary of the perspectives that are generally emphasized.

Evaluation axisPoints observedFirst-year benchmark
Trust from customersWhether field staff consult with youDirectly receiving consultations on issues
Establishment of deliverablesWhether they continue to be used after deploymentOperations running primarily by the responsible staff
Degree of self-sufficiencyWhether you found the next issue without instructionProactively proposing the next improvement plan

What behaviors tend to be evaluated highly?

FDEs who are evaluated highly share progress and issues promptly with customers and internally, and when decisions are needed, consult with options and a recommended course of action attached.

The quality of reporting affects trust as much as technical skill. It is important not to hide problems and to share them early.

What perspectives should you use for self-reflection at the end of year one?

At the end of the first year, it is good for an FDE to check each of three points—"explaining issues," "establishing deliverables," and "the next proposal"—and decide the focus areas for the following year.

The weakest of the three becomes the top priority for the next year. For volatile information such as salary and job opening conditions, note the date of confirmation when making judgments.

The FDE Research Institute views the first year not as a period to "do everything perfectly" but as a period to "learn in the field and leave behind deliverables."
