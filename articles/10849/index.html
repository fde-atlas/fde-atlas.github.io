The 12 Core Competencies of an FDE: A Ready-to-Use Development Checklist (Worth Saving)

Original title: FDE的12项核心能力：一份可直接落地的培养清单（建议收藏）

53AI · 2026-08-05 · 14 min read

Summary

This article argues that while FDE job postings grew 42-fold between 2023 and 2025, capable FDEs remain scarce, and proposes a practical 12-competency training checklist. Each competency—covering needs translation, scoping, process mapping, validation, boundaries, specs, knowledge architecture, iteration, role assignment, relationship protection, risk, and replicability—is broken down with definitions, cases, actions, and pass criteria. Cases include Palantir, NBA, AdventHealth, and Ricoh, such as inspection accuracy rising from 67% to 96% after defining acceptance standards. The author contends most AI projects fail on delivery, not technology, and suggests using the list as a weekly team review tool.

Key points

  • FDE roles grew 42x from 2023 to 2025; median senior salary is $485K.
  • 12 competencies each include definition, case, actions, and a pass standard.
  • AdventHealth: AI replaced only insurance cross-checking, boosting efficiency 80%.
  • Defining acceptance standards lifted a QA model's accuracy from 67% to 96%.
  • Deliver replicable templates, not one-off projects—portable within 48 hours.

Full text

“

FDE roles have exploded 42x in three years, but what's truly scarce are people who can take AI delivery to the battlefield

FDE (Forward Deployed Engineer), originating from Palantir, was simultaneously bet on in 2026 by OpenAI ($4 billion to establish DeployCo), Anthropic ($1.5 billion enterprise AI joint venture), and AWS ($1 billion FDE division).

LinkedIn data shows that from 2023 to 2025, FDE roles worldwide surged 42x, with the median annual salary for senior FDEs reaching $485,000. Roles are exploding while talent is extremely scarce.

This article skips the concepts and focuses only on how to develop an FDE who can go to the battlefield.

12 capabilities, each with: definition → case → core actions → verification criteria. Can be used directly as a team development SOP.

TRANSLATE

Definition

Transform the business side's vague expressions into executable technical requirements.

Case

When Palantir served US intelligence agencies early on, clients simply couldn't describe their needs in words—because the needs themselves were highly classified and tacit. After FDEs were deployed on-site, instead of asking "what features do you want," they sat next to analysts and observed how they pieced together data, made judgments, and where they got stuck every day.

An intelligence analyst said "I need better reports." After probing, the FDE found the real pain point wasn't the report format, but that cross-source join queries across three data sources required manually switching between interfaces four times. "Better reports" was translated into "one-click switching for cross-source join queries."

When the AWS FDE team entered the NBA in 2026, the league's initial requirement was "use AI to enhance the fan experience." After two weeks on-site, the FDE translated this into three concrete features: real-time game data visualization push, personalized viewing path recommendations, and automated post-game content generation and distribution.

Core actions

Require all requirements to be output in the following structure; free-form narratives are not accepted:

Business context → Goal → Current process → Gaps and problems → Specific requirements → Technical solution

Probing questions (take and use directly):

When you say "improve efficiency," which team, which process, and which step specifically?

Who does this step now? Who do they hand it off to when done?

If you could only change one thing, which would it be?

Verification criteria

💡 Within 15 minutes, be able to compress a business side's 30-minute description into a one-page executable requirement. If you can't, don't start working.

SCOPE

Carve out the smallest deliverable closed loop from the full business picture.

A manufacturing company wanted "AI to fully empower the production line," and the blueprint involved a dozen-plus directions including quality inspection, production scheduling, energy optimization, and equipment alerts. After the FDE arrived, instead of rolling out everything at once, they spent a week with the line supervisor and found the most painful point was quality inspection: manual visual inspection had a daily miss rate of 12%, and night-shift worker fatigue drove the miss rate up to 23% between 2 and 4 a.m.

Ultimately, phase one only tackled this single point—using visual AI to assist night-shift quality inspection—and within three months the miss rate dropped below 3%. The other eleven directions were scheduled for phases two and three.

Counterexample: A consumer goods company launched 8 AI projects simultaneously, asking each business unit to explore on its own. Six months later, the tech stack changed, 5 projects were cut, and the remaining 3 couldn't be delivered independently due to interdependencies—all failed. Several hundred thousand dollars invested, zero output.

Drill down layer by layer according to the following hierarchy, cutting only one slice at a time:

Business blueprint → Overall SOP → Sub-item SOP → Single scenario → Single key process → Specific feature point

Decision principles:

Phase one: do only one point and do it thoroughly

Don't be greedy; master one scenario before expanding

Selection criteria

Strongest business pain × highest AI feasibility × shortest delivery cycle

No "building an agent whenever you see a feature"

Scenario first, agent second

💡 Be able to answer "why this one and not that one," with quantified reasons (number of people affected, frequency, current time cost).

MAP

Before introducing AI, fully reconstruct the existing business process.

When OpenAI DeployCo partnered with AdventHealth (a healthcare system covering 9 US states and serving millions of patients annually), the first thing the FDE did was not deploy ChatGPT, but spend three weeks walking clinical administrative staff through the entire patient admission review process: who registers, who verifies insurance information, who generates the pre-review report, who sends it to the attending physician, and who receives returned exceptions.

After mapping the original process, they found the real time sink wasn't "generating reports," but the step of cross-system insurance information verification—averaging 22 minutes per patient. In the end, AI replaced only this one node, improving review efficiency by 80%.

AdventHealth's Chief AI Officer Rob Purinton later summarized: "We didn't talk about AI as automation. We talked about giving time back." The premise of that statement is—you first have to know where the time is actually going.

Answer 7 questions for each process node:

Who triggers it?

Who extracts and judges?

Who is the information passed to?

Who generates the result?

Who reviews it?

Who ultimately sends / executes it?

Where do exceptions go back to?

First map the original process completely, then gradually mark which steps can be replaced by AI / automation.

💡 Be able to clearly draw the before-and-after differences on a single sheet of paper, understandable at a glance by non-technical people.

VALIDATE

Prove the solution works at the lowest cost before investing in engineering.

In a training session, one group wanted AI to evaluate "whether a painting is well drawn." I didn't let them build a system directly; instead, I first ran a test: take 10 paintings of varying quality, have the AI give scores and reasons, then have three art teachers blindly evaluate for comparison. Result: the AI's accuracy on the "compositional completeness" dimension was 72%, but only 31% on the "color and emotional expression" dimension—far below the business-usable threshold.

Conclusion: at the current stage, subjective aesthetic evaluation isn't suitable, but objective judgments of the "color-block comparison" type are (similar to urine test strip indicator comparisons).

When AWS FDEs entered Ricoh, the first move wasn't deploying a system, but manually running 20 document processing samples over 5 days, and only starting engineering after proving AI classification accuracy reached 94%. AWS's design principle: each on-site engagement cycle is about 45 days, and minimal validation must be completed in the first 10 days, or development doesn't begin.

Counterexample: A company spent 2 million launching an AI agent customer service system, skipping validation and going straight to engineering. The result: customer service found it hard to use, tech complained about maintenance burden, and in three months it saved only a few data-entry positions—not even recouping a fraction of the investment.

Strictly follow this sequence; skipping steps is not allowed:

Capability validation (manual run-through) → Engineering (stable deployment) → Productization (permissions / billing) → Operational optimization

Standards for the first stage:

Manual copy-paste is allowed

Don't pursue automation; first get the judgment logic working

No UI is allowed

Command line or spreadsheets are both fine

Only validate whether the core judgment logic holds

Don't build peripheral features

Sample size: 10–30 entries is enough

A small sample is enough to expose core problems

💡 Complete the full process from requirements confirmation to minimum validation within one week. If you're still building infrastructure after two weeks, you've gone off track.

BOUNDARY

Clearly define the respective responsibility boundaries between the FDE and the business team.

A retail chain built an "AI Store Manager Operations System," and the FDE team tried to define the replenishment logic and customer acquisition strategy themselves. Result: excellent store managers passively resisted, the knowledge base remained incomplete for a long time, the AI's replenishment and customer acquisition suggestions kept being inaccurate, and the project was shelved for six months, unable to scale. Root cause: the FDE overstepped by making professional judgments on behalf of the business, and the business team lost ownership.

The correct approach comes from Palantir's FDE model: the FDE is embedded on-site to acquire knowledge, but ownership of the knowledge and the right to make judgments always remain with the business side. The FDE's role is "translation" and "engineering," not "replacing decision-making."

AWS's FDE design also makes this explicit: a team of 5–6 people embeds at the customer's company, working closely with the customer's business, engineering, and security teams, with the goal of leaving the customer with a self-sufficient AI team, not making the customer permanently dependent on the FDE.

Role division:

RoleResponsibilities
Business teamDefine evaluation criteria, prepare data, get the minimum scenario working, prove value
FDESystem integration, permissions, deployment, stability, monitoring, productization

Red lines:

The FDE does not make professional judgments on behalf of the business

The business does not make technology choices on behalf of the FDE

Knowledge base content must be provided by the business; the FDE is responsible for structuring it

💡 At project retrospectives, you can clearly state "which decisions were made by the business and which by me," with no gray areas.

SPEC

Before getting started, write down clearly "what counts as done well."

A manufacturing company hired an FDE to do AI quality inspection. The first thing the FDE did on-site was not tune the model, but spend three days with the veteran production line workers defining a complete set of judgment criteria:

Input criteria: photo resolution ≥1080p, shooting distance 30–50cm, lighting ≥300lux

Judgment dimensions: scratches, dents, color difference, burrs

Pass/fail criteria: scratch length

Output template: defect type + location coordinates + severity level + handling recommendation

Exception rules: when judgment is not possible, mark as "pending manual re-inspection"

Next action: defective products automatically enter the rework queue

Before these criteria existed, the model's accuracy was only 67%. After the criteria were clearly defined, the same model's accuracy rose to 96%. The model didn't change; the criteria did.

Counterexample: Gartner data shows that in 2025, the failure rate of enterprise generative AI pilot projects worldwide reached 95%, with "inability to quantify acceptance criteria" among the top three failure reasons.

Every deliverable must include the following 6 criteria; work doesn't start if any one is missing:

Input criteria — what format and quality of data can come in

Judgment dimensions — from which angles to evaluate

Pass/fail criteria — what the thresholds are and who set them

Output template — the fixed format of the deliverable

Exception rules — how to handle edge cases

Next action — who does what after the results come out

💡 Before starting, you can write a complete acceptance criteria document. If you can't write it = you haven't fully understood the requirements; go back and ask again.

ARCHITECT

Design unstructured business knowledge into a routable, callable judgment structure.

A manufacturing company spent 500,000 yuan building an AI knowledge base, with the IT department uploading 15 years of technical documentation. Result: employees complained they "couldn't find what they wanted," the AI Q&A always said "Sorry, I don't know," and after 3 months online, usage was under 5%. The problem wasn't that the AI was bad; it was treating the knowledge base as a "document repository" instead of a "judgment structure."

The correct approach comes from an artwork critique project in training. Instead of dumping all the art textbooks into the model, the FDE designed a three-layer routing structure:

Layer 1: identify the object → which painting the user drew and what learning stage they're at

Layer 2: evaluate performance → composition, color, line, perspective, scored dimension by dimension

Layer 3: provide action → based on weak dimensions, retrieve the corresponding practice methods and improvement suggestions from the knowledge base

The essence of personalized generation is not letting the model improvise freely, but selecting appropriate modules from the knowledge base and combining and adjusting them linguistically according to the user profile.

Palantir's Ontology architecture is essentially this idea: modeling enterprise knowledge as a structured graph of objects, relationships, and attributes, rather than a pile of unstructured documents.

Three-layer model for building a knowledge base:

Identify (what is this) → Evaluate (how is it) → Act (what to do)

Design principles:

Not piling up documents, but designing routing logic

Each knowledge domain can be invoked independently

Personalization = module selection + user profile matching + language adjustment

💡 Given a 100-page business manual, you can break it down into the number of knowledge domains, routing logic, and combination rules. If you can't, your architecture skills aren't sufficient.

ITERATE

Small-batch validation → manual calibration → scaled-up batch processing; don't do it all at once.

A fintech company had 30,000 historical sales conversations and wanted to use AI for customer intent classification. The FDE didn't have the AI process everything at once, but executed the following process:

StepActionTime
1AI rough classification, outputting preliminary labels2 hours
2Extract 300 samples (about 1%)1 hour
33 senior salespeople manually correct the labels2 days
4Form an explainable classification template of 12 intent categories1 day
5AI processes all 30,000 entries according to the template4 hours
6Business side checks new categories and edge cases1 day

The final classification template is one that business people can explain in their own words. If steps 3–4 were skipped and everything was run at full volume directly, the model would output a "seemingly sophisticated but incomprehensible" clustering structure that the business side could neither use nor maintain.

Among Baidu Intelligent Cloud's top 10 enterprise AI agent cases of 2025, ARCFOX used the "Famou" agent for wind resistance testing, also first calibrating model parameters with a small sample, then gradually scaling up to full vehicle model coverage, ultimately compressing a traditionally 10-hour wind resistance test down to minutes.

Process:

AI rough classification → manual sample calibration → form explainable template → AI full-volume execution → business side reviews edge cases

Iron rule: the results must be explainable by business people. The model outputting a "seemingly sophisticated but incomprehensible" structure = invalid delivery.

💡 Give the deliverable to the business side; they can restate the logic in their own words. If they can't restate it, send it back for rework.

ROLES

Clarify which steps should be executed by humans, AI, or automated programs.

When a legal tech company launched its AI contract review system, it handed the entire pipeline of "clause extraction → risk assessment → revision suggestions → final draft confirmation" to AI end-to-end. The result: the step where AI extracted clauses and automatically compared them against a case library performed well (this is the pattern matching AI excels at), but the "risk level assessment" step frequently went wrong (because it requires professional legal judgment), and the "final draft confirmation" step absolutely could not be left unattended (because it involves legal liability).

The corrected division of labor:

StepExecutorRationale
Clause extractionAIPattern recognition, high accuracy
Case comparisonAutomated programClear rules, no judgment needed
Risk level assessmentAI initial assessment + junior lawyer reviewAI suggests, humans decide
Final draft confirmationSenior lawyerLegal liability, cannot be delegated

Another common mistake: calling "data automatically synced from CRM to ERP" "AI empowerment." That's not AI—that's an automation script. An FDE must be able to tell the difference.

What each executor is suited for:

执行主体 适合的事
AIRecognition, judgment, classification, generation, making suggestions
Automated programsCollection, transfer, computation, status updates, system writes
HumansSetting standards, review, complex exceptions, emotional value, ultimate responsibility

💡 Given a complete business scenario, you should be able to annotate the executor at every node, with a reason for each annotation.

RELATIONSHIP

Identify which steps cannot be automated, and protect valuable human touchpoints.

An online education company used AI to fully take over "after-class student Q&A": student asks a question → AI replies automatically → practice questions pushed automatically. Efficiency did improve, but three months later the student renewal rate dropped 18%. The post-mortem found that students messaging their class advisor was itself a core touchpoint for the advisor to understand students' status and build trust.

After full automation, the class advisor no longer knew what students were studying, where they were stuck, or how they felt, so renewal conversations completely lost their personal relevance.

The corrected approach: AI handles "knowledge point explanations" (low-value transfer), while the class advisor retains "learning progress follow-up and encouragement" (valuable human contact). The renewal rate recovered.

The AdventHealth case also reflects this principle: AI took over administrative work such as "insurance information verification" and "pre-authorization report generation," but doctor-patient communication was fully preserved. AI "gives time back" to doctors, letting them spend more time on things that genuinely require humans.

When redesigning a process, ask about every node slated for automation:

Does this step carry relationship-building between business staff and users?

After automation, can business staff still understand user status?

Is this step "low-value transfer" or "valuable contact"?

Principle: automation eliminates low-value transfer, not valuable human contact.

💡 Your solution should explicitly identify at least one step that is "deliberately not automated," with a business rationale.

RISK

Design safety policies and fallback mechanisms for scenarios where AI reaches users.

An e-commerce platform launched an AI customer service agent using a "blacklist mode" (most questions answered automatically, only sensitive words blocked). In the first week, the AI replied to a complaining user with inappropriate content; screenshots spread on social media, and the topic hit 10 million views within 24 hours, damaging the brand's reputation. After an emergency switch to "whitelist mode," only the 200 questions confirmed safe in the FAQ could be answered directly, with everything else routed to humans. Although response speed dropped 30%, no further incidents occurred.

In OpenAI's deployment at AdventHealth, all medical scenarios used whitelist mode: every pre-authorization report generated by AI had to be confirmed by clinical staff before entering a patient's record. The cost of errors was too high to allow "most automated, few blocked."

Selection logic:

Scenario characteristicsChoiceRationale
Medical diagnosis, legal opinions, financial adviceWhitelistExtremely high error cost, human confirmation required
E-commerce FAQ, logistics tracking, calendar remindersBlacklistLow error cost, prioritize labor savings
Educational tutoring, customer complaintsGradual (whitelist first, then blacklist)Accumulate safe samples first, then expand gradually

Every delivered solution must include:

Risk rating (high / medium / low)

Fallback strategy (who handles errors, how fast the response)

Blast radius control (upper limit of impact scope)

Rollback plan (how quickly you can switch back to humans)

💡 A solution without a risk rating and fallback strategy is not allowed to go live.

REPLICATE

What you deliver is not a project, but a replicable template.

The reason Palantir's FDE model could support its stock price rising from $6 to over $200 is not that every project was custom-built from scratch, but that the custom solutions FDEs built on the front lines were abstracted into platform capabilities that flowed back into the product. The data modeling methods and industry process solutions accumulated with Customer A could be reused for Customer B. The hidden value of an FDE is not "finishing a project," but "making the next project faster."

After OpenAI DeployCo acquired Tomoro and its 150 deployment experts, the first thing it did was not have them continue doing projects, but abstract the delivery methodology accumulated from serving clients like Mattel, Red Bull, and Virgin Atlantic into a standardized deployment framework.

Take the painting critique and nutrition critique projects from the training: the input formats were completely different (images vs. text), but the business SOPs were similar (recognition → evaluation → suggestions). The FDE first got painting critique working end-to-end; after confirming the "evaluation → knowledge retrieval → generation → review" pipeline, they swapped in the knowledge base and input parsing modules and got nutrition critique running within two days.

AWS FDE's 45-day on-site design also reflects this: the goal of each engagement is not "delivering a system," but enabling the client team to independently develop and maintain AI applications—this itself is an expression of replicability.

Steps:

First get one category running end-to-end and confirm the complete pipeline

Abstract a category-agnostic general framework

Swap in the knowledge base and model to extend to other categories

Delivery documentation must include a "reuse guide."

💡 If you swap in a different person or a different scenario, can your solution be up and running within 48 hours? If not = the abstraction layer isn't sufficient.

THE END

An FDE is not "someone who can code + can travel."

Palantir spent fifteen years validating this model. In May 2026, OpenAI and Anthropic announced the creation of FDE departments on the same day; in June, AWS followed suit with a $1 billion investment. Three companies simultaneously confirmed the same thing with real money:

The last mile of AI adoption is not a model problem, it's a delivery problem.

An MIT study analyzed 300 enterprise AI deployment cases and concluded that 95% of AI projects fail to produce a quantifiable impact on corporate finances. Within that 95%, the vast majority are not cases of inadequate technology, but of missing one or more of the 12 capabilities above.

Take this checklist and hold it up against your team. Whatever's missing, fill it in.

Worth saving. Building an FDE doesn't happen overnight, but these 12 points can serve as a weekly review checklist.
