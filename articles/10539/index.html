The most expensive job in the AI era, FDE, can actually be done by one person using WorkBuddy

Original title: AI时代最贵的岗位FDE，其实一个人用WorkBuddy就能干

腾讯云 · 2026-07-22 · 7 min read

Summary

This article argues through a case study that an FDE (Forward Deployed Engineer) in China can work solo without backing from a large software company. Wang Guanghui, a Tencent Cloud architect with 10 years of experience, used WorkBuddy to win 18+ enterprise clients in three months across education, manufacturing, and park management. His AI-driven workflow turns meeting recordings into deliverable proposals in 15-20 minutes instead of 3-4 hours, and generates role-specific training manuals in 15 minutes with reusable templates. He follows three disciplines: assigning concrete tasks, confirming outlines first, and enforcing strict safety and delivery rules. The article concludes that when delivery costs drop enough to run in parallel, one person's capacity can match a former small team's.

Key points

  • FDE roles surged 42x on LinkedIn (2023-2025); Indeed postings up 729%, salaries from $170k.
  • Debate in China: whether FDEs require well-funded teams stationed at clients.
  • WorkBuddy compresses proposal delivery from 3-4 hours to 15-20 minutes.
  • Training manuals became template-based and reusable across job roles.
  • Wang also uses Cursor and Claude Code, but delegates project management to WorkBuddy.

The most expensive job in the global AI world right now is called FDE — Forward Deployed Engineer.

You may never have heard of the term, but Silicon Valley has gone crazy for it. LinkedIn data shows that from 2023 to 2025, the number of FDE positions grew 42-fold; on Indeed, job postings for such roles surged 729% over the past year, with salaries generally starting at $170,000. OpenAI set up a dedicated deployment company, Salesforce promised to hire 1,000 in one go, and Anthropic teamed up with Goldman Sachs and Blackstone — what the big companies are competing for isn't people who can train models, but people who can embed AI into customers' real businesses and make it actually run.

Simply put, an FDE is the kind of engineer sent to the customer's site: running research in the morning, observing workflows on-site in the afternoon, and coming back to write code at night — selling AI products while teaching that AI to land in the customer's messy business.

The buzz quickly crossed the Pacific. But domestic discussions immediately split into two camps: one side stares at job postings with million-yuan salaries and calls it the hottest job in the AI world; the other sneers that FDE doesn't take root in China — customers won't pay for the exploration process, companies can't afford long-term on-site teams, and an industry with "weak vendor status" can't retain such versatile talent.

The debate actually hinges on the same assumption: an FDE must be supported by a high-gross-margin software giant, which dispatches an entire team to stay on-site at the customer for months, then slowly recoups the cost through the platform.

But what if that assumption itself doesn't hold? What if, in China, an FDE doesn't need a big company's backing and one person can do the job?

We recently met just such a person. His name is Wang Guanghui, a member of the Tencent Cloud Architect Technical Alliance, an architect with 10 years of technical experience who has now fully transitioned to AI enterprise-level delivery. He has no big-company team and no budget for months-long on-site stays; relying on himself alone, in three months he developed more than 18 enterprise clients across education, manufacturing, industrial parks, and several other industries. He calls himself the "Xinya AI Super Individual" and runs a blog of the same name.

And what supports him in single-handedly handling this entire FDE workload is WorkBuddy. In his words, WorkBuddy is "not a tool, but a collaborator." Out of curiosity, we chatted with him about how these three months actually went.

The hard part of the FDE job is that it's not one skill — it's a string of skills.

You have to be able to visit clients, understand their vaguely expressed needs, propose solutions on the spot, deliver training, and track the progress of a dozen-plus projects. Silicon Valley spreads these tasks across an entire team; Wang Guanghui has to shoulder them all alone.

"In the morning it helps me sort out client priorities, in the afternoon it accompanies me while I polish deliverables, and at night it takes over content generation that requires deep focus. I meet several groups of clients a day, and every group is waiting for your proposal. The moment you leave, a WeChat message comes chasing — 'Wang, when will the proposal be ready?'"

What truly wears down a solo FDE is never technical difficulty — it's the volume, fragmentation, and urgency.

Before using WorkBuddy, Wang Guanghui's day went like this: after meetings, first hand-writing meeting minutes to clarify requirements, half an hour; building a PPT from scratch and adjusting formatting, an hour or two; then writing a Word technical proposal, an hour; finally manually sending via WeCom and uploading files to the server. The delivery chain for a single proposal took a minimum of three to four hours.

"You clearly know how to do it — it's just too slow, so slow that you don't dare promise the client on the spot. That passive feeling of being the vendor — everyone knows it."

This is exactly the "can't afford it" that the doomsayers in that two-camp debate talk about — one person's capacity can't sustain the delivery intensity of FDE. Unless something can compress those three or four hours into a non-issue.

This is the first system Wang Guanghui built on WorkBuddy, which he named "AI Architect."

"What this thing does is turn the recording or transcript of a client meeting directly into a professional proposal ready to deliver."

Before: hand-written minutes + building a PPT from scratch + writing a Word proposal + manual distribution, 3–4 hours.

Now: one sentence after the meeting — "I talked with client XX for an hour today; help me organize the requirements."

Then WorkBuddy automatically did all of this:

  • Pulled the meeting transcript and distilled it into a structured requirements document;
  • Matched the most suitable one from 23 preloaded industry templates;
  • Generated a complete PPT and Word technical proposal based on the template;
  • Supported four distribution methods — local export, direct WeCom send, public web link, and Tencent Docs — depending on which the client prefers.

Throughout the whole process, he only needed to confirm twice in the middle. From the end of the meeting to the proposal being sent out: 15 to 20 minutes.

That's roughly a 10x efficiency boost. But Wang Guanghui says two other things are worth even more than the 10x.

"First, quality is stable. My proposal quality used to fluctuate with my energy — the one written in the morning when I was fresh looked great, the one in the afternoon when I was tired was mediocre; now it's standardized template output, and every client gets the same standard. Second, my mindset changed — before, after a meeting I'd feel exhausted thinking about having to go back and write the proposal; now I just sip coffee and wait for the finished product."

And precisely because of this, he now dares to say to a client's face: "I'll send it to the group in half an hour." Response speed itself has become a plus when closing deals.

This is exactly the ability Silicon Valley FDEs value most — turning a prototype-level one-off demo into a production-grade system that can be delivered reliably. The only difference is that Silicon Valley does it with a team, while Wang Guanghui does it with one sentence.

An FDE doesn't just deliver proposals — they also have to teach the client's people to actually use it. A big chunk of Wang Guanghui's work is enterprise training — providing AI skill upgrades for employees in different roles at client companies, with manuals to go along with it.

This work used to be even more grueling than writing proposals.

Before: an hour researching role pain points; two or three hours writing scripts and finding cases scenario by scenario; then formatting, adjusting tables, and converting to HTML for easy sharing. The whole set took 4–5 hours, and there was almost no reuse across roles — finish the admin role and switch to finance, and you'd start all over again.

Now: one sentence — "Generate an AI skills manual for the admin role, in table format, dark-themed HTML."

WorkBuddy fills in the content in batches following the structure of "role pain points → AI scenario breakdown → prompt templates → hands-on examples → quantified results," and directly generates dark-themed HTML. 15 minutes.

"What really made me feel it was different is that once this structure works, switching roles is just switching inputs. After finishing the admin role, the finance and sales roles can be produced with minor tweaks. Before, one role manual was half a day's work; now one sentence produces a draft, and ten minutes to finalize it."

And precisely because the cost dropped to a level where things can run in parallel, he now dares to take on batch requests for several roles at once.

Silicon Valley pays FDEs a $200,000 salary, and one hidden threshold is: this person must know when to use which tool and how to prevent AI from causing trouble inside an enterprise. This isn't mysticism — it's discipline. Wang Guanghui shoulders the FDE workload alone by following three simple rules that he uses every day.

Rule one: don't ask WorkBuddy "what can you do" — directly tell WorkBuddy what you want.

"The more specific you are, the stronger it gets. Assign it tasks like a colleague, don't question it like a search engine."

The time that impressed him most was when he casually asked it a question about a proposal, originally just wanting a rough direction — but it considered several extreme cases for him and directly gave handling methods. "That was the first time I felt it was more thorough than my own thinking."

Rule two: confirm the outline first, then let WorkBuddy expand on it.

Before generating any formal content, have it produce an outline first, and only proceed with writing once you've confirmed the content meets expectations.

Rule three: Set hard rules for WorkBuddy, especially around safety and delivery order.

"Run a security scan before installing any Skill, and stop immediately if there's any risk; deliverables must strictly follow the order of images, format conversion, and copywriting—no skipping steps."

He admits WorkBuddy isn't omnipotent either—it occasionally stumbles when making PPTs. "That's why I value these boundaries even more than features. The stronger AI gets, the more humans need to hold the line."

These three rules say the same thing as that line in Silicon Valley FDE job descriptions—"you need both technical depth and the ability to set aside the engineer's dominance of the conversation in front of clients"—namely, let AI charge ahead, and let humans guard the judgment.

When asked "besides WorkBuddy, which AI tools do you use heavily," Wang Guanghui's answer was blunt.

His answer was Cursor and Claude Code. But he drew the division of labor clearly:

"Cursor is an extra pair of quick hands when you write code—it's the co-pilot; WorkBuddy is an extra colleague who can work independently when you manage projects. You use Cursor to figure out how to implement a feature, and WorkBuddy to figure out who should actually do the work. One manages the hands, the other manages the brain."

This sentence happens to nail the answer to the debate over "whether one person can be an FDE."

Writing code is only a small part of an FDE's work. The bigger part is managing clients, producing proposals, delivering training, and tracking progress—things that require continuous context and personalized understanding, which "code-writing hands" like Cursor simply can't handle. WorkBuddy, however, remembers every client's follow-up status, has memorized every training script, and even knows better than Wang Guanghui himself which project is stuck at which step.

In other words, FDEs used to require the backing of big-company teams because that pile of "brain work" couldn't be handed to machines—it could only be done by stacking people. Once WorkBuddy takes over that part, one person's capacity gets stretched to the scale of what used to be a small team.

When asked "what's your biggest takeaway from using WorkBuddy," his answer left a deep impression on us:

"It's not that it made me faster—it made me dare to take on work I didn't dare to before. When delivery costs drop low enough to run in parallel, the bottleneck shifts from 'can't finish it all' to 'do you dare to ask for it.'"

That's worth pondering. In the past, there was work he wasn't incapable of doing—he just calculated the time cost, found it not worthwhile, and didn't dare take it. A person only has so many hours a day; take on too much and you can't deliver, and it's your own reputation that gets wrecked. But when the delivery cost of a proposal dropped from three or four hours to twenty minutes, the work he dared to take on visibly multiplied.

So back to the debate at the beginning: can the FDE model actually work in China?

At least the sample of Wang Guanghui gives this answer—yes, and it doesn't necessarily require a big company's backing. His path to turning one person into an FDE can actually be condensed into three steps:

  • Feed in your business background and standard templates (spend time once, save countless repetitions);
  • Solidify repeatedly used workflows into your own Skills (summon them with a single sentence next time);
  • Strictly enforce the rules on safety and delivery (the stronger AI gets, the more humans need to hold the bottom line).

Silicon Valley uses million-dollar salaries and entire teams to fill the chasm of AI implementation. Wang Guanghui has proven another possibility: one person, with one capable collaborator, can also stand in front of that chasm.

Three months, 18 enterprise clients. As for whether to take a step further, whether to take on that harder job—that's still his call to make. It's just that now, he dares.
