Why Do AI Projects Need FDEs? Solving the Core Problem Behind the 70% Failure Rate in Deployment

Summary

Drawing on Gartner, McKinsey, Deloitte and other data, the article argues that roughly 70% of enterprise AI projects stall at PoC stage, and that failures stem mostly from organizational, data and integration issues rather than model capability. It identifies six root causes of failed AI deployments and shows how the FDE model—full-stack delivery, agile iteration, knowledge transfer—addresses each. It also compares costs, outlines which projects genuinely need an FDE, and offers a maturity self-assessment for enterprises.

Key points

  • 72% of AI projects fail or underdeliver; only 11% of AI agent pilots reach production.
  • 84% of failures are attributed to leadership and organizational issues, not technology.
  • Six failure causes: data gaps, business disconnect, talent shortage, scope drift, integration, maintenance.
  • FDE delivery reportedly lifts PoC-to-production conversion to ~85% and cuts timelines 60-70%.
  • Simple standardized scenarios suit SaaS; complex decision and workflow AI needs FDEs.

Companies spend millions on AI, only for the models to end up gathering dust on servers.

This is not an exaggeration—it is the current state of the industry. 70% of enterprise AI projects remain stuck at the PoC (proof of concept) stage, never making it into production, let alone delivering real business value.

Why does this happen? Are the models not good enough? No—today's large models are already powerful enough, and in many scenarios they even outperform humans. Do companies not want to do it? No—almost every company is talking about AI and wants to do AI.

The problem lies in the last mile: between "the model can do it" and "the business can use it," there is a huge gap.

The FDE (Forward Deployed Engineer) was born to bridge this gap. This article breaks down the six core reasons AI projects fail to land, and how the FDE model solves each one.

Six reasons AI projects fail to land

Reason one: Data disconnect—the model was trained on "clean data," but the business runs on "dirty data"

The AI model performs brilliantly in the lab, but falls apart the moment it hits a real business environment. Why? Because training data and real data are simply not the same thing.

Typical scenarios:

  • Lab data is cleaned, structured, and accurately labeled
  • Real business data is incomplete, messy, and inconsistently formatted
  • Historical data contains lots of manual entry errors, duplicates, and outdated information
  • Data is scattered across multiple systems—CRM, ERP, Excel, WeChat—each with its own format

The result: the model hits 95% accuracy on the test set, but drops to 60% after launch—worse than human judgment.

The dilemma of traditional approaches:

  • Data scientists only know how to tune models, not do data governance
  • IT departments only know how to manage systems, not understand business data logic
  • Business departments only know how to use data, not clean it
  • Three departments each handle their own piece, with no one bridging the gaps

How the FDE breaks through: An FDE is "full-stack"—responsible end to end, from data ingestion, cleaning, and governance to model integration and performance tuning. They go deep into the business frontline, understand where the data comes from and where it goes, know which data is trustworthy and which is garbage, then build a data pipeline that turns dirty data into clean data the model can use.

Data problems are not a "precondition" of AI projects but "part of" the FDE's work. The first thing a good FDE does on arrival is a data quality diagnosis—if the data isn't good, fix the data first rather than forcing the model through.

Reason two: Business disconnect—the tech team doesn't understand the business, the business team doesn't understand the technology

The most common conversation in AI projects:

Business: "I want an intelligent customer service bot that can automatically answer customer questions." Tech: "Sure, we'll build one with a large language model." Three months later... Tech: "Done. Look, it answers questions." Business: "None of these answers are right—that's not what customers are asking." Tech: "That's just how LLMs are; give me more data and I'll tune it." Business: "..."

Where's the problem? The tech team doesn't understand the business scenario, and the business team doesn't understand the technical boundaries. The two sides seem to speak the same language, but they're not on the same channel at all.

The dilemma of traditional approaches:

  • Product managers act as translators, but AI product managers are already scarce, and many don't understand LLMs either
  • Requirements documents run dozens of pages, but AI project requirements simply can't be fully written down upfront
  • Waterfall development—by the time it's built you discover it's wrong, and there's no time to fix it

How the FDE breaks through: The FDE embeds directly into the business team and works alongside business staff. They don't "take requirements remotely and develop with their head down"—they "learn, build, and adjust on-site."

  • Within the first week, they map out the business processes and know where the pain points are
  • Whatever they build is immediately tried by business staff, and problems are fixed on the spot
  • They understand both technical boundaries (what can and can't be done) and business language (explaining things in terms business people understand)
  • They act as the "translator" and "connector" between technology and business
One of the FDE's core values is eliminating the communication loss between technology and business. They are neither purely technical nor purely business—they are the "bridge" standing in between.

Reason three: Talent gap—AI talent is expensive, scarce, and hard to retain

To do an AI project, you first need AI talent. But the reality is:

  • Top AI talent is all at big tech companies, with starting salaries of a million a year—small and medium businesses simply can't compete
  • Even if you hire someone, it takes 3 months to get up to speed and half a year before they can work independently
  • One project requires several people—algorithm engineers, data engineers, frontend engineers, backend engineers
  • What happens when the project is done? You can't just lay people off, and keeping them costs too much

Many companies' dilemma: they want to do AI but can't afford an AI team; outsourcing offers no guarantee of results.

The dilemma of traditional approaches:

  • Hiring in-house: high cost, long cycle, high risk
  • Outsourcing: poor quality, difficult communication, no guarantee of results
  • Consulting: plans without implementation—beautiful slides but useless

How the FDE breaks through: The FDE offers a "third way"—using the capabilities of external senior experts to solve the problem of insufficient internal talent.

  • Fast start: on-site within 1-2 weeks, no months spent recruiting
  • Controllable cost: pay per project or per person-month; it stops when the project ends, with no sunk cost
  • Top-tier capability: FDEs are senior engineers with rich experience—they've hit more pitfalls than you've seen projects
  • Knowledge transfer: at the end of the project, capabilities are transferred to the internal team, avoiding dependency
It's like "renting" an elite AI squad for 6-8 weeks: solve the problem, leave the capability behind, then leave. Far cheaper than building a team in-house, and far more effective than outsourcing.

Reason four: Requirements drift—the requirements of an AI project are always changing

Traditional software projects can lock down scope with a "requirements document," but AI projects can't.

Why? Because AI projects are highly exploratory:

  • No one knows "how good is good enough"—you only find out after building it
  • Business scenarios change constantly—after launch, you discover many edge cases weren't considered
  • Model performance is unpredictable—the same model can perform much worse on a different batch of data
  • User expectations keep shifting—at first, "being able to answer questions" is enough, but after using it, they want "automatic ordering"

The traditional "acceptance against requirements documents" model simply doesn't work for AI projects. The more detailed the requirements document, the higher the cost of rework when you later discover it's wrong.

The dilemma of traditional outsourcing:

  • Requirements are locked in; changes mean more money and more time
  • The outsourcing company delivers per the contract, regardless of whether your business changes
  • Change processes are complex; by the time approval comes through, the business environment has changed again
  • It ends up as "what was built isn't what I wanted, but that's what the contract says"

How FDE breaks through: The FDE model is inherently designed for "uncertainty"—agile iteration, small fast steps, with the ability to adjust direction every two weeks.

  • It doesn't aim to "get it right the first time"; it aims for "rapid validation, continuous optimization"
  • Demo results every two weeks, so the business team can adjust priorities at any time
  • Wrong direction? Pivot immediately, instead of going further and further down the wrong path
  • The goal is "final business impact," not "delivery against a requirements checklist"
The essence of AI projects is exploration. Using deterministic methods (waterfall, fixed requirements) to tackle uncertain problems (AI implementation) is likely to fail. The advantage of the FDE model is that it embraces uncertainty and converges on the right answer through iteration.

Reason five: the integration dilemma—AI is not an isolated system; it must connect with existing systems

The model is built and performs well, but it just can't be put to use. Why? Because it's just an isolated demo that hasn't been integrated into business processes.

For example, suppose you build an AI lead scoring model:

  • The model runs on a separate server
  • Sales has to manually check scores in another system
  • After checking, they have to go back to the CRM to take action
  • One extra step, and sales finds it too cumbersome to use
  • If it's not used, the data doesn't update, and model performance keeps degrading
  • A vicious cycle

AI is not a standalone product; it is a "capability enhancement" embedded into existing business systems. If it can't connect with existing systems like CRM, ERP, and customer service systems, AI is just a castle in the air.

The dilemma of traditional approaches:

  • The AI team only knows how to build models, not do system integration
  • The IT department knows the existing systems well, but doesn't understand AI
  • The two sides collaborate with high communication costs and unclear boundaries of responsibility
  • It ends up as "the model is the model, the system is the system"—two disconnected pieces

How FDE breaks through: FDEs are full-stack engineers—they understand both AI and system integration.

  • They can not only train/invoke models, but also handle API integration, data pipelines, and frontend integration
  • They deeply understand the existing system architecture and find the optimal integration approach
  • They embed AI capabilities "seamlessly" into existing workflows without adding burden to users
  • From day one, they think about "how to get users to actually use it," not "how to build the model"
Many AI projects fail not because the model is bad, but because integration wasn't done well. A good FDE won't focus only on model performance; from day one, they'll think: how does this AI capability fit into business processes? How will users use it? Is it convenient to use?

Reason six: the maintenance challenge—AI systems aren't "done once launched"; they require continuous operation

After traditional software launches, it's basically stable as long as there are no bugs. But AI systems are different—they are "alive" and need continuous feeding and optimization.

After an AI system goes live, it still faces these problems:

  • Data drift: user behavior changes, data distributions shift, and model performance declines
  • New scenarios emerge: after launch, many previously unconsidered scenarios appear
  • Performance decay: the model becomes less accurate over time and needs periodic retraining
  • Monitoring and alerting: how do you detect problems in an AI system? Rely on user complaints?
  • Continuous iteration: as the business grows, AI capabilities must keep up

Many companies think an AI project is a "one-time deal"—spend money to build a system, launch it, and you're done. In reality, launching an AI system is just the beginning; the real cost lies in subsequent operation and iteration.

The dilemma of traditional outsourcing:

  • The outsourcer leaves once delivery is done—who handles ongoing maintenance?
  • Hire outsourcing again? Slow response, and they're unfamiliar with the previous implementation
  • Maintain it yourself? No one on the internal team understands AI
  • The result: the system is gradually neglected, performance keeps declining, and it's eventually abandoned

How FDE breaks through: FDEs don't "deliver and leave"; they "help you onto the horse and ride along with you for a while."

  • Knowledge transfer: fully hand over maintenance methods, monitoring systems, and iteration processes to the client team
  • Establish operations standards: how to monitor, how to alert, how to troubleshoot
  • Leave behind reusable tools and templates: future new scenarios can follow the same approach
  • Optional ongoing support: after the project ends, ongoing FDE services can be provided as needed
Give a man a fish and you feed him for a day; teach a man to fish and you feed him for a lifetime. An FDE not only gives you the "fish" (a launched AI system), but also teaches you to "fish" (how to maintain, iterate, and build new scenarios). That way, even after the FDE leaves, the system keeps running—and may even get better with use.

The right way for enterprises to adopt FDE

If you decide to use the FDE model for your AI project, the following suggestions can help you maximize value:

1. Start small with a single-scenario pilot

Don't jump straight into "full AI transformation." First pick a scenario with the clearest pain point, the best data foundation, and the easiest path to results, and run a pilot over 4-6 weeks.

  • If it works well: you gain confidence and a case study, then expand the scope
  • If it doesn't work well: cut losses in time—the loss is small, and you've gained experience

2. Always have internal staff engaged throughout

FDE is not "outsource it and forget about it." There must be a business owner and a technical counterpart fully involved throughout—not to "supervise," but to ensure the direction is right and knowledge gets transferred.

How much effort? About 0.5 FTE. This investment is worth it.

3. Focus on outcomes, not features

When agreeing on success criteria with the FDE, talk about "business outcomes," not "feature lists."

❌ Bad agreement: "Build an AI lead scoring feature with manual scoring, batch export..." ✅ Good agreement: "Within 1 month of launch, the sales team's lead conversion rate increases by 15%, and the sales satisfaction survey scores 4 or above"

4. Prioritize knowledge transfer and avoid dependency

The goal of the project is not "have the FDE finish the project," but "have the internal team learn to do AI."

  • Require the FDE to hold regular technical sharing sessions and training
  • Arrange for internal engineers to develop and review code together with the FDE
  • At project end, have a clear knowledge transfer checklist and confirm each item

5. Build a long-term partnership and keep iterating

AI is not a one-and-done effort. The business evolves, data changes, and models need continuous optimization.

You can establish a long-term partnership with the FDE—for example, a fixed number of FDE days per month for performance optimization, exploring new scenarios, and technical guidance. This maintains flexibility while providing ongoing professional support.

Final Thoughts

In the AI era, a company's core competitiveness is shifting from "whether you have AI technology" to "whether you can deploy AI into your business."

Technology itself is no longer the bottleneck—anyone can call large model APIs, anyone can download open-source models. The real bottleneck is: who can combine these technologies with their own business to actually create value.

The rise of the FDE model is precisely a reflection of this trend. Companies no longer need to "buy AI technology"—technology is everywhere. What companies need is "someone who can help me put AI to use"—someone who understands technology, understands the business, can deliver, and is accountable for results.

That is the value of the FDE.

Not because FDEs are some mystery, but because traditional models can't solve AI deployment—this gap must be filled by a new role.

❓ Frequently Asked Questions FAQ

Q1: Our company is small with a limited budget—can we still use an FDE for AI? Yes, but you need to choose the right entry point. Don't start with "company-wide AI transformation." Instead, pick a minimal scenario for a pilot—such as "AI auto-reply to common customer questions" or "AI-assisted sales follow-up email generation." These scenarios can show results in 2-4 weeks with modest investment. Once the value is validated, expand gradually. One advantage of the FDE model is flexibility—you can be billed by the day or by scenario, without committing to a months-long large project.

Q2: What's the difference between an AI project FDE and a traditional software project FDE? The core competency requirements differ. FDEs for traditional software projects focus on system architecture, business processes, and integration development; FDEs for AI projects also need AI/ML engineering capabilities—data pipelines, model integration, evals systems, prompt engineering, and performance tuning. Simply put: AI FDE = traditional FDE + AI engineering capabilities. Precisely because the requirements are higher, AI FDEs typically cost 20-50% more than traditional FDEs.

Q3: What if the AI an FDE builds doesn't perform well? That's a great question, and it's exactly what the FDE model is designed to address. First, a good FDE will assess feasibility during the first week's diagnosis phase—if the data is too poor or the scenario isn't suitable, they'll tell you directly "this project won't work" rather than pushing ahead anyway. Second, the FDE model is iterative—you see results every two weeks, and if the direction is wrong, you can adjust in time. Finally, if the final results truly fall short of expectations, both parties can negotiate next steps—continue optimizing, change direction, or terminate the project. The key is shared risk and timely course correction, not the traditional outsourcing approach of "money spent is money gone."

Q4: After an AI project goes live, will model performance degrade? How do we handle it? Yes, it will—that's normal, and it's called "data drift" or "model decay." User behavior changes, market conditions change, and model performance naturally declines over time. There are several ways to address it: first, build a monitoring system that tracks model performance metrics in real time and triggers alerts when they drop below a threshold; second, establish a data feedback loop to continuously collect new data and periodically retrain the model; third, set up an iteration plan—evaluate performance quarterly and keep optimizing. An FDE will help you build this entire system to ensure the system runs stably over the long term after launch.

Q5: Our company already has a technical team—do we still need an FDE? It depends on your AI capability. If you have a complete AI team (algorithms + data + engineering), you may not need an FDE and can do it yourselves. But if you only have traditional Java/frontend developers and lack AI experts, an FDE is very valuable—it's like "renting" an AI expert for a period of time to help you get the project off the ground while also bringing your internal team up to speed. Many companies do exactly this: use an FDE for the first AI project while developing the internal team, and once the team has grown, they can handle subsequent projects on their own.
