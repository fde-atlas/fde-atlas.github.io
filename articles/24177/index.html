Why Do FDE Implementations Fail? Causes from Three Perspectives and PoC Withdrawal Criteria

Original title: FDE導入の失敗はなぜ起きる？3視点の原因とPoC撤退基準

FDE総研 · 2026-11-05 · 10 min read

Summary

This FDE総研 article explains why forward deployed engineer (FDE) engagements fail, attributing failures to misaligned assumptions across three perspectives: the client (missing problem owner, slow data access), the FDE/vendor side (person-dependent work, pushing the vendor's own platform), and the business model (one-off custom builds that don't scale or transfer). It proposes diagnosing failures along four axes—problem, data, decision-maker, contract—and recommends agreeing on PoC exit criteria (business, data, and decision-making indicators plus deadlines) before starting, with a five-step process for exiting gracefully. It also includes tables for judging which problems suit FDEs, comparing FDEs with SIer, IT consulting, PdM, and in-house SWE roles, and discusses Japanese contract types (jun-iin vs. ukeoi) and disguised subcontracting risks.

Key points

  • Failures stem from misalignment across client, vendor, and business model perspectives.
  • Four-axis diagnosis: problem, data, decision-maker, contract; two missing axes raise risk.
  • PoC exit criteria should be set numerically with deadlines before work begins.
  • FDEs suit uncertain, field-adjacent problems; fixed-spec work fits SIer better.
  • Japanese contracts: jun-iin for exploratory PoCs, ukeoi for fixed-spec builds.

A summary by FDE Soken. The higher the layer where a failure occurs, the harder it is to recover at lower layers.

  • Before starting: Agree in writing on business KPIs, exit conditions, decision dates, and decision-makers
  • Mid-term review: Check data acquisition, on-site usage, and KPI changes, then narrow down the issues
  • Production transition decision: If there is no outlook for operations staff, budget, or security review, make it an exit candidate
  • Handover/exit: Create a transfer plan to the in-house IT department, or end while documenting the lessons learned

The exit line should be documented by both the client and the FDE side before starting. Timeframes are editorial guidelines from FDE Soken.

  • Can access business data from day one
  • On-site decision-makers are involved weekly
  • Requirements only solidify through on-site validation
  • Results can be reused by other departments or customers
  • Data provision takes months of internal approval
  • The requesting department and the using department are different
  • Specifications are fixed and can be ordered as a fixed-scope contract
  • Ends as a one-off custom development

FDE Soken's assessment criteria. If two or more items on the "unsuitable" side apply, consider SIs, IT consultancies, or in-house development first.

In FDE implementations, failures tend to occur not from the technology itself but from misaligned assumptions across three perspectives: the client side, the FDE/vendor side, and the business model. FDE Soken breaks down the causes of failure along four axes: "problem × data × decision-maker × contract." On that basis, we recommend setting PoC exit criteria in terms of numbers and deadlines before work begins.

What are the main causes of FDE implementation failure? What do the three perspectives—client, FDE/vendor, and business model—reveal?

FDE implementation failures tend to occur when two or more of FDE Soken's four axes (problem, data, decision-maker, contract) are missing. We organize this across the three perspectives of client, FDE/vendor, and business model. The job description of Palantir, the origin of the FDE model, can be found on the official Palantir blog.

An FDE (Forward Deployed Engineer) is a role that works embedded close to full-time at the customer's site, handling everything end-to-end from problem definition through implementation to operational adoption. This way of working was popularized by Palantir, and hiring and adoption are spreading in Japan as well. On the other hand, if expectations alone run ahead—believing that "bringing in an FDE will transform the workplace"—projects tend to stall at the PoC (proof of concept) stage. The basics of the role are summarized in What is an FDE.

What causes are common on the client side (implementing company)?

Client-side failures tend to concentrate on two points: the absence of a problem owner and delays in data provision, wasting the FDE's capacity.

CauseTypical symptomsActions to take first
No problem owner is assignedStarts with "Can we do something with AI?" and requirements change every weekDesignate one manager-level person who owns the business KPI
Data access approval is slowThe first few weeks are spent entirely on account applicationsProvide viewing permissions and sample data before the kickoff
Divide between operations and IT departmentsStalls at connecting to the production environmentInclude the IT department in regular meetings from the PoC stage
Vague definition of successEnds with "it became more convenient" and no budget followsComplete ROI and KPI design before starting

From the perspective of on-site practitioners, the first thing an FDE gets stuck on is not a technical issue but a state of not knowing "who to ask for a decision." We believe the designation of a problem owner should be treated with the same weight as signing the contract.

What causes are common on the FDE/vendor side and in the business model?

Failures on the FDE/vendor side and the business model side boil down to two points: over-reliance on individuals and "one-off development that cannot be scaled horizontally."

  • Over-reliance on individuals: A single FDE handles requirements, implementation, and operations all alone, and everything stops when they leave. The countermeasure is to start designing system handover from the first month.
  • Steering toward in-house products: An FDE belonging to a vendor prioritizes deploying their own platform even when it does not fit the problem.
  • Business model mismatch: Even if one-off developments accumulate, nothing feeds back into the vendor's products, and the client gains no in-house development capability either.

For vendors, the FDE has the character of an investment to make customers adopt their products. Clients need to understand this structure and confirm the ownership of deliverables and the scope of reuse.

What are the criteria for deciding whether to continue or exit a PoC?

The principle for continuing or exiting a PoC is to agree on FDE Soken's recommended "3 indicators × deadline" criteria before starting and decide mechanically at the review meeting in the second or third month. The METI "DX Report" (published September 7, 2018) also cites the repetition of PoCs with vague objectives as a problem.

Which indicators should determine the exit criteria?

Exit criteria are set by three indicators—business, data, and decision-making—and if even one is unmet, the default is to exit or redesign.

IndicatorGuideline for continuing (example)Signs to exit or redesign
Business indicatorThe agreed business KPI is trending toward improvementImprovement cannot be measured, or the KPI itself is undefined
Data indicatorReproducible with production-equivalent dataOnly works with sample data
Decision-making indicatorBudget and responsible department for production are visibleDecision-makers do not attend the review meeting

The "guidelines" in the table are examples of how to think for use in this article, not industry-standard numerical benchmarks. Set specific thresholds according to your company's own KPIs. How to structure a PoC is covered in PoC design.

What is the procedure when deciding to exit?

Exiting is not a failure. Treat it as a process for preserving lessons as assets, and proceed through the following five steps.

  • Compare the criteria agreed at the start with the actual results
  • Identify which of the four axes caused the unmet indicators
  • Assess whether the code, data pipelines, and documentation can be reused
  • Document the conditions for a retry (data preparation, staffing, etc.)
  • Report both the "reason for exit" and the "conditions for a retry" to management as a set

Regarding generative AI PoCs, MIT's research group NANDA reported in its 2025 report that most corporate generative AI pilots have failed to produce measurable effects on profit and loss. However, it has been pointed out that the survey's scope and methodology have limitations. Rather than applying it directly to Japanese companies, it is reasonable to read it as grounds for preparing exit criteria based on the assumption that "PoCs typically do not reach production."

Which problems suit FDE and which do not? How to tell them apart with an assessment table?

Whether a challenge is suited to FDE can be judged using the FDE Soken's assessment table, based on two factors: "requirements uncertainty" and "proximity to the field." Challenges where both are low fall within the scope of traditional IT investment as organized in the IPA "DX White Paper 2023".

What are the characteristics of challenges suited to FDE?

FDE is suited to challenges where requirements are determined while iterating and where field-level judgment and data are closely intertwined; a typical example is support for business decision-making.

Assessment itemSuited to FDENot suited to FDE
Requirements uncertaintyHigh (decided while iterating)Low (a specification can be written)
Proximity to the fieldChanges field-level judgment on a daily basisDoes not affect field operations
State of the dataScattered but accessibleNot accessible at all
How results are measuredMeasurable via business KPIsMeasurable only by the presence of deliverables

For details on how to select challenges, see Use Case Selection.

What are the characteristics of challenges not suited to FDE?

FDE is not suited to core system renewals with fixed specifications or projects that are purely maintenance and operations; for these, SIers or in-house SWEs offer better cost-effectiveness.

If you assign an FDE to a project that simply requires building to specification, highly paid talent ends up spending their time just confirming requirements. From the perspective of frontline practitioners, I find that making "Can a specification be written?" the first question makes it easy to quickly judge suitability.

How does the risk of failure change with company size, in-house IT department, English proficiency, and years of FDE experience?

Failure risk varies according to the FDE Soken's matrix of prerequisites (four conditions: company size, IT department, English proficiency, and years of experience), and it rises especially when "no in-house IT department" and "less than one year of FDE experience" overlap. Hiring considerations are summarized in How to Hire FDEs.

How does it change with company size and the presence of an IT department?

Rather than employee headcount, whether the in-house IT department can take over the FDE's results has a strong impact on failure risk.

PrerequisitesLikely failuresRecommended countermeasures
Large company with IT departmentStalls due to approval processes and security reviewsInvolve the information systems department from the PoC stage onward
Mid-sized company with a small IT departmentNo one to hand over to, leading to person-dependent operationsPair it with a plan for in-house DX development
Small company with no IT departmentOperations stallUse an external contract that includes operations

How does it change with English proficiency and years of FDE experience?

English proficiency matters when using overseas-made platforms, and years of FDE experience matter for the ability to define challenges. When experience is limited, compensating with a review structure is a realistic approach.

  • English proficiency: With overseas platforms such as Palantir products, primary documentation and support tend to be English-centric, making research time-consuming. For details, see English Proficiency Required for FDEs.
  • Less than one year of FDE experience: Even if implementation is possible, there is a tendency to hesitate in defining challenges with customers and negotiating priorities. A pairing structure with an experienced person is effective.
  • From SWE or IT consulting backgrounds: Those from SWE backgrounds are said to struggle with client interactions, while those from IT consulting backgrounds struggle with the volume of implementation. Required skills are organized in FDE Skills.

How should FDEs be used alongside SIers, IT consultants, PdMs, and in-house SWEs? Which companies are not suited to FDEs?

The five roles—FDE, SIer, IT consultant, PdM, and in-house SWE—are distinguished using the FDE Soken's comparison axes: "Who defines the challenge, who implements it, and who operates it." The FDE's distinguishing feature is taking on all three alone (for the archetypes of these roles, see the Palantir official blog).

The differences lie in the scope of responsibility: FDEs cover everything from defining challenges to operational adoption, SIers focus on building to specification, and IT consultants center on strategy and recommendations.

What characteristics do companies and people not suited to FDE have?

FDE is not suited to companies where decision-makers are not involved in the PoC, and to engineers who prefer development strictly to specification. In both cases, the gap with expectations becomes large.

  • Unsuited companies: Assume "delegating everything" to external talent and do not place a challenge owner in-house
  • Unsuited companies: Cannot permit taking data outside the company or viewing it during the PoC period
  • Unsuited people: Feel strong stress from client interactions and shifting requirements
  • Unsuited people: Prefer a specialist career deeply developing a single technology

How can failure be prevented with Japanese-specific contracts such as quasi-mandate (jun'inin) and contract for work (ukeoi)?

The choice between quasi-mandate and contract for work is determined by the FDE Soken's "degree of certainty of results" axis. FDE-type work with uncertain requirements is fundamentally a quasi-mandate, and understanding the difference between Articles 632 (contract for work) and 656 (quasi-mandate) of the Civil Code is a prerequisite.

How should quasi-mandate and contract for work be used differently?

PoCs where requirements are decided while iterating should use a quasi-mandate, and production builds with fixed specifications should use a contract for work; separating contracts by phase makes it easier to prevent failure.

Contract typeLegal basisObject of responsibilityCompatibility with FDE work
Contract for work (ukeoi)Article 632 of the Civil CodeCompletion of the work○ if it is a production build with fixed specifications
Quasi-mandate (performance-ratio type)Article 656 of the Civil CodeProcessing of affairs (duty of due care)◎ for PoC and exploration phases
Quasi-mandate (results-completion type)Article 648-2 of the Civil Code (revised Civil Code effective April 1, 2020)Payment of compensation for results○ if the definition of results is clear

If FDE work is contracted as a contract for work, requirement changes tend to become "specification changes" requiring negotiations over additional costs, undermining the exploratory strength of FDE. For cost considerations, see FDE Costs.

How can the risks of disguised contracting and full-time on-site placement be avoided?

With an on-site (resident) FDE arrangement, if the client's employees give direct work instructions to the FDE, it risks being deemed disguised contracting, so the point of contact for instructions is specified in the contract.

The Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare publishes the criteria for distinguishing worker dispatch from contracting (Public Notice No. 37 of 1986 from the Ministry of Labour) on its Worker Dispatch Business page. Even when accepting an FDE under a quasi-mandate (jun-i-nin) contract, operations must be set up so that work instructions go through the vendor's supervisor. To be safe, record who decides daily work priorities in both the contract and the minutes of regular meetings. The differences from SES are summarized in Differences between FDE and SES. For the final judgment on the contract type, please consult your legal department or an attorney according to your specific circumstances.
