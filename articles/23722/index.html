Where FDEs Work — The Last Mile

Original title: FDE 在哪里工作 — 最后一公里

Agentic AI Wiki · 2026-10-01 · 18 min read

Summary

This chapter argues that employer type matters more than the FDE title: two offers with the same title can be entirely different jobs. The author sorts employers by what they buy with an engineer's time—labs buy production usage and model-failure signal, application startups buy deals and a working deployment, Palantir-style platforms buy customer expansion, and deployment companies sell hours outright. A Plank census (September 2026) counted 1,333 open FDE postings across 565 companies, about two-thirds in the US, with frontier labs and Palantir at only 9%; Bloomberry found 58% of roles at companies of 11–200 people. Base salaries overlap widely (Plank median $193K), so the real differences lie in structure: wide bands mean your level is set in the offer, OTE means shared commercial metrics, and equity is a bet on the field-to-product loop. The chapter includes a pre-signing question checklist and a brief China market survey, concluding that you should classify the employer's lean before comparing pay, then negotiate level.

Key points

  • Employer type—not the title—determines the work, pay structure, and exit paths.
  • Plank counted 1,333 open FDE postings at 565 companies; labs plus Palantir are only 9%.
  • Four employer types buy production signal, deals, expansion, or hours respectively.
  • Median base pay is $193K; level, OTE, and equity structure matter more than the band.
  • A pre-signing checklist tests whether the job feeds a product or sells capacity.

Where do FDEs work

Two offers with the same FDE title can be two different jobs: one turns your frontline work into product, the other sells your hours by the hour. The type of employer matters more for this job than the title, so first classify the employer: it determines what the job is for, how compensation is structured, and where you can go next. Then compare salaries.

Where the roles are

First, correct an impression. The FDE hiring market is mainly not at the few AI labs and Palantir. Most of it is at companies you have never heard of, selling a product to enterprises that cannot set it up on their own.

The best census of job postings comes from Plank: on September 12, 2026, it counted 1,333 open FDE postings at 565 companies, with links for each. About two-thirds are in the US, and the frontier labs plus Palantir together account for 9% (Plank, FDE hiring market, September 2026; T2, a hands-on count published by a company that sells FDE services). In an analysis published ten months earlier, based on roughly 1,000 postings, Bloomberry found that 58% of FDE roles were at companies with 11 to 200 employees (Bloomberry, November 2025, updated January 2026; T2). The two samples cover different time windows and use different criteria, so do not merge them. But they paint a consistent picture: the typical FDE role is at a growth-stage company, not at a lab.

Counting by job title distorts this picture in two directions at once. A count done for this book illustrates the point.

Roles with FDE in the title, read from each employer's own recruiting data source, October 17, 2026 (as a count of "what has been posted" this is T1; it says nothing about how many people were hired). Roles whose title contains "forward deployed" as a share of all open roles: Palantir 77 of 310; OpenAI 26 of 814; Mistral 15 of 217 (16 earlier the same day; these numbers change by the hour); Scale AI 14 of 182; Cohere 11 of 118; Anthropic 6 of 655; ElevenLabs 4 of 156; Harvey 3 of 345; Glean 3 of 134; Sierra 0 of 186; Decagon 0 of 143. On OpenAI's deployment company's own careers page, 6 of 19 roles carry the FDE title.

Undercounting. Anthropic has 50 roles with "Applied AI" in the title, versus only 6 titled FDE. Sierra writes the job as "Strategist, Agent Development," and Decagon as "Agent Deployment Engineer."

Overcounting. Of Palantir's 77, 32 are internships, new-grad roles, or the "Year at Palantir" program, and 28 were created more than two years before the count date. The oldest is a Forward Deployed Software Engineer role in London, created in December 2009. Plank's own notes say the same: one posting may represent several seats, or a permanently open talent pipeline.

An echo number. In September 2026, Chuanguan News's summary of an industry report by Xiaguang Consulting gave "565 companies" hiring FDEs and a US median base salary of $193,000 (Chuanguan News, September 2026; T3). These are exactly Plank's census figures, relayed without attribution, and mislabeled as a US median, whereas Plank's $193K comes from all postings that disclosed salary, not only US ones. Count it once.

Growth numbers are deliberately left out. They measure different time windows, and several are echoes of the same Indeed data series; Appendix D lists them separately without merging them, and the main echo numbers appear in "How to Use This Book". Besides, they do not help with choosing an employer: the market can double, and the job you land can still be the wrong one.

Four employer types, classified by what they buy with your time

"Origins and Economics" sorts employers into three economic models. This chapter asks what each one does to your week, your pay, and your next job. This chapter also adds a fourth type that the economics chapter does not cover: application startups, because Section 15.1 shows that most of the roles are there.

The test: when an employer pays for an hour of your time, what is it buying?

  • AI labs (OpenAI, Anthropic, Mistral, Cohere) buy two things: production usage by the few customers large enough to matter, and evidence of where their own models fail. Customers are already paying for the models. Your hours are an investment in usage and signal.
  • Application startups (Sierra, Decagon, Harvey, Glean, and hundreds of smaller companies) buy closed deals and one usable deployment of their product. Your hours are a cost of sales today; if the product absorbs what you build, they are a product investment tomorrow.
  • Palantir-style platforms buy customer expansion. Pilots are loss-making by design, and your hours pay off when a customer grows into a platform license that no longer needs you.
  • Deployment companies or FDE consultancies (companies spun out of the various labs in 2026, and consultancies that adopted the title) sell your hours. The delivered result is the product, and utilization is the cost line.

Real companies mix these. Classify an employer by which one it leans toward, then check that lean with the question list in Section 15.8. These types predict three things a title cannot: who sets your priorities, what gets you a raise, and what you will be good at when you leave.

The postings say what the job is

Job postings are T4 evidence of how the work is done, and first-hand evidence of what employers ask for. Read postings from the four types side by side, and the differences show up in how each posting defines success.

Labs measure production usage and signal. OpenAI's San Francisco FDE posting says: "You will be measured by adoption in production, measurable workflow impact, and eval-driven feedback that changes the product and model roadmap" (OpenAI, FDE - SF, September 2026). Anthropic's Staff FDE posting says: "Customers bring you in to lead, not just implement: you decide what should be built, then make sure it gets built" (Anthropic, Staff FDE, October 2026). Lab work is closest to this book's definition: few customers, deep ownership, and an obligation to bring the learnings back. Its risk is the one "Origins and Economics" flags for labs: serving one big customer so well that nothing comes back to the product.

Applied startups measure closed deals. Decagon's Engineering Lead, Strategic Deployments will "own the technical build for 1–3 strategic deals end to end, operating like a founding CTO for each deal," identify technical risks "and defuse them before they threaten the deal," and turn "custom deal requirements into lasting improvements to Decagon's core product" (Decagon, July 2026). Sierra's flagship Deployment Strategist is asked to "bring revenue upside to Sierra and internally drive progressively lower cost-to-serve and higher margins" (Sierra, September 2026). Glean's founding FDE posting says the product promise outright: "The best thing you build will ship to every Glean customer in the world" (Glean, first posted January 2026). Startup FDEs are closer to revenue than lab FDEs, and closer to the product: there is only one product, and whatever your customers lack becomes its roadmap. The risk is that in every fight with the roadmap, the deal wins.

Platforms measure scale, and hire young. In this sample, only Palantir's careers page has a full talent pipeline: internships, new-grad roles, a year-long program, and specialized forward deployed tracks in infrastructure, reliability, security, and customer enablement, alongside the Deployment Strategist role, the non-coding counterpart. Its FDSE posting still describes the role as "CTO-like responsibilities at a startup" (Palantir, FDSE - US Government). Platform work teaches the most and specializes earliest. Its risk: if the platform never absorbs the frontline work, the FDE is just a consultant with equity.

Deployment companies measure delivery, and say what they're worried about. OpenAI's DeployCo calls itself "OpenAI's deployment arm," operating "at the intersection of frontier AI, enterprise transformation, and scaled service delivery." Its founding FDE will "own technical and business value outcomes end to end" and "move quickly toward management responsibilities." The candidate it wants "can make disciplined decisions between standardization and customization, rather than defaulting to custom work" (OpenAI DeployCo, FDE - US, June 2026). That is the most candid line in the whole sample: a company that makes money on engineering capacity knows customization is its natural drift, and hires people specifically to resist it. Another posting invites "an existing team of two to five people" to apply together, with the caveat that "people who apply together may be assigned to different deployment projects" (DeployCo, Apply with Your Team). That's how a services company configures headcount: it hires capacity, then allocates it.

Where you sit during the week also varies by type, and the evidence here is contradictory.

Time on site. OpenAI tells new FDEs to expect "roughly 50% of your time on site"; some people in the UAE and Japan are on site "about 80% of the time," while those in Europe or the US "may spend 2-3 weeks per month with customers" (Gregor Ojstersek, August 2026; T3, citing Colin Jarvis). Pragmatic Engineer reports that Palantir "expects FDEs to spend roughly 25% of their time at customer sites," while Commure is "up to 50%" (Pragmatic Engineer, August 2025; T3). Nabeel Qureshi, who joined Palantir as an FDE in 2015 and left in 2023, says FDEs "typically need to 'go forward,' working out of the customer's office 3-4 days a week" (Qureshi, October 2024; T4). These Palantir figures contradict one another and may describe different periods.

What the postings say. OpenAI FDE: travel "up to 50%." Anthropic FDE: 25–50%. Palantir new-grad FDSE: 25–50%, "preferred but not consistently required." Glean: hybrid, "3-4 days" a week in the office, plus 25–50% travel. Decagon: "we are an in-office company." DeployCo FDE: three days a week in the office, travel up to 50%. Harvey: "2–3+ times per month... typically... 1–3 days each."

The usable rule (this is an inference from a handful of postings and one paraphrased interview) is: labs and deployment companies send you to the customer; startups keep you near the product team and send you out in bursts. If you can't spend half your time traveling, you'll be excluded from more lab and deployment company roles than startup roles. Ask about actual travel last quarter, not the ceiling in the posting.

Where the money comes from: look at composition, not the midpoint of the range

Base salaries across the four types overlap heavily. The differences that actually matter are in how the whole compensation package is composed, and in what the width of the salary bands conceals.

Salary ranges listed in postings are US base salary unless noted, read on October 17, 2026. Labs: OpenAI FDE (including healthcare, legal, and financial services tracks) $185K–$300K plus equity; OpenAI FDSE, New York and Seattle, $153K–$325K; Anthropic Staff FDE $280K–$320K; Anthropic FDE London £225K–£255K; Scale AI's three-tier ladder $180K–$225K, $216K–$270K, and $252K–$315K; Cohere FDE CA$175K–CA$385K. Applied startups: Decagon Agent Deployment Engineer $175K–$230K (London £110K–£150K); Decagon Engineering Lead, Strategic Deployments $200K–$400K; Sierra Strategist, Agent Development $200K–$290K, with the posting not specifying whether that is base or target total compensation; Glean founding FDE $160K–$270K, noting that "some roles may be eligible for variable compensation and equity"; Harvey Forward Deployed Finance Engineer "OTE $254,600 - $350,000 USD (75/25 split)," without specifying how the variable portion is paid. Platforms: Palantir FDSE and Forward Deployed AI Engineer, estimated $135K–$200K, "may also include restricted stock units, sign-on bonuses"; new-grad FDSE $135K–$145K. Deployment companies: DeployCo FDE $170K–$400K plus equity; London £90K–£150K; Paris €115K–€200K.

The aggregate numbers. The median of the midpoints of base salary ranges in the postings Plank tracks is $193K, from 388 of 1,333 postings (29%) that disclosed salary. By funding stage, it ranges from about $150K at seed to about $243K at frontier labs (16 postings). Plank's body text says Series B is $235K, but its own chart gives a Series B median of $185K, based on only five postings; the gradient between stages above seed should be treated as unknown. Plank emphasizes these are base salary only, and that equity makes up the bulk of lab compensation packages; that proportion is Plank's estimate, not a figure stated in the postings (T2). Bloomberry's median, based on an earlier sample, is $173,816 (T2). Different samples and time windows: do not average the two. Bloomberry also reports that 8% of postings mention OTE and that "exactly 0%" are quota-carrying, yet calls its "Sales Engineer+" category, at 30%, "quota-carrying." Its own numbers contradict each other; treat the share of variable compensation as unknown.

There are four rules that turn a salary range like this into something you can use.

A wide range means level gets set in the offer. A rule of thumb: when the top of the range is more than about 1.5 times the bottom, the employer is using one posting to hire several levels. Glean's posting says it outright: "This range covers multiple levels for this role; your level will be determined during the interview process." Your number depends on where they place you. Ask which level the offer is set at, what the next level requires, and who decides. The rubric in "The Skills Map" gives you evidence for arguing a level, not a number.

Variable compensation tells you whose metrics you share. On-target earnings (OTE) means part of your pay depends on a business outcome. For a domain-expert FDE who takes customers from pilot to daily use, this can be fine. First ask whether the posted range is base salary or OTE; some postings don't say, and Anthropic's postings carry a standard note: "For sales roles, the range given is the role's On Target Earnings," without saying whether FDE roles count as sales roles. Then ask what the variable portion pays on: deals signed, renewals, usage, or delivery. If it pays on deals signed, the job is pre-sales, and the checklist in Chapter 1 will mostly reach the same conclusion.

Equity is a bet on the product loop. Startup stock is only worth something once the product scales beyond custom work, and the field-to-product loop exists precisely to make that happen. Before you value it, ask them to name one feature that came out of the field. If no one can, the stock is a bet that the purpose of your job will fail.

A lower base salary can be deliberate platform design. Qureshi's account of Palantir paying below market and making it up in stock is covered in "Origins and the Economics" (T4, one former employee's account, and the stock's later rise is a confounder). The platform model asks you to wait while customers and company mature together; whether that's a good deal depends on which company.

Upside and exits

First, the gap. No one has tracked a cohort of FDEs to see where they went: no attrition data, no tenure data, and no study of destinations by employer type. What follows is anecdote, employer claims, and this book's inference, each labeled as such.

What the record shows. At the platform company that invented the role, the best-documented exit is into product. Pragmatic Engineer reports that "until roughly 2016, Palantir had more FDEs than 'regular' software engineers," and that after Foundry launched, "more FDEs moved back to being software engineers working on Foundry, bringing their field experience with them" (T3). Qureshi believes "former FDEs tend to be great founders," adding that "in every YC batch, founders from Palantir usually outnumber those from Google" (T4; he gives no numbers, and a former employee is not a neutral witness). Employers now recruit from this exit in turn: Anthropic's and Glean's postings welcome applications from former founders, Decagon's deployment lead posting lists "former technical founders" among the profiles it wants, and DeployCo invites entire teams. Anthropic's Staff FDE posting describes a senior individual-contributor path leading "deliveries done with partners" (all T4).

Inference. Your exit depends on what you owned, and employer type determines what you can own. At a lab, you own production systems for a few demanding customers and the evidence of having changed a product or model. That leads to product and research-adjacent roles, or to founding a company in a vertical you now know from the inside. At a startup, you own deal-making and product gaps in one market, which leads to early-employee and founder roles in that market, or to running deployment at the next startup. At a platform company, you see the widest variety of customers earliest, and the exits are into the platform's product teams or founding. At a deployment company, you own delivery across many customers, which leads to delivery program management, delivery leadership, and the consulting-partner track. Moving from there into product is harder, unless your field work actually fed a product.

On this last point the skeptics are right: the disillusioned joiners in "What an FDE Is and Isn't" encountered "typical IT services mindset" (Pragmatic Engineer, March 2026; T4), work that had lost the stages that made it FDE work. The career cost is concrete: two years of services work under an FDE title makes you better suited to more services work. That can be a good career, but it is not the one most applicants think they are choosing. "Levels, Burnout, and What Comes Next" takes up the level ladder and exits further.

China: the same title, very different jobs

The Chinese market makes this chapter's argument more starkly than the American one. "What an FDE Is and Isn't" shows the same title being filed under product, R&D, and sales support. "Origins and the Economics" shows a market dominated by in-house teams at large platforms.

The job titles themselves sort employers by type. Tencent hires Forward Deployed Engineers; ByteDance hires "AI FDE Engineers" under its R&D track, one posting titled "Doubao AI Large Model FDE," tied to its own model. These are the in-house platform and lab end of the market. At the other end, an industrial software company in Tangshan hires an "AI Delivery Engineer (FDE)": an AI delivery engineer, with the title itself flagging it as the services end.

Monthly salaries listed in postings, first half of 2026. Jiemian News (June 2026, republished by 36Kr) read postings on Liepin and BOSS Zhipin: ByteDance's Doubao AI Large Model FDE pays ¥35,000–70,000 per month over 15 months (up to ¥1.05M a year); Ant Digital Technologies' B2B FDE pays ¥40,000–60,000 per month over 15 months; Zhipu's FDE lead pays ¥60,000–80,000 per month (Jiemian News, republished by 36Kr, June 2026; T3, a reporter reading the postings). In the same article, an industry practitioner under a pseudonym at a Shanghai AI company said junior FDEs earn ¥20,000–30,000 per month, while senior FDEs mostly receive packages above ¥400K a year; the interviewee said million-yuan salaries are concentrated among top talent and are "not the industry average" (T4).

Single source. xdash's open-source book supplements Tencent Cloud's ¥35,000–65,000 (number of months' salary not stated), Alibaba Cloud's ¥20,000–50,000 (16 months), and the Tangshan listing's monthly salary of ¥8,000–16,000, which requires a master's degree and on-site work at manufacturing enterprises (xdash, Chapter 1, August 2026; T3). Its ByteDance, Ant, and Zhipu figures are identical to those from Jiemian News, so they are echo numbers, not corroboration. Its source appendix has no links, so the Tencent Cloud, Alibaba Cloud, and Tangshan figures cannot be verified.

Growth. Sichuan Guancha News, citing Maimai data, reports that FDE-related job postings grew 21x year-over-year in the first half of 2026 (T3, low credibility). Its platform, market, and time window differ from all the US numbers. Do not compare them side by side.

There are two lessons to take away. First, convert to annual salary: domestic offers quote monthly salary and number of months, with large companies paying 15 or 16 months, so the offer with the lower monthly salary may actually be the bigger one. Second, for gaps within the same title, be clear about what they reflect. The verifiable numbers mainly reflect seniority: from ¥20,000 monthly for a junior FDE to ¥80,000 for an FDE lead at a model company, while the ranges in several large employers' listings overlap substantially (ByteDance ¥35,000–70,000, Ant ¥40,000–60,000, Zhipu lead ¥60,000–80,000; all monthly, Zhipu's number of months not stated). Only one number points to a gap by employer type: the Tangshan listing on the services end, at ¥8,000–16,000 monthly, roughly one-tenth of a model company lead's pay by xdash's calculation, and this listing cannot be verified. So "the pay gap reflects employer type" is plausible but not proven. Either way, the question worth asking holds up. As xdash puts it, ask a company whether it "gets paid for outcomes or for headcount." That is this chapter's question, condensed into one sentence.

Comparison table

Each row describes a tendency, not a verdict.

Work: Production environments for a handful of large clients, plus eval-backed evidence that can change the product or model. Heavy travel.

Compensation: High base salary, with equity the largest share by Plank's estimate; variable pay is rarely stated, so ask.

Upside: The hardest problems and the most visible signals.

Exits: Product, research-adjacent roles, or founding a startup in a vertical.

Traps: Serving one client so well that nothing comes back, and code obsoleted by the next model release.

Work: Winning and landing deals for one product; whatever clients are missing becomes the roadmap. Close to the product team.

Compensation: Base salary range overlaps the labs at the top; equity depends on whether the product scales, sometimes with a target total revenue.

Upside: The most direct path from frontline work to product.

Exits: Early employee or founder in the same market; head of deployment elsewhere.

Traps: Every fight with the roadmap is a deal won, and you become a Sales Engineer.

Work: Scaling clients until they run on the platform; early specialization; paired with a strategist who doesn't write code. Recruiting from universities.

Compensation: Lower base salary, stock, and a long wait for both to mature.

Upside: The most comprehensive training, and the earliest exposure to the widest variety of clients.

Exits: Product teams on the platform, or founding a startup.

Traps: The platform never absorbs the frontline work, and you become a consultant with stock.

Work: Delivering results across many clients; hired as capacity and then assigned; a fast path to managing people.

Compensation: The widest range, set by level; in some markets the floor is lower than the labs'.

Upside: Volume. Many deliveries early, and managing people early.

Exits: Delivery leadership, delivery project management, partner track.

Traps: Defaulting to custom work, being measured on utilization, and a title that can no longer distinguish you from IT services.

Read this table alongside your skills self-assessment. If your weakest area is product, a deployment company will keep it weak unless its frontline work actually feeds into a product. If your weakest is clients, a startup will force it up fastest. Pick the type whose traps you are least likely to fall into and whose exits are the ones you want.

Before signing: a question checklist

Take these questions to your prospective lead, at the latest after receiving an offer. Accept only answers that name a specific client, person, or date. The five questions in Chapter 2 read the employer's economics; this checklist reads the specific job. In the trap check in Part 4 of the checklist, count an answer as pointing toward services if any of the following occur: thresholds are set by someone else, no change from frontline to product can be named, the team is measured on utilization or bookings, variable pay depends on bookings, or the only path upward is management.

Most answers are ASK. For a company only a few months old, that is normal and not a reason to walk away, but get the answers from your future manager before signing: from the posting alone, you cannot tell whether a deployment company is feeding back into the product or selling capacity. And the one line that was fully answered-the salary band-is precisely the least informative.

The insurer's FDE, about eighteen months after the pilot ended. The FDE from Part II finished the next delivery at the vendor, interviewed elsewhere for senior roles, and now has two senior-level offers: a founding role at a deployment company, and a deployment role at an app startup doing insurance claims. Based on the posted base salaries, the two differ by only a few tens of thousands. The FDE filled in two checklists. The deployment company's trap check came out at three out of five, and it could not name a single change that flowed from the field to the product. The startup scored one, because its manager could name that claims-intake feature, which began as a customer workaround. The month-nine self-assessment showed product as the weakest area, and the shape check concluded "services"; the one filled in after the next delivery still had product as the weakest area, improved by at most one level. The FDE accepted the startup's offer with the lower base salary, valuing that it would force the field upward, and asked them to write down the criteria for the next level. The pattern behind this passage is the shape check in "The Skills Map", along with the deployment company's own warning in its posting against "defaulting to custom work."

For readers who want to manage people early, choosing the other offer is entirely defensible. This checklist does not make the choice for you; it makes the choice about the job, not the title. If you are still in the interview process, "Getting the Offer" covers what interviews test for.

Before comparing salaries, write down what the employer is buying with your hours. Production usage and signals, a closed deal, an expanding customer, or your capacity: each leads to a different week, a different compensation structure, and a different next job. Pick a type whose traps you can resist and whose exit is the one you want, then negotiate level, because within a wide band, level is compensation.
