An FDE (Forward Deployed Engineer) is someone who goes into the customer's field to define problems, designs systems including AI, and takes responsibility all the way through to production deployment and results. As attention has grown, schools and training programs calling themselves "FDE" have also increased. However, even with the same "FDE" label, the course content varies considerably.

In this article, we at BizCodeX-FDE (operated by Lime Design Lab, Inc.) have summarized how to choose an FDE school into 7 checkpoints. In the second half, we also cover how to spot courses whose appearance doesn't match their substance. Finally, we introduce the contents of our own school, sticking strictly to the facts.

Choose an FDE school based on "who it's for," "how far you can learn," and "how completion is judged"

When you're unsure how to choose an FDE school, the criteria can be organized into the following 7 points.

Among these, the biggest differences tend to appear in ① and ⑤. If the admission requirements don't match you, even great content won't fit your learning, and if the completion criteria are vague, there's no way to verify whether you've truly acquired the skills. Both are easy to check on official websites or at information sessions, so if there's a school you're interested in, we recommend starting with these two points.

What is an FDE (Forward Deployed Engineer)? A quick recap

The FDE is a role that originated at Palantir in the United States. It involves immersing yourself in the customer's field to understand their operations, defining problems, designing and developing systems including AI, and taking responsibility through to production use and measurable results.

The difference from traditional SIers, IT consultants, and product managers (PdMs) is that FDEs don't stop at "proposals" or "deliverables"—they take responsibility for the customer's results. Rather than building exactly what's requested, they redefine the problem, build small to verify, deliver to production, and measure the impact.

The workflow can be diagrammed as follows. Understand the customer's reality, decide what to change, build it completely, make it actually used, measure the results, and feed them back into the next decision. An FDE connects these 5 stages as a single person.

Why are FDE schools attracting attention now?

Value is shifting from "building" to "deciding what to build" and "delivering to production"

With the spread of generative AI and AI agents, the barrier to writing code itself has dropped. As a result, a common frustration has become visible: teams can produce prototypes (PoCs) but can't get them to the point of being used in real production operations. Three skills are being demanded more than ever: the ability to decide what to build with the customer, the ability to design what can be delegated to AI, and the ability to safely deploy to production and hand off to operations. The FDE is the role that connects all three as a single person.

Movement is also visible in job postings and awareness

The change is visible in the numbers too. According to an announcement by Findy, Inc. on April 24, 2026, of the roughly 6,000 job postings on their job-change service "Findy," about 50 positions correspond to FDEs, compared to nearly zero six months earlier. While the count is still limited, it shows the role is emerging as a new profession (Findy, Inc. press release).

Also, in a survey of 243 IT engineers conducted by Levtech, Inc. in May 2026, 68.3% answered that they are "interested in FDE roles" and 72.0% answered that "demand for FDEs will increase going forward." The most common reason for expecting increased demand, at 61.1%, was "I believe understanding individual operations and the field will become important for applying AI to business" (Levtech, Inc. survey). This survey measured engineers' awareness, not the number of job postings.

The more learning options there are, the more choosing well matters

More places to learn means more options for students. However, since content labeled "FDE" differs greatly from school to school, choosing without a basis for comparison makes it easy to end up with something different from what you expected. First, let's organize what types exist.

The 4 main types of FDE schools

FDE schools and training programs can be roughly divided into 4 types based on their target audience and purpose. The following are general tendencies; actual content varies by individual school.

① Introductory type for beginners and career changers

This type is aimed at people with limited experience in programming or IT work, or consultants and sales professionals who want to get involved in AI-driven business transformation. It covers how to use AI tools and organizing business processes to build simple applications, starting at a relatively gentle level. It's suitable for getting a foothold into FDE work. However, whether it goes deep into production operations and security varies by school.

② Skill-building type for experienced professionals

This type is for people already working in IT or DX who want to build up areas such as upstream processes, AI-driven development, and production operations design, aiming to become capable of doing FDE work. These programs often require practical work experience as a prerequisite or include a selection process, and the content goes deeper into judgment and design rather than tool operation. Accordingly, learning time tends to be longer.

③ Corporate training programs

This type is provided by companies for their employees. It's adopted for purposes such as developing talent to lead internal AI adoption, or strengthening the upstream and AI capabilities of SIer and SES engineers. Unlike schools individuals choose for themselves, the deciding factor is whether the content and format can be tailored to the company's own challenges and structure.

④ Short-term seminars and study groups

This type lets you grasp the overall picture of FDE work in a few hours to a few days. It's best suited for gathering information before committing to a full-fledged school. Since the amount you can learn in a short time is limited, rather than asking "will I gain skills?", it's better to view it as "can I judge whether this is right for me?"

How to Choose an FDE School | 7 Checkpoints to Avoid Failure

Now for the main topic. When comparing FDE schools, here are 7 points to look for on official websites and at information sessions, along with tips on how to evaluate them.

① Do the target level and admission requirements match who you are now?

The first fork in the road is "what kind of person is the school designed for?" First, check whether the admission requirements or recommended level specify things like whether you have practical experience, what technologies you can use, and your AI experience.

If the level doesn't match, one of two things usually happens: it's easier than expected and leaves you unsatisfied, or you lack the prerequisite knowledge and can't keep up. If someone with practical experience chooses an introductory course, the content tends to feel shallow; conversely, if someone without experience chooses a course aimed at experienced professionals, they'll likely struggle because the prerequisites aren't in place.

What to look for: whether admission requirements (practical experience or screening) are clearly stated, and whether it's clear if the course is "beginner-friendly" or "for experienced professionals." A school where you can discuss whether your background is a good fit at an information session or individual consultation is even more reassuring.

② Does the curriculum connect the upstream process all the way to production operations?

FDE work continues from identifying customer problems all the way to the point where the solution is used in production and its effectiveness can be measured. So whether the curriculum is skewed toward just AI tools and development is a major dividing line.

The three areas to examine are:

  • Upstream processes and consulting: Field observation, problem definition, KPIs, how to decide on an MVP, building consensus with stakeholders
  • AI-driven development: Designing what to delegate to AI and when to stop, harness engineering, evaluation (Evals), reviewing AI-generated code
  • Production operations design and security: Architecture decisions and design reviews, trust boundaries and authentication/authorization, preparing for failures, staged releases and rollbacks, monitoring, handover to operations

FDE work cannot stand on any one of these alone. If it's skewed toward development, you may be able to build things but can't decide "what to build"; if skewed toward upstream work, you can make proposals but can't deliver something that can be trusted in production. Whether these three areas are connected within the flow of actual customer projects is surprisingly easy to see just by scanning the curriculum list.

③ Are the instructors active professionals who know the FDE field?

Much of FDE work is hard to learn from textbooks alone. Interacting with customers, making judgment calls when requirements change, getting through security reviews—learning about these from instructors with field experience deepens your understanding far more.

Key indicators: whether the official website describes instructors' backgrounds in concrete detail, whether they currently work in the field (not just past experience), and in what form you can receive feedback.

④ Can you keep up the study time and duration while working?

FDE schools vary widely in both duration and study volume. It's not a matter of "shorter is better" or "longer is better"—whether the workload is something you can see through to the end within your own life is the most important criterion.

Total study hours, duration, frequency of lectures and seminars, and time required for exercises and assignments are the minimum information you should know. It's also reassuring to ask how to proceed during busy periods at work and whether there are ways to catch up if you fall behind.

⑤ Are the deliverables and completion criteria clear?

Of the seven points, this is where the differences are most pronounced. Many courses effectively become "attend and you pass." But in FDE work, the quality of your judgment is what matters. That's why you want a school that is clear about what counts as "being able to do it."

  • Are the deliverables you create during the course concretely specified?
  • Who evaluates completion, and from what perspectives?
  • If you don't meet the criteria, are there opportunities for resubmission and feedback?
  • Rather than "everyone who attends completes," is there a mechanism to verify judgment ability?

Examples of deliverables include documents organizing problems, KPI designs, prototype evaluation results, and production operations design documents. The experience of creating deliverables in the formats actually used at work, with review, is hard to gain through self-study.

⑥ Are the teaching materials updated in line with the latest AI trends?

The AI field changes within a few months. Content that is several years old may not be usable in the field. How the materials and seminars are updated is worth paying attention to.

Is there a stated policy for updating materials, along with the last update date? Does it teach principles that remain valid even when tools change, rather than instructions for specific tools? Is there a mechanism for the insights of instructors working in the field to be reflected in the materials and seminars? These are good points to check.

In particular, courses centered on "how to use a specific AI tool" become outdated the moment that tool changes. Whether the course is built around principles that endure even when tools change—such as problem definition, evaluation, design, and operations—is the dividing line between learning that lasts and learning that doesn't.

⑦ Is the support system and scope of assistance realistically presented?

An environment where you can ask questions, feedback frequency, connections among students—support for continuing your studies is also important. And when it comes to career support and project introductions, it's essential to read the stated content calmly and critically.

Does it show how to ask questions or submit reviews and how quickly to expect replies? Is there a mechanism for continuing to learn, such as daily reports or one-on-one meetings? Is job change and project acquisition support described as "support content" rather than a "guarantee"? Absolute claims like "you will definitely get a job if you complete the course" or "you will definitely land projects" should be taken with caution. Schools that describe the scope of their support concretely feel more trustworthy.

Easy-to-Miss Pitfalls: Courses That Look Impressive but Lack Substance

Phrases like "Learn FDE" and "AI will change your work" line the page, and it looks very impressive. But when you open up the content, it turns out to be nothing more than explanations of basic AI operations. This pattern, common in dubious information products, is unfortunately not rare in the world of FDE schools either.

For example, courses centered on very basic content such as Claude Code skills, connector configuration, and how to connect MCP. Learning how to use tools as an entry point is not a bad thing in itself. However, if the course ends there, several problems arise.

  • You complete the course, but you are left with no real sense that you have "gained practical skills"
  • When you actually start using the tools, you keep pressing "Yes" on approval buttons without understanding what will happen
  • As a result, something that works gets built while permissions and security remain vague

The last point is hard to notice from appearances, but can later become a major problem. When you delegate file rewriting or connections to external services to an AI agent, how much do you allow? When do you stop? What happens when incorrect input arrives? If you use it without being able to design these things yourself, every time you press "Yes," you take on more risk.

What is required of an FDE corresponds to levels 2 and 3 in the diagram. Design the scope delegated to AI and the conditions for stopping, verify through evaluation, solidify permissions and security, and then go to production. On top of that, if you cannot explain to the client "why it is acceptable to delegate this much," you will not be trusted with AI in the field.

So Your Learning Doesn't End at PoC: Look at the Depth of Architecture, Security, and Production Operations

Building prototypes with AI has become much easier these days. Precisely because of that, many courses end once you have built a working demo (PoC). Security handling is lax, and understanding never reaches the design of production operations before you complete the course. So when you try to actually go to production in the field, you fail security reviews or operational requirements. That is the dead end people tend to hit.

Here are the differences between learning that ends at the PoC level and learning that reaches the production level, organized by perspective.

Seven Questions to Identify Courses That Stop at Basic Operations and PoC

Asking about the following at information sessions or on official websites will reveal the depth of the course.

  • Do they cover permission design? Can you learn to draw lines such as separating reads from writes and requiring human approval for writes?
  • Are stop conditions and prompt injection countermeasures included in the curriculum?
  • Is there a process for verifying AI output through evaluation (Evals)? Does it include not only normal examples but also malicious input?
  • Are there opportunities for code review? Is there practice in finding hard-to-spot problems in AI-written code, such as missing authorization checks?
  • Are there architecture reviews? Does the course include the experience of submitting architecture diagrams for third-party review and defending them orally?
  • Is security discussed using concrete terms such as trust boundaries, threat modeling, and authentication/authorization?
  • Do the exercises include taking what you built to production, monitoring it, and handing it over to operations?

If a course only lists phrases like "how to use Claude Code" and "MCP configuration" and can barely answer these seven questions, it is probably centered on basic operations.

By type | How to choose the FDE school that fits you

Building on the seven points, here is what to prioritize depending on your situation.

Engineers, or those with 1+ years of experience in IT, DX, or AIX departments

If you already have practical experience, we recommend focusing on the target level (①), the curriculum's coherence and depth (②), and completion criteria (⑤). Introductory courses tend to cover a lot you already know. A program that handles everything from upstream processes to production operations through the flow of real client projects, with instructors reviewing your deliverables, will build on your existing foundation.

Even if you are not in an engineering role, if you have built experience in an IT, DX, or AIX department, your experience thinking about "how much to entrust to AI" in the field will serve directly as the foundation for your learning.

Those with no experience or limited experience

If your IT experience is still limited, focus on admission requirements (①), support (⑦), and study time (④). It is important whether the course progresses step by step from the basics and whether there is an environment where you can easily ask questions. Jumping straight into a rigorous school aimed at experienced professionals may leave you struggling with prerequisite knowledge. One option is to first grasp the overall picture through an introductory course or short seminar, then move on to the next stage when needed.

Corporate HR, DX promotion, and talent development staff

When choosing training for employees, the criteria differ from those for individual schools. Key indicators include whether the content can be adjusted to your company's challenges, whether support extends to producing results in the field after completion, and whether there is flexibility in group size and duration. BizCodeX-FDE also accepts inquiries about corporate FDE training (Corporate training inquiries).

Those who want to gather information first

There is no need to decide right away. It should not be too late to compare multiple schools after grasping the overall picture of FDE work and curricula through free study sessions or information sessions. Keep the seven checkpoints at hand and ask the same questions of each school, and the differences will become much easier to see.

Six common mistakes when choosing an FDE school

Having read this far, you may think "these are all obvious." Yet they are also mistakes people surprisingly often make when they don't know about them in advance.

  • Choosing based on the name alone: Even if something is labeled "FDE," the content may be entirely different. It is worth reading the actual curriculum details at least once.
  • Mismatched level: You may choose a course that is too easy and leave you unsatisfied, or too hard and leave you falling behind. Compare admission requirements and recommended levels against your own experience.
  • Drawn in by appearances without examining the content: The marketing looks impressive, but the content is just learning to configure skills, connectors, and MCP. Without the ability to design permissions, stop conditions, evaluations, and security, you will not be entrusted with real work. It is worth reflecting on whether your usage has quietly become just pressing "Yes" over and over.
  • Getting stuck at the PoC stage: In courses without architecture and code reviews, security, or production operations design, you may be able to build a working demo, but it will not pass production review.
  • Not checking completion criteria: In courses where you complete just by attending, there is no way to know whether you actually learned anything.
  • Taking promises of results at face value: Be cautious of claims that flatly guarantee job changes or project acquisition. It is reassuring to ask specifically what the support entails.

All of these can be prevented by asking questions in advance. At information sessions or individual consultations, feel free to ask without hesitation.

Frequently asked questions about FDE schools

Q Can I enroll in an FDE school with no prior experience?

A It depends on the school. Some offer introductory programs aimed at beginners or people from different professions, while others require practical work experience as an admission condition. What matters is whether your current position matches the type of student the school envisions. BizCodeX-FDE is intended for engineers, or those with at least one year of practical experience in IT, DX, or AIX departments; those with less than one year are not eligible.

Q Can people other than engineers take an FDE school?

A It depends on the school. BizCodeX-FDE accepts applicants who are engineers, or who have at least one year of practical experience in IT, DX, or AIX (AI Transformation) departments. For non-engineers, experience in IT, DX, or AIX departments is the condition. If you are unsure whether your background qualifies, please tell us in a free individual consultation.

Q When choosing an FDE school, what should I look at first?

A The first two criteria are "who it is for (target audience and admission conditions)" and "what and how far you can learn (whether it connects from upstream processes to production operation)." If these two don't match you, the learning won't click no matter how good the other conditions are.

Q What is the difference between a course teaching how to use AI tools and an FDE school?

A Courses on tool usage focus mainly on "how to operate" things, such as skills, connectors, and MCP configuration. What an FDE school should really cover is the ability to "make things delegatable to AI," including designing the scope delegated to AI and the conditions for stopping, verification through Evals (evaluation), designing permissions and security, and production operation. BizCodeX-FDE builds its curriculum around the latter.

Q To build skills that go beyond PoC and work in production, what should I look for in a school?

A Key indicators are whether there are architecture and code reviews, and whether exercises include security (trust boundaries, authentication/authorization, permission design, etc.) and production operation design (monitoring, staged releases, rollback, operational handover, etc.). Whether these terms appear concretely in the curriculum list reveals the difference in depth.

Q What is the typical duration and study time for an FDE school?

A They range from intensive programs of a few days to long-term programs of around six months. Rather than the length itself, what matters is whether the study load is sustainable while working, and whether you can produce deliverables in that time and receive instructor feedback. BizCodeX-FDE runs for 6 months, with a guideline of 400+ total study hours.

Q Is an FDE school really more advantageous than self-study?

A It cannot be said definitively. Much of the knowledge needed for FDE can be researched through self-study. The value of a school lies in things that are hard to create alone: the design of the learning sequence, feedback on deliverables, exercises with a stand-in client, and completion criteria.

Q If I attend an FDE school, will I be able to work as an FDE?

A Completing a school does not guarantee employment or winning projects. Be cautious of claims that promise guarantees. BizCodeX-FDE presents the abilities you should acquire by completion as goals, but it does not promise results.

Q As a company, can we have our employees take FDE training?

A BizCodeX-FDE also accepts inquiries about FDE training and AI adoption talent development. The criteria for choosing differ between individual enrollment in the school and introducing training as in-house talent development. For details, please see our corporate training consultation page.

Q Where can I find BizCodeX-FDE's pricing?

A Tuition is provided at our free study sessions and individual consultations. After hearing about your current experience and desired direction, we will together confirm whether enrollment is a good fit.

Summary | Choose an FDE school based on "whether it fits you"

We believe that in choosing an FDE school, what matters more than popularity or name recognition is whether it matches your current position and the direction you want to go.

  • ① Target audience and admission conditions, and ⑤ deliverables and completion criteria, should be checked as early as possible
  • ② For the curriculum, check whether it connects from upstream processes to production operation along the flow of a client project. Also check whether it goes beyond basic AI operations and PoC, and examine the depth of the content
  • ③ For ③ instructors, ④ study time, ⑥ up-to-dateness, and ⑦ support, verify both on the official website and at information sessions
  • Approach claims that definitively promise results or career changes with caution

If there are multiple schools you are interested in, ask the 7 checkpoints in the same order and compare the answers. The differences will be surprisingly clear.
