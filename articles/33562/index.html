Book Review of *Prediction Machines*: How FDEs Can Clearly Articulate the Value of AI Projects

Original title: 《预测机器》书评：FDE 如何讲清 AI 项目价值

FDE Times · 2026-11-25 · 4 min read

Summary

This review covers the updated edition of Prediction Machines by economists Ajay Agrawal, Joshua Gans, and Avi Goldfarb (Harvard Business Review Press, 2022, 304 pages), arguing it gives FDEs a shared vocabulary with executives. The book frames AI progress as a collapse in the cost of prediction, while the expensive part is judgment: pricing the consequences of each possible outcome. An insurance fraud example shows how payoff figures yield decision thresholds. Since prediction and judgment are complements, cheaper prediction makes good judgment more valuable, so FDEs should build complementary skills like translating vague requests into concrete numbers and thresholds. Suggested reading order: the 2022 edition first, then the NBER chapter, or a short MIT IDE talk.

Key points

  • AI progress is fundamentally a drop in prediction costs.
  • Judgment—pricing each outcome's consequences—is the real bottleneck.
  • Cheaper prediction makes good judgment more valuable, not less.
  • FDEs should convert vague client asks into payoff numbers and thresholds.
  • Read the 2022 edition first, then the NBER chapter.

Key Takeaways

  • The book's core argument is that AI makes prediction cheap, so FDEs should look for customer decisions that rely on expensive predictions.
  • Judgment means clarifying the consequences of each outcome. This work genuinely takes effort, and AI projects often get stuck at this step.
  • As long as judgment isn't too hard, the cheaper prediction becomes, the more valuable good judgment is, so FDEs should cultivate skills that complement prediction.

In 2022, Harvard Business Review Press released an updated edition of a 304-page book. The entire argument can be summed up in one sentence: the rise of AI is, at its core, a decline in the cost of prediction.

The book is Prediction Machines, Updated and Expanded: The Simple Economics of Artificial Intelligence, written by economists Ajay Agrawal, Joshua Gans, and Avi Goldfarb. According to the publisher, The Economist once named it one of the five best reads for understanding AI.

The book is written for managers, but Forward Deployed Engineers (FDEs) may get even more out of it. FDEs often sit between two worlds: on one side, a working demo; on the other, executives who want to know what difference it can make to the business. The book provides a language both sides can use, without mentioning model architecture once in the entire conversation.

One Economic Concept Is Worth Ten Slides

The publisher's blurb captures the book's tone: AI's impact is far-reaching, but the economic framework for understanding it is surprisingly simple.

The academic foundation comes from a chapter the three authors wrote for a collection published jointly by NBER and the University of Chicago Press. That chapter treats recent advances in AI as advances in prediction technology.

For FDEs, this perspective changes how discovery conversations open. When a customer says they want to "build a chatbot" or "bring in AI agents," try flipping the question: in your workflows, which predictions are currently expensive, slow, or purely intuitive? The answer usually points to a specific decision, and that decision is exactly what the customer's management cares about.

The Most Expensive Part of a Decision Is Judgment

The second argument is where the book shows its real strength. The authors call the work of determining the payoff of each outcome "judgment" and clearly distinguish it from prediction.

Judgment is expensive because for every scenario that might occur, someone has to think through the consequences. At an event hosted by MIT IDE, Goldfarb gave an actionable definition: everything that affects a decision other than prediction and data counts as judgment.

Take a hypothetical example. An insurance company wants to use a model to score claims for fraud. The model outputs only a probability. It doesn't know that paying out a fraudulent claim costs about 10,000 units, while sending a claim for manual review costs only 100 units.

These two numbers are set by people. From there, you can derive that any claim with a fraud probability above 1% is worth reviewing, because 1% × 10,000 = 100.

If the claims department refuses to commit to these two numbers, no amount of model accuracy translates into action. The first thing to do at the customer is to sit down with the process owner and fill in the complete payoff table for all four right-and-wrong scenarios.

Why Cheaper Prediction Makes People More Valuable

The third argument runs counter to the reflexive "AI replaces people" thinking. In the authors' model, as long as judgment isn't too hard, prediction and judgment are complements. In that case, the cheaper prediction becomes, the more valuable good judgment is. The NBER chapter also derives what this means for automation, contracts, and firm boundaries.

When scoping a project, this argument can serve as a filter. Decisions with clear, stable payoff tables are suited to automation. Decisions where every case requires someone to reweigh the consequences should keep a human in the loop, with the system simply delivering the prediction to the right person at the right time.

Goldfarb also offers career advice: the most valuable skills are those that complement prediction. For FDEs, this means translating customers' vague statements into concrete numbers for consequences, designing thresholds, and clarifying who is accountable for the final decision.

When writing your resume, describe projects in terms of "which decision was improved and which costs the thresholds were based on," rather than listing the models you used.

Read the 2022 Edition First, Then the NBER Chapter

The updated edition adds two parts. One explores where prediction sits within decision-making; the other discusses how foundational technologies like quantum computing will affect business decisions. The former is essential reading for FDEs; the quantum section can be skimmed.

For engineers, a sensible reading order is: start with the 2022 HBR edition to master the language for conversations with executives.

Then open the chapter Prediction, Judgment, and Complexity on the NBER website to understand the underlying models. If you only have ten minutes, the summary of Goldfarb's MIT IDE talk is a short primer.

This book won't teach you how to write evals or deploy AI agents to production. It's for FDEs who are technically strong but lose the thread the moment a meeting shifts from model accuracy to money. For engineers moving from software engineering into FDE work, it helps you learn to speak the customer's language quickly.

The next time a customer executive asks what AI is worth, don't rush to report model scores. Instead, ask a harder question in return: when prediction is nearly free, how much does each wrong guess cost?
