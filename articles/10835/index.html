If the boss won't delegate authority, what else can an FDE do?

Original title: 老板不放权，FDE还能干什么？

53AI · 2026-09-11 · 7 min read

Summary

Drawing on Mark Scianna's 11 years as a Palantir FDE deployed to Afghanistan and other conflict zones, the article traces the role's military roots and its Auftragstaktik logic: headquarters sets the goal, the front line decides the method. The author argues FDE projects fail at two gates. First, the client's boss must accept being part of the transformation—relinquishing control over processes, tolerating errors, and letting outsiders make decisions on-site. Second, an FDE must map the company's real decision chain within two weeks and build quick, ugly working paths before formal products. The author shares early experiments with embedded staffing and vibe coding.

Key points

  • The FDE role has existed at Palantir for nearly 20 years, born on real battlefields.
  • Its command logic is Auftragstaktik: headquarters defines goals, the front line decides execution.
  • Gate one: the client boss must accept that he himself is within the transformation's scope.
  • Gate two: an FDE should map the real decision chain within two weeks on-site.
  • Build gravel roads first, formal products later—reversing the order kills most projects.

Full text

Let me break down the two hardest hurdles of being an FDE with you.

In 2010, two engineers arrived at a military base near Kandahar, Afghanistan. Their entire instructions from headquarters in Silicon Valley came down to one sentence: go there, win it, and call if you need anything.

That was it. No thick requirements documents or guidance manuals, no schedules. The acceptance criterion was a single word: win.

One of them was Mark Scianna. He spent several months in Afghanistan, setting up servers on classified networks during the day—installing machines, wiring cables, doing data integration;

at night, he took helicopters to forward outposts near the Pakistani border. Those places had no internet, so they had to set up their own satellite link to connect communications back to headquarters.

On his first deployment to Kandahar, he stayed for three months, for a simple reason: there was too much to learn.

01 FDEs Are Truly on the Front Line

In the summer of 2011, he was operating alongside a Navy SEAL team. After spending an entire week dodging rockets, a colleague forwarded him a blog post titled "Why Palantir Gives Me a Headache."

The article contained a judgment that these people were nothing but consultants with a fancy new title. Keep in mind, Palantir was the very company he worked for, whose main business was providing technical support to the US government and military.

The FDE (Forward Deployed Engineer) role everyone is talking about these days may sound like it only got hot in the past six months, but the position has existed at his company for nearly 20 years. Mark spent 11 years as an FDE and only left to start his own venture in 2019.

He has been stationed everywhere from Afghanistan to Iraq to conflict zones in Africa. I found a great many photos and details on his blog.

So he said that dismissive characterization of him as a "consultant with a fancy title" felt deeply unreal to him. Because what he did that week was dodge rockets, connect data, train soldiers, and debug those infuriating databases. And just two months before that, the US military had completed the raid on Osama bin Laden.

When I read this passage, I realized something. Everyone talking about FDEs—Forward Deployed Engineers—is almost exclusively talking about the second word, "Engineer": what the salary is, what skills are required, whether it's worth switching careers.

Few people pay attention to the words "Forward Deployed." Who would have thought that the "front line" in this job title originally referred to an actual front line filled with smoke and gunfire.

02 Headquarters Sets the Goal, the Front Line Decides the Approach

Behind that phrase "go there, win it" lies a clear command philosophy. I looked it up specifically, and it actually has a dedicated term: Auftragstaktik.

It's understandable if you don't recognize it—it's German, meaning "mission-type command." It comes down from the Prussian army: headquarters is only responsible for making the objective clear, while how to fight, what to fight with, and where to strike first are almost entirely delegated to the people on the front line.

In fact, what Huawei used to say—"let those who hear the gunfire make the decisions"—expresses the same idea.

For a frontline worker like Mark, the work changed completely. What counts as winning was decided by headquarters. Everything else was up to him: which data to migrate first, whom to train first, which department to expand from.

He only called back in two situations: one, when he genuinely needed technical support from headquarters, and two, when he was completely stuck.

Headquarters sets the goal, the front line decides the approach.

He probably never imagined back then that what he was doing would become one of the hottest tech jobs in the world by 2026.

Quite a few friends around me are considering switching careers into this field, and several business owners have asked me whether they should build such a team internally. They're all asking essentially the same thing: what makes this job hard, and what kind of people should they look for.

In my view, many FDE projects get stuck before anyone is even dispatched. There are two hurdles here, and the first one isn't on the vendor's side at all—it has nothing to do with which AI tools or AI models you use.

03 The Boss Is Also Within the Scope of Transformation

The first hurdle is whether the client boss who pays the bill can accept this: they themselves are within the scope of the transformation.

Let's get this clear first, because I've seen many founders completely underestimate the impact of AI transformation on a company, thinking that hiring someone to implement AI is just buying a product and installing it.

In reality, the first thing an engineer runs into when they get to work is the processes. Suppose an order used to pass through five people and get five stamps; now they discover three of those steps are unnecessary, and the work of those three people has to be reassigned.

Once processes change, job relationships shift too: who reports to whom, which departments no longer need as many people—all of it gets dug up.

Going deeper, what gets touched is how the company treats mistakes. Your company used to assign blame whenever something went wrong; now the forward deployed engineer tells you the first version will probably be wrong, that it needs to run first and collect failure data in order to get better and better.

What's more, since quite a few people's power or interests may be affected, some will certainly do everything they can to prove that AI can't do their jobs.

And that's not all. You also have to tolerate an outsider who's been there two weeks making decisions on your turf that you yourself would never make. He may not ask for approval at all, because asking would be too late, or the opportunity would be gone.

Looking at it from the outside, it's easy to attribute this to traditional bosses being unwilling to give up power or change. But if you truly stand in their position, the risks they bear are very real.

They have to hand over their company's most critical data and most profitable processes to an external team they've only recently met. If the changes fail, customers complain, quarterly reports look bad, or even those loyal middle managers come crying in their office.

All these costs happen immediately, while the benefits of AI transformation may not be visible for six months or a year. And of course, they might never be visible at all.

Not to mention that once processes are streamlined, many positions that used to survive on relaying information up and down, or on information asymmetry, simply disappear.

The people sitting in those positions are often veterans who fought alongside him for many years. In many companies, there's a common phrase: "So-and-so is so-and-so's person." That phrase alone reveals a great deal.

So for many bosses, AI transformation is genuinely a matter of survival, and it's hard. I deeply understand that feeling.

But if you can't get past this hurdle, even the world's strongest FDE can't solve your company's problems.

Because if you don't delegate authority, they can't make decisions on-site. All they can touch are the trivial bits that affect no one, and what gets delivered in the end is a few harmless, inconsequential little tools—like generating marketing short-video copy or something—very safe, very boring.

That kind of engagement is closer to mature technology outsourcing. Technology outsourcing is fine too—it solves a well-defined problem—just don't expect it to bring the tsunami-scale change of an AI transformation.

04 An FDE Must Map the Real Decision Chain

The second hurdle is where the engineer we care about finally comes in.

This hurdle is far higher than most people imagine. Many think any senior engineer can handle it, but the gap is actually quite large. On the client site, the hardest part is figuring out how the company actually operates.

Who really calls the shots, who seems uninvolved but actually holds veto power, which unwritten rules nobody dares to touch. None of this is written in any document.

There's a more practical way to test whether an FDE can succeed: within two weeks of arriving, they should be able to draw the company's real decision chain.

For many companies, this is completely different from the org chart in collaboration software like Feishu or DingTalk. This kind of insight into an organization is genuinely demanding.

So bosses can use this line to vet candidates, and FDEs can use it to check themselves, to see whether they can actually do it.

One more thing: the way this work is done runs counter to the instincts of product people. When you arrive on-site, the first task is to blaze a path that works.

Where it's muddy, lay down a couple of stones; where you can't get around an obstacle, put up a ladder first. It's crude and ugly, but it gets through, and the customer can use it today.

The Palantir people call this "paving the gravel road first." Once enough people travel that road and the patterns stabilize, you extract the recurring parts, build them into a proper highway, turn them into a formal product, and offer it to more customers.

Never get the order backwards: build the highway first and then find people to walk it—that's where most implementation projects die. So never start by overhauling someone's core system.

Speaking of which, I remember discussing this with an investor when I was in Silicon Valley. He told me that the leading FDE companies he's seen send core members of their product teams to customer sites—usually people at a high level who can make decisions independently on-site.

Meanwhile, many of the positions I've seen labeled FDE are, in essence, still the same outsourcing arrangement SaaS companies have always used. When some projects get stuck, it's often because those people can't change anything.

05 Lay the Gravel Road First

At this point, after all this history and all these problems with FDE, I honestly don't have an answer for what to do.

I'm experimenting myself. One approach is to place people directly into another department—not as external support, but embedded as employees of that department.

Another approach is to have one or two colleagues stand behind business colleagues and focus on one very specific, granular step. Watch and observe first, then use vibe coding to solve the problem directly.

What does that mean? No scheduling, no project kickoff. Whoever spots a problem writes a small thing on the spot to patch it. As long as it works, that's enough—no productization for now, no plugins with fixed features.

This is especially relevant because many companies have built large amounts of complex back-office systems over the years. I've been thinking lately about how, without touching those legacy back-office systems, we could use a more AI-driven, simpler approach to replace functions that previously required going into the back office.

This is what I meant earlier by laying the gravel road first instead of rushing to build the highway. Since I'm still experimenting myself, I can't say which approach is right. Once I have some cases worth sharing, I'll come back and talk with you about them.
