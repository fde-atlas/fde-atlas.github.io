Breaking Out of AI PoC Stagnation | Three Disconnections That Prevent Production Deployment and How to Move to Production

"The demo went well. The accuracy was there. But we can't put it into production." Not a month goes by without hearing these words on the front lines of AI adoption.

Generative AI has arrived. According to the Ministry of Internal Affairs and Communications' 2026 Information and Communications White Paper, 86.4% of Japanese companies now use generative AI in some form of their operations. Meanwhile, Gartner predicted in July 2024 that at least 30% of generative AI projects would be abandoned after proof of concept by the end of 2025, citing data quality, insufficient risk management, rising costs, and unclear business value. There is a large gap between getting AI in and running it in production.

In my view, this is not a failure of technology. It is a failure of contracts and organizational structure. Projects that stall at the PoC stage share a common pattern, which can be explained as three disconnects. Below, I will describe the nature of these disconnects, the steps to move to production, and the criteria for deciding whether to rebuild or productionize a stalled PoC. For the definition of the FDE (Forward Deployed Engineer) role, see What is an FDE?; for how to identify a support company, see How to Choose an FDE Support Company.

Why Doesn't It Run, Even Though the PoC Succeeded?

The purpose of a PoC is to verify "whether it can be done." The purpose of production is to "move the business numbers." These are different questions, often pursued by different people, under different contracts, with different metrics. Three things fall through the cracks between them.

Disconnect 1: Problem Definition and the Front Lines

PoC problems are often decided in conference rooms by management or IT departments, such as "automate inquiry handling with AI." But when you go to the front lines, what the staff are really struggling with may not be the number of inquiries, but the fact that the internal information needed to answer them is scattered across five systems.

Even if you build a model with 95% accuracy, it won't be used if the front lines feel "that's not the point." This disconnect occurs when the front-line work isn't inventoried before starting the PoC. The fix is simple: put the people who handle that work every day into the meeting where the problem is defined.

Disconnect 2: Technical Validation and Business Processes

A PoC runs in a notebook. Production must run within core systems, authentication, permissions, logging, exception handling, and monitoring. Between them lies the "last mile." The moment a consultant hands the proposal document to engineers, no one is left to run that last mile responsibly.

The fix is to introduce one production constraint at the PoC stage: the same data as production, the same permissions, the same input variability. Rather than competing on accuracy in a notebook, building something small that runs under production constraints gets you to production faster.

Disconnect 3: Success Metrics and Business KPIs

"Achieved 98% accuracy" is a PoC success metric, not a business KPI. How many processes were reduced in production, how many hours of work were saved, how much did usage increase? Projects that don't design for this from the start cannot answer the question "so what improved?" after going to production.

The fix is to replace PoC evaluation metrics with business numbers. Accuracy is the means; the KPI is the goal. Once the KPI is set, the required accuracy level also becomes clear.

Four Steps to Reach Production

There are four steps to bridge the disconnects. The order matters, and skipping step 1 to start at step 3 is the classic pattern of a stalled PoC.

  • Inventory the work and design KPIs. Break down the work and decide on one number to move, drawing from both management KPIs and operational KPIs.
  • Translate the division of roles between AI and humans into an implementation plan. Separate what AI can do, what it cannot, and where humans should make judgments, so it doesn't remain a pie in the sky.
  • Build within production constraints. Include authentication, permissions, exception handling, logging, and monitoring from the start. Using AI coding tools here is faster than writing by hand in person-months.
  • Start operations and fix weekly. Observe how it is used and reflect improvements the following week. Keep iterating until the KPI moves.

If the four steps are split across different companies or departments, the disconnects are reproduced as-is. Dividing the work—consultants do the inventory, IT does the planning, SIs do the build, and the front lines run operations—looks rational at first glance, but information is lost at every handoff and responsibility is diluted. Having the same people, the same team, own all four end to end is the reason the FDE role exists.

Rebuild or Productionize?

Faced with a stalled PoC, the first decision is "do we reuse this PoC's deliverables, or discard them?" Evaluate against five criteria.

CriterionQuestionIf Missing
DataDid it run on the same data and variability as production?Accuracy collapses after production deployment
MetricsAre the evaluation metrics business numbers, or just accuracy?Benefits can't be explained even after productionization
ConstraintsAre authentication, permissions, and exception handling designed?It stalls at the last mile
OperationsIs someone assigned to operate and keep fixing it?It degrades immediately after production launch
CauseIs the reason it stalled technical or organizational?Trying to solve an organizational problem with technology fails

If three or more are missing, it's faster to demote the PoC deliverables to reference material and restart from the work inventory. If two or fewer, build out the missing parts under production constraints. Either way, the decision can be made in a 30-minute meeting. All you need is the stalled PoC itself (the code and evaluation results), the KPI you want to move, and the front-line owner.

Who Takes Responsibility to the End?

The root cause of AI projects never reaching production is ambiguity about where responsibility lies. Consultants end with recommendations. SI companies build to the requirements definition. AI companies deliver the model. None of these contracts is wrong, but no one is on the hook "until results appear."

FDEs are different. Their contract doesn't end at delivery; it ends where the KPI moves. That's why they engage in problem definition, production constraints, and operations. It's that simple, but when the structure changes, the results change.
