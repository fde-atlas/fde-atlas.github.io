FDE Delivery Quality Metrics: From "The Customer Says It's Good" to Verifiable Metrics

Original title: FDE 交付质量度量：从「客户说好」到可验证指标

FDE中国 · 2026-09-04 · 9 min read

Summary

This article tackles a common FDE problem: delivery quality is proven only by subjective client praise, which is incomparable, delayed, and impossible to attribute. It proposes a layered metric system separating business outcomes (renewals, acceptance cycles), technical process, and reusable assets. For Agent projects it recommends tracking task success rate, per-task cost, and latency (median plus tail). Delivery health relies on first-pass acceptance rate, defect escape rate, and response time, compared against self-built baselines rather than borrowed thresholds. It warns against vanity metrics and Goodhart's law, suggesting paired metrics, sampling audits, and scenario protection, and prescribes weekly, monthly, and quarterly review cadences tied to concrete decisions. In China's toB context, acceptance criteria are often contractual, so definitions should be aligned with clients early.

Key points

  • Layer metrics into business, technical, and asset tiers, each serving a different audience.
  • For Agent projects, measure task success rate, cost, and latency with machine-checkable, scenario-grouped definitions.
  • Track delivery health via acceptance pass rate, defect escape rate, and response time against your own baselines.
  • Counter Goodhart's law with paired metrics, sampling audits, and locked core-scenario eval tasks.
  • Run weekly, monthly, quarterly reviews where every metric must lead to a decision.

An FDE said during a performance review, "The customer is very satisfied with the delivery," and was immediately asked three questions: how satisfied exactly, how does that compare to last quarter, and which contributions came from team assets versus sheer headcount—they couldn't answer a single one. This is not an isolated case. An FDE's work spans the customer site and engineering implementation, so its effectiveness is inherently hard to quantify, but if only subjective impressions exist over the long run, the team loses out in three ways: no evidence when fighting for resources internally, no clear articulation of value when pricing externally, and no visible trends when assessing personal growth. This piece discusses how to turn delivery quality into a verifiable metrics system: how many layers, which quantities to watch, which pitfalls to avoid, and at what cadence to review.

Why "the customer says it's good" cannot support measurement

Subjective evaluation has three structural flaws. First, it is not comparable: customer A saying "not bad" and customer B saying "not bad" are not the same thing; satisfaction across different projects has no common yardstick. Second, it lags: the customer's true assessment often only surfaces at renewal or expansion, by which point months have passed since the delivery actions, and you cannot know which change to credit or blame. Third, it is not attributable: customer satisfaction may stem from your excellent delivery, or simply from low customer expectations or a business relationship propping things up—subjective evaluation cannot distinguish these components.

This doesn't mean customer feedback is useless; rather, it should be structured before use: break "the customer says it's good" into verifiable objective facts, such as whether acceptance passed on the first attempt, how long it took from issue report to resolution, and whether the customer expanded usage beyond the contract. Only quantities that can be recorded, compared, and reviewed qualify as measurement. The metrics system should serve the entire delivery process, from pre-sales validation in the project lifecycle to operations and scaling; each stage focuses on different things, and the layering below is organized along this logic.

Metric layering: build business and technical layers separately

The first principle of a metrics system is layering: business outcomes and technical processes are two different things, and mixing them in one layer leads to mutual contamination—using technical metrics to masquerade as business value, or blaming technical execution for business fluctuations. The reference layering is as follows:

LayerQuestion answeredTypical metricsAudience
Business layerWhat the delivery brought the companyRenewals and expansions, acceptance cycle, customer usage depthManagement, sales
Technical layerHow good is the delivery process itselfTask success rate, defect escape rate, response timeFDEs and team leads
Asset layerWhat reusable capabilities the team has accumulatedComponent reuse count, eval set size, documentation coverageTeam leads

The value of layering is that each layer's metrics answer to their own audience. Telling management about task success rate is wrong—what they care about is renewals and acceptance cycles; holding frontline FDEs accountable for renewal rates is also wrong—that is a business outcome they cannot directly influence. In domestic toB projects there is another reality: acceptance criteria are often written into contracts, so the business layer's acceptance cycle metrics should align definitions with the customer early—what counts as passing, who confirms it, how long counts as overdue; clarify these before delivery and acceptance won't devolve into each side talking past the other. The asset layer is the evidence distinguishing an FDE team from an outsourcing crew; the knowledge feedback mechanism discussed in the FDE team design article ultimately shows up in this layer's metrics.

Evals metrics for Agent projects: task success rate, cost, and latency

A large share of what FDEs currently deliver are Agent or LLM applications, and quality measurement for these projects has its own structure. The first core metric is task success rate: the proportion of tasks in the eval set completed end-to-end by the model plus engineering pipeline. It sounds simple, but three definition issues must be settled first. First, split by scenario: an overall success rate may look high, but it cannot hide the fact that core scenarios are only at eighty percent—always group statistics by business scenario. Second, success criteria must be machine-judgeable: evals relying on humans eyeballing "roughly correct" cannot scale. Third, failures must be attributed: is it a model capability issue, a retrieval quality issue, or an engineering pipeline issue—attribution determines the direction of improvement. For how to build and maintain eval sets, see the systematic discussion in the Agent special topic.

The second dimension is cost: average invocation cost per task, usually converted into model call volume or direct API fees. The purpose of the cost metric is not saving money per se, but detecting degradation—if a change leaves effectiveness flat while cost doubles, it usually means an inefficient link has appeared in the pipeline; cost structure also helps you judge which scenarios warrant a stronger model and which should take a lightweight path. Cost collection also has team significance: when project-loss disputes arise, the per-task cost structure is the basis for deciding "keep optimizing or change the approach," and it is one of the few pieces of evidence that is both objective and favorable to you in customer negotiations. The third dimension is latency: user waiting time; record both the median and the tail (e.g., the 95th percentile), since averages mask the long-tail requests that hurt experience most. Beyond these three dimensions, if the business is sensitive to stability, also watch the variance of multiple runs of the same task—a system that is sometimes right and sometimes wrong is harder to debug than one that fails consistently, and more likely to blow up at the customer site.

Delivery health: acceptance pass rate, defect escape, and response time

Beyond in-project metrics like Agent evals, the delivery process itself needs a set of health metrics; three collectible, hard-to-fake quantities are recommended. Acceptance pass rate: the proportion of stage acceptances passed on the first attempt, directly reflecting the quality of your understanding of customer requirements. When it drops, the cause is usually not coding but clarification—requirements weren't probed thoroughly, and no matter how fast you build, rework follows. The lever for improving this metric lies early in delivery: breaking acceptance criteria into a checklist signed off by both parties is far more effective than after-the-fact remediation. Defect escape rate: the proportion of defects found by the customer out of total defects, measuring whether your self-testing and evals system actually works. Be careful with definitions here: of the issues customers report, how many count as defects and how many as requirement changes—agree on classification rules with the customer in advance, or the numbers will be diluted by day-to-day communication.

Response time: the interval from the customer reporting an issue to your starting to handle it, plus a second interval from start of handling to resolution. The first interval reflects whether the channel between you and the customer is smooth; the second reflects real technical handling capability—only by looking at them separately can you locate the bottleneck. What these three metrics share is that absolute values have limited meaning; the focus is trends and baselines: record two or three projects first to establish your own baseline, then compare each subsequent project against it for deviations. A widespread industry observation is that "healthy values" vary enormously across customers and scenarios, so directly copying someone else's thresholds is meaningless—baseline comparison is the actionable approach.

Collection in practice: definitions first, tools second

The most common cause of death for a metrics system is not choosing the wrong metrics, but failing to collect data or having definitions drift. When implementing, write the metric definition document first: one section per metric, specifying the calculation formula, data sources, update frequency, and definition boundaries. For example, for "acceptance pass rate," which acceptances count in the numerator and whether re-acceptances caused by customer delays are included in the denominator—these seemingly trivial boundaries are precisely the root cause of numbers losing comparability three months later. Submit the definitions document for team review so everyone shares the same understanding of each number.

For collection methods, automate whatever can be automated: eval metrics should be produced directly from eval scripts to avoid errors introduced by manual transcription; response times should be pulled from ticket records rather than recalled from memory. For what cannot yet be automated, use the simplest possible spreadsheet to standardize manual reporting—prefer rough but continuous data, because continuous rough data is far more useful than intermittent precise data. One easily overlooked step: reusing metric data on the customer side. Showing customers the trend of the eval results you maintain together during quarterly reviews is both a gesture of transparency and an asset of trust, which is especially valuable in the domestic toB context where acceptance and renewal set the rhythm.

Metric pitfalls: vanity metrics and Goodhart's Law

There are two classic pitfalls on the road to building metrics. The first is vanity metrics: numbers that only go up, look good, but carry no decision-making information. Typical examples include "cumulative delivered requirements"—it only increases, and cannot distinguish ten small changes from one critical feature; "tickets closed"—a fast close rate may mean there are too many problems, or that difficult tickets are simply being closed out; "lines of code" contains almost no quality information at all. The litmus test for identifying a vanity metric is to ask: if this number dropped, what different decision would I make? If you can't answer, it's a vanity metric.

The second pitfall runs deeper: Goodhart's Law—when a metric becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure. Once the team is assessed on task success rate, someone will move hard tasks out of the eval set or loosen the criteria for failed tasks; once response time is assessed, it breeds perfunctory responses that "pick up instantly but never solve." There are three countermeasures. First, paired metrics: success rate must be paired with attribution records of failed samples, and response time must be paired with resolution time, so that optimizations that sacrifice one thing for another become immediately visible. Second, sampling audits: the Lead should regularly review a sample of "successful" tasks to confirm the criteria haven't been quietly loosened. Third, metric rotation and scenario protection: eval tasks for core scenarios are locked separately and may not be removed from the eval set because they "keep failing." The designer of a measurement system should assume by default that it will be gamed—this is a design premise, not an accident.

Metric review cadence: getting numbers into decisions

The greatest waste of metrics is being collected and then never used. A three-tier review cadence is recommended. Weekly: look at trends and anomalies; no meeting needed—the Lead glances at the curves, and when an anomaly appears, asks the person involved, with the goal of catching and handling issues within the same week. Monthly: review against baselines and link metric changes to specific actions—did the success rate improve this month because retrieval logic was fixed, or because standards were loosened? The output of a monthly review should be conclusions and actions, not a report that merely lists numbers. Quarterly: calibrate the metrics themselves by asking three questions: which metrics influenced no decisions this quarter (consider cutting them), which newly emerging problems need new metrics (consider adding them), and are there signs the metric definitions have been gamed (tighten the criteria). The metric system itself must evolve; a rigid metric will degrade into a new vanity metric within six months.

There is a practical technique for review meetings: every metric must be followed by "so here is what we've decided to do." Metric discussions without decisions are theater. Conversely, if you find yourself looking at metrics for two consecutive months without taking any action, it means these metrics don't cover the problems you're actually wrestling with—what needs adjusting is the metrics, not the process. The mark of a mature measurement system is that opening the report tells you what to do next, rather than looking at the report first and then wondering what it's for.

Summary and next steps

To recap: delivery measurement starts with structuring subjective evaluations, with the business, technical, and asset layers built separately; for Agent projects, focus on task success rate, cost, latency, and stability, with definitions defined first; for delivery health, look at acceptance pass rate, defect escape, and response time, using baseline comparisons instead of absolute thresholds; identify vanity metrics by asking "what would you do if it dropped," and guard against Goodhart's Law with paired metrics, sampling audits, and scenario protection; finally, get the numbers into weekly, monthly, and quarterly reviews, where every metric can answer "so what do we do."

Recommended next steps: first, write down the definitions, criteria, and collection methods for three metrics in your current project, and record them for two weeks to establish a baseline; to go deeper into building eval sets, read Agent Topics; to learn how metric results feed back into product and model iteration, read The FDE Workflow in the AI Era; if you are designing an assessment system for your team, be sure to read FDE Team Design before setting weights—if the assessment metrics are wrong, all the measurement work before it will become an object of gaming.
