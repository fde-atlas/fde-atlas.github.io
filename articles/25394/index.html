How to Define Requirements for FDE: 5 Steps to Decide on the Spot and Common Pitfalls

Original title: FDEの要件定義の進め方は？現場で決め切る5ステップと落とし穴

FDE総研 · 2026-09-03 · 11 min read

Summary

This FDE Soken article argues that requirements definition for Forward Deployed Engineers differs fundamentally from traditional SI practice: instead of producing specification documents, FDEs identify which customer decisions are slow, error-prone, or person-dependent, then validate hypotheses with working prototypes on real data. It proposes a five-step process—on-site observation, decision identification, hands-on data inspection, prototyping, and operational handover—with roughly 60% of effort going to data inspection and prototyping. Requirements should be mapped to Palantir-style ontology elements (objects, properties, links, actions), and data freshness, missingness, uniqueness, and permissions must be verified directly. Common pitfalls include accepting all requests, isolated PoCs, absent decision-makers, optimistic data assumptions, and missing handover plans.

Key points

  • FDE requirements work targets changing business decisions, not writing specs.
  • Five steps: observation, decision identification, data inspection, prototyping, handover.
  • Spend over 60% of time on data inspection and prototyping.
  • Map requirements to ontology: objects, properties, links, actions.
  • Key pitfalls: scope bloat, isolated PoCs, absent decision-makers, data optimism.

How does FDE requirements definition differ from conventional requirements definition?

FDE requirements definition differs in that it is not the work of producing a specification document, but of building hypotheses that work in the customer's actual operations and driving them all the way through validation.

Forward Deployed Engineer (FDE) is a role popularized by Palantir along with its deployment model, in which engineers embed themselves at the customer's site and take responsibility all the way to transforming the business itself. That is why requirements definition is not a process of "transcribing everything the customer says," but a process of "identifying decision-making bottlenecks the customer has not yet articulated and demonstrating them with something that works."

The goal is not "solidifying specifications" but "changing business decision-making"

What an FDE defines first is not a feature list, but a definition of outcomes: whose decisions, and how much faster and more accurately.

In conventional requirements definition, you first draw a business flow diagram, break it down into screen and feature lists, and obtain approval. With FDEs, this order tends to be reversed. The first question asked is "in this business, who decides, when, and based on what," and once you identify the points where decisions are slow, inaccurate, or dependent on specific individuals, the skeleton of the requirements is essentially settled.

From a practical standpoint, the longer a list of feature requests gets, the less value it holds. Conversely, if you can establish a single statement like "enable the 8 a.m. dispatch decision to be made with the same accuracy even by staff without ten years of experience," subsequent design decisions fall into place almost automatically.

The deliverable is not a document, but a working workflow and ontology

The outputs of requirements definition are not a thick specification document, but two things: a prototype running on real data and agreed-upon operational rules.

In FDE practice, the end of the requirements definition phase often features not "a document to read and approve" but "a screen to touch and approve." Requirements agreed in writing drift in interpretation, but in front of a screen running real data, the drift is exposed on the spot.

Main questionWhat to build (What)Which decisions to change (Why/Who)

The right column of this table is close to what is called product management. However, unlike a PdM, an FDE writes the data pipelines themselves and goes as far as permission design. You can consider the fact that requirements definition and prototyping are not separated as the biggest structural difference.

How should FDE requirements definition be run? How do you structure the basic steps?

FDE requirements definition holds together best when structured in five steps: field observation, identifying decisions, data verification, prototyping, and operational handover.

Overview of the five steps (from field observation to operational handover)

The order of the five steps is where the value lies; starting prototyping before data verification is the most likely to cause major rework.

  • Field observation: Go to where the work actually happens, not a conference room, and watch the actual work firsthand.
  • Identifying decisions: Extract the "moments of decision-making" from your observations and organize them by frequency, time required, and cost of errors.
  • Data verification: Open the systems yourself and confirm where, at what granularity, and how fresh the information needed for those decisions actually exists.
  • Prototyping: Build the minimal screen or workflow that supports the decision and have field staff try it.
  • Operational handover: Decide who updates it and who fixes it when it breaks, and document where responsibility lies.
StepCentral questionMain deliverablesRough duration (small projects)
1. Field observationWhat is actually happeningField observation notes, candidate decision listA few days to 1 week
2. Identifying decisionsWhich decision, if changed, has impactDefinition of target decisions (frequency, cost)A few days
3. Data verificationCan that information really be obtainedData inventory sheet, quality notes1 to 2 weeks
4. PrototypingWill the field actually use itPrototype running on real data, usage logs1 to 2 weeks
5. Operational handoverWho will keep it runningOperational rules, definition of permissions and update responsibilitiesA few days to 1 week

These durations are only rough guides for small projects and vary greatly depending on the number of departments involved and the difficulty of data integration. What matters is not absolute values but ratios; in practice, an allocation that puts "60% or more of the total effort on data verification and prototyping" tends to work well.

How to spend your time to "finalize decisions" at each step

The key to finalizing decisions is not reaching conclusions in meetings, but operating so that you bring a working screen to the next meeting.

FDE requirements definition is fast because the decision cycle is short. Rather than building up discussion in weekly regular meetings, you show a prototype the day after shadowing the work and finalize requirements based on the reactions. The very design of reducing "we'll take it back and consider it" is itself the process design of requirements definition.

On the other hand, short cycles are not a panacea. Data access requests and security reviews depend on the customer's internal approval process and won't move at your speed. In practice, the standard approach is to start work that involves waiting time (permissions, accounts, data provision requests) on day one. If you postpone these, the prototyping phase will grind to a complete halt.

How do you interview and structure the customer's business challenges?

Business challenges should not be structured by asking about "pain points," but by structuring them in the order of: the moment of decision, the information used, and the action taken after the decision.

Start questions from the "moment of decision," not the "business process"

The first question should be "What decision did you struggle with most yesterday?"—this yields far more information than abstract problem interviews.

If you ask "What are your challenges?", the answers tend to be generalities already verbalized internally. Answers like "we don't have enough staff" or "our data is scattered" are correct, but as-is they don't translate into design.

Instead, have them reconstruct a specific recent day. "What system did you open first yesterday morning?" "What did you decide looking at that screen?" "When you couldn't decide, who did you ask?" At this level of granularity, realities that never appear in flow diagrams emerge—manual work in Excel, verification by phone, and so on. Most of the value of an FDE going on-site lies here.

Three frameworks for structuring meeting notes (decisions, data, constraints)

If you sort meeting notes into three columns—decisions, data, and constraints—they become a draft ontology design as-is.

  • Decisions: Who decides what, and when. Frequency, and the loss when decided incorrectly.
  • Data: The information used for that decision. Source system, update frequency, reliability.
  • Constraints: Unmovable conditions such as laws, internal regulations, labor rules, and existing system contracts.

A practical tip is to create the constraints column from the start. Constraints that surface later can overturn the design, but if enumerated early, the discussion shifts to a constructive one: "What is the best option under these constraints?" In particular, if personal data handling and permission separation come up late, the prototype will almost entirely need to be rebuilt.

How do you translate requirements into ontology and data design?

Requirements connect directly to data design when broken down into four elements: objects, properties, links, and actions.

In Palantir's product philosophy, an "ontology" defines business concepts (people, equipment, orders, cases, etc.), their relationships, and the operations that can be performed, as a common model across systems. FDE requirements definition ultimately converges on drawing this model. This is based on the approach published in Palantir's official documentation, and similar modeling works effectively even when Palantir products are not used.

Start by writing out the four elements: objects, properties, links, and actions

If you use business terms as object names and verbs as actions, you can converse with the customer in a shared language.

ElementDefinitionHow to extractExample (equipment maintenance)
ObjectA "thing" the business handlesWhat the field calls with nounsEquipment, worker, inspection record
PropertyAttributes of an objectItems looked at when making decisionsOperating status, last inspection date, degradation score
LinkRelationships between objectsExpressions connected by "of"Equipment — is handled by — worker
ActionOperations that can be performedVerbs the field usesFile an inspection, assign a task

What matters here is not to normalize internal terminology arbitrarily. If you unilaterally rename what the field calls "Unit No." to "equipment master," understanding diverges in review sessions. Using business terms as-is ultimately speeds up design consensus.

Include "inspecting the actual data" in requirements definition

Unless you open the actual data within the requirements definition period and visually check for missing values, duplicates, and update frequency, later stages will inevitably break down.

The answer "we have that data" and "it is in a usable state" are two different things. In practice, things like a table you were told updates daily actually updating weekly, or the same piece of equipment appearing in multiple rows in a master due to notation variations, happen routinely.

The minimum to check is the following four points.

  • Freshness: When was it last updated? When does the batch run?
  • Missing values: What is the missing rate for key items used in decisions?
  • Uniqueness: Is the same business entity split across multiple records?
  • Permissions: Can you access the same data at the same granularity in production?

For services handling highly real-time data—for example, in domains like VACAN that handle congestion information—how freshness and missing values are handled directly determines the quality of the user experience. If you decide as early as requirements definition "what the UI will display when data is delayed," rework in later stages decreases.

What are the typical patterns of failure in requirements definition and how do you avoid them?

Most failures come down to three things—taking on every request, an isolated PoC, and the absence of decision-makers—and they can be avoided with early design.

PitfallWarning signsCountermeasure
Taking on every requestThe request list balloons to dozens of items and no agreement on priorities can be reachedNarrow down to a single target decision and explicitly list everything else as "out of scope this time"
Isolated PoCThe prototype is well received but has never touched production data or production permissionsStart production-equivalent data provision and permission requests in the first week
Absent decision-makerThe field is enthusiastic, but the person who decides budget and operations is not presentHave the decision-maker confirm the target decision at Step 2
Data optimismTaking "we have the data" at face valueOpen the actual data yourself and check freshness, missing values, and uniqueness
Missing operations handoverWhen the FDE leaves, no one can update the systemInclude the update owner and the contact path for incidents in the requirements definition deliverables

Sinking under "collecting everyone's requests"

If you collect stakeholders' requests equally, priorities disappear and you end up with a generic tool that speeds up no one's decisions.

The act of collecting requests itself helps build relationships, but taking the collected requests as-is into requirements leads to failure. In practice, the prudent approach is to sort collected requests along a single axis—"does it help the target decision this time?"—and for those that don't, rather than deleting them, visualize them as "candidates for the next phase." By deferring rather than deleting, you can protect the scope while avoiding political friction.

Stalling with "the PoC never leads to production"

Unless you engage with production data and permission design from the PoC stage onward, even a successful PoC will require a rebuild when moving to production.

A prototype built with sample CSV data is fast, but the moment you put it on real production data volumes, update frequency, and access control, the assumptions can fall apart. In particular, permission design is hard to retrofit, so "who can see which rows of which objects" needs to be included in the model at the requirements definition stage.

Decision-makers and the operations owner never appear until the end

Projects that proceed based only on field-level agreement see requirements shift dramatically once the budget and operations stakeholders finally show up.

Because FDEs work so closely with the field, they risk having requirements close around field voices alone. When you define the target decision in Step 2, confirm with the decision-maker whether "making this decision faster and more accurate is worth the investment"; this significantly reduces backtracking later. At the same time, identifying early which department will take over operations determines the success of Step 5.
