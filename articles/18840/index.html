Summary

The author, from Japanese firm ゴムマリ, argues that AI projects ending with zero results usually fail due to structure, not tool quality or staff skill. Three structural causes are identified: goals drifting toward tool adoption, units of work being too large, and no measurement of time saved. Countermeasures: make the business task the subject of approval documents, build up task by task, and record baseline time, volume, and frequency before starting. The common missing piece is a person bridging tools and operations—the FDE (Forward Deployed Engineer) role, now emerging in Japan. Key points

  • AI adoption fails structurally, not because of tools or people.
  • Approval documents should start from hours saved in a specific task.
  • Prove one task works with measured time savings, then expand.
  • Measure baseline time, volume, and frequency before implementation.
  • The missing role is the FDE, bridging tools and frontline work.

AI initiatives stall at the proof-of-concept stage, or tools are deployed but no one uses them—these failures arise from structure, not from the capabilities of the people in charge. From the perspective of an implementer, this article lays out three structural factors—turning goals into tool deployment, units that are too large, and the absence of measurement—along with their paired designs: business-process units, actual measurement, and implementers.

When AI initiatives fail to produce results, the cause lies more in the project's structure than in the capabilities of the people involved or the performance of the tools. The structural factors can be boiled down to three—

  • The goal gets replaced with tool deployment
  • The units are too large
  • There is no design for measuring results

This article lays out each of these structures and their paired designs from the standpoint of someone who actually implements AI into business operations at companies. The intended readers are promotion leads, corporate planning, and IT departments whose AI initiatives have stalled at the proof-of-concept stage (or are about to).

Factor 1—The goal gets replaced with "deployment"

In failed initiatives, the subject of the internal approval request is the tool. "What to deploy" is decided first, and "which processes will save how many hours" is searched for afterward. In this order, the project is considered achieved the moment deployment is complete, and business results become no one's responsibility.

Behind this lies the difficulty of seeing the structure of the AI market. General-purpose platforms, specialized AI products, and on-the-ground implementation are separate layers, and if you lump everything together as "we deployed AI" without distinguishing which layer you are investing in, expectations and reality diverge. I have organized a map of these three layers in Buying a specialized AI tool or implementing on top of a general-purpose platform.

The paired design is to make the business process the subject of the approval request. A plan that starts with "how many hours will this process save" continues to be verified even after deployment.

Factor 2—The units are too large

A large unit like "company-wide AI adoption promotion" fits within the manager's field of view, but it does not function as a unit of execution. Each business process has a different nature, and the form of AI that works also differs.

Plans with large units tend to drift into indirect measures such as developing guidelines or training, without ever deciding on the first concrete step.

The paired design is an accumulation of business-process units. First make the fact that AI actually runs in one process and time was saved, then expand to the next process. It may seem like a roundabout way, but it is the shortest path. Each of the case studies our company has published started from "a single process."

Factor 3—Not measuring the time saved

Projects that do not measure business hours before and after deployment have no means of claiming results.

Because results cannot be demonstrated, the next investment does not get approved, and the initiative stops once the initial enthusiasm wears off—much of the "zero results" includes cases where there were no results not because nothing was achieved, but because there was no design for measuring results.

The paired design is measurement before starting. First record the current time required, volume, and frequency of the target process, then measure with the same yardstick after deployment. It is not flashy, but simply having this changes the deployment discussion from "a clash of impressions" to "a confirmation of numbers."

A common vacancy across all three—the person who implements on the ground

What the three structural factors have in common is the absence of someone to bridge the gap between tools and business processes.

Breaking down the process, fitting AI to it, shaping it so the front line can keep using it, and measuring the effect—this role has recently come to be called FDE (Forward Deployed Engineer), and the formation of this category has begun in Japan as well (Japan FDE Market Map 2026). Whether to place this role in-house or seek it externally is a choice, but a deployment with no one taking on this role is structurally headed for a stall at proof of concept.

The next step

For the AI initiative you are currently planning (or that has stalled), try writing in one sentence "which process will save how many hours." If you cannot write it, that is the first task. FAQ

Frequently Asked Questions

Our AI initiative has stalled at the proof-of-concept stage. What should we fix first?

Start by narrowing down to a single target process and measuring the current time required for that process. Whether the tool used in the proof of concept can be leveraged is a question for later. I have organized the pitfalls specific to the PoC stage and how to avoid them in Why AI PoCs never reach production.

Can we proceed with AI adoption even without engineers in-house?

Yes, you can. I have organized the criteria for deciding when outsourcing implementation in the article In-house or external FDE.

How should we persuade management about AI adoption?

The persuasive material is "actual measurement of a single process." Even for a small process, if you can show the time before and after deployment in numbers, the next investment decision will come faster.
