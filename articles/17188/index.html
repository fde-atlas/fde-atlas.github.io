FDE and Business Ethnography: Learning by Living in the Field and Turning Tacit Knowledge into Product

Original title: FDEとビジネス・エスノグラフィー｜現場に住み込むように学び、暗黙知を製品に変える

TIMEWELL · 2026-09-15 · 13 min read

Summary

In the third article of TIMEWELL's FDE practice series, Hamamoto argues that forward deployed engineers should go beyond observation methods and use ethnography, staying weeks at customer sites to capture what an organization treats as self-evident. He traces the method from Geertz's thick description and Lucy Suchman's research at Xerox PARC to Polanyi's tacit knowledge and Nonaka's knowledge-creation model, framing FDE work as socialization followed by externalization into product. He distinguishes this from resident contracting on three points: the object of study is the company's assumptions rather than its workflows, learnings feed back into the vendor's product as standard features, and engagements have fixed end dates. Practical guidance includes joining morning meetings and month-end closings, three-layer note-taking, building a meaning dictionary, and flagging phrases like normally or at our company. AI helps extract frequent terms, but meaning still requires human judgment.

Key points

  • Ethnography captures why things are taken for granted, not just what people do.
  • Method lineage: Geertz, Suchman at Xerox PARC, Polanyi, Nonaka and Takeuchi.
  • Tacit knowledge transfers only through shared presence and experience.
  • Unlike resident contracting, learnings return to the product and engagements end early.
  • AI drafts dictionaries from notes, but humans must write the meaning layer.

Hello, this is Hamamoto from TIMEWELL Inc.

This is the third installment in the FDE practice series. In the first installment I wrote about the skill of listening, and in the second about the skill of observing. This time, I will discuss the skill of "being present" in the field, which goes one step further.

The observation method captures what a person is doing. However, there are things in the field that you cannot understand even by watching what people do. Why is it that person who speaks first at the morning meeting? Why do documents circulate on paper even though there is an approval screen? Why does the atmosphere change when someone mixes up "allocation" and "reservation"? These are not behaviors; they are the group's "taken-for-granted" norms. The method for capturing these norms is ethnography, which comes from anthropology. In this article, I will cover why ethnography entered corporate workplaces, how FDEs use it, and how it differs from live-in contracting. If you want to check in advance how well your own team can handle the "taken-for-granted" norms of the field, please use the AI literacy diagnostic.

An anthropology method that passed through a copier research lab and arrived at the factory

Ethnography was originally a method by which anthropologists lived among groups in distant lands and described their way of life. In his 1973 work, the anthropologist Clifford Geertz called this form of description "thick description." Even for a single glance, instead of simply writing "he closed his eyelids," you write down what it meant—whether it was a signal, a tease, or an imitation. It is not a record of behavior, but a description of the meaning that behavior carries 1.

One turning point when this method entered companies was Xerox's Palo Alto Research Center in the 1980s. The anthropologist Lucy Suchman observed people using copiers and argued that people do not act according to a pre-made plan, but rather assemble their actions according to the situation at hand. Her 1987 work "Plans and Situated Actions" demonstrated, through observation, the gap between the "plan-following human" that machine design assumes and the actual "human acting within a situation" 2. The procedures designers envision and what people in the field actually do are different. This discovery is also the starting point of the FDE.

In the 2000s, communities formed where people practicing ethnography in companies gathered, and it came to be used in product development and business design. In Japan as well, it is used in manufacturing and service industries to capture the "taken-for-granted" norms of customers and the field. Our engineers began to be conscious of this method because they repeatedly ran into things that the observation method alone could not explain. We saw all the procedures. We built working prototypes. Even so, they were not used. The reason lay outside the procedures, in the company's taken-for-granted norms.

Tacit knowledge. "People know more than they can tell."

The reason ethnography is necessary, in a word, is tacit knowledge. In his 1966 work, the philosopher Michael Polanyi wrote that people know more than they can tell. How to ride a bicycle, how to recognize faces. Knowledge that one can exercise but cannot explain how one does it 3. Most of the work in the field is made up of this kind of knowledge.

In their 1995 work, the management scholars Ikujiro Nonaka and Hirotaka Takeuchi explained the process by which organizations create knowledge in four stages: socialization, externalization, combination, and internalization. The starting point, socialization, is the stage where tacit knowledge is transferred while remaining tacit through shared experience, corresponding to master-apprentice relationships and imitation in the field 4. This is the crucial point. Tacit knowledge does not transfer through interviews in a conference room. It transfers only when you are in the same place and share the same experiences. The reason an FDE sits next to the person in charge, attends the same morning meetings, and experiences the same closing days is socialization.

And the stage after socialization is externalization, that is, converting tacit knowledge into words and diagrams. The FDE's job carries all of this alone. Socialize in the field, externalize it as a dictionary of nouns and verbs, convert it into something that works for combination, and internalize it as the person in charge uses it. While Nonaka's model explains organizational knowledge creation, our company reads it as a description of the process by which an FDE turns the field's tacit knowledge into a product.

A person who served as an FDE at Palantir for eight years wrote that the essence of this role is to deeply understand the business processes of difficult industries and to design software that truly solves problems using that knowledge, explaining the division of labor in which the product team turns what the FDE creates into a product 5. Deeply knowing the industry's business corresponds to socialization, and productization corresponds to externalization and combination. The reason he spent a year visiting an aircraft factory is that tacit knowledge does not transfer in a week.

Differences from the observation method. Thickness, participation, time

What is the difference between the observation method I wrote about last time and ethnography? Here is a table.

PerspectiveObservation method (last time)Ethnography (this time)
QuestionWhat is the person doing?Why is that taken for granted in this group?
DurationA few daysA few weeks
PositionWatching from diagonally behindParticipating in morning meetings and closings
RecordsTime, actions, screens, utterances, sense of oddityThick description. Behavior and the meaning of that behavior
DeliverablesCandidates for something that works, placed the next dayA dictionary of the company's "taken-for-granted" norms and the structure of their meanings

On thickness. The observation method's record ends with "opens the approval screen." Thick description goes further: "Opens the approval screen, but since the approver does not look at the screen, the person in charge prints it out and brings it. The section manager it is brought to treats the stamp on the paper as proof of having 'seen' it. Therefore, on-screen approval is not regarded as approval in this section." You attach the meaning it holds within that company to the same behavior. Once you understand this meaning, you understand why the approval screen is not used even after being fixed. What should be fixed is not the screen, but where the proof of having "seen" it is placed.

On participation. In the observation method you do not interject, and in shadowing you are a shadow. In ethnography, you participate. You attend morning meetings, stay for overtime on closing days, and eat lunch together. Participating increases the observer's influence, but there are things you cannot understand without participating. Who speaks first at the morning meeting, who stays until the end on closing day, who sits with whom at lunch. This is a map of who can stop things and who can move things in that company. When you put something that works into production, without this map, something that is technically correct will be stopped politically.

On time. The observation method ends in a few days. Ethnography takes a few weeks. Month-end closing comes only once a month, and the busy season comes only once a year. Much of the "taken-for-granted" only becomes visible through repetition. Last time, I wrote as a limitation of observation that "you can only see the work of the people observed on the days observed." Ethnography is a method that fills this limitation with time.

An FDE's ethnography is for building a product. How it differs from live-in contracting

Here, let me clarify the difference from contract development. Merely being in the field for a few weeks is no different from on-site system development. There are three differences.

The first difference is the object of study. Contracted business analysis targets "business processes," producing workflow diagrams and requirements documents. An FDE's ethnography targets "the company's taken-for-granted assumptions," producing a dictionary of meanings. A workflow diagram says "approval," but the dictionary of meanings says "in this section, approval means the section chief stamping a paper document." When you build something that works, what you need is the latter.

The second difference is where the deliverables go. In a contracted engagement, the analysis results become the customer's asset, and on the next project you start the analysis from scratch with a different customer. In FDE work, what you learn flows back into your own product's standard features. If three companies in a row turn out to treat "approval as stamping," that is a pattern, and the product gains the ability to configure "a place to store evidence of approval." The next customer no longer needs to relearn the same taken-for-granted assumptions. In a January 2026 essay, a16z cited whether hours decrease at mature customers as a test for distinguishing genuine FDE patterns 6; if the taken-for-granted assumptions learned through ethnography accumulate in the product, hours will decrease. If they don't, it's not ethnography—it's just being embedded on-site.

The third difference is the time boundary. Ethnography takes a few weeks, but it has an end. The FDE organization AWS launched in June 2026 centers its design on leaving the customer able to run on their own at the end 7. We do the same: the on-site period is decided at the start, and within that period we leave behind something that works, a dictionary of meanings, and a state where the person in charge can fix things themselves, and then we leave. For embedded contracting, the longer you stay, the more revenue you book; for an FDE's ethnography, the sooner you can leave, the more learning has flowed back into the product.

The person who led Palantir's commercial business called the FDE the "CEO with zero authority" over the customer's business, and demanded deep involvement in the customer's mission 8. Deep involvement and staying long are different things. You learn the on-site taken-for-granted assumptions in order to be deeply involved, and once you've learned them, you return them to the product and leave. This sequence is the boundary line with embedded contracting.

On-site procedure: participate, write thickly, build the dictionary of meanings

Let me describe the concrete procedure. This is what our engineers do during a stay of several weeks.

First, decide which gatherings to join: the morning meeting, the weekly meeting, the month-end close, and lunch. You don't need to attend everything, but the morning meeting and the close are non-negotiable. At the morning meeting, you observe the order in which people speak and who nods at whose remarks. At the close, procedures not written in any manual appear most often. Lunch should be a place for small talk, not questions. "Actually, nobody ever looks at that report" only comes out at lunch.

Next, write in three layers. The first layer is scribbled notes on-site, identical to the five-column observation format. The second layer is field notes written that evening, adding context to the scribbles. To "opened the approval screen but didn't use it," you add "the approver said they don't look at the screen; the neighboring section runs the same way." The third layer is a memo written at the weekend, laying out the "taken-for-granted assumptions" seen that week: "In this section, evidence of approval is a paper stamp." The third layer corresponds to thick description.

Then you build the dictionary of meanings. You write down the company's nouns and verbs, together with their meanings within that company, without translating them into system terminology. "Allocation: assigning inventory to a specific order. Distinct from reservation; reservation refers to a state where sales has verbally held something. Reservations without allocation cause problems at month-end." You start collecting this dictionary in the first interview, verify it in the second observation, and thicken the meanings through the ethnography. It then becomes the prototype of the structure of meanings that AI can traverse, which we will cover in subsequent articles.

There is one thing we are careful about when writing: marking the "taken-for-granted assumptions." When the person in charge says "normally," "usually," or "in our shop," you mark the words that follow. After these three phrases comes the company's taken-for-granted assumption. "Normally, the section chief looks at it before passing it to the department manager." This single sentence contains the order of approval, the section chief's role, and the fact that the department manager doesn't look at details.

Our practice and its limits

Let me be honest. We are a small company, and placing an engineer on a single site for several weeks is not a decision we take lightly. The reason we do it anyway is that we have seen, time and again, things built without learning the taken-for-granted assumptions go unused even when technically correct. We saw every procedure. We delivered something that works. The person in charge said "looks good." And yet the following month, that screen was never opened. Tracing the reason led back to the company's taken-for-granted assumptions.

Let me also state the limits. In a few weeks, you cannot observe the once-a-year busy season. The assumptions you cannot observe, you ask about in the event-based interviews described in the first article: "During last year's busy season, what was the most troublesome issue?" Also, participation strongly influences what is observed. If an outsider attends the morning meeting, the morning meeting changes. We assume things will change, write down both before and after, and use the difference to separate "assumptions being performed" from "real assumptions." Regarding confidentiality and consent, as written last time, we decide up front whose what, when, for what purpose, and how the records will be handled. Because ethnography goes deeper than observation, these commitments carry even more weight.

Let me also note how we use AI. Of the three layers of notes, we feed the first-layer scribbles and second-layer field notes to AI and have it extract frequently occurring nouns and verbs, and the words that follow "normally" and "in our shop." Machines are faster at drafting the dictionary. But the third layer—what a word means in this company—is written by a person. Meaning does not emerge from frequency. That the same word "approval" refers to stamping in one section and to screen operations in another is something only someone who was there can know.

Summary

The observation method captures "what is being done"; ethnography captures "why it is taken for granted." The method came from anthropology, broke down the assumption of the "human who works as planned" at Xerox's research labs, and entered corporate workplaces. Its basis is tacit knowledge. People know more than they can articulate, and that knowledge transfers only by being in the same place. That is why the FDE spends several weeks on-site.

What distinguishes it from embedded contracting is that the object is not business processes but "taken-for-granted assumptions," that the learning returns not to the customer's assets but to the product's standard features, and that there is a time boundary—the sooner you can leave, the better. The procedure is: decide which gatherings to join, write in three layers, build the dictionary of meanings, and mark what follows "normally" and "in our shop."
