Why Japanese Companies Need FDEs Now: The Key to Moving Beyond PoCs

Original title: なぜ今、日本企業にFDEが必要なのか｜PoC脱却の鍵

FDX株式会社 · 2026-04-22 · 11 min read

Summary

This FDX article argues that Japanese generative AI projects stall at the PoC stage: citing MIT NANDA's 2025 report, roughly 95% of investments fail to produce measurable business results. The cause is structural, not technical—business understanding, implementation, and operations design are split across three models (vendors, in-house teams, strategy consultants), each with gaps. The FDE role, designed at Palantir in 2003, covers all three in one person or team. As AI agents now require absorbing tacit business knowledge, OpenAI and Anthropic have formally created FDE positions. The article covers three use cases where FDEs fit, common failure patterns (e.g., treating them like SES staff augmentation), and FDX's Japan-adapted delivery model.

Key points

  • MIT NANDA 2025: ~95% of GenAI investments yield no measurable results.
  • PoC failures stem from fragmented ownership of business understanding, implementation, and operations.
  • Vendors, in-house teams, and consultants each leave gaps; combining them worsens fragmentation.
  • AI agents demand deep business knowledge; OpenAI and Anthropic now hire FDEs.
  • Success requires avoiding SES-style contracts, executive sponsorship, and a planned handover exit.

1. Why does generative AI at Japanese companies "end at the PoC stage"?

The implementation barrier in numbers

  • MIT NANDA "The GenAI Divide: State of AI in Business 2025": About 95% of generative AI investments fail to reach measurable results, and only about 5% delivered value in production
  • METI "DX Report 2.2": Of Japanese companies' DX investments, only around 10% reached "business transformation"
  • Various industry surveys: While more than 70% of companies have conducted generative AI PoCs, fewer than 20% run them in production

In Japan as of 2026, adoption itself has spread. The problem lies beyond that: only a handful of companies have delivered results on the ground.

Four typical reasons PoCs never reach production

  • No design for embedding into business processes A PoC exists to see whether something "might work." But the design for putting it into business processes (who uses it when / how it is operated / fallbacks on failure) is missing.
  • Permissions, auditing, and SLAs are added as an afterthought The information security, permission management, audit logs, and availability requirements needed for production operations get worked out only after the PoC is complete. The cost: 3–6 months of rework.
  • Frontline users cannot use it No matter how much the developers build, frontline staff walk away saying "this isn't it" or "I don't know how to use it." Training, operational documentation, and improvement cycles are not built into the design.
  • "Who will operate it next?" is never decided The vendor that ran the PoC leaves, and internal operational handover is never settled. Six months later, it is abandoned.

What these four have in common is that the technology did not fail. A working product was built. Things stall afterward, where the design of post-implementation processes is missing.

Diagram: The valley of PoC death and the difference FDE involvement makes

A contrast between the traditional pattern, where value plunges in the production phase even after passing the PoC, and the trajectory that reaches production operations when an FDE handles everything end-to-end from business redesign to operational handover.

2. Why does this fragmentation arise? | The structural limits of Japanese companies' three models

The root cause of the valley of PoC death lies in the structure of options for which organizational model Japanese companies use to pursue "AI adoption."

Model A: Vendors (SIers / cloud vendors / SaaS vendors)

Strengths: Technology selection, product implementation, building operational infrastructure. Weaknesses: Shallow understanding of the customer's tacit business knowledge. Skilled at fitting products, but does not go as far as redesigning business processes.

Model B: In-house (internal engineers / DX promotion office)

Strengths: Understanding of the company's own business, long-term commitment. Weaknesses: Engineers find it hard to enter business meetings "with decision-making authority." They can implement, but lack the managerial authority to involve frontline staff.

Model C: Strategy consulting

Strengths: Making it a management agenda item, business redesign, roadmap planning. Weaknesses: Takes no implementation responsibility. Withdraws at the recommendation stage with "the rest is up to you."

Diagram: The gaps in the three models and FDE's coverage

Each of the three models lacks one of "business understanding," "implementation capability," or "operations design." Japanese companies try to compensate by combining the three models, but the combination itself creates fragmentation.

  • Hiring a vendor and a consultant in parallel → ambiguous division of roles, mutual blame-shifting
  • In-house and vendor in parallel → the in-house side cannot keep up, slowing implementation speed
  • Consultant → handover to in-house → the triangle of "implementation by a separate vendor" → slower speed

The FDE fills, with one person or one team, the "intersection of gaps" that each of these three models misses. It is a role designed on that premise.

3. What changed in the AI agent era

An era where "tacit business knowledge" is embedded into the product

Enterprise AI adoption in 2024–2026 differs from earlier SaaS adoption in one respect.

  • The SaaS era (through 2023): Fitting product features to the business ("product → business")
  • The AI agent era (2024 onward): Teaching the AI the tacit knowledge of the business and making it act ("business → product")

AI agents cannot be completed with generic APIs alone. They only work once they are made to understand business procedures, exception handling, stakeholders' roles, and even the semantics of the data. A level of understanding of customer business one step beyond what has been demanded of SaaS vendors becomes a prerequisite.

OpenAI and Anthropic establish FDE roles organizationally

OpenAI and Anthropic explicitly list the Forward Deployed Engineer (Applied AI) role on their careers pages, building systems that send technical talent directly to enterprise customer sites. Both companies position the business value of generative AI as determined not by general-purpose APIs but by embedding it into customer operations, and they practice the FDE model.

The "simultaneous design" of operations × tools × processes is a prerequisite

Implementation in the AI agent era requires designing the following four elements simultaneously.

  • Business processes: Who uses it, when, and for what purpose
  • Tool selection: Foundation models, agent frameworks, data sources
  • Data design: What to train on, what to reference, what to output
  • Operating structure: Who monitors, and who drives improvement

If these four are divided among different people, consistency breaks down at the seams. Because an FDE designs all four elements end-to-end as one person, or one team, consistency is maintained.

4. Three situations where FDEs deliver impact

Not every AI project needs an FDE. In the following three situations, the strengths of an FDE come into play.

Situation 1: Generative AI adoption involving business process redesign

Example: Automating 70% of inquiry-handling operations with AI agents. This is not simply deploying a chatbot. The entire business must be redesigned, including inquiry classification, response flows, escalation criteria, and KPI design. An FDE can carry out this redesign through dialogue on-site with the customer and translate it into implementation.

Situation 2: Integration of existing core systems × AI

Example: Inserting an AI layer into existing systems such as SAP, Salesforce, and Workday. To deliver new value without breaking existing systems, implementation that understands both sides' behavior, data structures, and operational constraints is essential. FDEs excel at hybrid implementation that "respects the existing while overwriting with AI."

Situation 3: Unique operations that SaaS cannot reach

Example: Quality control of specific manufacturing processes, compliance checks at financial institutions, hospital operations. These are areas where generic SaaS cannot deliver business value. A "company-specific AI operations platform" must be built, and FDEs handle this through business understanding × custom implementation.

5. Failure patterns | What happens when FDEs are run as "just resident engineers"

The FDE model is powerful, but it fails when operated incorrectly. Common failure patterns seen in Japanese companies are as follows.

Failure 1: Buying FDEs with the same mindset as SES contracts

If the engagement starts as "dispatch excellent engineers at a monthly rate," the FDE's strengths go to waste. An FDE's role is to step into decision-making on business redesign; it is not a staffing service.

Countermeasure: Include "business KPI improvement" as a success metric at the contract stage. A combination of quasi-delegation plus performance-based compensation is preferable.

Failure 2: Locking FDEs inside the IT department

FDEs need to engage directly with both management and frontline staff. If they are confined within the IT department, they never gain the authority to redesign business processes, and the project becomes a formality.

Countermeasure: Assign the FDE directly under a business unit sponsor (at the division head level). Have the IT department run alongside in a technical advisory role.

Failure 3: No defined timeline for when the FDE will leave

The essence of the FDE model is "Transfer Skills." But at Japanese companies, engagements tend to become "stay forever" requests, which erodes the FDE's motivation to advance internalization.

Countermeasure: Specify "handover completion criteria" and "development outcomes for internalization candidates" at the contract stage. Design the FDE's exit from the very beginning.

6. The FDX approach | Translating the Palantir-style FDE model for Japanese companies

FDX Inc. offers an approach that optimizes the Palantir-style FDE model to fit the organizational structures of Japanese companies.

  • FDX AX Design: Diagnosis and business redesign phase. Engaging both management and frontline staff to finalize the To-Be business model.
  • FDX Integration: Implementation phase. The FDE writes code on-site at the customer while mentoring one or two internalization candidates.
  • FDX Training: Development and handover in the internalization phase.
  • FDX BPO / SPO: Operational bridge (temporary operations outsourcing until handover is complete).
  • FDX Expert: Assignment of specialists in specific industries or business domains.

Systematized as an implementation pattern under the principle: "Don't stop at the vision—deliver a mechanism that works on the ground."

Frequently asked questions (FAQ)

Q1. Can an FDE be brought in later to an existing project that is stuck at the PoC stage?

A. Yes. In fact, "we got stuck after completing the PoC and reached out for help" is the most common case. However, if the PoC's design philosophy differs significantly from the FDE approach, it is preferable to start with a baseline re-diagnosis. The diagnosis phase redefines the gap between the current state and the To-Be state, after which the implementation plan is rebuilt.

Q2. Isn't an FDE more expensive than leaving generative AI adoption to a vendor?

A. On a unit-price comparison, FDEs often look more expensive. However, when compared on full cost—vendor adoption → poor business fit → additional customization → failed operational handover → reinvestment six months later—the FDE model is cheaper in most cases over an 18–24 month TCO (Total Cost of Ownership).

Q3. Is there any point in bringing in an FDE even if we already have in-house engineers?

A. Yes. In fact, a "hybrid FDE" that runs alongside in-house engineers tends to produce the best results. The FDE leads business redesign and advanced implementation, while in-house engineers work alongside them and accumulate knowledge. The ideal model is for the FDE to step back after six months to a year, with in-house talent becoming the operational leaders. See External FDE Utilization vs In-house Development for details.

Q4. Does the FDE model also work for business transformation beyond AI agents?

A. Yes. The FDE model originally emerged from pre-AI data integration projects (Palantir Foundry). It can be applied to any domain requiring business understanding × technical implementation × operational handover, such as BPR, SaaS adoption, and data platform construction. AI is simply the FDE's latest main battleground.

Q5. What preparations should be made before introducing an FDE?

A. The following three: (1) Alignment with management: since FDEs come with authority at the executive agenda level, top-down approval is required, (2) Narrowing down target business processes: clarify the challenges of specific business areas rather than pursuing "DX for its own sake," (3) Selecting candidates for internalization: without a designated recipient of Transfer Skills, there is no exit plan for when the FDE leaves.

Q6. Are there industries or business types that are more or less suited to FDEs?

A. There is no clear suitability by industry, but industries with the following characteristics benefit greatly from FDEs: (1) complex, non-standardized business processes (manufacturing floor operations, healthcare, financial back offices, etc.), (2) high dependence on legacy core systems, (3) heavy operational design due to regulatory requirements (finance, healthcare, government). Conversely, in industries where operations are almost fully standardized and completed with SaaS (pure e-commerce and some service businesses), the FDE's advantage is weaker.

Summary

  • Japanese companies' generative AI investment suffers from a structural problem: over 70% of projects stall at the PoC stage
  • The cause is not a lack of technical capability. The ability to understand business, implement solutions, and design operations is fragmented across three models: vendors, in-house development, and consulting
  • In the AI agent era, capturing tacit business knowledge has become a prerequisite, and the cost of fragmentation is rapidly growing
  • The FDE (Forward Deployed Engineer) is a role designed to fill these three gaps with a single person, or a single team
  • If you use one, do not confuse it with an SES contract; assign it directly under executive leadership and design the handover exit from the start. This determines success or failure
