What Is an FDE Career Path? Explaining the 3 Types, the Order of Progression, Next Roles, and Common Sticking Points

Original title: FDEのキャリアパスとは？3つの型と進む順序・次の職種・詰まる原因を解説

FDE総研 · 2026-08-12 · 13 min read

Summary

This article from FDE总研 maps out career paths for Forward Deployed Engineers into three types: deepening expertise in an industry and product, scaling through team building and repeatable delivery methods, or transitioning into product and business roles. It argues these are parallel options, not a hierarchy, and that the natural sequence is running projects, then templating your approach, then extending it to others—skipping steps backfires. Citing Anthropic's job postings, it stresses agency and discovery skills. Palantir alumni founding Addepar and Anduril illustrate the entrepreneurial route. The article identifies three stagnation causes: becoming a handyman, lacking articulable repeatability, and being evaluated on output volume rather than customer outcomes, recommending project inventories and semiannual role experiments.

Key points

  • FDE careers split into three types: deepening, scaling, and transitioning—none superior.
  • Recommended sequence: run projects, template your method, then extend to others.
  • Anthropic's FDE postings require high agency and discovery communication skills.
  • Stagnation causes: handyman drift, no repeatable method, output-based evaluation.
  • Suggested first step: inventory recent projects and test a new role every six months.

What types of career paths exist for FDEs?

FDE career paths can be organized into three types: the deepening type, which builds deep expertise; the expanding type, which builds organizations; and the pivoting type, which moves to the business side. None is superior to the others. They are parallel options that diverge based on how you stack up projects and your own interests.

An FDE (Forward Deployed Engineer) is a role that embeds deeply in customer sites. You handle everything from workflow design to implementation and testing yourself (Palantir official blog). The experience of owning a problem from definition through implementation to adoption builds a composite skill set that is hard to acquire in other roles. Where you invest that asset determines your next step as an FDE. The role itself is outlined in What is an FDE.

Deepening typeIndustry domain + product expertiseSenior FDE / Lead FDE, Solution ArchitectPeople who want to keep going deeper in a specific area
Expanding typeA repeatable pattern for running projectsFDE Team Lead, Delivery LeadPeople who want to multiply results through people and systems
Pivoting typeCustomer understanding gained in the fieldProduct Manager, Business Development, EntrepreneurshipPeople who want to move to the side that decides "what to build"

Deepening type: deepening expertise as an FDE

The deepening type turns expertise in a specific industry domain and product into an asset. You keep the same role while raising the difficulty and scale of projects you can handle.

After stacking three or four projects in the same industry, problem analysis that took weeks the first time can be done in days. Industry-specific constraints, decision-making sequences, and data structures become patterns in your head. At this stage, an FDE transforms from "an engineer who can implement" to "an engineer who can solve that industry's problems by the shortest path." People who grow fastest on this path tend to document their industry knowledge after every project. What they record isn't deliverables, but industry assumptions and exceptions.

Expanding type: building teams and organizations

The expanding type turns your own project approach into a repeatable pattern and extends it to FDE team hiring, development, and systemization. Your evaluation axis shifts from your own workload to the total output of the whole team.

FDE results tend to be person-dependent, creating a structural risk that projects stall when one strong performer leaves. The expanding type confronts this risk head-on. You turn project kickoff procedures, customer alignment methods, and deliverable handoffs into templates so that even new FDEs can run projects at a certain standard. The likely first branching point is the timing of onboarding the second or third FDE. Whether you keep carrying everything yourself or build a system to delegate determines your subsequent role.

Pivoting type: moving to the product or business side

The pivoting type leverages the customer understanding and implementation experience gained in the field to move to the side that builds products and businesses. Your perspective shifts from optimizing for one company at a time to optimizing for the whole.

Because FDEs see across multiple customer sites, they occupy a rare position from which to judge "is this problem unique to one company, or industry-wide?" When you're convinced a problem is industry-wide, the pivoting type means moving to the side that provides solutions as a product rather than as individual projects. It is widely known that Palantir's co-founders and early members went on to help found companies like Addepar and Anduril. These are examples showing how well deep field experience pairs with starting a business.

In what order does an FDE career path progress?

The basic order of an FDE career path is "run projects → turn your approach into a pattern → spread it to others." Which of the three types you pursue naturally emerges in the latter half of this flow.

Skipping a stage means having to come back to it later. It's especially dangerous to move into team building without enough experience running projects. You end up creating templates with nothing worth templating, and no one in the field uses them. Conversely, even if you spend a long time running projects, without records of your decisions, nothing carries over as material for the next stage.

What to build in your first project

The top priority in your first project is the experience of redefining the customer's problem in your own words and fully delivering something that works. The dividing line is not technical difficulty, but whether you delivered completely.

Anthropic's FDE job posting lists two capabilities as requirements, in addition to strong implementation skills in Python: high agency—taking initiative even amid ambiguity—and communication skills for discovery with customers (Anthropic official job posting). Your first project is the first opportunity to use both simultaneously. If you simply implement what the customer first asks for and stop there, this experience doesn't accumulate. Consciously pausing to restate "where is the customer really struggling?" in your own words changes how much you grow afterward. The full picture of required skills is outlined in Skills needed for FDEs.

How does the role change from the second project onward?

From the second project onward, the weight shifts from implementation toward project kickoffs and customer decision-making. Hands-on time decreases, but the weight of your judgment increases.

Once you get a feel for delivering something that works on your first project, you can start reading the quality of a project's approach to some degree before you even begin the second one. As a result, you start being called in before the requirements are even finalized—at a stage where the customer hasn't yet decided what to build. What's required here is the ability to move the customer's decision-making forward. That doesn't mean implementation skills become unnecessary. Precisely because you can see how the implementation would go, you can answer immediately—"that can be done in three days" or "that will take three months"—and speed up decision-making.

When do the types diverge?

The types diverge when you become able to hand off your way of working to others and your attention turns beyond individual projects. Which direction you go is determined by what you become interested in at that point.

Once you can hand things off, you gain spare time. How you use that spare time determines which type you move toward.

Use of spare timeType you move toward
The next project in the same industryDeepening type
Developing the next FDEExpanding type
Solving problems common to multiple projectsTransitioning type

This branching isn't decided by anyone else. It often becomes clear after the fact, as you choose how to use your spare time several times. Even just choosing consciously will make your path considerably clearer.

What roles can you move on to from FDE?

After FDE, there are paths such as solution architect, product manager, and entrepreneurship. What they have in common is that they are roles premised on both customer understanding and implementation skills.

Options for going the technical route

On the technical side, moving to solution architect or platform engineer is realistic. You generalize the problems that repeatedly came up in projects into designs or infrastructure.

A solution architect designs system configurations for each customer. Because during their FDE days they experienced firsthand the failure of "this design won't work in actual operations," they can produce designs that don't stay on paper. There is also the path of moving to the platform side. If you've written the same implementation three times across multiple projects, that's a feature that belongs on the platform. Only an FDE who has worked across the field can make that judgment.

Options for going the business / customer route

On the business side, customer-facing roles such as product manager or customer success lead are a good fit. FDE experience pays off when deciding what not to build.

The hard part of being a product manager is choosing which requests to accept and which to reject. FDEs have seen many times how features customers said they "wanted" ended up never being used. As a result, they develop the habit of questioning the actual business reality behind a request. This cannot be learned from documents. The differences between FDE and PM are compared in The difference between FDE and product manager. Note that at Japanese offices of global companies, English tends to be a prerequisite even in this area, and OpenAI's Tokyo FDE job posting requires Japanese-English bilingual skills.

The option of entrepreneurship / going independent

Entrepreneurship is a path that pairs well with FDE experience, as shown by the multiple companies founded by Palantir alumni. Having firsthand knowledge of customer problems becomes your greatest initial asset.

Most startups stumble at the stage of deciding which problem to solve. FDEs have directly verified, at multiple customers' sites, which problems customers are willing to pay to solve. Furthermore, they can build the first product themselves and deliver it to the first customers themselves. However, the ability to make an individual project succeed and the ability to build a product that sells to many customers are different things. It's important not to skip the process of verifying whether "what you built for one company can be sold as-is to ten."

What causes FDE career paths to stall?

Most causes of stalled FDE career paths fall into three categories: becoming a handyman, lack of reproducibility, and misaligned evaluation criteria. All of these are problems arising from how you are positioned rather than from your own ability.

Cause of stallingWarning signsCountermeasures
Becoming a handymanRequests lack consistency, and you can't articulate a through-line in your resumeBring an industry or theme axis into project selection
Lack of reproducibilityWhen asked why you succeeded, you can only say "because I worked hard"Keep a record of your decisions for each project
Misaligned evaluation criteriaBeing evaluated on hours worked or volume of implementationAgree on customer-side outcome metrics before starting work

Becoming a handyman and failing to build expertise

Becoming a handyman means that, as you handle the requests in front of you, you lose any axis you can speak of as expertise. If you feel busy but not growing, that's a warning sign.

Because FDEs have a wide scope of responsibility, it's a role that easily becomes "the person you can ask for anything" within a company. Each request may be legitimate, but if you spend three years on projects scattered across industries and themes, no consistent through-line remains in your career. To prevent this, proactively communicate your axis when projects are assigned—"I want to keep working in this industry" or "I want to go deeper on this theme." Even if you can't choose every project, if you can deliberately choose one out of every two, the trajectory of your career will change.

Failing to articulate the reproducibility of your projects

Lack of reproducibility means the project succeeded, but you can't explain why it went well. In that state, you can't show the basis for moving on to your next role.

FDE results are sometimes helped by rapport with the customer's staff or by the coincidental availability of good data. If you don't separate what came from skill and what came from luck, your successes become a pile of coincidences that can't be handed down to your team. It's effective to keep notes on the decisions you made during each project as they happen. If you record "why you built that feature first" and "why you declined that request," you can later reconstruct them as a reproducible methodology. If you're heading toward the expanding type, this record becomes an asset in itself.

Evaluation criteria skewing toward implementation volume

Misaligned evaluation criteria means being evaluated on implementation volume or hours worked, making your contribution to customer outcomes hard to see. This is the form in which an FDE's value is most likely to be underestimated.

The essential contribution of an FDE is identifying what doesn't need to be built and carrying the work through until the customer's operations actually change. Yet in evaluations, easily measurable metrics like feature counts or working hours tend to be used. Before starting a project, agree on the customer-side outcome metrics. If you decide in advance whether it's reducing processing time or improving decision accuracy, you can explain your results in your own words. This habit connects not only to internal evaluations but also to how you present yourself when changing jobs.

What is the first step in mapping out an FDE career path?

The first step in mapping out an FDE career path is to take inventory of your most recent projects and put into words which of the three types your way of working most resembles. This is more reliable than working backward from an ideal image.

Take inventory from your current projects

In the inventory, for each project you handled, write down where you created value: problem definition, implementation, or adoption. Look not at how you allocated your time, but at how much you contributed to the outcomes.

Listing about three projects will reveal your biases.

Where you created valueClosest type
Problem definitionTransformation type
Implementation and designDeepening type
Supporting other membersExpansion type

This exercise also serves as a draft for your resume and as material for negotiating your role within the company. As the editorial team at VACAN Technologies tracking FDE-like roles in Japan, we've observed that people who are uncertain about their career direction tend to lack material for reflecting on their own projects.

Verify in six-month increments

Verification in six-month increments is realistic. In your next project, shift your role by one step and see how it feels. You can try this within your company without assuming you'll change jobs.

Type you want to tryWhat to take on in your next project
Deepening typeContinue handling projects in the same industry
Expansion typeVoluntarily take on the onboarding of new members
Transformation typeAsk to join a project from the requirements definition stage

When you actually try it, it's not uncommon for the reality to differ from what you imagined. If you can't make a judgment in six months, another six months is enough. This trial will never be wasted, no matter which type you pursue.
