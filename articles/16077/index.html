A Day in the Life of an FDE: A Full Picture of the Daily Work of an On-Site Delivery Engineer

Original title: FDE 的一天：驻场交付工程师的日常工作全景

FDE中国 · 2026-09-07 · 9 min read

Summary

This article maps out a typical day of a Forward Deployed Engineer (FDE) during the delivery phase: mornings go to short client syncs and on-site observation, afternoons are protected for coding and evals, and incoming issues are triaged throughout the day. The author stresses there is no universal schedule—priorities shift with project stage and client type, and in China on-site presence is more common. Key disciplines include converting verbal information into reproducible written records for cross-timezone work, maintaining dual communication lines to client and internal teams, and reserving Friday for knowledge consolidation. A self-check for aspiring FDEs: measure how much of your day is truly continuous coding, and whether interruptions feel like cost or value.

Key points

  • Core rhythm: clients in the morning, engineering in the afternoon, triage all day.
  • No standard schedule; workload depends on project stage and client type.
  • Cross-timezone rule: anything spoken or chatted is assumed lost—write it down.
  • Communication runs on two lines: client-facing and internal feedback.
  • Fix a weekly slot for consolidation to prevent delivery collapse when people leave.

Before deciding whether to take the FDE path, what most people really want to ask is not salary or title, but a simpler question: what does a day in this role actually look like? If you have already read the site's article on the Forward Deployed Engineer (FDE) role definition, you know it is "a role that does engineering while embedded in the client's context," but a definition cannot answer the question of rhythm. This piece breaks down the FDE's daily routine along a single day's timeline, then stretches it to a week. Whether you are a current practitioner calibrating how you work, or a prospective candidate judging whether this role suits you, you can use it as a time-granularity reference.

First, let's be clear: there is no standard template for an FDE's day

An FDE's focus strictly follows the project phase. During pre-sales and validation, much of your time goes to requirement clarification, solution demos, and rapid validation, with development in short sprints; once formal delivery begins, development and evals become the main axis, and client meetings recede into fixed sync points; in the operations and scaling phase, more of your time goes to incident response, version iteration, and knowledge consolidation. For the full phase breakdown, see the article on the FDE project lifecycle; the "typical day" described below assumes the delivery phase, and other phases will deviate significantly.

Another variable is the client type. What is commonly observed in domestic toB projects: large clients in finance and manufacturing have a high proportion of on-site work, so your day is embedded in the client's schedule and security rules; internet-sector clients are more accepting of remote and hybrid modes, giving you more autonomy over your schedule. So two FDEs at the same company may have completely different days—this is not an anomaly but a characteristic of the role. Judging whether an FDE is doing well is not about the calendar, but whether the main threads in the competency model are advancing.

Morning: client syncs and on-site observation

A delivery-phase FDE's morning usually starts with a short meeting. The sync with the client's point of contact is kept to fifteen to thirty minutes and covers only three things: yesterday's progress, today's plan, and what is blocked. The discipline is to state blockers at an "actionable" level of granularity—not "the model isn't performing well," but "these three scenarios have error rates clearly higher than the others; we need the client to provide fifty labeled samples per category." Run the sync as an information briefing, not a problem discussion; schedule complex issues for dedicated time separately, otherwise thirty minutes is never enough and you drag other attendees into the details.

After the sync comes a period of on-site observation, the most irreplaceable part of the on-site mode. Walk over to real users' desks and watch how they use the system: which entry points they never touch, which steps force them to switch back and forth between tools, which verbal agreements never made it into documentation. These details almost never appear in requirement documents, yet they often determine whether delivery actually lands. Remote FDEs compensate in another way: review operation logs the client has authorized, ask the client to record usage sessions, and hold regular fifteen-minute interviews with frontline users. To emphasize: observation itself is an FDE's work, not slacking off—many key requirements hide in these informal moments.

Beyond observing people, observe the environment too. Can the client's production network reach external dependencies, can data be exported, which approval nodes must be traversed—these constraints determine what technical approach you can take in the afternoon. It's best to map them out early in the project and write them into the delivery documentation, to avoid discovering a compliance wall halfway through building the solution. The earlier the environment reconnaissance, the less rework.

The morning also often involves handling "fragmented but high-priority" matters: issues reported ad hoc by the client. The triage standard is simple: anything blocking the client's usage gets handled immediately; anything with a workaround goes into that afternoon; pure consultations get batched into a fixed Q&A slot. Without this triage step, the day gets shredded by ad hoc issues and the afternoon's deep-work block cannot be protected.

Afternoon: development, evals, and issue consolidation

The afternoon is the FDE's deep-water zone; the goal is to protect two to three hours of continuous time. What you do depends on the phase: during delivery, it's mainly writing integration code, tuning prompts and retrieval logic, and handling data issues. Each change isn't "done" when it feels okay—it has to pass evals: run the task suite and check changes in success rate and failure samples. The eval set is one of the FDE's core assets; the site's Agent topic discusses it systematically, so here we only stress one habit: every real issue reported by the client, as long as it is representative, should be consolidated into an eval case, so you step in the same pit only once.

The other half of the afternoon goes to consolidation: organizing the morning's on-site observations and client feedback into structured records. The client-side issue list, product requirements to relay back internally, technical decisions worth writing into the delivery documentation—each goes in its place. This is done in the afternoon rather than rushed before end of day because it requires judgment, not typing speed—which items are noise and which are patterns often only becomes visible when you review a week's records.

The common wrong rhythm is filling the entire afternoon with development and pushing consolidation and evals to "when there's time." Two weeks later you'll see two consequences: issues recur more often, because similar failures weren't固化 into cases—because similar failures weren't captured as cases; and the client starts questioning progress, because you can't produce comparable records. The hour you saved gets paid back with interest later.

Cross-timezone collaboration: write async clearly

At model companies or teams with globally distributed products, the FDE is often the only person in the client's timezone. Your morning sync is exactly the previous evening at headquarters; the issues you compile before end of day won't be seen until the next morning on their side. The core discipline of cross-timezone collaboration is just one: any information that exists only verbally or in chat logs should be assumed lost.

In practice, three actions. First, write issue reports to the level where "the other party can act without asking another round of questions": full reproduction steps, input/output samples, your initial assessment, and a suggested priority. Second, land important conclusions in documents the same day; treat chat logs as leads, not evidence. Third, agree on fixed overlapping hours for matters that must be discussed in real time, and keep everything else async. A usable self-check: if you took three days off, how much could the person taking over sustain using only the records you left? The closer the answer is to "everything," the better your async quality.

Many people think the hard part of cross-timezone collaboration is late-night meetings. It isn't. Occasional overlapping meetings are unavoidable, but what really drains people is round after round of follow-up questions caused by information that wasn't written clearly. Writing each issue thoroughly in one pass seems slow, but it is actually the fastest path in cross-timezone collaboration.

On-site vs. remote: the real differences between the two modes

The advantage of being on-site is information density. You see what lies outside the system: how the client's organization operates, who makes the decisions, the real emotions of frontline users—things remote work can never capture. The cost is less autonomous time: client meetings, constant requests for help, and security rules all fragment your schedule, and deep work often has to be squeezed into early mornings or evenings. Remote is the opposite: you control your schedule, and documentation is naturally enforced—everything must be written down, so knowledge consolidation ends up more systematic. The costs are information lag and slower trust-building: you can't see the scene, so problems often make a detour on the client's side before reaching you; and relationship-building depends on deliberately designed rituals, such as fixed demo sessions.

A common observation for domestic toB projects is that key customers tend to require a certain proportion of on-site presence, and fully remote delivery remains rare among mid-to-large customers. So the more realistic expectation is a hybrid model—present on-site at key milestones, remote for day-to-day work. Either way, the structure of a day stays the same; what changes is the cost of customer-side communication: on-site relies on walking around, remote relies on documents and video calls.

Communication structure: one line to the customer, one line internally

An FDE's communication is a dual-line structure. On the customer line, your fixed contacts are the customer's interface person and technical lead, with a rhythm of daily syncs plus weekly written progress reports; for higher levels of the customer organization, you usually reach them through milestone reviews, prepared jointly by you and the customer's interface person. The principle is not to report upward directly over the interface person's head, unless an escalation path has been agreed by both sides—if this boundary is not held, trust erodes quickly.

On the internal line, you feed information back to two places. To product and model teams, you relay requirements and defects: with on-site evidence and priority recommendations, not just verbatim quotes from the customer; to your own team, you relay reusable experience: pitfalls encountered, scripts written, delivery boundaries negotiated. Many FDEs neglect the second line, and as a result they become the team's sole carrier of memory—once the project changes hands, everything resets to zero. Structured accumulation of reusable experience is precisely the prerequisite emphasized when discussing knowledge feedback mechanisms in FDE team design.

The shared discipline across both lines is: anything promised to the customer must have a corresponding person and time allocated internally; any internal schedule promised to the customer must be confirmed by you before it goes out. An FDE is a converter between the two lines, not a megaphone—an FDE who acts as a mere megaphone will be disliked by both sides at once.

Weekly rhythm: stagger work of different natures

Zooming out to a weekly scale, a more sustainable arrangement is to stagger work of different natures. Monday is for alignment: customer weekly meetings, internal weekly meetings, and the week's plan; Tuesday through Thursday are protected as deep-work zones, with as few meetings as possible—development, evals, and on-site observation are concentrated in these three days; Friday is for convergence: clearing the issue list, comparing eval results, knowledge accumulation, and preparing materials for next week's customer report.

The key point of this structure is "accumulation has a fixed slot." The most common failure in FDE work is not some unsolved technical problem, but rather that after three months, the customer's environment is full of special configurations only you understand, documentation has drifted from the actual system, and delivery collapses the moment you leave. Fixing accumulation on every Friday is the cheapest anti-collapse measure. It requires no willpower, only a time slot that cannot be repurposed.

If your current week has meetings scattered randomly and a fragmented deep-work zone, don't try to fix everything at once. Start with one thing: agree with the customer's interface person on a fixed daily sync time, and route ad-hoc questions to that slot. This single change alone usually frees up enough contiguous time, and other improvements can be layered on gradually from there.

A calibration exercise for aspiring entrants

If you haven't yet worked as an FDE, you can use an exercise to predict whether you're suited to this rhythm: recall a past day of writing code, and calculate the proportion of time you spent in truly continuous programming. If the answer is below half, and you don't feel anxious about it—perhaps even enjoy the back-and-forth discussions with customers—then an FDE's day is not a drain for you, but a different kind of satisfaction, one closer to the business. Conversely, if you view "being interrupted" as pure cost, proceed with caution—this role's value grows precisely in the space between interruptions and consolidation.

Summary and next steps

To recap: an FDE's day revolves around "customers in the morning, engineering in the afternoon, triage at all times"; cross-timezone collaboration is sustained by asynchronous discipline; communication is a dual-line structure spanning customer and internal sides; a sustainable weekly rhythm depends on whether accumulation has a fixed slot. These are not process perfectionism, but consequences of the role's nature—you are the only person standing on both the customer's site and internal engineering at once, and your time structure is itself part of delivery quality.

As next steps, I suggest going deeper along two lines: to understand the capability requirements behind this daily routine, read the FDE capability model; to see the phase breakdown of a complete project from pre-sales to operations, read the FDE project lifecycle. If you're still hesitating about this career path, first answer the self-check question in this article: can you accept spending a large amount of time on communication, observation, and consolidation, with coding taking up only part of it? If the answer is no, this role may not suit you; if yes, you'll find it is the closest to business value among engineering career tracks.
