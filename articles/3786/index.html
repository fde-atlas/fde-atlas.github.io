Forward Deployed Engineer vs Product Manager | Where the Roles Split

Scaler Topics · 2026-09-10 · 11 min read

Summary

This Scaler Topics article contrasts the forward deployed engineer (FDE) with the product manager (PM). FDEs embed in customer environments to build and integrate products, judged by whether deployments keep working; PMs own what gets built and why, judged by product direction. It also covers the emerging Forward Deployed Product Manager (FDPM) hybrid, which Scale AI is hiring for, though the title is not yet standardized. The piece offers a decision framework for choosing between paths and notes that in India these titles remain rare, concentrated in Bengaluru GCCs and AI startups, advising readers to pursue the underlying work rather than the label.

Key points

  • FDEs build and deploy in customer environments; PMs decide what and why
  • FDE success is a working deployment; PM success is building the right thing
  • FDPM blends both roles; Scale AI hires for it, but definitions vary
  • Enterprise AI deployment difficulty drives the FDPM trend
  • In India the titles are rare; chase the work, not the title

A forward deployed engineer (FDE) sits inside the customer's environment and builds and integrates the product to make it work. A product manager (PM) owns what gets built and why the problem, priorities, and roadmap usually from the product org. The FDE is measured on whether the deployment works; the PM on whether the right thing got built. This page compares both in full and explains the FDPM hybrid emerging between them.

If you're weighing a client-facing career that leans engineering-heavy (FDE) or product-heavy (PM), and you've started hearing about a third thing that blends the two the Forward Deployed Product Manager (FDPM) this is the comparison you need.

What Does a Forward Deployed Engineer Actually Do?

The FDE role was popularised by Palantir: engineers embedded inside the client rather than in the product org. The work is concrete and customer-facing: discovery (mapping the client's messy data and systems), building and integrating (making the product actually work in the client's environment), and staying on to keep the system working after launch.
Success for an FDE is measured by whether the system still works in six months, not whether it shipped on day one. The coding-to-client-work balance varies a lot by company: some FDEs code 70% of the time, others spend 70% of their time in stakeholder meetings and on-site work.

FDEs are increasingly deploying enterprise AI and agentic systems on-site. For what agentic AI systems actually are, that guide covers the kind of AI systems FDEs now build and deploy for customers.

The skill set this role demands strong engineering, system design, cloud deployment, and client-facing communication is exactly what the FDE course for software engineers builds through real deployment projects and structured practice.

What Does a Product Manager Actually Do (In This Context)?

The PM owns the problem and the priorities. Discovery (understanding what the customer actually needs), defining what to build, sequencing the roadmap, and aligning engineering, design, and the customer around a shared direction. In an enterprise AI or deployment setting, the PM is close to the customer's needs but usually one step back from writing the integration code.

The contrast with the FDE is explicit: the PM decides what and why; the FDE makes it work in the client's reality. The PM's success is measured by whether the right product was built; the FDE's by whether it actually runs in the customer's environment.

For how a product manager's career path is structured, that guide covers the full PM track in detail. The PM role in a deployment-heavy company is closer to the customer than a typical product PM, but the core ownership stays the same: the problem, the priorities, and the roadmap.

Where Do the FDE and PM Roles Overlap and Where Do They Split?

Where They Overlap

Both roles are deeply customer-facing. Both live with ambiguity: the customer's problem is never as clean as the spec suggests. Both are measured on outcomes (did the customer succeed?) rather than output (did we ship features?). Both translate between the customer's world and the product org. If you enjoy talking to customers and solving real-world problems, both roles scratch that itch.

Where They Split

The FDE builds and owns the technical reality: the code, the integration, the deployment, the system that runs in the customer's environment. The PM owns the decision about what's worth building: the priorities, the roadmap, the trade-offs between features.

The split is depth of code vs breadth of influence. An FDE goes deep on one customer's technical problem. A PM goes wide across many customers and internal teams to align on direction. An FDE's success is local (this deployment works). A PM's success is strategic (the product is heading the right way).

For the difference between a full-stack developer and a software engineer, that guide shows a parallel example of how adjacent tech roles get conflated. The FDE-vs-PM split has a similar dynamic: the titles sound different, but the day-to-day overlaps more than people expect.

Why the Line Is Blurring

In enterprise AI deployments, the person closest to the customer often needs both to build fast and to decide what matters. When you're deploying an AI system at a client's site, the "what should we build?" and the "how do we make it work here?" questions happen simultaneously, often in the same conversation. That pressure is exactly what produces the FDPM.

What Is a Forward Deployed Product Manager (FDPM)?

An FDPM is a product manager who works embedded with the customer, close to deployment owning the problem and priorities and getting hands-on with how the product actually lands in the client's environment. It's the product-side answer to the same forces that created the FDE.

  • This is a real but still-emerging title. Scale AI has been hiring for Forward Deployed Product Manager roles (check Scale AI's careers page for current openings). Product thought-leaders are actively debating the concept: Adam Vagley's "Forget FDEs, you need FDPMs" framing is gaining traction in product circles. SVPG's perspective on forward deployed engineers provides the authoritative product-org context for why this trend is happening.
  • The honest frame: the FDPM isn't a settled ladder at most companies. Its shape varies: some "FDPM" roles are closer to a technical PM, others closer to an FDE with product authority. The title is real enough to hire for (Scale AI proves that), but not standardised enough to assume every company defines it the same way. Say that plainly.
  • Why it's rising: enterprise AI makes deployment the hard part. Shipping a model is easy; making it work inside a customer's messy, regulated, legacy environment is hard. Someone has to own both the "what" and the "does it actually work." That someone is the FDPM.

For the engineering foundations behind enterprise AI systems, that roadmap covers the technical depth an FDPM needs to be credible at the deployment layer. You don't need to code all day, but you need to understand the systems well enough to make product decisions that survive contact with reality.

FDE vs PM vs FDPM: Which Path Should You Choose?

A simple, honest framework. Not a personality quiz — a decision guide based on what you actually want to do with your days.

Pick FDE if:

You want to stay deeply technical. You enjoy building under real-world constraints: messy data, legacy systems, tight timelines. You like being the person who makes it work, not the person who decides what to build. You're comfortable with travel and client pressure. You'd rather own one customer's technical outcome than influence a product roadmap.

Pick PM if:

You're energised by deciding what to build and why. You prefer influence and alignment over writing code. You want breadth: across customers, across teams, across the business. You're good at saying "not now" and explaining why. You'd rather shape direction than execute deployments.

Aim for FDPM if:

You genuinely want both: enough engineering to build and enough product judgement to own priorities. You're comfortable that the title is still forming, so you'll have to define the role partly yourself. You want to be the person closest to the customer and closest to the product direction. You're okay with ambiguity in the role itself, not just in the work.

The honest caveat: most people lean naturally toward one side. The FDPM suits the rarer person who's strong at both and okay with the fact that the career path isn't mapped yet. If you're choosing, ask yourself: do I get more energy from building something that works, or from deciding what's worth building? That answer tells you which side of the fork to take.
What to ask in any offer conversation for these titles:

  • What percentage of my time is building vs deciding?
  • Who writes my review engineering or product?
  • What's the promotion path from here?
  • How much travel and on-site work is expected?

Do These Roles Exist in India?

The honest picture: the FDE and FDPM titles are still rare in India, concentrated in global companies' India offices (GCCs), a small number of Indian AI startups, and some services firms adapting the label. The roles cluster in Bengaluru, with some presence in Hyderabad, Gurugram, Pune, and Mumbai.

But the underlying work client-facing engineering and deployment-focused product management is common and growing. Indian engineers at GCCs (Google, Microsoft, Amazon) and AI startups (Sarvam AI, Krutrim, and similar) are doing FDE-shaped work under titles like Solutions Engineer, Customer Engineer, Deployment Engineer, and Technical Consultant. Indian PMs at enterprise-SaaS companies are doing FDPM-shaped work under titles like Product Manager (Enterprise), Technical PM, or Solutions PM.

The practical advice for Indian readers: chase the work and the skills, not the exact US title. Verify any company against a live posting before assuming it hires for these roles. The concept matters here; the specific titles are still catching up.

FAQs

What is the difference between a forward deployed engineer and a product manager?

The FDE is measured on whether the deployment works; the PM on whether the right thing got built. The FDE goes deep on one customer's technical problem; the PM goes wide across customers and teams to align on direction.

What is a Forward Deployed Product Manager (FDPM)?

It's a real but still-emerging title. Scale AI has been hiring for it; product thought-leaders are actively debating it. The shape varies by company.

Is an FDPM more technical than a normal PM?

Usually yes. The FDPM role leans toward deployment and hands-on product landing, which requires enough technical fluency to make product decisions that survive contact with real systems. How technical varies by company: some FDPM roles are closer to a technical PM, others closer to an FDE with product authority.

Do forward deployed engineers become product managers?

Some do. The client exposure and deployment experience build product instinct: understanding what customers actually need, not what they say they need. The FDPM is one natural bridge between the two paths. The FDE-to-PM transition is common enough that many companies recognise it as a career track.

Which pays more, FDE or PM?

Both are well paid at top firms. The gap depends on company, level, and location, not the title. At Google, Palantir, and similar companies, FDE and PM roles at the same level tend to have comparable compensation. Don't state a figure without a named, dated, public source. Should I choose the FDE or PM path?

FDE for technical depth and hands-on building. PM for the what-and-why and breadth of influence. FDPM if you genuinely want both and are comfortable that the title is still forming.
