What is a Forward Deployed Engineer? Role and pay

FDE Times · 2026-10-21 · 6 min read

Summary

Using job postings from Palantir and Anthropic, the article argues that the defining feature of a forward deployed engineer is production: writing code that runs inside a customer's real systems, not building demos. It distinguishes FDEs from sales engineers, whose work typically ends at contract signing, and describes Palantir's internal split between Deltas, who do the technical build, and Echoes (Deployment Strategists), who work like product managers. Citing Paraform, it puts FDE base pay at $150,000–$217,000 (midpoint $183,000), and advises candidates to compare base, equity, and bonus separately. It also suggests building a CV around real systems and real users rather than polished demos.

Key points

  • Both Palantir and Anthropic postings center on production applications in customer systems.
  • Deltas build; Echoes (Deployment Strategists) work closer to product managers.
  • FDEs stay past contract signing until systems deliver measurable results.
  • Paraform data: FDE base pay $150,000–$217,000, midpoint $183,000.
  • Candidates should show production experience with real users, not just demos.

In brief

  • Palantir was the first company to have FDEs. Internally, Deltas handle the technical build, while Echoes work closer to a product manager role.
  • Palantir and Anthropic postings differ in context but ask for the same thing: applications running in production inside a customer's real systems.
  • According to Paraform, FDE base pay runs from $150,000 to $217,000. When comparing an offer with this data, set base against base and ask for details on equity and bonus.

Two job postings this week carry the same title, Forward Deployed Engineer. Palantir’s talks about building custom applications, LLM workflows and production solutions designed around the realities of a specific customer.

Anthropic’s asks candidates to work directly inside customers’ systems to build production applications with Claude models, and requires existing experience running LLMs in production.

The two companies are very different, yet both postings return again and again to one word: production. Understand that word and you understand the job, along with what to practice before you apply.

It also helps engineers moving into FDE work avoid two common traps: confusing the role with sales engineering, and comparing pay the wrong way.

Delta is the one who builds

A Substack analysis of the profession identifies Palantir as the first company to have FDEs. Palantir’s job postings describe its approach: send engineers to work on site with customers to solve their most urgent problems.

Inside Palantir these engineers are called Deltas. According to FDE Academy, Deltas work alongside Echoes, or Deployment Strategists. Echoes are close to product managers; Deltas carry primary responsibility for the technical build.

The telling detail is that even where the name was born, the FDE was never the go-between relaying messages from customer to product team. Someone else did that, under a different title. The Delta is the one who sits down and writes the code.

Paraform, a recruiting platform, defines the FDE as a hybrid of software engineering and direct customer deployment. It also pins down what separates an FDE from a consultant: an FDE stays with one customer for the long term and writes production code. The whole profession rests on those two ideas, staying the course and writing code that actually runs.

A day at the customer

Take an FDE at an AI company who has been sent to an insurer. In the morning, a claims handler reports that the LLM workflow built last week cannot read data from the old case-management system.

The FDE does not open a ticket with headquarters and wait. They go into the customer’s system, write the missing integration and test it on a few real claims. In the afternoon, they measure whether the workflow really shortens processing time, because the customer will use that number to decide whether to keep it.

The example is hypothetical, but every step matches real postings. Palantir writes about solutions fitted to the customer’s reality; Anthropic writes about working inside the customer’s systems.

Christian & Timbers, an executive search firm, supplies the rest. In its view, a sales engineer’s job ends when the customer signs, while an FDE’s responsibility runs past the signing, until the system is in production and producing measurable results.

Read a job posting like a spec

One easy method: sort each line of a posting into one of three groups, namely what must be built, where the work happens, and what is required. Sorting the two postings above that way gives the following table.

Part Palantir Anthropic
What must be built Custom applications, LLM workflows, production solutions Production applications with Claude models
Where the work happens On site with customers Inside the customer’s systems
Emphasized requirement Fit to each customer’s reality Experience running LLMs in production

Split out like this, the two postings plainly match in the build column: whatever you make has to run in production for a specific customer. The difference lies in the requirements. Anthropic wants someone who has already taken an LLM to production; Palantir stresses the ability to work within each customer’s particular context.

If you have never taken an LLM to production, do not rule yourself out, but do not apply to postings like Anthropic’s with only a demo either. Build a small workflow, hand it to a real user and keep it running reliably. That is the kind of experience the requirement is looking for.

Pay: what does the number include?

Paraform’s data puts FDE base pay between $150,000 and $217,000, with a midpoint of $183,000. A roundup by Valletta Software shows US FDE base pay varying widely between companies. Palantir alone lists $125,000 to $200,000 base, excluding equity and bonus.

Run the numbers. Say you are offered $165,000 base. That sits inside both Paraform’s range and Palantir’s posted range, so it sounds fine, but it is $18,000 below Paraform’s midpoint.

Your decision depends on what lies outside the base. Palantir’s posted range excludes equity and bonus, so before you answer, ask specifically about both and write base, equity and bonus down as three separate lines.

If equity and bonus are substantial, an $18,000 gap in base may not be worth fighting over. If they are small, $18,000 is a concrete figure to negotiate on, and you have market data to cite.

Anthropic’s posting lists EUR 205k–220k a year. That is a different currency and a different market, so do not convert it and set it beside the US dollar ranges. Ask the recruiter directly what the figure includes.

Why a polished demo is not enough

The most common trap is treating an FDE as a sales engineer who can code. FDE Academy points out that sales engineers usually write little code, mostly for demos or prototypes, while FDEs write integration code that runs in production. A CV with nothing but polished demos is telling the other role’s story. Yours needs real systems and real users.

Another mistake is a CV that only lists technologies. Every line in the two postings above revolves around a specific customer and a system that has to work for them, so a CV should be built on the same axis.

A good CV line usually answers four questions: which team had the problem, who you worked alongside, what you built, and how the system is running now.

To start in your current job, ask to take on a request from an internal team that nobody else wants. Sit with that team’s users for a session, ship the fix to production yourself, then track whether it really helps them. Do that for three months and you will have an interview story that fits the job.

The name Delta may be Palantir’s alone, but the way of working behind it is something anyone can practice: when others treat the signed contract as the finish line, you stay until the code works for the customer.
