Party B: When Is It Worth Running an FDE Business

Original title: 乙方：什么时候值得经营 FDE 业务

pafa.github.io · 2025-06-11 · 5 min read

Summary

This article advises vendors on when building an FDE function is justified. It argues that customer willingness alone is insufficient; the key test is whether a genuine, recurring gap exists between the product and customer work. The author splits vendor commitments into three types—mature product delivery, exploratory co-building, and explicitly billed custom or managed services—and warns against mixing them in one role. A pilot should demonstrate changed customer work, accountable delivery, product decisions on field findings, and explainable costs. Founding an FDE team requires naming a product owner and a business owner. If conditions are unmet, vendors should pause, fix the product, or decline rather than sell unsecured promises. The piece draws on Baseten's experience (Vlad Shulman) but notes the company's self-reported account lacks independently verifiable margin data.

Key points

  • Build FDE only when a real product-customer gap justifies sustained co-building.
  • Classify work as product delivery, exploratory co-building, or paid custom services.
  • Pilots must show changed work, accountability, product decisions, and explainable costs.
  • Name a product owner and business owner before forming an FDE team.
  • Decline or pause when key conditions are missing; don't book unsecured promises.

A customer's willingness to let engineers on site does not mean the vendor should build an FDE team. Some requests require configuring mature products, some require explaining technology, and some require long-term maintenance of a dedicated system. All of these can be valuable business, but they demand different staffing, pricing, and product commitments.

The vendor must first answer: why does this work need to be close to both the customer and the product at the same time? If the answer is merely "it makes deals easier to sign," engineers will likely end up absorbing everything that was not made clear during the sale.

Start from product gaps, not from job-title hype

The earlier chapter Models and delivery changes already compared Baseten's self-serve customers with frontier inference customers. The vendor now needs to carry this distinction into deal intake: is there a gap between the existing product and the customer's work that is worth sustained co-building? This is the basis for that company's choice, and it cannot be turned directly into an organizational mandate for all vendors. 1

A tool company can first examine recent requests that failed to be delivered smoothly. If most problems are hard-to-find documentation, parameters that cannot be configured, or standard interfaces that were not properly integrated, improving product usability and implementation capability may be more direct. Only if different customers repeatedly hit the same capability boundary, and being on site helps discover and validate solutions, is there a reason to make a dedicated investment in sustained co-building.

"Recurring" still does not mean it should be productized immediately. Customers may use the same words while the underlying rules differ completely. At this point, a bounded exploration can be funded; it must not be treated in advance as replicable revenue, let alone have every loss-making contract covered by anticipated replication gains.

First divide business commitments into three types

For ease of management, this book divides the work a vendor might accept into three categories. They are not an industry-standard taxonomy, but a way to prevent different commitments from being mixed into the same role.

Mature product delivery: the customer chooses existing capabilities, and the team is responsible for configuration, integration, and adoption. The core question is how to reliably deliver the agreed scope. If every deal requires intensive on-site exploration, go back and check what went wrong with the product or documentation.

Product-exploration co-building: the customer's problem is not yet well solved by the product, and both parties agree to validate an approach with limited investment. The vendor is responsible both for the work itself and for judging which findings deserve to enter the public product. Delivery budgets and product investment each need their own approver.

Clearly priced custom or managed services: the customer genuinely needs special capabilities and is willing to pay for development, operation, and staffing. This can be a legitimate business that does not need to be packaged as a software product destined to scale later. Its difficulty lies in whether maintenance and service resources can grow as customers are added, and whether profits can cover these obligations.

The three types of work can coexist in one company, but they cannot be renamed at will. For example, sales promises a fixed launch date as a mature product when exploration is actually required; or the product team refuses to maintain a dedicated branch while delivery assumes the company will take it over. Departmental reports may look good for a while, but the real costs are left to on-site staff and the customer.

How can the customer's and the vendor's choices both hold at the same time

Following this book's teaching scenario of an equipment repair company, suppose the vendor's platform can already aggregate materials but cannot preserve the state between a supervisor's confirmation and actual dispatch. This may be a gap in public product capability. The customer needs fewer misdispatches, and the vendor wants to validate whether state capability can serve more workflows, so the two have a reason to explore together.

But if what the customer urgently needs is a special report used only by its internal audit, and the product team judges it will not enter the public version, the situation is different. The vendor can still offer a separately priced extension service, hand it to a capable partner, or clearly decline. It cannot use "customer co-creation" to secure long-term investment on one hand while failing to explain who will maintain that report on the other.

Co-building partners must also receive their own returns: validated work improvements, usable deliverables, necessary handover capability, or explicitly agreed commercial terms. The customer is not a free product testing ground providing data and staff. Conversely, the vendor's investment in the general product should not, absent an agreement, be understood as giving this customer ownership of all the results.

This alignment can be settled in a single kickoff decision: what must be delivered this time, what is validation-only, which materials are returned if validation fails, and who approves and budgets which product improvements. Rights and pricing are covered in Contracts and Pricing: Boundaries for Committed Delivery and for Exploration; here, first decide whether the company is willing to run this kind of commitment.

Take on responsibility in a small scope first, and test whether it can become a business

Baseten's retrospective mentions that two early engineers who worked directly with customers later built the FDE team. 1 This does not imply every company should first send two people, but it offers a testable starting sequence: first see whether existing staff can close the loop, then turn the conditions for success into organizational arrangements.

For the vendor, one complete test should at least show management four things: a customer's work has genuinely changed; someone is accountable when engineering delivery goes wrong; problems brought back from the field receive product decisions; and both current and follow-on costs can be explained. Merely completing a demo, or merely signing a contract, has not yet finished this journey.

When preparing to build a dedicated team, a product receiver and a business owner must be designated at the same time. The product receiver answers which capabilities will be maintained in the public version; the business owner answers how much resources one exploration may consume and when a new decision is required. FDEs can make recommendations, but should not simultaneously approve long-term maintenance on behalf of product, promise prices on behalf of sales, or accept business risk on behalf of the customer.

If the team only keeps patching product defects while the product side has no resources to fix them, co-building will degrade into long-term firefighting. The more sensible move at that point may be to pause expansion, fix the product, and strengthen routine support, rather than hiring more people who can endure the field. If the company chooses a long-term services route, it should also formally account for operations, rotations, and knowledge handover as part of service capability.

Being able to run the business does not mean accepting every customer

Operators can use the same set of questions to examine new opportunities, rather than rushing to score potential customers on a composite scale. Is the customer's problem important and accessible through real work? What exactly is missing from the existing product? Can both sides provide decision-makers and the necessary materials? What evidence in the next phase is most likely to overturn the plan? Who bears the system left behind after delivery ends?

If any one item is missing, it can become a preparation task, such as first confirming data availability. If key conditions cannot be met, "we'll coordinate later" should not be written down as certain revenue. For sales, this removes some seemingly positive commitments; for the team, it avoids selling ungranted permissions, unscheduled product capabilities, and unhired people all at once.

Project Economics: Working Out Both Parties' Numbers will distinguish customer value, project surplus, and product investment, and Project Portfolio: When to Add Headcount, When to Decline the Next Customer will handle multiple projects competing for resources. The business decision here comes earlier: whether the company is willing to treat field learning as work with an owner, a budget, and tolerance for failure, and to take follow-on responsibility for the capabilities it leaves behind.

Only if the answer is yes, and the company can deliver on it, is there a reason to build this team. If mature products and routine services are already sufficient, doing them well is itself a clear business choice.
