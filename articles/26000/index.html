Fractional Forward Deployed Engineer: What You Get, What It Costs, and When It Beats a Full Time Hire

Hayat Amin · Operator · 2026-11-01 · 9 min read

Summary

Hayat Amin, a former technology CFO now offering fractional forward deployed engineering through Beyond Elevation, explains the model: an FDE working one or two days a week who builds AI systems inside a company's existing stack and hands each to a named owner. He compares costs—full-time FDEs median $220,000 with 8-12 week searches and 25% recruiter fees, versus fractional work from $5,800/month—though the daily rate can exceed a full-time hire's. He cites MIT NANDA and S&P Global data on pilot failures, lists when fractional beats full-time (and when it doesn't), and offers a six-step scoping checklist plus vetting advice. The piece is partly promotional for his own services.

Key points

  • Fractional FDEs work one or two days weekly, building AI into existing systems with a planned handover.
  • Full-time FDE median pay is $220,000; searches take 8-12 weeks plus a 25% recruiter fee.
  • Beyond Elevation fractional pricing starts at $5,800/month; fixed projects from $30,000.
  • MIT NANDA found vendor-built AI succeeds about 67% of the time; internal builds far less often.
  • Hire full-time if AI is your product, workflows exceed ten, or projects cost $5-20 million.

A fractional forward deployed engineer works inside your company one or two days a week, builds AI systems into the tools you already run, and hands each one to a named person on your team. You pay for the days, not a $220,000 salary, and you stop when the work is done.

I'm Hayat Amin. I spent twenty years as a technology chief financial officer and sold three companies in that seat, and I now work as a fractional forward deployed engineer myself, so I've looked at this decision from the budget side and from the build side. Below is what the arrangement gets you, what it costs next to a full time hire, when it's the wrong call, and what to do this week before you hire anyone.

The work a fractional forward deployed engineer does

The job is the same as the full time one. You sit with the people doing the work, find the step where the week breaks, and build the fix inside the company's own systems. I explained the full time role in what is a forward deployed engineer. The fractional version changes three things: the hours, the contract and the exit.

The hours are one or two days a week. The contract has no notice period and no equity. The exit is planned from day one, because the point is that your team runs the system after I leave. On my own engagements the timeline is written down before we start. Week 1 maps the process. By week 4 the first system is in production. Week 8 widens it to the next workflow, and week 12 hands it over, with me staying on only if there's more to build.

The demand behind it is real: forward deployed job listings grew about 800% between January and September 2025, and Anthropic launched Ode with Anthropic in July 2026 on top of its purchase of a company called Fractional AI. The cost next to a full time hire

These are the numbers I'd put in front of a board, each from a page I read this morning.

  • Full time FDE, total pay: Paraform puts the Levels.fyi median at $220,000 a year and the 75th percentile at $300,000. Base salaries on Paraform run $150,000 to $217,000.
  • Finding one: Paraform says a full search takes 8 to 12 weeks, and its fee is 25% of first year base, which is $45,000 on a $180,000 salary.
  • Fractional through Beyond Elevation, my firm: from $5,800 a month for one to two days a week, stop any time.
  • A fixed project through Beyond Elevation: from $30,000 for 8 to 14 weeks.
  • A two week audit before either: $3,000 fixed.

My arithmetic: $5,800 a month is $69,600 a year, about a third of the full time median, with no recruiter fee and no 8 to 12 week search. I'll be straight about the day rate, though. At the starting price and two days a week that's about $670 a day. At one day a week it's about $1,340 a day, more than the roughly $880 a day a $220,000 hire costs over 250 working days. You aren't buying cheaper days. You're not paying for the three or four days a week a company your size wouldn't fill.

Signs fractional is the right call

I'd choose fractional when two or more of these are true:

  • You have one to five workflows worth fixing, not twenty.
  • An AI pilot worked in the demo and never reached production. You aren't alone: S&P Global found the average company scrapped 46% of its AI proofs of concept before production, and 42% of companies abandoned most of their AI initiatives, up from 17% a year earlier.
  • Your systems don't talk to each other, and someone on your team retypes data between them every week.
  • The board wants an AI plan and you'd rather show them a working system than a slide.
  • You can't say yet whether there's a full time job here.

The MIT NANDA study, reported by Fortune and drawn from 150 leader interviews, a survey of 350 employees and 300 public AI deployments, found that tools bought from specialist vendors and built through partnerships succeeded about 67% of the time, and internal builds about a third as often. It also found the biggest returns in back office automation, while more than half of AI budgets went to sales and marketing tools. MIT's own reading is that model quality is rarely the problem, because generic tools don't learn from or adapt to a company's workflows. My view, from the finance seat, is that the gap is usually data that wasn't ready and a system nobody on the team was told to own. I wrote up the full pattern in why do my AI pilots never reach production.

Signs you should hire full time instead

If the AI work is your product, hire. A software company that ships AI features to its own customers needs someone in every standup, and two days a week will leave your engineers waiting. The same goes if you have more than about ten workflows queued, or if the systems you're joining change every week. Gartner put the cost of implementing an enterprise generative AI project at $5 million to $20 million, and at that size a full time team, usually with an FDE leading it, is the right shape. The pay for the full time role is in FDE salary.

Six steps to take this week, before you hire anyone

You can do most of the scoping yourself, and it'll make any hire faster. In order:

  • List every place someone copies data from one system to another. Name both systems, for example HubSpot to QuickBooks, or Shopify to a Google Sheet.
  • Next to each, write the hours a week it takes and who does it. Ask them. Don't guess.
  • Pick the one with the most hours and a single clear owner. That's your first system.
  • Check the data before anyone writes code. Export 50 recent records from the source system and count how many are complete and consistent. If fewer than 45 are, fixing the data is the first job, because poor data quality is the first reason Gartner gives for abandoned proofs of concept.
  • Write down the one number that should move, such as hours retyped a week, days to send an invoice, or quotes chased by hand, and its value today.
  • Name the person on your team who will own the system after it's built. If nobody can, stop here and fix that first.

Some companies finish that list and find the fix is a native integration they already pay for. That's a good outcome. Turn it on and keep the money.

If the list shows three systems that won't join, a pilot that never left the demo, or a number the chief executive can't see until month end, the job is bigger than a weekend fix, and that's when you bring in a forward deployed engineer. Vetting a fractional forward deployed engineer

Ask for one system they built that is still running after they left, and ask to speak to the person who owns it now. If all they can show is decks, you're talking to a consultant. I covered the difference in is a forward deployed engineer just a consultant. Ask what goes live in week 4. Ask whether there's a notice period, and walk away from a long lock in. A fractional engineer who is confident in the work doesn't need one.

Questions people actually ask

What is a fractional forward deployed engineer?

A senior engineer who works inside your company part of the week, usually one or two days, on your real systems and data. They map one broken process, build the AI system that fixes it into your own stack, put a named person on your team in charge of it, then move to the next one. You pay for the days, not a salary, and you can stop when the work is done.

What is a fractional AI forward deployed engineer?

The same arrangement aimed at AI work: agents, model calls and the integrations around them, built into the tools you already run. The AI part is usually the smaller job. Most of the days go on joining the systems, cleaning the data the model reads, and making sure a person checks the output before it reaches a customer.

How much does a fractional forward deployed engineer cost?

Through Beyond Elevation, fractional work starts from $5,800 a month for one to two days a week, with no notice period. A fixed project starts from $30,000 over 8 to 14 weeks. For comparison, Paraform puts the Levels.fyi median total pay for a full time FDE at $220,000 a year, with a 25% recruiter fee on base salary if you hire through an agency.

What is a forward deployed engineer hourly rate?

I haven't found a published hourly benchmark I trust, so I won't quote one. I can give you the arithmetic instead. $5,800 a month at two days a week is about $670 a day. At one day a week it's about $1,340 a day. A full time FDE on the $220,000 median, spread over roughly 250 working days, is about $880 a day before payroll tax and benefits.

Is a fractional forward deployed engineer just a consultant?

No, if they're doing the job. A consultant leaves a recommendation. A fractional FDE leaves a working system in your stack with a named owner on your team. Ask any candidate to show you something they built that is still running after they left.
