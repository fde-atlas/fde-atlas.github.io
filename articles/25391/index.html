What Are AI Guardrails? How They Work, Their Types, and Implementation Steps for FDEs in the Field

Original title: AIガードレールとは？仕組み・種類とFDEが現場で実装する手順

FDE総研 · 2026-10-19 · 11 min read

Summary

This Japanese article explains AI guardrails: an external layer that inspects inputs, outputs, tool executions, and operations to stop dangerous or erroneous generative AI behavior before it affects business processes. Citing OWASP Top 10 for LLM Applications 2025 and NIST's AI RMF, it categorizes guardrails into input, output, action, and operational layers, reviews tools like NeMo Guardrails, Llama Guard, and Amazon Bedrock Guardrails, and lays out a six-step implementation process. It argues FDEs are well suited to translating implicit client business rules into guardrails, and warns against over-blocking, relying on system prompts alone, and neglecting post-launch updates.

Key points

  • Guardrails sit outside the model, restricting behavior without altering the LLM itself.
  • Four layers: input, output, action (tool execution), and operational monitoring; the action layer is often overlooked.
  • Implementation follows six steps, starting in log-only mode before enabling blocking.
  • FDEs surface implicit client rules and convert them into permissions, filters, and approval flows.
  • Common failures: over-blocking, prompt-only defenses, and no ongoing maintenance after deployment.

What are AI guardrails?

AI guardrails are mechanisms that inspect inputs to and outputs from generative AI, stopping dangerous content, errors, and unexpected behavior before they reach business operations.

When generative AI was still being tried out in chat interfaces, an incorrect answer could simply be read and discarded by a human. Today's generative AI handles everything from searching internal databases and drafting emails to creating tickets and calling external APIs. Since incorrect output can directly become a business action, the approach of placing a layer of inspection and rules outside the model is becoming the standard.

Definition: An external "safety barrier" placed outside the model

AI guardrails do not modify the model itself; they are an external layer that inserts inspection and rules before and after the model to limit the range of its behavior.

Just as a road guardrail prevents vehicles from falling off without changing their performance, AI guardrails use the LLM's capabilities as-is and only stop deviations. This differs in role from the safety measures taken by model providers during training (such as RLHF).

AspectSafety training on the model sideAI guardrails
Implemented byModel providers (OpenAI, Anthropic, Google, etc.)The deploying company and its engineers
ScopeGeneral harmfulness (violence, illegal activity, etc.)Company-specific business rules, permissions, and confidential information
Ease of changeCannot be changed by usersCan be updated at any time to fit the business
Typical exampleRefusal responses to dangerous requestsMasking personal information, blocking output of confidential documents

In practical terms, model-side safety is like "general common sense," while guardrails are like "this company's rules." The latter does not exist unless the deploying side designs it.

Why AI guardrails are needed now

With LLMs now connected to business systems and tool execution, incorrect output can lead directly to real harm. This makes guardrails an indispensable layer.

The "OWASP Top 10 for LLM Applications 2025" published by OWASP lists prompt injection as LLM01, sensitive information disclosure as LLM02, excessive agency as LLM06, and misinformation as LLM09. None of these can be prevented by model intelligence alone; they are risks that must be addressed through system design.

On the regulatory side, the U.S. NIST published the "AI Risk Management Framework (AI RMF 1.0)" in January 2023 and a generative AI profile (NIST AI 600-1) in July 2024. In Japan, the Ministry of Internal Affairs and Communications and the Ministry of Economy, Trade and Industry published the "AI Guidelines for Business" in April 2024, and on March 31, 2026 released version 1.2, which adds descriptions related to AI agents. The responsibility for managing AI risks on the user side is becoming clear, and guardrails are one concrete implementation method for this.

What types of AI guardrails are there?

Designing AI guardrails across four layers—input, output, actions (tool execution), and operational monitoring—makes it easier to prevent gaps.

Trying to catch everything at a single point either makes inspection heavy or leaves loopholes. Dividing roles by layer is the standard practice in the field.

Four-layer classification

Input inspects user requests, output inspects AI responses, actions inspects operations the AI performs, and operations inspects overall trends.

LayerWhat is inspectedRepresentative countermeasures
Input guardrailsUser requests, ingested documents and web pagesPrompt injection detection, topic scope restrictions, removal of personal information
Output guardrailsResponses generated by the LLMHarmful content filters, detection of confidential information, detection of unsupported assertions, validation of output format (JSON schema)
Action guardrailsTool calls and API executionLeast-privilege permissions, human approval before execution (HITL), limits on amounts and counts
Operational guardrailsOverall logs, usage trendsAudit logs, monitoring of block rates, shutdown procedures for incidents

What FDEs most often overlook in the field is action guardrails. Even when careful attention is paid to the wording of responses, "the range of operations the AI can perform" may remain broader than the person in charge assumes.

While guardrails can be built from scratch, it is common to combine open-source frameworks with cloud managed services.

Since Japanese detection accuracy is hard to judge from public information alone, it is safest to run a small validation with your own data before deciding on adoption.

How do you implement AI guardrails?

First, identify risks and place checks at each layer. Then measure effectiveness with evaluation data, and proceed in order while updating through operation.

6 Steps for Implementation

In guardrail implementation, deciding "what to block" takes up more than half of the work, and writing code comes in the latter half.

  • Inventory of use cases and risks: List the users, the data handled, and the operations the AI can perform, and identify expected risks by referring to resources such as the OWASP Top 10 for LLM.
  • Agreement on blocking criteria: Divide into three levels—"absolutely must not go out," "requires human confirmation," and "acceptable"—and reach agreement with the business owner.
  • Placing countermeasures at each layer: Assign rule-based checks and model-based checks to each of the input, output, action, and operation layers.
  • Creating an evaluation dataset: Prepare examples of legitimate requests that should pass and attacks or deviations that should be blocked, in a form that can be run as automated tests.
  • Phased release: Initially run in a mode that only detects and logs, confirm false positive trends, and then enable blocking.
  • Operation and updates: Periodically review blocked cases and cases that slipped through, and update rules and evaluation data.

If you skip the fifth step of "logging only at first," legitimate business operations may be blocked on release day, and you tend to lose the trust of the field.

Choosing between rule-based and model-based checks

Use rule-based checks for things you want to block reliably, and model-based checks for things requiring contextual judgment, combining both.

MethodSuitable targetsAdvantagesDisadvantages
Rule-based (regex, dictionaries, schemas)My Number and card number formats, output formats, prohibited wordsFast, predictable results, easy to explainWeak against paraphrasing and context
Model-based (classification models, judgment LLMs)Harmfulness, topic deviation, injection intentCan understand contextIncreased latency and cost, inconsistent judgments
Human approval (HITL)Money transfers, external transmission, final answers to customersClear accountabilityStops working as processing volume increases

How to measure effectiveness

Guardrails are evaluated by both the proportion of things that should be blocked that were blocked, and the proportion of things that should pass that were mistakenly blocked.

There are three main metrics to look at: the rate of missing dangerous inputs and outputs (false negatives), the rate of mistakenly blocking legitimate requests (false positives), and the added response time due to checks. Target values vary greatly by business, so there is no uniform standard. You need to set priorities with the business owner—for example, prioritizing low false negatives for work close to financial transactions, and low false positives for internal inquiry handling.

How do FDEs engage with AI guardrails?

FDEs interpret business rules at the customer's site, implement "what to block where" as guardrails, and support operations through to ongoing use.

FDE (Forward Deployed Engineer) is a working style popularized by Palantir as a role that sends engineers to customer sites. Rather than just delivering a product, FDEs get deeply involved in the customer's business and handle implementation. In generative AI adoption, this "getting into the field" nature is a particularly good fit for guardrail design.

Translating business rules into guardrails

The content of guardrails is the business rules themselves, specific to each customer. Eliciting implicit rules in the field and translating them into check conditions is the core work of an FDE.

For example, rules like "do not show this business partner's information to anyone outside the sales department" or "do not send quote amounts externally without manager approval" are often not written in policy documents. Through interviews with field staff and observation of business operations, FDEs discover these implicit rules and convert them into permission settings, output filters, and approval flows. This is a part that is hard to see from a headquarters product team, and we believe it is the area where the value of having FDEs in the field is most apparent.

Building consensus with decision-makers

"How much to block" is a judgment about risk tolerance, so the healthy approach is for the FDE to present technical options and the customer's decision-makers to decide.

If the FDE unilaterally decides how strict the guardrails should be, accountability becomes unclear when an incident occurs. We recommend using the three-level table mentioned earlier (prohibited, requires approval, acceptable) as a starting point, reaching agreement with the business owner, information systems department, and legal, and keeping a record of what was agreed.

Skills required of FDEs

Guardrail implementation requires, in addition to technical knowledge of LLMs, security fundamentals, evaluation design, and the ability to bridge business understanding.

Skill areaSpecific content
LLM application developmentRAG, tool calling, output schema validation
Security fundamentalsLeast privilege, authentication and authorization, understanding prompt injection techniques
Evaluation designCreating test cases, measuring false positives and false negatives
Business understanding and communicationEliciting implicit rules, articulating risk tolerance
Governance knowledgeUnderstanding frameworks such as the NIST AI RMF and the AI Business Operator Guidelines

For engineers aiming to become FDEs, guardrails are a theme that readily demonstrates "understanding both technology and business." It is also a strong candidate as a portfolio project.

What are common failures in AI guardrail adoption?

Most failures arise not from a lack of technical skill, but from no one being assigned to balance over-blocking versus under-blocking, and from updates stopping after adoption.

Over-blocking until it goes unused

If checks are made too strict, legitimate business operations get blocked. When users avoid the AI and drift to personally contracted external services, risk actually increases.

If users are not told "why it was blocked" and "how to get through" when they are blocked, frustration rises sharply. It is effective to prepare the block message and a channel for requesting reviews or exceptions from the start.

Relying only on the system prompt

Simply writing "Do not output confidential information" in the system prompt can easily be broken through prompt injection. Defenses need to be placed outside the system.

The OWASP Top 10 for LLM Applications 2025 also lists system prompt leakage (LLM07) as a standalone item. The safe assumption is that the system prompt is neither a secret nor a barrier. Permission control should be handled not by the LLM but by the application's authorization mechanisms.

Leaving it unattended after deployment

Since both attack techniques and business rules keep changing, guardrails are not a one-time build; they must be treated as an operational process of continuous updating.

Failure patternWhat happensCountermeasure
Over-blockingUsage declines and users move to external servicesConfirm false positives in log-only mode before enforcing blocks
Relying on promptsBroken through by injectionImplement permission control and output inspection on the application side
Overlooking the action layerThe AI performs unexpected operationsApply least privilege and require human approval for critical operations
Neglect after deploymentCannot keep up with new techniques or business changesBuild periodic reviews and evaluation data updates into operations
No responsible ownerDecisions on false positives stallAssign a guardrail owner on the business side

I believe the key to whether guardrail deployment succeeds is whether you can build an operational framework that keeps running even after the FDE has left the customer site.
