Buyer's guide6 questions

What does an AI consulting firm for business process automation do?

It finds the repetitive work in your back office, such as invoices keyed into the accounting system, quotes copied from email into the CRM or claims checked by hand, and builds bots, document readers and AI agents to do it. The good ones then run what they built: monitoring, fixing and updating it when a screen or a supplier's invoice layout changes.

How much does business process automation cost?

On the pages read on 5 November 2026: qBotica from $9,000 a month for three bots with UiPath licences and hosting included, Kanerika's typical $10,000 to $50,000 per bot or $75,000 to $200,000 for a first phase of three to five processes, and Bosio Digital from $25,000 for a founder led program. Seven of the ten print no price.

RPA, AI agents or both?

RPA bots follow fixed steps on screens and are reliable when the input never changes. AI agents and document processing handle the messy part: an invoice in a new layout, an email that needs reading. Most of the firms here sell both, and the useful question is which steps of your process need judgment and which only need speed.

Why do automation projects stall?

Usually because the process was automated as it stood, data errors and all, or because nobody owns the bots after launch. Ashling Partners, qBotica and Bits In Glass sell the running as a service for that reason, and West Monroe redesigns the process first.

Does the platform matter?

Yes, because you inherit its licences. qBotica bundles UiPath licences into its fee, Bits In Glass builds on Appian and Pega, and Accenture Edge leans on Salesforce Agentforce. Kanerika and BP3 Global work across several platforms. Ask who holds the licence and what leaving costs.

Should the automation firm be in the US?

Only if you need it. Ashling Partners is in Chicago, qBotica in Phoenix, BP3 Global in Austin, Bits In Glass in Denver and Accelirate in Florida, and most also deliver from India, Europe or Latin America. Ask where the person who builds and fixes your bots sits.

How to choose

  • 1This week, list every task where someone copies data from one system into another: the system it comes from, the system it goes into, how many times a week, and how long each takes. Ten rows is enough. The top three by hours are your first automation brief.
  • 2For each of the three, pull 50 real examples, such as invoices, quotes or claims, and count how many arrive in a format that changes. If most are identical, a rules based bot will do; if many differ, you need document processing or an agent, and qBotica, Ashling Partners and Kanerika sell both.
  • 3Ask every firm for the price of the first process in writing, including platform licences, and what the monthly cost is to keep it running. qBotica prints that cost ($9,000 a month for three bots, licences included); Kanerika prints a range.
  • 4Ask who owns the bots, the code and the platform accounts if you leave, and whether leaving means buying new licences. A bundled licence is convenient until the day you want to change firm.
  • 5Ask what happens when it breaks. Screens change, suppliers change invoice layouts, and a bot nobody watches fails without telling anyone. Ashling Partners runs a 24/7 operations centre, and Bits In Glass sells SLA backed support.
  • 6Keep the first commitment short and measured: one to three processes, a baseline of hours or error rate taken before the build, and a review at 30 days. Accenture Edge documents outcomes at 30, 60 and 90 days; ask the others to do the same.
  • 7If the real problem is that your CRM, accounting system and spreadsheets do not agree, bots will only move bad data faster. That is when you bring in a forward deployed engineer.
