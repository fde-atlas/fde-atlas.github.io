Sierra FDE Interview: AI-Native Onsite Interview Preparation Guide

Original title: Sierra FDE 面试：AI 原生现场面试准备指南

FDE Times · 2026-10-27 · 6 min read

Summary

This guide walks through Sierra's AI-native onsite interview, which has three stages: Plan, Build, and Review. During the 2-hour Build stage, the interviewer leaves the room and the candidate works alone with a coding agent. The author proposes a compressed 90-minute solo drill to practice scoping decisions under tighter time pressure. Key advice: write down scope cuts and deadlines in PLAN.md, log pivots in DECISIONS.md, verify agent output with known test cases, and be ready to defend every decision in Review. These habits mirror what forward deployed engineers need on-site with customers. AI usage rules vary by interview stage, so candidates should check their invitation.

Key points

  • Sierra's interview has Plan, Build, and Review stages; Build lasts 2 hours alone with an agent.
  • The drill compresses all three stages into 90 minutes for extra time pressure.
  • Candidates should document scope cuts, deadlines, and pivots in PLAN.md and DECISIONS.md.
  • Agents speed up implementation, but scoping and pivot decisions must remain the candidate's own.
  • AI usage rules differ per round; Aced notes some rounds are 60-minute TypeScript/React debugging sessions.

Key takeaways

  • Sierra's real interview has three parts—planning, building, and review—with the build stage alone lasting 2 hours. The 90-minute drill in this article deliberately compresses the time to get you used to greater time pressure.
  • An agent can help you write code faster, but scope, deadlines, and when to pivot remain your decisions.
  • Rules on AI usage vary by interview round; defer to the interview invitation you receive.

In Sierra's AI-native onsite interview, at some point the interviewer stands up and leaves the room. From then on, you have 2 hours to turn the idea you just defined together into a working product, and you can pivot or cut scope at any time.

Sierra says this format makes it easier to see whether a candidate shows initiative—for example, whether they switch directions when stuck. For a Forward Deployed Engineer (FDE), this is also a skill needed in the first week on-site with a customer: requirements are vague, time is tight, and every cut has to be explained clearly to others.

This article explains how to set up a 90-minute drill on your own laptop. The real interview isn't this short: beyond planning and review, Sierra's build stage alone is 2 hours. Squeezing all three stages into 90 minutes is intentional, so you get used to cutting scope under more time pressure than the real thing.

How does the real interview work?

Sierra splits this round into three parts. In the Plan stage, you and the interviewer define the product to build. In the Build stage, the interviewer leaves, and you build the product alone within 2 hours.

In the Review stage, you discuss the main product flow and the choices you made. The interviewer then reads your code to assess your engineering judgment.

When planning, remember something Sierra has explicitly said: you can cut scope as you go, and you can skip boilerplate like CRUD and authentication to focus on what makes the product unique. With only a few hours, this permission is valuable.

Don't assume every round works this way. A guide from Aced (formerly Exponent) shows that the hiring process for Agent Engineers includes a 60-minute live debugging round using TypeScript and React.

Aced also notes that Sierra has announced changes to some rounds. Another guide on PracHub points out that AI usage rules may differ by round; defer to the interview invitation you receive.

What to prepare before starting the timer

You need a laptop with the coding agent you use daily, an empty repository, a timer, and someone to play the interviewer. If practicing alone, write five follow-up questions in advance and answer them yourself at the end. According to Aced, the whole hiring process usually takes 2-5 weeks.

The table below is a suggested allocation of the 90 minutes, not Sierra's schedule:

StageDrill durationRequired output
Plan10 minutesA PLAN.md stating the main flow and the cutoff line
Build65 minutesMain flow working, with checks and error handling
Review15 minutesDemo and decision log

Step 1: Finish planning in 10 minutes and write down the cutoff line

Suppose the person playing the interviewer gives you this prompt: the customer support team needs an interface showing which conversations should be escalated to a human, and why. That's a vague requirement. In these 10 minutes, ask enough questions to narrow it down, then write it down:

# PLAN.md
用户：客服值班主管，在班次开始时查看
主流程：加载示例对话列表 -> 标记“需人工”并给出原因 -> 按标记筛选
数据：示例 JSON 文件，20 段对话（跳过 CRUD：静态数据足以验证流程）
跳过：登录（跳过认证：单用户，仅本地运行）
截止线：主流程跑通后 -> 停止加功能，转向检查、错误处理、演示
备选方案：如果标记功能卡住超过 15 分钟 -> 改用简单的关键词规则

Both "skip" items come with reasons, and that's intentional. PracHub's guide suggests skipping boilerplate only when you can articulate why, and setting a cutoff line in advance: once the main flow works, pivot to checks, error handling, and the demo.

A quick test: if the person playing the interviewer can't tell from reading your PLAN.md where you'll stop, the plan isn't good enough.

Step 2: Tell the agent what it can't see

Claude Code's best practices documentation boils down collaborating with an agent to three things: tell the agent what it can't see, give it a check it can run itself, and enforce rules without exceptions.

This principle applies to any coding agent. Before writing the first line of code, organize it into a short brief:

# AGENT.md（简版）
背景：供客服值班主管使用的内部工具，仅本地运行。
不要做：登录、数据库、管理页面。
每次改动后必须检查：运行测试套件；3 段结果已知的示例对话
  必须得到正确的标记。
硬性规则：未经询问不得引入新库；每次只改一个部分。

The most important part is the three conversations with known answers. The agent uses them for checks, and you use them as evidence during review. This is a simplified version: in the real interview, write the checks with the testing framework you know best.

Step 3: Hit the cutoff line, then stop polishing

Keep the loop short: give the agent a small task, run the checks, read the diff, and only then hand off the next task.

PracHub's guide draws the roles clearly: the agent is a tool to speed up implementation, but you are the product owner.

Once you accept a diff you can't explain, you've handed control of the product to the agent.

Every time you change direction, write a line in the decision log. It takes less than a minute but pays off big during review:

# DECISIONS.md
[第 32 分钟] 基于模型的标记在 3 个测试样例上结果不稳定
  -> 改用关键词规则 + 长度阈值。原因：先要有一个可靠的流程。
[第 51 分钟] 主流程跑通。到达截止线 -> 停止添加日期筛选。
[第 55 分钟] 增加对损坏 JSON 文件的处理：显示提示信息，而不是白屏。

The line at minute 32 is exactly what Sierra wants to see: switching direction when stuck instead of sinking deeper. Sierra allows pivoting and cutting scope as you go. Because the 15-minute threshold was written into PLAN.md beforehand, you make that decision with a clear head rather than in a panic.

Step 4: Rehearse the review as a real debate

Sierra's review has two layers: a discussion around the product flow, and a code read assessing engineering judgment. The person playing the interviewer should genuinely push back: Why does the on-call lead filter by flag first? Why cut the date filter? Where's the code that handles corrupted files?

For each question, you should be able to open two things: a line in DECISIONS.md, and a piece of code where you can explain every branch. If a part was written by the agent, say so directly, then explain why you kept it.

PracHub's guide warns: never use an AI assistant to circumvent explicitly stated rules, and never hide who wrote the code.

Mistakes that waste the drill

The most common mistake is letting the agent make decisions for you. You ask the agent "what should I do next," then follow it—and by review time you have no story of your own to tell. The second is spending the first 20 minutes on login and the database, even though Sierra allows skipping them.

The third is not stopping at the cutoff line. An extra feature added at minute 70 almost never beats error handling and a smoothly running demo.

The last is practicing every round the same way. The 60-minute TypeScript and React debugging round described by Aced requires different preparation, and the AI rules may differ too.

Where does this skill show up at the customer site?

Swap the interviewer for the customer's head of operations, and the 2 hours for a short sprint before a demo—the steps are exactly the same: define the main flow with them, write down what was skipped and why, stop at the cutoff line, then explain every choice to the people who weren't sitting next to you watching you write code.

For job hunting, every drill leaves you three things worth putting in a portfolio: PLAN.md, DECISIONS.md, and a working repository. On a resume, a line like "built a prototype in 2 hours with a coding agent, documenting scope cuts and pivot decisions" is more persuasive than listing tools.

When reading FDE job descriptions, watch for phrases like scoping, prototyping, and working with ambiguity. They often signal that the role values exactly the skills you've just practiced, and they make good entry points for telling the stories in your DECISIONS.md.

In these interviews, a coding agent is in the room with you. What employers want to see are the decisions only you can make.
