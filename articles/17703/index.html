What's the difference between an FDE and consulting: Forward Deployed Engineer

Original title: FDE 和咨询有什么区别：前线部署工程师

FDE Times · 2026-10-08 · 6 min read

Summary

This article argues that what separates FDE work from consulting is not speed or tech taste, but whether deployment experience flows back into the product. Palantir rejected projects resembling "Accenture with better software" because its FDEs are the primary input channel for product management. Yet many FDE roles at AI labs today are effectively consulting or solutions architect jobs. The author offers four interview questions to tell the difference and advises candidates to pick roles where experience shapes the roadmap.

What distinguishes an FDE from consulting is not speed or technical taste, but whether the experience from each deployment flows back into the product.

Key takeaways

  • Delivery speed and a preference for new technologies describe people, not the model; even FDE supporters concede that consultants also take a holistic view of problems, and that FDEs complement consulting.
  • Palantir-style FDE work is not consulting, because FDEs are the primary input channel for product management: experience from customers flows back into the product rather than staying with the customer.
  • But today, many FDE roles at AI labs are essentially consultants or Solutions Architects, so the question to ask is "Is this role consulting?" rather than "Is FDE consulting?"

Palantir once rejected a category of projects it called "Accenture-with-better-software." A company that makes its living by sending engineers to work directly on-site with customers rejected precisely the business that, at first glance, looks identical to its own daily work. An article analyzing Palantir's FDE playbook argues that the company drew a clear line against the "consultant model" in exactly this way.

If you are considering a move into a Forward Deployed Engineer (FDE) role, "Is FDE consulting in engineering clothing?" is not just a matter of wording. It determines what you will have to show two years from now: a string of delivered projects, or a product bearing your imprint. Honestly, the answer depends on where the experience from your work ultimately flows.

The case for FDEs is weaker than it looks

FDE advocates usually start with speed. An opinion piece in SD Times writes that traditional implementation projects often take months, sometimes years, to bring a solution to market, while FDEs excel at delivering value in weeks with production-ready products.

The author also discusses technical taste: consultants tend to choose mature solutions rather than the latest options.

But speed and technical taste describe people, not the model. A fast-moving consultant who loves trying new tools does not thereby become an FDE. The SD Times author also concedes, when discussing the habit of looking at problems holistically, that traditional implementation consultants do the same.

The article concludes that FDEs complement consultants, filling in where consultants are weaker, rather than replacing them.

The second argument is stronger: accountability. FDE Academy, a supplier of FDE talent, puts the distinction succinctly: technical consultants give advice and hand off; FDEs build hands-on and own the outcome. In its view, a consultant's deliverable is advice, a roadmap, or a design, not a running system.

This argument is worth remembering, but remember who is saying it. This organization sells FDEs, so "owning the outcome" is both a description and a sales pitch. Moreover, if the test stops at "building a working system instead of writing documents," an excellent systems integrator would also pass. The real dividing line must lie elsewhere.

The real dividing line: where the experience flows

Back to Palantir. That playbook analysis points to the most important structural feature: at Palantir, FDEs are the primary input channel for product management. Not one of many channels, but the primary one. What engineers learn at customers does not stay with the customer; it flows back to shape the product.

A Palantir insider quoted by Cloud Authority put it even more bluntly: the FDE model is a product development strategy that merely looks like services from the outside. Oxagile, an engineering services company, lists "treating it as a consulting role" as one of the biggest misconceptions, and emphasizes that Palantir's FDEs are software engineers.

Viewed this way, the rejection of "Accenture with better software" makes sense. By this logic, such projects bring in services revenue but produce no product. The engineer finishes, hands off, and moves to the next customer, and the company is no richer at the product level. Even if the people doing the work write code eight hours a day, this is consulting.

FDE Academy expresses the same idea from another angle: FDEs can bridge the gap between strategy and execution because strategy emerges from building. In consulting, strategy is the deliverable and execution is someone else's job. In a genuine FDE role, you build first, and what you build tells the company what product to make next.

But many FDE roles today really are consulting

The Palantir model is an ideal, and the hiring market does not necessarily replicate it. According to daily.dev's summary of the May 2026 issue of Gergely Orosz's The Pragmatic Engineer, modern FDE roles at AI labs are essentially consultant or Solutions Architect roles.

Both can be true at once. Palantir-style FDE work is not consulting because it is the product engine. And by the judgment above, many jobs carrying the FDE title today remain close to consulting.

The reason is not hard to see: if an employer has no mechanism for bringing customer experience back to the product team, then whatever the title, the work stops at deploying for the customer. Same title, different job.

So the question to ask is not "Is FDE consulting?" but "Is this FDE role consulting?" The table below turns the signals from the arguments above into questions you can use in your next interview.

The last column is the most useful. Treat it as a rule of thumb, not a verdict: a company that can answer concretely "which feature came from an FDE" very likely has the kind of feedback loop back into the product that Palantir describes.

If the interviewer hedges on this question, that is a warning sign: the role may be closer to consulting than the title suggests.

Choose which kind of FDE role, not whether to be an FDE

If you have two to eight years of experience and your goal is to become an FDE, prepare for both scenarios: FDE roles that are consulting in disguise, and genuine FDE roles where experience flows back into the product. If you can choose, aim for the latter.

If the description above holds, the skills required in both cases are largely the same: building production-ready products in weeks and owning the outcome, rather than handing off and walking away.

The difference lies in what you bring back.

On your resume, do not just write "deployed a solution for customer X." Spell out what the experience from that deployment changed in the product or in how the team works.

For example, a sentence like "identified a recurring pattern across three customers, proposed a solution, and turned it into a shared module" tells the hiring manager that you understand the FDE as a product engine, not a construction crew.

When reading job descriptions, watch for wording that points to feedback loops: "feed back into product," "shape roadmap," "work with product team." If a JD talks only about customer onboarding, integrations, and customer success without ever mentioning the product, treat it as a warning sign: whatever the title, the role is likely closer to consulting.

There's nothing wrong with doing consulting; the key is to be clear about what you're choosing.

You can practice this in your current job: every time you complete a piece of work for an internal "customer," write down one change the product should make as a result.

This habit trains exactly the mechanism that the Palantir analysis says distinguishes FDE from consulting: every deployment becomes an input to the product, rather than stopping at handoff.

Why does Palantir reject "Accenture but with better software" style projects? When FDEs are the product's main input channel, the most reasonable interpretation is: a project that brings nothing back to the product isn't worth doing. Apply the same test when choosing your next FDE role.
