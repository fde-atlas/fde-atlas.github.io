How to conduct a project wrap-up retrospective as a Forward Deployed Engineer

Original title: 前线部署工程师项目收尾复盘怎么做

FDE Times · 2026-11-05 · 6 min read

Summary

This FDE Times guide explains how forward deployed engineers should run project closeout retrospectives. Drawing on PostHog's employee handbook, Google SRE's postmortem culture, and a project closeout guide, it argues that every open item falls into one of three categories: lessons learned, remaining debt, or product issues, while commercial requests are separated and handed to the CSM or TAM. Each item needs a named owner and honest priority. Product issues require a four-part issue (what broke, who's affected, what's blocked, how to reproduce), and workarounds must be logged as debt linked to that issue. Retros should include the customer, avoid blame, be stored in a shared repository, and end with a deliberate handoff of the client relationship. The article includes a worked example table, an issue template, and a practice exercise.

Key points

  • Classify every open item as a lesson, debt, or product issue.
  • Route commercial requests to the CSM or TAM, not the FDE.
  • File four-part issues for product problems; never hide workarounds.
  • Hold retrospectives with the customer and store results in a shared repo.
  • Deliberately hand off the client relationship in the closeout email.

Key takeaways

  • Forward Deployed Engineers hit the product's rough edges first, and the final meeting is the moment to turn those frictions into owned work.
  • Business requests go to whoever owns the client relationship; every other item goes into exactly one category, with a clear owner and priority.
  • A retrospective nobody reads is worthless, so store it in a shared repository and deliberately hand off the client relationship.

The last week of a deployment is always packed: handing over accounts, revoking access, sending documentation. The closing retrospective usually gets squeezed into the final half hour and ends with a single sentence: "everything went fine." Months later, the next client steps into the same pit, and nobody remembers how it was handled last time.

PostHog's employee handbook describes the Forward Deployed Engineer (FDE) as the first person to hit the product's rough edges under real customer load. The handbook also states explicitly that context gathered in the field helps later customers resolve problems faster.

So the final meeting is not a formality. It is the one time you can make sure the right people are in the room and turn those frictions into owned work. Once you move on to the next project, that knowledge goes with you.

Put every item into one of three categories

Anything still open at project close belongs to exactly one of three categories. Lessons learned change how you work next time. Remaining debt is work the client or your team still needs to complete, with an owner and a deadline. Product issues are problems only the product team can fix.

Before categorizing, first remove one type of item that doesn't belong in this technical meeting: business requests, such as a client wanting to add scope. At PostHog, the business relationship with a client belongs to that client's Technical Account Executive (TAE), Technical Account Manager (TAM), or Customer Success Manager (CSM), not the FDE. Make this clear at the start of the meeting, note these requests, and hand them off directly.

The categorization question for everything else is: if the next client hits this problem, who has to fix it? If the answer is the team that owns the feature, PostHog has a clear rule: when a project is blocked by an issue only the product team can fix, the FDE should hand the work over to that team rather than work around it with a patch.

This rule guards against a specific risk. Tandem summarized Marty Cagan's critique of the FDE model: the danger is gradually sliding into building custom software for each client. The model only works when what is learned in the field flows back into the shared product, rather than settling into custom code.

Whatever category an item falls into, the whole meeting needs one shared precondition: no blame. The chapter on postmortem culture in the Google SRE book says you cannot "fix" people, but you can fix systems and processes.

The client must also be present: a guide on project closeouts emphasizes that lessons-learned meetings should be held with the client, not as a private debrief within your own team.

Picture an analytics platform deployment

Suppose you've just finished an event tracking deployment for an e-commerce app. Before the meeting, you organize tickets, messages, and notes into a raw list of six items. The table below shows the categorization, with one item removed before sorting.

ItemCategoryOwnerNext step
Sync jobs to the data warehouse are slow because they run during the client's peak hoursRemaining debtClient's data engineerReschedule the jobs and set a deadline
The SDK loses events when users are offline for long periodsProduct issueTeam that owns the SDKFile an issue covering the four points
Temporary code that works around the SDK defect aboveRemaining debtFDEAttach the link in the issue and record a removal date
Script that renames old events to new namesLessons learned, converted into a toolFDEAdd to the internal repository as a reusable template
Waiting for access provisioning delayed things by several daysLessons learnedFDE teamRequest access at the kickoff meeting
Client wants an additional custom dashboardRemoved upfront: business requestCSM or TAMHand off to whoever owns the business relationship

Look closely at three of these rows. The workaround code isn't hidden: it's recorded as debt and linked to the product issue, so once the SDK team ships a fix, someone remembers to delete it.

The event renaming script became an asset. PostHog treats building reference implementations, examples, and internal tools that speed up future projects as part of FDE work.

The last row is the item removed at the start of the meeting. When a client raises new scope during the retrospective, the right move is to note it down and hand it to whoever owns the business relationship, not to promise it on the spot.

For product issues, PostHog requires the FDE to notify the team that owns the area and file an issue stating what's broken, who hits it, what it blocks, and how to reproduce it. Here is a copy-paste template:

标题：设备长时间离线时 SDK 丢失事件

哪里坏了：离线期间记录的事件在恢复连接后没有发送
谁会遇到：电商客户，移动 App，信号较弱地区的用户
阻塞了什么：结账漏斗报表缺少数据，客户暂时还不信任这些数字
如何复现：打开飞行模式，浏览几个页面，等待较长时间，恢复连接，检查事件
当前绕行方案：客户仓库中的绕行代码（链接），修复后移除

Running the meeting, step by step

Before the meeting, send the draft table to the client and let them add to it. Nobody wants to hear a list of problems for the first time mid-meeting. In the meeting, go through each item: agree on the category first, then assign an owner—and name a specific person, not "another team."

The hardest step is prioritization. The Google SRE book poses one question for every retrospective: is the action plan sound, and are the fixes prioritized appropriately? If a product issue blocking the client's main reports is marked "low," the meeting has already half failed.

After the meeting, store the results in a shared repository. Google recommends keeping postmortems in a central place so others can find and learn from them, and states plainly that an unreviewed postmortem might as well never have existed. The project closeout guide says something similar: store it where the team will find it next time.

One final task remains: the same guide points out that ending a project isn't just sending the invoice—even after the engagement ends, the client relationship should be deliberately handed off. In the closing email itself, introduce the client to whoever will handle them going forward.

Mistakes that make the meeting pointless

The most common mistake is turning the meeting into a trial, such as pressing "who delayed the access request?" The client goes quiet, and you lose exactly the experiences most worth learning from. The second mistake is holding an internal-only meeting without inviting the client, leaving items in the remaining debt list with no one to receive them on the client's side.

The third mistake is harder to spot: quietly working around product defects because it's faster than filing an issue. Every such patch is exactly the kind of custom code Cagan warned about.

PostHog writes that the point of bringing client problems back is a better roadmap. A patch that exists only in a client's repository improves no roadmap.

The last mistake is the vague issue that says only "the SDK has an offline bug." If the product team can't reproduce it, it won't get scheduled for a fix. The four points in the template above are exactly what turns what you witnessed in the field into work another team can act on.

This skill also belongs on your resume. PostHog describes feeding lessons learned back to the product team and building reusable tools as part of the FDE role, so it's worth showing concretely rather than just writing "completed deployment for client X."

State how many issues you filed with the product team, which ones were fixed, and which of your tools were reused by later projects. When reading job descriptions, watch for mentions of a feedback loop with the product team: it's a signal the company takes this work seriously.

Exercise: how would you categorize these six items?

Suppose you've just finished a deployment of a customer service agent. The raw list has six items. One: the product API times out when importing large documents. Two: code that splits files into chunks to work around that defect. Three: the client's IT team hasn't decommissioned the test accounts yet.

Four: the eval suite you wrote for scoring the agent's answers. Five: the project was delayed two weeks because no one knew who on the customer's side approves the data. Six: the customer asks about pricing for expanding to more departments. Sort them yourself first, then look at the answers below.

Answers: Item six is removed first and handed off to the CSM or TAM. Item one goes to the product team—file an issue covering the four points. Item two is the FDE's debt; attach the link in that issue. Item three is debt, owned by a designated person on the customer's side, with a deadline set.

Item four is a lesson learned converted into a tool, added to the internal repository. Item five is a lesson learned: next time, ask for the data approver's name at the kickoff meeting. If you sorted item two incorrectly, reread why patches can't be hidden.

A project is only truly finished when the next customer no longer has to pay the price this customer has already paid.
