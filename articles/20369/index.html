How to Read FDE Salary Ranges: A Guide to US Salary Transparency Laws

Original title: FDE 薪资范围怎么看：美国薪资透明法解读

FDE Times · 2026-10-29 · 6 min read

Summary

This article explains how US state pay transparency laws affect FDE job seekers. California, Colorado, Virginia and Maine require salary ranges in job postings, often covering remote roles, but each state defines "salary range" differently. In Colorado, final offers may fall outside the posted range, so ranges are good-faith estimates, not promises. Citing Recruiting From Scratch, the author notes 43% of FDE postings still lack salary ranges, the median base salary range at seed-to-Series-B startups is $175K–$225K, and only 55% mention equity, with under 1 in 25 specifying a percentage. Practical advice: ask about level and equity numbers early, and use reading salary ranges as a chance to demonstrate FDE-style analytical skill.

Key points

  • Four states require posted salary ranges, and rules often extend to remote roles.
  • Colorado's definition allows very wide ranges, and offers may legally deviate from them.
  • 43% of FDE postings lack salary data; median startup range is $175K–$225K.
  • Only 55% of postings mention equity, and few disclose actual percentages.
  • Washington employment class actions jumped from 54 (2023) to 773 (2025).

Key Takeaways

  • California, Colorado, Virginia, and Maine all require job postings to include salary ranges; California's and Colorado's rules also cover remote positions.
  • A salary range is only a good-faith estimate; in Colorado, the final offer can be above or below that range.
  • Only 55% of startup FDE job postings mention equity, and fewer than 1 in 25 specify a percentage.

In 2023, Washington State saw only 54 employment-related class action lawsuits. By 2025, that number reached 773, with 658 more added in the first half of 2026. The law firm Davis Wright Tremaine lists pay transparency as one of the fastest-growing litigation areas in the state.

Despite the legal pressure, a tally by the recruiting firm Recruiting From Scratch shows that 43% of Forward Deployed Engineer (FDE) job postings still don't list a salary. Since this figure comes from a recruiting firm itself, it's best treated as a signal. Even so, it reminds job seekers that nearly half of FDE job postings may not provide any number to reference at all.

So the real question has two parts: when must an FDE job posting disclose a salary range? And once you have the numbers, how should you interpret them?

A salary range is a legal concept with a precise definition, and each state defines it differently. Misread the definition, and you may quote expectations that are too low, or expect a number the company never intended to pay.

How far do the laws go in making salary ranges public?

In California, Labor Code Section 432.3 defines a "pay scale" as the salary range the employer reasonably expects to pay for the position. The state's labor department interprets this more broadly: the requirement applies to any position a California resident could hold, including remote work.

The second point matters if you're targeting remote FDE roles. No matter where the company is headquartered, as long as the position is open to people in California, it falls under this law.

Colorado follows the same logic: remote positions that could be performed in Colorado (while also being performable anywhere else) must have compliant job postings.

Virginia's newly enacted law requires every job posting to state a good-faith salary range. Maine's rules apply to employers with 10 or more employees, which includes very small in-state FDE startups.

States set different thresholds for company size, and the US has no single nationwide rule. But the direction is clear: more states are legislating, coverage is expanding to remote work, and in Washington State, pay transparency is one of the fastest-growing litigation areas.

A salary range is an estimate, not a promise

This is exactly where many job seekers go wrong. The table below puts the state rules side by side, showing what a salary range actually means in different places.

Looking at the second column, you'll see that two definitions allow two different kinds of salary ranges. California ties the range to the salary the employer reasonably expects to pay for the role. Colorado allows the range to stretch from the lowest possible value to the highest possible one, so in theory a single posting could fold several job levels into one interval.

Colorado's EPT Rule 4.1.2 also explicitly states that the final salary may differ from the published range. So a salary range is only a good-faith estimate, not a contract—don't assume you'll get the midpoint.

How to read an FDE job posting with a salary range of $175K–$225K

Recruiting From Scratch calculated that the median base salary range for FDE job postings at seed-to-Series B startups in the US is $175K–$225K. This is the best reference benchmark among the figures in this article. Here's a hypothetical example of how to use it.

Suppose you're comparing two postings. Posting A is subject to California law and lists $180K–$220K: a $40K-wide range with a midpoint of $200K. Posting B is a remote role subject to Colorado's rules and lists $140K–$260K: a $120K-wide range with the same $200K midpoint.

The midpoints are identical, but the messages differ. Posting A's narrower range may indicate the company already has a fairly specific budget, and the negotiation mainly revolves around whether you land in the upper or lower half.

Posting B's floor is $35K below the median floor and its ceiling $35K above the median ceiling, so the range likely merges several job levels into a single interval.

For Posting B, the key question isn't "how much" but "which level." If you're slotted at a lower level, the $260K ceiling means almost nothing to you. So before quoting your own number, ask: what level does this role correspond to? Where do people with experience like yours typically fall within the range?

In California, this question carries extra leverage. If the company hires through a third-party recruiter, it must disclose the salary range to them. If a recruiter claims not to know the salary range, they're skipping information they're supposed to have.

What a salary range doesn't tell you

The published salary range is only part of the offer. According to Recruiting From Scratch, 55% of startup FDE job postings mention equity, but fewer than 1 in 25 specify a percentage.

Just saying "equity included" without a number isn't enough to compare two offers. Ask for the number of shares, total shares outstanding, and the vesting schedule, then calculate the percentage yourself.

What about the 43% of postings that don't list a salary? Treat it as a signal to ask earlier. Ask about the salary range on the first call, before stating your own expectations. Whether they answer directly or dodge, you'll learn something about how the company operates.

If you're outside the US, keep one more thing in mind: these rules target jobs that California or Colorado residents could perform, so the published ranges don't necessarily apply to candidates elsewhere. Ask the company directly: do you pay candidates in my country within the same range?

Turn salary-range literacy into an interview advantage

Interpreting a salary range is exactly what FDEs do every day: take a number someone hands you, first ask how it's defined, then cross-check it against other data, and only then make a decision. A candidate who can do this with their own offer is demonstrating that skill right in front of the hiring manager.

When reading an FDE job description, watch for three things: whether a salary range is given, whether the job level is stated, and whether equity comes with a number. If any one is missing, that's your first question. When negotiating, cite the range in the posting itself and the $175K–$225K benchmark, rather than a number you made up.

More states will pass legislation, and more FDE job postings will include numbers. But this won't make negotiations any easier—it just shifts the question from "how much do you pay" to "how is that number defined." Whoever asks this question first gains the upper hand.
