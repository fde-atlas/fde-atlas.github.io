Why Do Chinese Enterprises Need FDEs More for AI Implementation? - The Economic Observer

Original title: 为什么中国企业AI落地更需要FDE？-经济观察网

m.eeo.com.cn · 2026-07-28 · 12 min read

Summary

The article argues that with powerful Chinese models like Kimi K3 and Qwen3.8-Max now abundant, the bottleneck for enterprise AI adoption has shifted to scenario selection, data access, permissions, and workflow redesign. Drawing on Alibaba Cloud practices and interviews (including with VP Huo Jia), it shows how FDEs shorten project timelines by front-loading validation—roughly 80% of effort goes to finding the right direction before building. Cases from CCTV Sports, CNPC Kunlun Digital Intelligence, and Peking University International Hospital illustrate AI running reliably in production. The author also examines FDE business models, from project fees to annual contracts to result-based pricing, and argues large tech platforms are best positioned to lead, since field experience must feed back into full-stack products.

Key points

  • Model supply is no longer the bottleneck; deployment context is.
  • FDEs front-load validation, shrinking total project time despite longer pre-sales.
  • Cases show AI staying in core production systems, not just demos.
  • Sustainable FDE economics need customer value, recurring revenue, and product reuse.
  • Big tech platforms suit FDE leadership because field learnings must flow back into products.

Full text

2026-07-28 11:33

For Chinese enterprises today, the model no longer seems to be the most obvious bottleneck in AI implementation.

On July 14, Moonshot AI released Kimi K3 with 2.8 trillion total parameters; a few days later, Alibaba's Qwen previewed Qwen3.8-Max-Preview with 2.4 trillion total parameters. In less than a week, two domestic models with over 2 trillion total parameters appeared one after another, pushing forward the technical capabilities enterprises can call upon.

Models certainly determine the upper limit of AI capability.

But when model supply has become so abundant in such a short time, the decisions enterprises need to make have not actually decreased; instead, they quickly extend from "which model to choose" to a series of more specific questions: where the model's capability boundaries have been pushed to, which system holds the business context data, who has the authority to access it, who takes over when the model makes errors, whether generated results can go directly into business operations, and whether business staff need to change work practices they have followed for years, and so on.

We often say AI empowers all industries, but industries are diverse and varied, and the people specifically responsible for AI implementation often most want to hear about a best practice first: has anyone in the same industry done this, what model did they use, and what were the results?

A best practice can prove that a direction is worth trying, but it is hard to replicate another company's data, permissions, and workflows along with it. When these conditions have not been sorted out, what gets copied most easily is often just the appearance of the product. As a result, over the past two years, the easiest AI projects to complete in enterprises have often been just a Demo. Gather some materials, connect them to a knowledge base, build a natural language interface for the large model, and within a few weeks the system can answer questions.

The gap between a Demo and a real environment is obviously enormous.

On launch day it seems capable of everything, but after a while no one continues to use it, and it no longer generates model calls. Monthly, weekly, or even daily disposable agents are very common.

Faced with this gap, people easily attribute the cause to large enterprises moving too slowly. But in decades of enterprise IT construction, "slow" was originally a reasonable norm, and in many cases it is also how large organizations control risk. In China, central and state-owned enterprises especially follow this approach.

During WAIC 2025, the World Artificial Intelligence Conference, SASAC released the first batch of 40 strategically high-value AI scenarios for central enterprises, with an interesting formulation: promoting "potted landscape-style" pilots to land as "rainforest-style" large-scale applications. So what "shortcut" can achieve this leap? An enterprise needs to find AI's destination while also building systems to production environments—who takes on that middle stretch of work that few people have been fully responsible for in the past?

At this year's WAIC 2026, I came to the conference again. On this question, I had some conversations with leaders of several tech companies, including Huo Jia, Vice President of Alibaba Cloud Intelligence Group. One conclusion that can be drawn is that people are gradually focusing the answer on a role that was previously relatively unfamiliar but has existed for a long time—the FDE.

FDE Steps into the Spotlight

Forward Deployed Engineer, usually translated in China as 前沿部署工程师 or 前线部署工程师, abbreviated as FDE. This title did not first appear with the emergence of large models.

More than a decade ago, Palantir already used it to describe a group of solutions and integration engineers who went deep into customer sites. They connected on one end to the problems customers were working on, and on the other to the company's platform R&D.

In the era of large models, FDE has become prominent again. Take a few AI labs as examples: OpenAI's description of this role's responsibilities extends from business discovery, project scoping, and system design all the way to development, production launch, and adoption outcomes. In May 2026, it also established a Deployment Company, making engineers entering complex organizations and transforming key workflows together with customers into a formal business line. Anthropic and other model companies have also set up similar roles in multiple regions.

The resurgence of the FDE is first a global phenomenon, but AI implementation in China seems to need FDEs even more.

We often say China has abundant AI application scenarios. Rich scenarios are certainly an advantage, but on the implementation side, it also means it is hard to solve all problems with a single set of standard answers.

China has both highly digitalized large enterprises and many companies still catching up on fundamentals; even within the same group, newly built data platforms, business software running for many years, systems from different vendors, and data scattered across departments may all coexist. When AI enters such an enterprise, it does not bypass the system relationships left by the past; instead, it encounters them one by one when reading data, invoking permissions, and changing processes.

Even if enterprise data is unified at the physical level, the data itself still has issues of responsibility boundaries.

The information handled by finance, energy, transportation, healthcare, and large industrial enterprises often involves personal information or critical production data. What the model can see, whether data can leave the local environment, who reviews generated results, and how the system keeps audit trails and rolls back—all must be implemented in the project.

Finally, modern AI represented by large language models looks simple to use, but the underlying technical principles and engineering implementation are extremely complex. Huo Jia shared an observation with me: in reality, even the proportion of people who can clearly explain the Transformer architecture is not high. If you know that something works but not why, how can you do AI implementation well?

The significance of the FDE lies largely here.

It is not just about adding a category of engineering headcount, but about letting people who understand AI architecture and capability boundaries enter scenarios early and work together with those who hold the business, data, and production responsibilities.

Pre-sales Takes a Larger Share, Yet Total Project Time Actually Shortens

From the practical feedback I received from Alibaba Cloud, FDE involvement has indeed shortened project cycles and increased customers' sense of gain.

Because the pre-sales phase (project justification) takes a larger share, the total time from idea to launch is actually shortened.

Two measuring sticks are used here.

One measures how time is allocated within a project; the other measures how long the entire project lasts.

If we use an approximate 20% and 80% to describe it, traditional enterprise projects typically first spend about 20% of the time confirming requirements, designing solutions, and signing the SOW, with most of the remaining time used for development, integration, testing, deployment, and acceptance. This arrangement continues the mindset of deterministic engineering: fix the scope as quickly as possible during pre-sales, and after the contract is signed, the construction team moves forward along the already-drawn route.

The proportions in AI projects are reversing.

Before formal construction begins, teams such as FDEs need to enter the business site to screen scenarios, check data and permissions, build an MVP (minimum viable product/solution), have actual users run through it, and then judge whether the technical path holds and whether the return on investment is reasonable. If a scenario has no value, or the data conditions simply do not support it, it should stop at this stage; only after the direction has been validated in a real environment can development, deployment, and launch proceed quickly.

Thus, in an already shortened project cycle, about 80% of the time may be spent finding the right direction, with the remaining 20% used for construction after the direction is clear.

This does not mean moving 80% of the original six months or a year wholesale into pre-sales. In traditional processes, a wrong assumption might not be overturned until development, integration, or even acceptance, with requirements then traveling back up the organizational hierarchy, turning prior investment into rework. FDEs instead use one or two weeks of small-scale trials to expose the judgments most likely to fail in real data and processes.

Total time is thereby compressed.

Merely moving validation earlier is not enough to achieve this speed. After the MVP is done, if requirements still have to be handed sequentially to sales, Solutions Architects, product managers, development, and delivery teams, the understanding just gained in the trial will be lost through repeated retellings.

Alibaba Cloud's FDE practice is to form a One Team combining the customer side's business, data, and IT staff with the vendor side's product, algorithm, and engineering personnel. The point is not whether everyone sits in the same office, but that people who can make different judgments enter the same working site.

In the 1970s, MIT scholar Thomas Allen studied communication networks in R&D organizations and found that physical location significantly affects technical exchange; he later called those who connect the organization to the external technical world "gatekeepers". Alibaba Cloud's FDE has become the "gatekeeper" of the AI era.

If the changes described earlier compressed project time, what is being shortened here is organizational space.

In model training and inference, communication overhead is the new bottleneck; the same is true for the organizational coordination of AI deployment.

Two projects, three capabilities of the FDE put to the test

Having covered the past and present of the FDE, let us now look at three Alibaba Cloud cases to see the results FDEs deliver.

The first is the World Cup "AI viewing assistant" in CCTV Sports' client app.

The World Cup has a schedule that waits for no project process: matches happen continuously, fixtures, players and scores change at any moment, and the timing of user questions may not align with data updates. The system must answer promptly while relying on official sources to prevent the large model from fabricating content. The Alibaba Cloud team pushed the application into production in about a week, letting real users and real-time data enter validation as early as possible.

Going live in a week is certainly fast, but speed is not the whole point of this case. Traditional projects try to eliminate uncertainty as much as possible before delivery; this project instead turned real usage itself into part of the validation. The system went live during the matches first, then kept optimizing and adjusting based on questions from the field, as long as results remained controllable.

The second project comes from PetroChina's Kunlun Digital Intelligence.

The atmospheric and vacuum distillation unit is the first step in refining and chemical production, and all downstream processes depend on its stable operation. When an AI application enters such a system, it faces not only whether a single answer is accurate, but also continuous operation, exception handling, and coordination with existing systems. According to customer feedback relayed by Alibaba Cloud, this was the first AI application running 24 hours a day in a production system within the customer's organization. At this point, what the FDE must prove is no longer whether the model can answer, but whether AI can stay in the core business.

The third comes from Peking University International Hospital.

In just three weeks, the FDE team completed full-chain delivery of AI agents across seven major patient service scenarios—from architecture design and joint debugging of interfaces with 24 business systems to the gray-scale launch of the WeChat mini-program "Xiao'an" AI health manager—with intent understanding and recall accuracy both reaching 90%, enabling 1.5 million patients to enjoy 7×24 one-stop intelligent services covering "registration, guidance, report interpretation, and follow-up". It validated the MaaS delivery model of "rapid deployment through small entry points, standardized solutions replicated across hospitals", providing a referenceable implementation sample for medical AI patient services to move from single-point pilots to large-scale, inclusive adoption.

These three projects are of course not a standard playbook that every enterprise can copy. Yet they reveal some common signals of high-value scenarios: the AI application can be Always On, usage continuously generates new high-quality data, and business results keep benefiting even after the base model is upgraded.

FDE in China needs big tech companies to take the lead even more

During our research, we found that anyone encountering the FDE concept initially raises the same question.

If the responsibilities of the role have changed, yet the contracts supporting these people are still mostly calculated by project, person-day, and deliverables. An FDE team composed of senior engineers is not cheap; if every customer requires the same investment of manpower from scratch, the more successful this approach is, the heavier the delivery burden on the vendor becomes.

So, how can the FDE become a sustainable business?

At least three sets of books need to balance at the same time.

The customer must first derive observable value from the application: how much time saved, how much revenue added, how much risk reduced. Launch, acceptance, and accuracy are only intermediate metrics; sustained usage and business outcomes will determine whether the contract is renewed the following year.

Only after the customer keeps using the service can the vendor gain long-term revenue from model calls, compute supply, data platforms, and operations services, so that the FDE does not have to survive entirely on one-off project fees.

Meanwhile, the experience FDEs accumulate in the field, the data feedback formed within authorization and compliance boundaries, and industry know-how must also feed back into models, agent platforms, and related cloud products. As products improve through field practice, they can serve more customers and industries, so the benefits created by FDEs no longer stay within a single project, and the delivery of subsequent projects may become lighter as well.

Customer value, vendor revenue, and product reuse—without any one of these, it is hard for the FDE to evolve from a batch of successful projects into a sustainable industry mechanism.

Payment models may also change accordingly. What is common today is still one deal at a time, with both parties specifying requirements, timelines, and deliverables in the contract. But when a new scenario can be judged within one or two weeks as to whether it is worth continuing, the time spent on re-initiating a project, tendering, quoting, and acceptance may be longer than the experiment itself.

Therefore, annual contracts for continuous services may suit the FDE better than purchasing projects one by one: what the customer buys is a team that continuously discovers, validates, and launches high-value scenarios within agreed boundaries; if one direction fails, resources can be shifted to the next.

One step further, the ideal state is paying for results—what is now widely discussed as Result as a Service. What the customer buys is no longer how many person-days engineers invested, nor merely how many agents were delivered, but shorter turnaround time, lower failure rates, increased revenue, or reduced risk.

But this step clearly requires more mature conditions. Enterprises need comparable business baselines, and both parties must distinguish which results come from the model and which from process adjustments, data quality, and organizational cooperation; a vendor can only be accountable for results within the scope it can control. How to price safety responsibilities, extreme events, and long-term benefits also requires more case data.

Project fees, long-term service contracts, and paying for results are more like three relationships evolving step by step; the switch will not happen simultaneously in all enterprises.

And precisely because this loop requires frontline experience to flow quickly back into models, agent platforms, and product development, big tech companies may be the more suitable leaders for the deployment of FDEs in China.

Palantir calls this mechanism "reverse backpropagation". The FDE is not meant to manually solve problems for each customer forever, but to bring recurring problems back into the R&D system. If a gap that required engineers in the first project becomes a standard platform feature in the second, field service can become progressively lighter.

A requirement raised by one enterprise may just be customization, but permission, evals, high-availability, and data governance problems encountered repeatedly across multiple industries are worth being built into standard capabilities. A platform company can observe such repetition across customers and also has the R&D resources to feed it into the product roadmap. If the frontline can only implement within a predefined scope but cannot influence the product, its work is closer to advanced delivery.

Moreover, the production problems of enterprise AI rarely stay on a single layer. A slow response may stem from model inference, compute scheduling, or database issues; an agent unable to enter a workflow may also involve data governance, security permissions, and production operations. The FDE does not have to solve every layer personally, but must find the corresponding product teams within the organization.

Take Alibaba Cloud: it has a product system spanning infrastructure, model services, AI development platforms, databases, security, and operations, so field problems have a fairly complete return path. The meaning of full-stack is not to stuff more products into customer solutions, but to ensure cross-layer problems can find their owners and have the chance to be consolidated into models, agent platforms, and cloud products, so they no longer need to be re-solved in the next project.

Once applications actually run, they continuously generate demand for model calls, tokens, compute, databases, and platform services. Cloud vendors can use engineering services to drive adoption and then earn returns from sustained usage, so they do not have to rely entirely on selling engineer person-days to cover costs.

Looking through the history of the Industrial Revolution and economic history, there is a passage strikingly similar to today's AI deployment.

In the late 19th century, factories began using electric motors, but many owners initially merely replaced steam engines with electric motors, leaving the belts, drive shafts, and machine layouts in the factory untouched.

When economist Paul David studied the history of electrification, he pointed out that the greater productivity gains came later: once each machine had its own small motor, factories no longer had to be arranged around a central drive shaft, and machine placement, material transport, and production processes could all be rearranged.

Real productivity gains don't come on the day a model is released, but at the moment AI rearranges workflows according to business logic—and the FDE is the person driving that rearrangement.
