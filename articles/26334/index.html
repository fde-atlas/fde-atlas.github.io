FDE Demand Intake: Who Assigns Work and When to Hand Off to the Product Team

Original title: FDE 需求准入：谁能派活、何时移交产品团队

FDE Times · 2026-11-19 · 5 min read

Summary

Drawing on handbooks and talks from Ramp, PostHog, AgileLab's Witboost, and Field-to-Product, this article lays out an intake process that decides who can assign work to FDEs and when they must hand it back. Requests should come through the account owner, with a named customer-side acceptor. Before coding, FDEs fill a five-line deliverable (problem, customer value, expected outcome, rough size, why it fits FDE scope) reviewed by a Product Owner. Work must stay small and bounded with short, credible effort estimates; when it crosses that boundary or stalls on issues only the product team can fix, FDEs stop, document findings, and hand over with an upstream PR. The piece closes with common mistakes and advice for showcasing this skill in resumes and interviews.

Key points

  • Requests enter only via the account owner, never direct customer messages.
  • A five-line deliverable is written before any code, reviewed by a Product Owner.
  • FDE work must be small, bounded, and credibly short in effort.
  • Stop and hand over when only the product team can fix the blocker.
  • Preserve field lessons via docs and upstream PRs during handoff.

A five-line intake form plus one clear boundary tells the FDE when to stop and hand the work over to the product team.

Key takeaways

  • Requests come in through the account owner, with the person on the customer side responsible for accepting the results designated from the start.
  • Every request must have a traceable deliverable stating why it belongs to the FDE, reviewed by the Product Owner.
  • When work crosses the boundary, or gets stuck on a problem only the product team can solve, stop, attach the documentation and an upstream PR, and hand it over.

At Ramp, the Forward Deployed Engineer (FDE) team takes over major blocking issues from account managers for existing customers and prospects.

This detail corresponds to a situation every FDE has encountered: the most expensive part of a request often happens before the first line of code is written. Who is making the request, what the customer actually wants, and whether the work is really yours to do. Answering these three questions too slowly can burn a whole week on work that belongs to the product team.

A request intake mechanism is a systematic way to answer these three questions. Without it, the FDE becomes the dumping ground for every urgent request, with no one weighing priorities. The intake mechanism has three parts: who can assign work, how requests get written down, and when to hand the work back to the team that owns it.

Requests should come in through the relationship owner

At Ramp, the entry point is the account manager. PostHog's FDE handbook reflects the same logic from the other end: at the end of a project, the FDE hands the customer relationship back to the account owner.

Since the customer relationship begins and ends with the account owner, it makes sense for requests to come through them. When a customer messages the FDE directly, no one on your side can judge how the request weighs against other commitments to that customer. So the "requester" field in your backlog should be the account owner, even if the idea was your own.

The customer side also needs a point of contact. PostHog writes that FDE work requires a clearly designated owner on the customer side. Only when this designated person approves the final deliverable is the project considered done; each milestone also needs customer sign-off before it begins, whether or not the project is paid.

Write five lines before writing code

AgileLab's Witboost handbook requires the FDE to record a traceable entry stating the problem, the customer value, the expected outcome, a rough size estimate, and the reason this work falls within the FDE's scope.

The Product Owner reviews each entry to confirm its value and alignment with the product direction. These few lines sound simple, and precisely because they are simple, writing them exposes whatever you haven't thought through.

Here's a hypothetical example. An account owner forwards a request from a logistics customer: "Let us export event data to a data warehouse, with warehouse codes attached." Written as a deliverable, it looks like this:

requester: account-owner          # 不是客户直接发来的消息
problem: 客户的延迟发货报表需要从另一个文件手动关联仓库编码
customer_value: 省去客户运营团队每天早上的手动关联
expected_outcome: 每条导出的事件都带有 warehouse_id 字段
rough_size: S — 在现有导出器中增加一个字段
why_fde: 小而有界，几天内完成
customer_owner: 客户方数据负责人（验收结果）
boundary_check:
  small_and_bounded: yes
  short_credible_effort: yes
  only_product_team_can_fix: no

The five lines from problem to why_fde are taken directly from Witboost's checklist. The other three fields aren't on the checklist, but are well worth adding.

The requester field records the rule from the first part. The customer_owner field designates the person who accepts the results, so you won't have to ask who has sign-off authority when acceptance time comes. The boundary_check field leads into the final part: when the work must be handed back to the owning team.

What work can an FDE complete quickly?

Witboost draws the boundary in one sentence: FDE work must be small and bounded, with a short and credible implementation estimate. PostHog adds another: when a project gets stuck on a problem only the product team can solve, the FDE hands the work over to the owning team.

QuestionFDE keeps goingStop and hand over
SizeSmall and boundedExceeds the agreed boundary
EffortEstimate is short and credibleCan no longer be estimated
Who can solve itThe FDE can do it themselvesOnly the owning product team can solve it
Product directionAlready confirmed by the Product OwnerRequires a new direction decision

Back to the example. Halfway through, you discover that warehouse_id simply isn't available in the event stream, and getting it would require modifying a shared schema owned by another team.

The "who can solve it" row has just switched columns. For work that crosses the boundary, Witboost is explicit: stop and move into the standard planning process.

Stopping at this point is the correct engineering decision, not a failure. Update the deliverable, note why the size grew from S, and tell the account owner before the customer asks.

Don't lose the lessons when handing over

How to hand over depends on the receiving team. PostHog notes that some teams prefer asynchronous written briefs, while others want you to attend their standups. Asking the schema team directly which they prefer is faster than guessing.

The easiest thing to lose is the lessons learned from the customer. The Field-to-Product pattern for FDEs advocates feeding frontline fixes back into the product as pull requests. Grizen describes an approach where the FDE first builds for a single customer, then hands ownership back along with documentation and a usage guide.

Insight Partners takes a broader view: once the results are validated, ownership transfers to the team responsible for productizing and scaling the solution. If you already shipped a temporary patch on the customer side to meet a deadline, leave a short document explaining why the customer needed the warehouse codes, and open a PR or ticket for the owning team.

Common mistakes

The first mistake is taking requests through the customer's private messages to seem responsive. You win over one person, but the account owner loses visibility. The second is leaving the size field blank, only to discover afterward that the "small" job actually reached into another team's territory.

The third is harder to spot: the work has grown, but you push through anyway because you already promised the customer. The last is failing to hand the customer relationship back to the account owner at the end of the project, so the customer keeps coming to you directly, bypassing the intake mechanism you just built.

Showing this capability on your resume

When reading FDE job descriptions, watch for phrases like intake, scoping, collaborating with product teams, and incorporating customer feedback into the roadmap. They tell you whether the company already has an intake mechanism, or is waiting for you to build one.

In interviews, telling a story about stopping halfway and handing work over to the owning team is usually more persuasive than one about working overnight. Your resume should show verifiable results, such as how many customer requests you turned into upstream PRs, or the deliverable template you built for the whole team.

Exercise for this week: take the vaguest request on your plate and work through the five lines and the two boundary questions. If you can't fill in any line, the next step is to ask the account owner; opening your editor can wait.
