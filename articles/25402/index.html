FDE interview tips: think aloud and drop a hypothesis

FDE Times · 2026-11-10 · 6 min read

Summary

This article argues that FDE interviews, which mostly simulate vague customer conversations rather than coding tests, score how you reason, not whether you reach a specific answer. Drawing on guides from Aced, techinterview.org, and Google's SRE book, it recommends voicing hypotheses aloud, labeling them as assumptions, and openly discarding them when evidence contradicts them—revising an earlier decision counts as a positive signal. It offers a four-part hypothesis template (assumption, reason, expected result, check) and a three-part rejection template, walks through a sample call about doubled late-order rates, and lists common mistakes: thinking silently, switching direction without announcing it, apologizing for revisions, and clinging to familiar causes.

Key points

  • FDE interview rounds are conversations simulating ambiguous customer work, not coding tests.
  • Interviewers score the path to a solution; silent thinking earns no credit.
  • Discarding a wrong hypothesis out loud is a positive signal, not a weakness.
  • A good hypothesis states assumption, reason, expected observable result, and how to check it.
  • Interviewers deliberately add new requirements midway to test whether your design bends or breaks.

In brief

  • FDE interview rounds are mostly conversation, because they simulate a job where requirements are always vague.
  • Interviewers score how you reach a solution, and going back to revise an earlier decision counts as a good signal.
  • Thinking in silence is a bad signal: nobody can score reasoning they cannot hear.

The interviewer, playing the customer, opens the final round with a vague complaint. Ten seconds later you already have a hypothesis. What gets scored is not only whether that hypothesis is right. It is whether you say it out loud, and whether, five minutes later, you are willing to say it was wrong.

This is not a rare setup. Aced’s interview guide describes Cognition’s final rounds as live customer simulations, such as a pitch to executives or a case-study call.

Techinterview.org explains that FDE interviews reproduce the ambiguity of customer work, so most of the time is spent talking rather than writing code. One thing the round tests is whether you can reason aloud while the situation is still unclear.

Candidates used to LeetCode-style interviews, where only the final answer counts, lose points where they least expect it. Aced puts it bluntly: interviewers score how you move towards a solution, not whether you arrive at a particular one. Your job is to make that path audible to the person grading you.

Why does a wrong guess earn points?

Treat the round as a debugging session. According to techinterview.org, FDE interviews reward a systematic debugging loop: reproduce the problem, form a hypothesis, narrow it down, then verify the fix.

Google’s SRE book describes troubleshooting in the same rhythm: repeatedly proposing possible causes and testing them, using an experimental method to keep or eliminate each hypothesis.

In that loop, ruling out a hypothesis is as much a step forward as proposing one. The SRE book also warns against latching on to causes seen in the past. In an interview room, the easiest way to show you do not suffer from that habit is to let the grader hear you discard an idea you believed at the start.

That is why Aced advises treating your first answer as a draft, revising it as new information arrives, and stresses that going back to change an earlier decision is a good signal, not a weakness.

Techinterview.org reports that in Palantir-style decomposition rounds, the interviewer deliberately adds a requirement midway to see whether your design bends or breaks. So prepare to receive new facts rather than hoping they never come.

A sample call, line by line

Take this prompt. The “customer” is the head of operations at a delivery company, who says: “The dashboard shows the late-order rate doubled since Monday. I need to know what’s going on, now.” No spec, no schema, just one worrying number.

A weak candidate goes quiet for thirty seconds, then proposes rewriting the pipeline. A strong candidate voices a hypothesis and labels it as an assumption, just as Aced recommends:

“First, this is only an assumption. Orders may not actually be later; the way ‘late’ is calculated may have changed, say a deploy that shifted the time zone. If so, end customers won’t be complaining any more than usual. Can I ask: has anyone changed anything in the reporting system since Monday?”

The customer deflects: “I don’t know what engineering has been doing. But the call center has had far more complaint calls this week.”

This is the moment that most clearly shows how you reach a solution. The strong candidate does not switch direction quietly, and says plainly: “Then we drop the display-bug hypothesis. If it were only a calculation error, end customers wouldn’t be calling the call center.”

And continues: “Now I lean towards orders really being late. There are two branches: one region is slow, or the whole system is slow. I’d check by region first, because if that’s right the fix is much smaller in scope.”

That last sentence follows techinterview.org’s advice: at a fork, say why you are taking this branch.

Then the interviewer drops a new requirement: “My boss needs a report by Friday.” The strong candidate updates the plan aloud: today, narrow it down by region and confirm the cause with a specific sample of orders; the root-cause fix waits until the report is in.

Write it on the board so the grader can keep up

The SRE book advises writing down which ideas you had, which tests you ran and what you saw. In an interview, those notes let the grader see and trace each step of your reasoning. A board for the call above looks like this:

Hypothesis Test Result
The “late” calculation changed; orders are not really late Ask whether the system changed and whether end customers are complaining Rejected: the call center is getting more complaints
Orders really are late in one region Break the late rate down by region or warehouse Waiting for data
The whole system is slow Check only if the second hypothesis fails Not yet tested

The row marked “Rejected” is worth as much as the row waiting for data. It shows you rule things out on evidence, not on gut feeling.

The four-part template and how to practice it

Every hypothesis you voice should have four parts: “My assumption is X, because Y. If X is true, we’ll see Z. To check, I need to ask about or look at W.” Z matters most. Without it, your hypothesis cannot be disproved, and the grader cannot tell when you would change your mind.

When the evidence points the other way, use a three-part rejection: “This rules out X, because it contradicts Z. Moving to the next hypothesis. I’m choosing it first because…” The sentence is short, yet it shows the grader your evidence, your flexibility and your reason for prioritizing.

Techinterview.org suggests a concrete way to practice: run a decomposition round aloud with a friend playing an evasive stakeholder who refuses to give a clear spec. Ask that friend to deliberately offer information that contradicts your first hypothesis, and to throw in a new requirement at the ten-minute mark.

The mistakes that sink strong candidates

The most common mistake is thinking in silence. Aced calls silence in a decomposition interview a negative signal, because interviewers cannot score reasoning they cannot hear. Your thinking may be entirely correct, but on the scorecard it is still blank.

The second mistake is changing direction silently: you start talking about a new hypothesis without announcing that you have dropped the old one. The listener sees you jumping around, not ruling things out. The third is apologizing when you revise an idea, as if it were a failure, when Aced counts going back to an earlier decision as a plus.

The last mistake is subtler: clinging to a familiar cause (“last time it was the cache too”) and bending every new fact to fit it. That is precisely the trap the SRE book warns about.

Put the skill on your résumé

This skill is not only for getting through interviews. At a real client, the spec is just as vague, and saying clearly what you are ruling out helps the whole room keep up with you.

When you write your CV or prepare stories for the behavioral round, tell an incident through the three columns above: what you suspected first, which test ruled it out, and what evidence led you to the real cause.

A CV line such as “ruled out a display bug using call-center data, narrowed the incident to one region within two hours” shows a reader how you work far better than words like “proactive” or “strong thinker”.

Next time a hypothesis collapses in front of the interviewer, do not rush to cover it up. Say where it went wrong and which direction you are taking next, because that path is what gets scored.
