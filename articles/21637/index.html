【True Story】Why 2 Out of 9 Deals Fell Through | A Case Study in Failure

Original title: 【実録】9件中2件が未達だった理由｜失敗ケーススタディ

デファクトリーNote｜事業開発/ソフトウェア/プロダクト開発ならDefactory株式会社 · 2026-09-08 · 7 min read

Summary

Shohei Tokumitsu, CEO of DeFactory, candidly reviews 2 failed projects out of 9 FDE engagements, arguing the failures reflect common structural problems rather than anomalies. He classifies causes into three types: flawed pre-contract agreement design, delayed or insufficient information from the client, and coordination failures between both parties. Case 1 failed because KPI definitions and pass conditions were never pinned down in writing before signing, letting expectations drift. Case 2 was detected early by his own side, but the contract contained no mechanism to formally pause and realign, so the project finished without reaching its targets. He describes current countermeasures: minimum two-person staffing, documenting exclusions and acceptance criteria pre-contract, monthly reviews, sprint-based incremental sign-offs, and demos of working software.

Key points

  • 2 of 9 FDE projects missed their targets; the author shares both cases openly.
  • Causes fall into three types: pre-contract agreement errors, client communication gaps, and joint coordination failures.
  • Case 1 lesson: define KPI measurement and pass conditions in writing before signing.
  • Case 2 lesson: contracts need built-in milestones for pausing and reassessing.
  • Current practice: two-person teams, documented exclusions, monthly reviews, sprint-level sign-offs.

Of the nine FDE projects I have been involved in as a practitioner, two fell short of the outcomes we originally envisioned. With real names and company names withheld, I will categorize what happened and share it here.

"We've done nine projects and all nine succeeded"—it would sound good if I could say that, but the reality is different. Two of the nine did not reach the outcomes we originally envisioned (※1).

※1 We define "not reaching the outcome" as a failure at our company.

In this article, I will look back as candidly as possible at what happened in those two projects.

I believe there is value in sharing these openly precisely because they are not exceptional failures but typical structures that could occur at many client sites.

Shohei Tokumitsu

Representative Director of DeFactory. Based on experience leading 0-to-1 new business development multiple times, both in-house and at other companies, in the software development domain, as well as driving business growth, he currently works as an FDE consultant/delivery lead and product manager (PdM), and also handles recruiting (FDE, upstream SE, PM, PMO, CPO, CTO, consulting).

1. Three Categories of the Main Causes of Shortfalls

Looking back at the two projects, the causes of the shortfalls can be broadly organized into three categories.

  • Misjudging the points of agreement before the contract (our responsibility)
  • Insufficient or delayed information sharing from the client (a client-side matter)
  • Coordination failures between the client and vendor (a problem of commitment on both sides)

The two shortfalls were not caused by any single one of these three categories alone, but by different combinations of them.

Case 1: The "Failed to Nail Things Down at the Entry Point" Pattern

Main cause: ① Misjudging the points of agreement before the contract

What was ambiguous in this case was the "definition" of the KPI and the "conditions for clearing it."

At the kickoff, in a positive atmosphere on both sides, we discussed "let's aim for this KPI," but we had not fully pinned down before the contract what exactly the figures would be measured against, or what state would count as "achieved."

As the project progressed, the expectations of both sides gradually drifted apart. Looking back, this was a matter where, before doubting the other party, we should first have asked ourselves (our company) how thoroughly we had nailed things down. The frank main cause was the weakness of our agreement design.

Lesson: The importance of "nailing things down at the entry point" (i.e., communicating what cannot be done)

This lesson connects directly to item ③, "clarifying judgment criteria," of the "Four Principles of FDE KPI Design." The practical checklist of agreeing in writing on the KPI definition and clearing conditions before the contract was born from the reflections on this case.

★CHECK

  • What exactly will this KPI figure be measured against? (definition)
  • What figure or state counts as "achieved"? (clearing conditions)
  • Have both parties agreed on these criteria in writing (contract, meeting minutes, etc.)?

Case 2: The "Noticed It, But Couldn't Correct It in Time" Pattern

Main cause: ② Insufficient information sharing from the client + ③ Mutual coordination failures

In this case, we noticed relatively early on that something felt off about the project not proceeding as planned. It was our side, not the client, that noticed first.

However, another unexpected event then compounded the situation. Information sharing from the client was slower than expected, and at the same time discrepancies arose in coordination with us, and as a result time passed with it remaining ambiguous whose problem it should be treated as.

Looking back, at that point we should have forcibly created an opportunity to "pause and align both parties' understanding." However, because the criteria for making that decision were nowhere explicitly stated in the contract, the project proceeded in a state of "having noticed, but finding no formal reason to stop." The project was ultimately completed, but it fell short of the outcomes we originally envisioned.

This is not an unusual failure. I feel that in projects where the criteria for deciding whether to continue or withdraw at each milestone are not explicitly stated at the contract stage, this is by no means rare.

Lesson: The operational reality that "even when you sense a problem, you may not be able to fully respond to the variable factors"

This lesson connects directly to Principle 6 of the "Six Principles of FDE Consulting Judgment"—"Is what was agreed upon documented in writing?"—specifically the decision to continue or reassess at each milestone.

★CHECK

  • Was there a mechanism for "pausing" to correct course once the problem was noticed?
  • If a key person on the team changes, how is knowledge transfer ensured?
  • Is a forum for deciding at each milestone whether to continue or reassess designed into the contract stage?

Even though both were "shortfalls," the necessary countermeasures are completely different between a case where things were misjudged at the entry point and a case where the problem was noticed partway through but there was no mechanism for course correction.

3. The Structure of Shortfalls, Seen Through the "Iceberg Model"

Looking back at the two projects, what emerges is the contrast between "the visible main causes" and the structure that may have lain beneath the surface.

Visible main causes: Concrete events that can be individually identified in hindsight, such as ambiguity in KPI agreement, delayed information sharing, and coordination failures.

Potential underlying factors: The fact that a "mechanism for pausing" was not designed into the contract stage itself. I believe this may have existed in common behind the individual events of both projects. However, this is merely a hypothesis drawn from reflection and cannot be stated with certainty.

Summary

  • The two shortfalls out of nine projects were not exceptional accidents but typical structures that could occur at many client sites
  • The lesson from Case 1 connects to agreement design before the contract, and the lesson from Case 2 connects to the post-contract "mechanism for pausing"
  • The points to check before and after commissioning have been turned into practical checklists in the "Six Principles of FDE Consulting Judgment" and the "Four Principles of FDE KPI Design"

4. How We Currently Operate After the Contract (Delivery Method)

Based on the two cases above, we reviewed our operations before and after contracting. It is not a special mechanism; we are simply diligently addressing the issues revealed in Cases 1 and 2.

Structure: A minimum of two people, avoiding dependence on individuals

  • Enter engagements with a minimum team of two: one consultant plus at least one developer capable of handling upstream processes
  • Never create a situation with a single key person. Address the risk of the "person-dependent structure" mentioned in Principle 6 from the stage of team design

Before contracting: Document even what will not be done

  • Before contracting, clearly state and document what will not be handled or cannot be done
  • Define completion criteria explicitly, down to test cases and acceptance criteria
  • This is exactly what was ambiguous in Case 1. When Principle 3 of the four KPI design principles, "clarifying judgment criteria," is put into actual practice, it takes this form

After project start: Accumulate small agreements

  • Based on the roadmap established at the start, hold regular monthly meetings and submit business reports in our designated format
  • At the regular meetings, visualize progress and deviations against goals, and disclose countermeasures and risks together
  • Divide the project into sprints of a few weeks each and accumulate agreements on the completion of small deliverables. Rather than judging a large deliverable all at once, break it into units that allow course correction along the way
  • Share and submit implemented content visually, so progress can be verified through "working things"
  • Conduct regular interviews with on-site staff to pick up nuances that reports alone cannot capture

The lesson from Case 2 was that "even if you sense a problem, you cannot act without criteria for when to stop." Monthly meetings, sprint-based agreements, and on-site interviews are mechanisms for building those criteria into operations in advance.
