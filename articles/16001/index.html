FDE vs Consultants: The People Who Write Proposals and the People Who Write Code

Original title: FDE vs 咨询顾问：写方案的人和写代码的人

FDE中国 · 2026-08-14 · 8 min read

Summary

This article compares management consultants with Forward Deployed Engineers (FDEs), arguing the fundamental divide is deliverable nature: consultants produce abstractions of reality (slides, roadmaps) while FDEs produce working systems running in client environments. This drives differences in time granularity, responsibility boundaries, skills, and business models—consulting sells judgment per person-day, while FDE work often uses small pilots that expand after validation, trading service for product penetration. The author notes convergence: BCG X, Deloitte and McKinsey QuantumBlack now hire FDEs, while OpenAI launched a delivery organization with over $4 billion in funding and acquired Tomoro. For consultants, the transition's hardest step is turning judgment into running code; in China's light-code consulting tradition, planning around engineering gaps is essential.

Key points

  • Consultants abstract reality into recommendations; FDEs execute until systems run in production.
  • FDEs bear end-to-end responsibility; consultants can deliver correct plans for failed projects.
  • a16z frames FDE services as trading gross margin for product penetration and moats.
  • Consulting firms are adding FDE roles; OpenAI built a delivery org with $4B+ backing.
  • Self-test: estimate days to implement one key recommendation in code.

Consultants are probably the role most similar to FDEs in temperament yet most distant in craft. Both go deep into the client's business, both must build trust with executives, both can command respectable rates, and even the work rhythm is similar—on-site engagements, sprints, presentations. But if you dig one level deeper and ask, "What does the client have in hand when the project ends?" the divide appears immediately: consultants leave behind proposals, consensus, and roadmaps; FDEs leave behind systems running in the client's environment. The Forward Deployed Engineer (FDE) role was invented by Palantir and has gradually spread to OpenAI, Anthropic, and other companies, and its underlying paradigm is precisely an engineering rewrite of traditional consulting services. This article compares the two paradigms item by item, helping you make a clearer-headed judgment in career choices or organizational design.

A fundamental difference: abstraction versus execution

The essence of consulting is reducing the uncertainty of client decisions. Through interviews, research, and benchmarking analysis, consultants abstract messy reality into frameworks and recommendations; the client takes the proposal and organizes resources to execute it themselves. Consulting's leverage comes from "the replicability of judgment"—one methodology can serve many clients, and execution is borne by the client.

The essence of the FDE is completing the hardest stretch of execution on the client's behalf. FDEs write code, do integrations, and run evals directly in the client's environment until the solution runs stably in production. Palantir's FDSEs work in pairs with Deployment Strategists, with engineers "working within the client's environment, on the company's tech stack, until the solution runs in production" (analysis from everestgrp.com). Its leverage comes from "personally eliminating uncertainty"—what you deliver is not "this is what should be done," but "it has been done."

This difference radiates downward into almost every dimension. The most intuitive one is time granularity: consulting advances milestones in units of "weeks," while FDEs debug and fix in units of "days or even hours." Someone writing a proposal can tolerate spending three weeks revising a report; someone writing code is facing a service that went down again tonight.

Dimension one: deliverables—PPT and code are two different kinds of commitment

The difference in deliverables determines the risk structure of the two roles. A consultant's proposal can be questioned, shelved, or revised, but rarely "goes down"; once an FDE's system is live, every outage carries their name. This leads to an often-overlooked corollary: the validator of consulting deliverables is the client's judgment, while the validator of FDE deliverables is objective operational results. The former can improve its pass rate through narrative skill; the latter cannot be passed by storytelling at all.

For AI projects, this difference is especially critical. The effectiveness boundaries of LLM applications depend heavily on real data and real workflows, and evaluation errors at the proposal stage are enormous—this is one of the contexts behind what the MIT NANDA report calls "95% of enterprise GenAI pilots show no measurable business impact" (cited by thenewstack.io). Under such uncertainty, the two-stage approach of "write the proposal first, then execute" often fails, because the most critical assumptions in the proposal can only be tested once the code runs. The FDE model moves testing earlier and internalizes execution—essentially a patch for the parts of the consulting paradigm that fail in the AI domain.

Dimension two: methodology—framework reuse versus problem reframing

The core of consulting methodology is reuse: industry frameworks, benchmark libraries, interview guides—making the twentieth project much faster than the first. A consultant's individual capability shows in the speed of "fitting new problems into old frameworks," and in reframing frameworks when necessary.

The core of FDE methodology is also reuse, but the object of reuse is different: not frameworks, but engineering assets and delivery playbooks—how to build eval baselines, how to define permission models, how to do data ingestion, how to monitor after launch. Palantir places FDSEs within its business development organization and trains them at scale through internal bootcamps (official statement on palantir.com), showing that this methodology can be taught in a structured way rather than relying on individual talent. Practitioners in China who want to systematically fill this gap can refer to the on-site FDE Knowledge Guide and Scoping Handbook, which cover the main stages from requirements definition to delivery handoff.

Notably, the two methodologies overlap heavily on "problem definition." Good consultants and good FDEs both refuse to build exactly to spec: when a client says they want an intelligent Q&A system, both will first probe, "Which step's time cost are you actually trying to reduce?" The difference is in the next step—the consultant writes that judgment as a section heading; the FDE turns it into the first test case in the eval set.

Dimension three: scope of responsibility and the structure of trust

A consultant's trust comes from "judgment": the client believes you have seen enough industry samples to point out blind spots they cannot see. This trust is vertical—aimed at the client's decision-makers. An FDE's trust comes from "reliability": the client believes that what you promise will happen, and that if something breaks you will fix it overnight. This trust must be aimed both at decision-makers and at the front line—on-site engineers deal daily with the client's business operators and IT teams, and their word of mouth determines whether you can stay on site.

The difference in scope of responsibility has another hidden consequence: a consultant can deliver a "correct proposal" while the project ultimately fails, because poor execution is the client's problem; the FDE has no such exit—delivery failure is delivery failure. So for people moving from consulting to FDE roles, the first psychological adjustment is accepting that "accountability for outcomes is non-negotiable." The on-site Expectation Management Handbook discusses how to clarify the scope of responsibility early in a project—this is precisely the most valuable transferable skill for consultants moving into FDE roles.

Dimension four: business model—person-days, outcomes, and product penetration

The consulting business model is mature and clear: charge by person-day or project, scale by having partners replicate teams, with high and predictable gross margins. When discussing the FDE phenomenon, a16z described the service-based delivery of traditional software as "trading gross margin for a moat"—the services themselves don't make money; what you earn is the product penetration and data accumulation that services bring (a16z.com, "Services Led Growth"). This is the deepest divide between the two paradigms: consulting sells services as products, while FDE treats services as the installation process of a product.

This leads to differences in contract structure. Consulting contracts almost always follow a fixed billing model; projects involving FDEs often start small and expand after validation—Palantir's bootcamp model is a classic example: within days, a working application is built on the customer's real data, and once the value is recognized, the contract rolls and expands (everestgrp.com). For customers, this "see it first, then scale up" structure significantly reduces procurement risk; for vendors, it ties revenue growth to delivery quality.

Paradigm convergence: two curves are crossing

If you remember only one judgment from this article, remember this: consulting and FDE are not substitutes—they are two curves that are penetrating each other.

First, consulting firms are moving into engineering. BCG X, Deloitte, and McKinsey's QuantumBlack have all opened FDE or similar roles (as compiled by fdeinterviews.com); Deloitte has even posted jobs explicitly titled Partner FDE; ServiceNow and Accenture jointly launched an FDE program in 2026 (reported by thenewstack.io). The consulting industry clearly sees that customers no longer pay a premium for "knowing what to do"—they only pay a premium for "getting it done."

Second, AI companies are moving into consulting. In May 2026, OpenAI established an independent delivery organization with over $4 billion in initial investment, and acquired the applied AI company Tomoro, bringing in roughly 150 senior delivery experts (announced on openai.com, reported by ciodive.com). Its shape resembles a consulting firm—large numbers of on-site staff, organized by customer—but what it delivers at its core is working code. These two curves are moving toward each other, and their eventual intersection is "a consulting-style organization with engineering delivery at its core."

How to choose: advice for professionals from different backgrounds

If you are a consultant: your structured thinking, trust from senior client executives, and industry knowledge are scarce assets in the FDE market, but you must fill in the hardest gap—turning judgment into a working system. A pragmatic starting point is to pick a business scenario you know well and independently complete a small project on real data, including evals and deployment, not just an analysis report. For transition paths, see FDE Transition Path and FDE Career Growth Path.

If you are an engineer considering a consulting-firm FDE role: FDE roles at consulting firms usually come with stronger client resources and deeper industry expertise, but be sure to check how much of the actual work is engineering—"a consultant who writes code" and "an engineer who understands the business" are two different jobs.

If you are designing the organizational approach for a client's AI project: a pragmatic hybrid structure is consulting-style discovery plus FDE-style delivery—use consulting methods up front for problem definition and prioritization, then immediately switch to on-site engineering delivery to avoid a gap between the plan and its execution.

Summary

Consultants answer "what should be done," while FDEs answer "how to get it done"—and in the highly uncertain field of deploying large language models, the answer to the second question often rewrites the first. This doesn't mean plans are worthless; it means the authority of a plan must be backed by a working system. The next time you face the choice of "plan first or prototype first," remember the answer the industry is giving: turn the most uncertain assumption into code first.
