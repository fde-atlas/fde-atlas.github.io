What Are Hallucination Countermeasures? Design Procedures to Reduce Generative AI Errors Used by FDEs in the Field

Original title: ハルシネーション対策とは？FDEが現場で使う生成AIの誤回答を減らす設計手順

FDE総研 · 2026-10-06 · 10 min read

Summary

This Japanese article explains how Forward Deployed Engineers (FDEs) should design defenses against LLM hallucinations in business systems. It argues hallucinations stem from how language models generate text probabilistically, citing OpenAI's September 2025 paper "Why Language Models Hallucinate," which claims accuracy-only evaluation encourages guessing over abstaining. Since hallucinations cannot be eliminated, the article proposes layered defenses across input (RAG, prompt constraints, structured data), output (mandatory citations, self-verification, LLM-as-a-Judge), and operations (human review, restricting use cases). Countermeasures should match business risk levels, with high-stakes decisions kept human-only. It also covers evaluation metrics like faithfulness, accuracy, and appropriate abstention rate, plus continuous regression testing.

Key points

  • Hallucinations are rooted in LLM mechanics; OpenAI's 2025 paper says accuracy-only scoring rewards guessing over abstaining.
  • No single fix exists; combine input, output, and operational layers of defense.
  • Classify use cases by error impact; highest-risk domains (medical, legal, credit) keep humans as final decision-makers.
  • Fetch facts like inventory numbers from databases or APIs rather than letting the LLM generate them, following Palantir AIP's ontology approach.
  • Evaluate with faithfulness, accuracy, and appropriate abstention rate, and keep adding real error cases to regression test sets.

Hallucination is the phenomenon where generative AI outputs factually incorrect or unsupported content in plausible-sounding text. When an FDE (Forward Deployed Engineer) integrates generative AI into business systems, countermeasures against these incorrect answers must be designed across three layers: technology, data, and operations.

What Is Hallucination? What Causes Generative AI to Give Incorrect Answers?

Hallucination refers to output that sounds natural but is factually wrong. The main cause lies in the language model's mechanism of "predicting the next word probabilistically."

Large language models (LLMs) are not systems that "search for correct facts and answer." They are systems that "arrange words that are highly probable given the context." As a result, when asked about information not in the training data or information newer than the training cutoff, they may produce plausible-sounding answers.

What Types of Hallucinations Are There?

Hallucinations are easier to organize when divided into two categories: "contradicting the provided information" and "contradicting facts."

ClassificationDescriptionTypical Business Example
Input-contradiction type (Faithfulness violation)Output that deviates from the provided documents or instructionsWhen summarizing internal regulations, conditions not in the regulations are added
Fact-contradiction type (Factuality violation)Output that deviates from real-world factsCiting nonexistent papers, case law, or API specifications
Reasoning-leap typePremises are correct but the conclusion is derived incorrectlyProducing incorrect aggregates or comparisons from correct figures

In FDE fieldwork, the input-contradiction type is especially problematic. Even when correct documents are provided via RAG (Retrieval-Augmented Generation), the model may fill in gaps with knowledge outside those documents.

Why Doesn't Generative AI Say "I Don't Know"?

It has been pointed out that LLMs tend to "benefit from answering, even by guessing, rather than not answering" during training and evaluation. This is why they answer even things they don't know.

In September 2025, OpenAI published the paper "Why Language Models Hallucinate." The paper argues that evaluation methods that score only on correct-answer rates encourage models to "guess rather than abstain." In other words, hallucination is not merely a bug but a property rooted in how models are built. Therefore, in practice, rather than aiming to "eliminate it entirely," a realistic design approach is to "assume it will occur, reduce it, detect it, and limit the damage."

What Methods Exist for Hallucination Mitigation?

The basic approach is to combine countermeasures across three stages: "strengthening input grounding," "verifying output," and "handling it through operations."

No single method is a decisive solution on its own. Multi-layered defense, stacking multiple layers, is the premise. Representative methods are summarized in the table below.

StageMethodPurposeMain Cost
InputRAG (Retrieval-Augmented Generation)Provide grounding documents and have answers based on themSearch infrastructure, document preparation
InputPrompt constraintsExplicitly state "answer 'unknown' if not in the documents"Small
InputStructured data integrationHave the model directly reference DB or API valuesData model design
OutputMandatory citations and sourcesHave the model indicate the grounding passages for answersSmall to medium
OutputSelf-verification and regenerationGenerate multiple times and check consistencyIncreased inference cost
OutputJudgment by a separate modelUse LLM-as-a-Judge to check consistency with groundingInference cost, managing judgment accuracy
OperationsHuman-in-the-LoopHumans approve important outputsLabor cost, lead time
OperationsLimiting use casesDo not use AI for decisions where errors have major impactMissed opportunities

What Changes with RAG and Prompt Design?

RAG is a method that makes the model answer based on provided documents rather than its own memory, and is effective at reducing fact-contradiction type incorrect answers.

RAG is a concept proposed in 2020 by Lewis et al. at Meta (then Facebook AI Research), and is now a standard architecture for business AI. However, if the search retrieves irrelevant documents, the model is influenced by them and produces incorrect answers. In practice, a common observation across many teams is that tuning retrieval accuracy (chunking, metadata, reranking) matters as much as prompt engineering.

On the prompt side, the basics are to explicitly state the following three points:

  • Answers must be limited to the scope of the provided documents
  • If no grounding is found, answer "cannot be determined from the documents"
  • For each answer, indicate the source document name and relevant passage

How Do You Build an Output Verification Mechanism?

For output verification, mechanically check "consistency with grounding" and "whether results vary across multiple generations," and route suspicious answers to humans.

Known academic methods include Self-Consistency (Wang et al., 2022), which generates multiple answers to the same question and takes a majority vote, and Chain-of-Verification (Meta research team, 2023), which has the model generate verification questions and check its own answers. In business systems, rather than implementing these in full, it is often easier to balance cost and effectiveness by starting with lightweight verification, such as rule-based checks that cited passages actually exist in the documents.

How Do FDEs Design Hallucination Mitigation in the Field?

FDE estimates "the impact of errors on the business" before considering model performance, and determines the depth of countermeasures accordingly.

FDE is a role popularized by Palantir, in which practitioners embed themselves in the customer's operations and handle everything from data integration to application implementation. The value an FDE can deliver in generative AI projects lies not in prompt tuning itself, but in being able to reach agreement with the customer on "which business decisions require what level of certainty."

How do you determine the level of countermeasures based on risk?

Classify use cases along two axes—"the impact of errors" and "whether humans can check the output"—and apply stronger verification and human approval to use cases with greater impact.

Risk levelExample use casesRecommended combination of countermeasures
LowDrafting internal documents, brainstormingPrompt constraints + warnings to users
MediumInternal FAQ responses, meeting minutes summariesRAG + mandatory citations + feedback collection
HighCustomer-facing responses, interpretation of contracts and regulationsRAG + automated verification + human approval (Human-in-the-Loop)
Extremely highFinal decisions in medicine, legal, credit approval, etc.AI is limited to presenting reference information; humans make the decisions

What is often seen in practice is a case where a use case that falls under "High" proceeds at the "Low" level of countermeasures during the PoC (proof of concept) stage and then stalls just before going to production. I believe risk classification should be agreed upon with the customer's business owner early in the requirements definition phase.

How do you solidify the evidence base with data and ontology?

To fundamentally reduce hallucinations, it is effective to structure the business data that the AI references and define "where the correct answers live."

Palantir's AIP uses an architecture that runs LLMs on top of an Ontology (a data model that defines business objects and their relationships). The division of labor—having facts such as numbers and statuses retrieved from databases or APIs, while the LLM is responsible only for interpretation, summarization, and suggesting actions—is an approach applicable beyond Palantir products. For example, values like "inventory count" or "seat availability" should never be generated by the LLM; the system's values must always be displayed. This alone greatly narrows the scope for factually inconsistent errors to creep in.

How do you evaluate the effectiveness of hallucination countermeasures?

The basic approach to evaluation is to create a set of business-relevant questions and correct answers (an evaluation dataset) and measure the same metrics before and after the countermeasures for comparison.

"It somehow got better" cannot be explained to the customer's decision-makers. An FDE is expected to leave the evaluation framework itself as a deliverable.

What metrics should be used?

For metrics, combining three—faithfulness to sources, answer accuracy, and whether the system appropriately answered "unknown" when it should—makes it easier to grasp the actual situation.

MetricWhat it measuresExample measurement method
FaithfulnessWhether answers are based on the provided materialsEvaluation libraries such as RAGAS, LLM-based judgment
AccuracyWhether answers match the correct-answer dataComparison against the evaluation dataset
Appropriate abstention rateWhether the system answered "unknown" for questions not covered by the materialsDeliberately include unanswerable questions
Citation accuracyWhether cited sources actually exist and match the contentRule-based checking + sampling verification

The appropriate abstention rate is often overlooked. If you chase accuracy alone, you risk evaluating "a model that answers by guessing," as OpenAI pointed out, as mentioned earlier.

How do you run evaluation continuously?

Do not make evaluation a one-time exercise; instead, collect examples of incorrect answers from production query logs and continuously add them to the evaluation dataset.

  • Provide a button or form that allows users to report incorrect answers
  • Classify reported cases on a weekly basis, for example, and isolate the causes (search failures, insufficient materials, generation errors)
  • Add representative cases to the evaluation dataset
  • Run regression tests with the evaluation dataset every time the model, prompts, or search settings are changed

It is not uncommon for behavior to change with model version upgrades. Having a regression testing framework in place enables safe decisions about model updates.

What skills does an FDE need for hallucination countermeasures?

In addition to technical knowledge, the role requires the ability to interpret business risks and to translate evaluation results into language that decision-makers understand.

Hallucination countermeasures are one of the areas where the defining characteristic of the FDE role—"bridging technology and business"—is most clearly expressed.

What technical skills are required?

The three core areas are building RAG, designing evaluation, and data modeling; in particular, hands-on experience with search and evaluation implementation is where engineers tend to differentiate themselves.

Skill areaSpecific content
Search / RAGEmbeddings, chunk design, hybrid search, reranking
Evaluation designCreating evaluation datasets, LLM-as-a-Judge, regression testing
Data modelingStructuring business data, API integration, permission design
Prompt designExplicit constraints, fixed output formats, enforced citations
Monitoring / operationsLog collection, classification of incorrect answers, cost monitoring

What is required on the business and communication side?

The key is the ability to honestly tell customers that "hallucinations cannot be eliminated to zero" and to reach agreement on the acceptable range of errors and operational rules.

What the customer's decision-makers want to know is not the name of the model, but "can this be used for this business?" and "who responds how when it makes a mistake?" An FDE translates evaluation results into business language and proposes where to draw the line between what is delegated to AI and what is handled by humans. If this line is agreed upon at the outset, even if reports of "the AI made a mistake" come in after production deployment, the team can respond calmly as an anticipated event.

For hallucination countermeasures, rather than knowing the latest techniques, what directly drives results is assessing risk for each business, combining countermeasures accordingly, and continuously demonstrating effectiveness through evaluation. For engineers aiming to become FDEs, this is a theme where it is easy to accumulate practical experience and one that readily translates into market value.
