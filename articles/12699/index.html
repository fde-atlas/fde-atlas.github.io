How do you learn to be an FDE? Don't rush to get certified—first walk through a complete delivery chain.

Original title: FDE 怎么学？先别急着考证，先走完一条交付链

53AI · 2026-11-03 · 5 min read

Summary

This article argues that learning to be an FDE should start with completing a full delivery chain, not collecting certificates. It states that no FDE certification is widely recognized by employers; certificates add at most a resume line and a learning skeleton, while offers come from verifiable delivered work. The recommended path has three steps: complete one small end-to-end engagement, from vague requirement through evaluation, launch, and renewal; then fill in unglamorous field work like data integration, permissions, and data lineage; only then learn scaling topics such as multi-agent systems. Full text

Chapter 02 covered the capability map: six capabilities, and if one is missing, the chain breaks. This chapter answers the immediate follow-up question: which one should I start learning? And how should I pick among the courses and certificates on the market?

Let's start with a common scenario: you've bought plenty of courses and collected a few certificates, but the moment an interviewer asks "walk me through a complete on-site closed loop you've delivered," you freeze. It's not that you learned too little—it's that you learned in the wrong order, and what you learned can't be cashed in on-site.

Learning to be an FDE is a bit like learning to run a restaurant. Memorizing recipes or earning a chef's certificate doesn't mean you've ever run a restaurant. What truly proves you can run one is going through the whole cycle—from choosing a location, sourcing ingredients, and cooking, to whether customers come back. Same with FDE: what gets you an offer isn't a certificate, it's a completed delivery chain.

The certificate section of the case library makes this premise clear first, so let's set the framing straight.

Currently, no FDE certificate is universally recognized by employers. This role has no unified certification body, and hiring processes don't screen candidates by certificates. This is the case library's industry-consensus judgment, not an official statement from any employer.

At most, a certificate can give you two things:

  • An extra line on your resume
  • A skeleton for your learning path

What actually gets you an offer is always delivered projects and verifiable work. Get this principle firmly established first, and you won't be misled later by buzzwords like "guaranteed employment" or "high value" when picking certificates.

The path has just three steps. Follow them in this order, and don't skip any.

Step one: complete one small, full closed loop first.

Don't start with a big project. Find a real, small process and take it from start to finish:

  • Starting from a vague requirement
  • Breaking it into tasks
  • Getting a handle on the data and systems
  • Clarifying permissions and acceptance criteria
  • Clarifying how evals will be done
  • Clarifying who uses it after launch and who's responsible when things go wrong
  • Clarifying why it gets renewed in year two

Only after completing this cycle have you truly touched the six capabilities from Chapter 02. Building a runnable demo doesn't count as completing a closed loop; being able to articulate the eval criteria, reproduce results, and tune things to the acceptance line—that's completing it.

Step two: then pick up the dirty on-site work.

Frontline practitioners' on-site observations are blunt: right now, ops people rule. Dirty work like networking, deployment, permissions, isolation, SSH, and tunnels is part of an FDE's real daily life at customer sites.

What this step fills in are the two areas most easily overlooked by courses, from Chapter 02:

  • Data and integration: dirty data, closed-off systems, permissions and compliance
  • Data lineage: labeling every result with its data sources so customers dare to trust it

Filling in engineering fundamentals early saves your life more than learning one more framework.

Step three: then learn scaling.

Once you have complete single-agent delivery experience, then learn multi-agent decomposition, production monitoring, eval hardening, and expanding engagements through iteration. Frontline trainers list multi-agent as a must-learn, but they're also very firm about the order: get complete single-agent delivery experience first, then learn multi-agent decomposition—otherwise you end up knowing only concepts with nothing landing in practice.

Conversely, there are two paths that don't work and should be avoided:

  • Staying stuck at the operational layer long-term—that is, only doing training and only teaching prompts. One judgment in the case library is spot-on: if you're at the operational layer long-term, you're not doing FDE, you're doing training.
  • Pursuing "everyone uses AI, everyone learns prompts." The real on-site structure is that a few people distill the process while most people follow it, click buttons, and go through approvals. The core is capturing the information needed for the next round with low friction—not everyone learning models.

What does a verifiable piece of work look like? Forget the certificate line—look at whether you can produce these:

  • The original requirement and its breakdown
  • Data source annotations
  • Eval criteria and reproduction methods
  • Acceptance criteria
  • Who uses it after launch and who's responsible when things go wrong

With these five in place, your work stands on solid ground.

Before picking a certificate, measure everything with the same ruler. Ask five questions about each certificate:

  • Who issues it: big tech companies, universities, independent certification bodies, training providers—authority decreases in that order, and a vendor certificate's endorsement basically only extends within its own ecosystem
  • How it's assessed: pure multiple-choice theory, hands-on practicals included, or judged by work defense—the closer to real delivery, the higher the value
  • How well it matches the role: which parts of the full workflow the syllabus covers—is it general capability, or a manual for one company's product
  • What it costs: fees, prep time, validity period, and renewal rules—if not publicly listed, mark it as "to be confirmed"; don't make up numbers
  • Where's the evidence of recognition: do recruiters explicitly mention it, is holders' employment data verifiable—with no evidence, write "no public evidence," not "widely recognized"

Applying this ruler, here's the verdict on the case library's first three certificates.

First: the Tencent Cloud FDE certification.

A big-tech vendor certificate. Theory exam, 120 minutes in person, 60 questions, 70 to pass. Each purchase includes only 1 exam attempt; failing or missing the exam requires repurchasing. The course has 12 modules covering multiple stages: entry, delivery, consolidation, and governance.

The fee isn't publicly listed on the page—visible only after login—so mark it as "to be confirmed"; don't make up numbers. Valid for 2 years; renewal requires repurchasing the exam. About 30% of the knowledge distribution is Tencent's product matrix; general FDE capability is about 70%. As for evidence of recognition: no public recruiter mentions or holder employment data.

The verdict: worth considering within the Tencent ecosystem, be cautious outside it. Consider it if your target role is on a Tencent Cloud-related delivery chain, or if you want to quickly add a line of official certification; if your goal is a general FDE role, spend the prep time on delivery projects instead.

Second: ADaSci CFDE / CFDAS.

An overseas independent certification body—neither a big tech company nor a university. CFDE is the 30-hour entry tier; CFDAS is 60 hours, essentially CFDE plus Agentic AI architecture, with 12 modules, 120 questions, and a 2-hour proctored exam.

The curriculum covers the full workflow, with more generality than vendor certificates. Fees and validity period aren't clearly stated on public pages—mark as "to be confirmed"; whether it includes hands-on work isn't clear on public pages—mark as "to be verified." Evidence of recognition: likewise, no public data.

The verdict: wait and see. It's currently a rare non-vendor, general-purpose FDE-specific certification—the direction is right; but the organization itself is new and has zero evidence of recognition, so before taking it, confirm the fees, proctoring format, and certificate verification method.

Third: the IIT Roorkee graduate certificate.

A university endorsement from the Indian Institute of Technology Roorkee's continuing education center: about 3 months, roughly 85 hours, weekend live online sessions, with a training path covering the complete deployment cycle of discovery, prototyping, validation, launch, and iteration.

Whether it includes a capstone defense isn't clear on public pages—mark as "to be verified"; fees likewise unclear—mark as "to be confirmed"; evidence of recognition: no public data.

The verdict: suitable for those who want to systematically fill gaps, not for those in a hurry to get a certificate. A university endorsement looks better on a resume than a training provider's, but the 3-month cycle is long, and its direct endorsement for domestic FDE roles is limited.

One more category: training providers' course certificates—covered together here.

There are plenty of these programs, and the certificates themselves offer little differentiation. The real value lies in the portfolio—capstone projects, deployment documentation, interview coaching—not in the certificate line. When choosing such a program, look at the work requirements and the mentors' backgrounds, not at "guaranteed employment" promises—no training provider can control hiring outcomes.

At the end of the day, the order for picking a certificate should be reversed: first decide which ecosystem your target role is in and which capability you're missing, then see which certificate's syllabus happens to fill that gap. Buy the certificate first and then look for a role, and with the order flipped, the certificate easily becomes a placebo.
