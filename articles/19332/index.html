What Is AI-Driven Business Improvement? 5 Steps to Get Started and How to Choose Which Tasks to Begin With

Original title: AI業務改善とは？進め方5手順と始める業務の選び方

FDX株式会社 · 2026-10-19 · 9 min read

Summary

This FDX article targets managers responsible for company-wide AI adoption and argues that distributing generative AI tools without redesigning workflows rarely changes business metrics. It distinguishes speeding up individual tasks from rebuilding end-to-end processes, proposes three criteria for choosing a first target process (routine, high-volume, clear decision rules), and explains how to choose between RPA, generative AI, and AI agents based on decision branching and how often assumptions change. A five-step redesign procedure is laid out, with results measured by usage rate, business adaptation rate, and time saved, tied to existing KPIs. Common failure patterns, such as stopping at PoC or losing external-built systems after handover, are also covered.

Key points

  • Adding AI to existing workflows shrinks task time but rarely changes lead time or volume.
  • Start with processes that are routine, high-volume, and have clear decision criteria.
  • Choose RPA, generative AI, or AI agents by branching complexity and assumption stability.
  • Redesign follows five steps, from process decomposition to measuring the new flow.
  • Measure results via usage rate, adaptation rate, and time saved, linked to existing KPIs.

Even after rolling out generative AI tools company-wide, the business numbers haven't changed. This is a common state of affairs in AI-driven business improvement. In most cases, the cause is not the tool's performance, but the fact that AI was simply added to existing work without changing the shape of the work itself.

In this article, we will go through, in order, which business processes to start AI-driven improvement with, which methods to choose, how to rebuild the work, and what to measure results by.

  • AI-driven business improvement means using AI to change the time, quality, and volume of work. There is a stage where individual tasks get faster and a stage where the workflow itself is rebuilt.
  • Choose the first processes to tackle from those that are routine, high in volume, and have relatively clear decision criteria.
  • Choose among RPA, generative AI, and AI agents based on the number of decision branches and how often the underlying assumptions change.
  • Measure results by three things: "whether it is being used," "which processes it is being used for," and "how much was reduced."

Who this article is for

  • Corporate planning and DX promotion leaders responsible for company-wide AI adoption
  • Staff who have introduced generative AI but cannot explain the business results to management
  • Division heads and managers who want to use AI to improve departmental operations
  • HR departments supporting employee AI adoption from a training perspective

What AI-driven business improvement is (3 key points)

AI-driven business improvement means using generative AI, AI agents, and similar tools to change the time, quality, and processing volume of work.

It starts with making individual tasks faster, such as drafting and summarizing text. However, to show results as business metrics, you need to rebuild the workflow, including downstream checks, rework, and approval waits.

FDX recommends separating these two stages and deciding upfront which processes to target with which approach.

The difference between "adding AI" and "rebuilding around AI"

If you replace just one step of the current workflow with AI, the time spent on that step shrinks. However, since downstream checks, rework, and approval waits remain for every case, the days until a case is completed and the processing volume often barely change.

When you rebuild around AI, people's roles change. Instead of continuing to process, people shift to approving, handling exceptions, and maintaining decision criteria. The concept of business process reengineering (BPR) itself is not new; what is different is that AI has joined as a party capable of making decisions.

For concrete steps on rebuilding, see "Redesigning Business Processes Around AI | 5 Steps of AI BPR".

Which processes to start with

Three conditions of processes that are easy to start with

  • Routine: proceeds in nearly the same steps every time
  • High volume: many cases, making it easy to show time saved as a number
  • Relatively clear decision criteria: you can articulate what counts as correct

Achieve your first results in processes that meet these three conditions, create in-house examples of "work changed by AI," and then expand to other processes. Starting everything company-wide at once is a classic recipe for failure.

Choosing between RPA, generative AI, and AI agents

Choose the method based on two factors: the number of decision branches in the work and how often the underlying assumptions change.

Nature of the workSuitable methodExamples
Few branches, stable assumptionsRPATransferring data to fixed screens, regular data imports
Handling text and contentGenerative AISummarization, extraction, classification, translation, drafting
Many branches, frequently changing assumptionsAI agentsSales prospect research, operations monitoring and first-line incident response

Applying generative AI to routine work with few branches is often slower and more costly than RPA. Conversely, applying RPA to work with frequently changing assumptions means maintenance effort piles up every time the rules change. For details, see "How to Choose AI for Business Efficiency | RPA vs. Generative AI vs. AI Agents".

Five steps for implementation

When rebuilding a workflow, proceed through the following five steps.

  • Break the current flow into steps: For each step, write out who receives what, what decisions are made, and what is passed on next. Also note where exceptions occur
  • Isolate the decisions and tasks to hand to AI: For each step, decide whether it goes to AI or stays with a person. Make human pre-approval mandatory for irreversible operations
  • Redefine people's roles: Decide which of the following the people who used to do the processing will take on: approvals, exception handling, or maintaining decision criteria
  • Decide where exceptions go: Decide in advance where cases the AI could not handle go, to whom, and by when
  • Measure after the redesign: Measure not only work time but also lead time, the percentage handled automatically, the percentage routed to humans, and the percentage sent back for rework

What separates success from failure is not the range AI can handle, but the design of where unhandled cases go.

How to measure results

Results are measured by the following three metrics.

MetricWhat to look at
Usage rateWhether employees are using the AI enablement environment
Business adoption rateOf the list of business processes, the percentage where AI is actually being used
Time savedTime reduced after introducing AI (compare the number of cases processed and processing time before and after implementation)

On the revenue side, aim to raise KPIs already defined for business processes, such as order unit prices and meeting conversion rates, using AI-first workflows. Connecting to numbers already used in management meetings is easier to explain than creating new metrics.

Common failures

  • Handing out tools and stopping there: Only training on how to use the tools, without changing the workflow. Usage rate rises, but no time savings materialize
  • Starting company-wide all at once: The scope is too broad, making it impossible to show which processes produced results
  • Getting stuck at PoC: Only confirming in validation that it "seems usable," without deciding when to go to production or which metrics to measure
  • Stopping when the builder leaves: Built externally with no handover, and nothing can be fixed in-house

The reasons PoCs fail to move to production are organized in "Five Structural Reasons AI PoCs Fail and How to Avoid Them".

How to choose when outsourcing

When using external support, check the following three points.

  • Whether they are involved up to redesigning the workflow, or only tool implementation
  • Whether a plan to hand over the built systems in-house is established at the contract stage
  • Whether results are shown with actual measurements before and after implementation

Frequently asked questions (FAQ)

Q1. Which business processes should AI-driven business improvement start with?

A. Success is more likely when starting with processes that are routine, high-volume, and have relatively clear decision criteria. Examples include first-line response to inquiries, creating meeting minutes and reflecting them in the CRM, drafting documents, preparing quotes, and data aggregation and classification.

Q2. Can't we get results just by adding AI to current processes?

A. Individual work time shrinks, so the effect is not zero. However, because downstream checks, rework, and waiting for approval remain, the time until a case is completed and the number of cases processed often barely change. To show results in business metrics, the workflow itself must be redesigned.

Q3. How should RPA, generative AI, and AI agents be used differently?

A. Distinguish by the number of decision branches and how often the premises change. If there are few branches and premises are stable, RPA fits; if there are many branches and premises change often, AI agents fit. Processes in between should be designed with generative AI or combinations of tools.

Q4. How long does it take to see results?

A. As a guideline, roughly 3 to 6 months per business area to go to production and see the first results.

Q5. Can't we start until the data is in order?

A. There is no need to perfect company-wide data. You can start as long as the data required for the first area you tackle is available.

Q6. What should results be measured by?

A. Measure by the usage rate of the AI enablement environment, the business adoption rate (the percentage of the business process list where AI is used), and the time saved after introducing AI.

Q7. If a problem arises from a decision delegated to AI, who bears responsibility?

A. AI cannot be a responsible party. During the redesign stage, assign an owner of outcomes for each process, and make human pre-approval mandatory for irreversible or high-impact operations.

Q8. Will AI-driven business improvement lead to headcount reductions?

A. FDX does not aim solely at replacing people. We embrace "Human Upgrade" as a philosophy: evolving people's roles, reallocating them, and moving them toward higher-value work.

Summary

  • AI-driven business improvement has two stages: speeding up individual work, and redesigning the workflow. Showing results in business metrics requires the latter
  • Choose the first process to tackle from those that are routine, high-volume, and have clear decision criteria
  • Choose among RPA, generative AI, and AI agents based on the number of decision branches and how often the underlying assumptions change.
  • Proceed with the redesign in this order: breaking down processes → scoping what to hand to AI → redefining human roles → routing exceptions → measuring after the redesign
  • Measure results by usage rate, business adoption rate, and time saved, and connect them to existing business KPIs
