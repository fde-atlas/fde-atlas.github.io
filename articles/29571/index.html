FDE Negotiation Skills: Getting to Yes and BATNA

Original title: FDE 谈判技巧：Getting to Yes 与 BATNA

FDE Times · 2026-10-26 · 5 min read

Summary

This article applies the negotiation framework from Getting to Yes (Fisher, Ury, Patton; Penguin 2011 edition, 240 pages) to the daily work of forward deployed engineers. Its core argument: positions are decisions, interests are the reasons behind them, so FDEs should negotiate scope, timelines, and offers at the interest level, and use BATNA to know when to walk away. A running example—why a client demands a dashboard in two weeks—shows how asking why can shrink scope dramatically. The author also cites PostHog's practices: keep scope small, define done, and confirm in writing before committing.

Key points

  • Positional bargaining yields unwise outcomes; negotiate over interests instead.
  • BATNA defines your walk-away point for offers and project scope.
  • Asking why a two-week deadline exists can reshape the whole project.
  • PostHog advises small scope, measurable outcomes, and written confirmation upfront.
  • Common mistakes: guessing interests, interrogating clients, and over-committing after discovery.

Key Takeaways

  • *Getting to Yes* (by Fisher, Ury, and Patton; Penguin 2011 edition, 240 pages) argues that bargaining over positions leads to unwise outcomes.
  • A position is a decision the client has already made; an interest is why they made it. Good FDEs negotiate at the level of interests.
  • Your BATNA determines the point at which you walk away—whether it's an offer or an overly broad project scope.

The client says: "I need this dashboard in two weeks." An inexperienced FDE will argue about the two weeks; an FDE who has read *Getting to Yes* will ask: why two weeks? The answer often changes the entire project.

Written by Roger Fisher, William L. Ury, and Bruce Patton, this book was not originally intended for engineers. But the job of a Forward Deployed Engineer (FDE) is constant negotiation: over scope, timelines, the definition of "done"—and, before all of that, over your own offer.

An Old Book Still in Print

The first edition was published by Houghton Mifflin in 1981, credited only to Fisher and Ury. Patton joined from later editions onward, including the 1991 edition and the 2011 Penguin edition. The one to look for is the updated edition released by Penguin Books on May 3, 2011, ISBN 9780143118756, 240 pages.

The publisher's pitch: a step-by-step negotiation method for any situation that produces agreements satisfying to both sides. The blurb sounds grand, but the book's core is remarkably concise: the three ideas below are enough to change how you work next week.

Idea One: Don't Bargain Over Positions

The opening chapter, "Don't Bargain Over Positions," points out that positional bargaining produces unwise and inefficient outcomes. When each side digs in on a number, the conversation becomes a tug-of-war: whoever concedes less wins, and the real problem goes untouched.

Take the two-week dispute as an example. You propose four weeks, the client pushes for three, and you settle on three and a half. Nobody asked what the dashboard is actually for, so you'll likely deliver on time something nobody ever opens. This is exactly the "unwise outcome" the book warns about.

Idea Two: Interests Lie Beneath Demands

The most central sentence in the book draws a clear line: a position is something you have decided; an interest is what caused you to decide that way.

Focusing on interests rather than positions is one of the four principles of what the authors call principled negotiation. The Harvard Program on Negotiation explains further: interests are the needs, motivations, and concerns that lie beneath a stated demand.

Back to the example. You ask why two weeks, and suppose the client answers: the CEO needs a churn number for the board meeting. The interest is a reliable number; the full dashboard is just one way to provide it. A clean query plus a chart can be done in three days, and the full dashboard can move to a later phase.

PostHog's FDE handbook says the same thing in engineer's language: the most powerful move is often to correct the client's mental model rather than take the request at face value and build exactly what was asked. And you can only correct that model once you understand the interest behind it.

Idea Three: Know Your Alternative Before You Sit Down

The book introduces BATNA—the best alternative to a negotiated agreement. The logic is simple: people negotiate to get a better outcome than they would without negotiating. So any terms worse than your alternative should be rejected.

Applied to an FDE offer, this means knowing exactly what you already have: your current job, another offer, or staying on your current team and moving into a role closer to customers.

Imagine a hypothetical scenario. You're a backend engineer at a stable company and receive an FDE offer that requires being on-site at clients for most of each month. Your BATNA is staying in your current role: decent, but far from customers.

The easiest position to blurt out is "I don't want to travel that much." Once you write down your real interests, you might find two: you need a few fixed days at home each week, and after some time, you want to bring what you learn from clients back into the product.

These two interests open up space for trade-offs, whereas "I won't travel" only buys you a tug-of-war.

So you can propose: accept the on-site arrangement as described, but with fixed remote days, plus a clearly defined path into the product team. If the company can't meet either condition and the final offer is still worse than your current job, your BATNA gives you a clear reason to decline without guilt.

On project scope, an FDE's BATNA is usually refusing vague work. PostHog requires every project to have measurable outcomes, a rough timeline, and a mutually agreed definition of "done" before implementation begins.

The company also recommends keeping scope small, because expanding a tight scope costs less than untangling a loose one; and before committing, send a short written summary for the client to confirm.

What Does Getting It Wrong Look Like?

The first mistake is guessing the client's interests for them. You assume "they probably just need that churn number" and cut scope without verifying. An interest the client hasn't confirmed is still just your assumption, and it can easily harden into a new position—this time, yours.

The second mistake is asking "why" like an interrogation. The question should lead the client to describe the pressures they're under—for example, "Where will this number be used?"—rather than forcing them to defend their request.

The third mistake is uncovering the interests and then taking on more work. Knowing what the client is worried about doesn't mean solving every worry in one project. PostHog's advice on small scope is exactly the brake here. The final mistake is not writing down your BATNA before negotiating an offer, then conceding bit by bit out of fear of offending anyone.

Who Should Read It, and in What Order

If you're an engineer with two to eight years of experience about to move into an FDE role, or you've just taken on your first project, read this before any sales book. Start with "Don't Bargain Over Positions" to recognize the trap; then read the sections on interests; then the sections on BATNA. At 240 pages, the whole book is a weekend read.

But don't read it just to highlight. After each section, take a real client request and rewrite it in two columns: position and interest. Get the second column right, and most scope disputes will disappear.

For your career, make this skill visible on your resume and in interviews. Don't write "collaborated with clients"; write something specific: you narrowed the initial request to a smaller scope that still met the need and produced a measurable result.

When reading FDE job descriptions, look for requirements about defining scope and aligning expectations with clients, and prepare exactly one such story to tell when asked.

More than forty years after its first printing, the question Fisher and Ury posed remains the most valuable one in a first client meeting: "Why?"
