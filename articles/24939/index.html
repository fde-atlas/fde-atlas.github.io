How to Run an FDE Kickoff Meeting: A Guide to Your First Week On-Site with the Customer

Original title: FDE 启动会怎么开：进驻客户第一周指南

FDE Times · 2026-10-08 · 6 min read

Summary

This guide argues that a forward deployed engineer's first week at a customer should not start with code, but with a kickoff meeting that settles three things: who signs off, what counts as measurable success and failure, and who decides when stakeholders disagree. Drawing on PostHog's handbook, FDE Academy, Rocketlane, AmpUp and Read AI, it says the usual two-week internal onboarding period should be used to prepare these questions and a permission checklist. Without this groundwork, new FDEs typically hit a wall around week five or six from out-of-scope requests and stalled approvals. A worked example with a ticket-triage agent shows how probing questions surface hidden blockers like security teams, followed by a one-page minutes template.

Key points

  • Use the internal onboarding weeks to prepare kickoff questions and permissions.
  • Identify the signer, the authorized approver, and hidden blockers early.
  • Define failure criteria, not just success metrics.
  • Assign every milestone and permission request to a named person.
  • Send corrected minutes within 24 hours of the meeting.

Settle who signs off, who grants access, and what counts as failure first, or the out-of-scope requests in week five will decide for you.

Key Takeaways

  • An FDE's first two weeks are usually spent on internal onboarding and requesting access; use that time to prepare the kickoff meeting.
  • The kickoff meeting must settle three things: who signs off, what counts as success and what counts as failure, and who makes the final call when parties disagree.
  • Every milestone and every access request should map to a specific person. Anything without a clear owner means the owner is you.

Picture this: after two weeks of internal onboarding, you walk into the customer's office on a Monday morning for the first time. The codebase is cloned, the notebook is open, but you don't have VPN access, can't read the staging database, and nobody in the room can say who will sign off on the demo.

It's tempting to write some code first so the time isn't wasted. But that's usually wrong. Code written before you know who approves, who grants access, and what the customer expects often has to be thrown away, because the problem it solves was never actually raised by anyone in the customer's organization.

For a Forward Deployed Engineer (FDE), the kickoff meeting and the first week aren't a formality before the real work begins—they are the project's first task: figuring out who you'll work with on the customer side, who will give you what, and who has the authority to say "yes."

On the blog of Rocketlane, a customer onboarding software company, Irit Eizips of CSM Practice states plainly that misaligned expectations can quickly derail the entire onboarding process. The article notes that this misalignment often stems from unrealistic promises made during the sales stage.

Use the internal two weeks to prepare the kickoff meeting

A good kickoff meeting is prepared before you ever meet the customer. According to FDE Academy, an FDE's first two weeks are typically spent internally: learning how the company usually deploys for customers, and requesting access to the tools and systems you'll actually use.

PostHog's FDE handbook likewise treats week two as the point where work with the customer begins.

Don't treat this time as waiting. It's a rehearsal for what you'll do at the customer: every access request has a specific person who grants it.

At PostHog, access to tools used in customer calls must be requested from specific people. Build the habit of writing down the grantor's name internally, because at the customer's site the list will be longer and the grantors harder to find.

Behind this is an attitude: don't wait for work to be assigned. PostHog's handbook treats onboarding as a self-driven process; when it's unclear who owns something, that person is almost certainly you.

These two weeks are also the time to clarify expectations on your own side. FDE Academy recommends asking your manager directly and early: what does good look like in their eyes for the first 90 days.

Next, find the channel where FDEs communicate with customer relationship owners. PostHog has dedicated cross-team channels for sales, customer success (CS), and support teams. Ask there what sales promised the customer, before the customer tells you themselves.

Three things the kickoff meeting must settle

First, the approver. Rocketlane recommends identifying all stakeholders involved in implementation—not just the person who made the purchase decision—as early as the introductory call before the kickoff meeting.

AmpUp's stakeholder mapping template distinguishes the economic decision-maker (the person who holds or approves the budget) from the authorized approver. A VP who says "I'll forward this to procurement" is usually just the authorized person.

The same template also warns to watch for blockers: people who never appear on calls but can slow down or even kill the deal entirely—like a chief security officer nobody has met who raises data residency concerns in the final approval round.

For an FDE, confirm in week one: is the person who approves data access such a blocker?

Second, expectations. Rocketlane recommends clarifying each stakeholder's role, expectations, and desired business outcomes during the introductions at the start of the meeting.

Read AI's agenda template goes further: define measurable success, and state explicitly what failure looks like. The failure question is harder to ask, so few people ask it, but it saves you when a demo turns into an argument.

Third, decision authority. Read AI recommends assigning every milestone to a specific person, not a team, and making clear who makes the final call when parties disagree. When two departments want two different things, this answer determines whether you lose an afternoon or two weeks.

A sample kickoff meeting: from questions to minutes

Suppose you're an FDE deploying a ticket triage AI agent for a logistics company. In the room are the customer service lead, an IT engineer, and the VP of operations. Sales introduced her as the decision-maker.

You start with success: "Six weeks after the AI agent goes live, what will you look at to judge whether the project succeeded?" The customer service lead answers: "More tickets routed to the right team." That's a wish, not an acceptance criterion.

You follow up: "What accuracy would you need before you'd give up manual triage? And even if the overall numbers look good, what situation would you still count as failure?"

He answers: "If claims tickets get routed to the order inquiry team, even just a few, that's failure." That's the real requirement: complaint-type tickets need a separate threshold; a single overall accuracy number won't do.

Then the approver question: "At the week-three demo, who says 'yes, go to production'? If customer service and IT disagree, who makes the call?" If the VP says "I'll have to ask my boss," you know: she's the authorized person, not the signatory. Before locking scope, get 15 minutes with the real signatory.

Finally, the blocker question: "For the AI agent to read tickets containing customer phone numbers, which department needs to approve? Are they here today?" This often surfaces a security team nobody invited. As soon as the meeting ends, write everything into a one-page memo:

What to do this week

Before the kickoff meeting, read the sales handover notes, ask in internal channels what was promised, and prepare the three sets of questions above. In the meeting, prioritize the failure and decision-maker questions, because customers rarely bring them up on their own.

Within 24 hours, send the memo to all attendees and ask them to correct any errors. A memo the customer has corrected is worth more than one nobody reads. From then on, check the access list every morning: if any row has passed its expected date, contact the person named in that row directly—don't wait for someone else to chase it.

The mistakes that make weeks five and six hit a wall

FDE Academy describes it: around week five or six, a new FDE hits their first wall—either the customer raises requests outside the agreed scope, or a technical problem can't be solved by conventional means. At that point, if no decision-maker was established from week one, every additional request looks like a must-do.

The first mistake is dealing only with the person who made the purchase decision. The second is treating the authorized person as the signatory, only to discover on demo day that someone more senior never saw any of this work. The third is forgetting the blockers, learning only when requesting access to real data that security team approval takes two weeks.

The remaining two are harder to spot. One is defining success without defining failure, so every acceptance meeting turns into a debate based on gut feeling. The other is waiting for someone else to grant you access, when the spirit of the PostHog handbook is clear: if no one owns it, it's yours.

An article from Perspective AI discussing Palantir-style FDE work emphasizes being on-site and sitting with customers, delivering usable software early. This advice doesn't contradict the above. Delivering early is only valuable when you know exactly who you're delivering to and how they measure outcomes.

Turning this skill into a job-hunting advantage

When reading FDE job descriptions, watch for phrases like customer onboarding, stakeholder management, or "own the customer relationship." They signal that the company wants you to personally lead kickoff meetings, not just take tickets. In interviews, describing a time you identified the real sign-off owner or a hidden blocker is far more persuasive than any claim of "strong communication skills."

On your resume, replace "worked with customers" with something more specific, for example: led kickoff meetings, defined acceptance criteria including failure scenarios, and secured full data access before week two. Internal projects, integrations built for other departments, and system handovers are all opportunities to practice this skill right now.

The first line of code you write at a customer should come after every line of the meeting notes has a name next to it. Only then do you know who you're writing code for.
