Comparing Palantir's Competitors | Differences from Databricks and Snowflake

Original title: Palantirの競合を比較｜Databricks・Snowflakeとの違い

FDE総研 · 2026-09-07 · 14 min read

Summary

This article argues that asking whether Palantir competes with Databricks or Snowflake is only half right. Palantir sells operational decision-making, not data storage, so competitors differ by layer: data platforms (Databricks, Snowflake, Microsoft Fabric) at the infrastructure layer; C3.ai, Anduril, and defense primes at the decision-application layer; and in Japan, SIers and consultants billing by person-month as the biggest hidden rival. The decisive difference is Palantir's ontology, which starts from business objects and actions and writes results back into operations, supported by Forward Deployed Engineers embedded at customers. The article offers selection criteria, PoC design advice (one business KPI, judged in 6-8 weeks), and career guidance for engineers, noting FDE models fail without a business-change decision-maker and fast data access approvals.

Key points

  • Competitors must be analyzed across three layers: infrastructure, decision apps, and implementation.
  • Palantir's ontology starts from business objects and actions, not tables.
  • In Japan, SIer person-month models are the main hidden competitor.
  • FDE deployment requires a business-change owner and quick data access.
  • PoCs should target one business KPI judged within 6-8 weeks.

Who are Palantir's competitors? What's the overall picture of the major players?

Palantir's competitors are not limited to specific companies. The rivals differ across three layers: data infrastructure, decision-making applications, and implementation SIs.

The question "Is Palantir's competitor Databricks or Snowflake?" is only half right in practice. That's because what Palantir sells is not a place to put data, but business decision-making itself. To correctly identify Palantir's competitors, you need to look at it by layer. Below, we break down the rivals across three layers: infrastructure, applications, and implementation.

Who competes in the data infrastructure layer?

Competitors in the data infrastructure layer include Databricks, Snowflake, and Microsoft Fabric, overlapping in the data integration space.

Palantir Foundry includes data integration capabilities (pipelines, data lineage, permission management). Isolating just this scope puts it in direct overlap with Databricks (Lakehouse/Unity Catalog), Snowflake (AI Data Cloud), Microsoft Fabric, Google BigQuery, and AWS's data services. It's not uncommon for procurement departments to write RFPs and line these up as "the same category" based on this overlap alone.

However, comparisons at this layer tend to focus on technical metrics like query performance, storage unit costs, and the number of supported connectors. Palantir claims value in the stages beyond that. A comparison table limited to the infrastructure layer cannot correctly determine the relative merits of Palantir and its competitors.

Who competes in decision-making applications and the defense sector?

Competitors at this layer are C3.ai, Anduril, and existing defense system vendors. The evaluation criteria shift to "does it change frontline operations?"

The rivals Palantir Gotham (for defense and intelligence agencies) and Palantir AIP (the layer connecting generative AI to business operations) fight are not data infrastructure vendors. They are C3.ai, which provides AI applications for industry, Anduril in the defense tech space, and the group of prime contractors that have delivered existing defense systems in various countries. The evaluation criterion here is not "are queries fast?" but "does it change mission and frontline operations?"

When the evaluation criteria change, the path to winning changes too. At the infrastructure layer, price and performance matter, but at this layer, implementation capability—how much you can rebuild frontline procedures—is what's tested. Even within Palantir, the competitors faced differ completely depending on the layer.

Why is the SIer/consulting man-hour model an "invisible competitor"?

In Japanese companies, the most likely major competitor is not a product but the man-hour model of consultants like Accenture and SIers.

When Japanese companies think "we want to advance data utilization," the actual object of comparison is often not Palantir but an SIer's proposal. In other words, it becomes a choice between buying a tool or buying people. One reason Palantir puts FDEs (Forward Deployed Engineers) front and center is that it is fighting on this "alternative to man-hours" battleground.

If you overlook this dynamic, internal discussions won't align no matter how detailed you make the product comparison table. Recognize upfront that the object of comparison is not the neighboring product but the man-hour rates listed on the estimate. The scale of the major players is as follows:

What is the decisive difference between Palantir and Databricks/Snowflake?

The decisive difference is the ontology. Palantir handles everything from defining business objects to executing decisions on the product side.

Databricks, Snowflake, and Palantir look similar if you only consider data integration capabilities. However, their natures clearly diverge on three points: the starting point of design, the delivery model, and the problems they address.

What changes depending on whether the starting point is "data" or "business"?

Databricks and Snowflake start design from data, while Palantir starts from business objects. This is the fundamental difference.

Databricks was designed from the start around the idea of "storing all data cheaply and running everything end-to-end through machine learning." Snowflake started as a "cloud DWH anyone can use with SQL" and has expanded into AI and application territory. Both are bottom-up approaches that start from data.

In contrast, Palantir Foundry's ontology first defines business nouns such as "orders," "flights," "inventory," and "patients," along with the actions people can take on them. The protagonist is not tables but objects, and analysis results can be written back directly into business systems. This structure of "not ending with just looking" is the biggest difference in design philosophy that separates Palantir from analytics platform vendors.

How do delivery models and cost structures differ?

Databricks and Snowflake are centered on usage-based billing. Palantir is centered on contracts that include implementation support.

The difference in cost structure also changes how you explain it internally. With usage-based billing, you only need to explain what you used, but with contracts including implementation support, you are asked to explain "what problem was solved." This difference matters, even down to how you write your approval request.

What determines which one works?

Which product works is determined by "the shape of the problem." If your analytics infrastructure is underdeveloped, go with Databricks-type; if decision-making is disconnected, go with Palantir.

From what I see and hear in the field, companies at the stage of "having data but nobody looking at it" tend to struggle when introducing Palantir, as the organization can't keep up with ontology design discussions and the effort stalls. Conversely, for companies where "analysis results exist but frontline Excel workflows never change," Palantir's action execution model works well. Before choosing a tool, the priority is to articulate whether the bottleneck is on the data side or the decision-making side.

How does the FDE (Forward Deployed Engineer) role factor into competitive comparison?

FDEs are a role that eliminates the distance between the product and the field. They create implementation and learning speeds that competitors find hard to imitate.

The difference between Palantir and its competitors cannot be fully explained by product features alone. I will organize, in order, what kind of work the FDE role does, how it differs from similar roles at competitors, and under what conditions it stops functioning.

What does an FDE actually do?

An FDE is a role that embeds into the customer's business and takes personal responsibility for seeing everything through, from requirements definition to implementation to adoption in the field.

Unlike typical contracted development, FDEs are not "the side that receives specifications." They observe the customer's operations, decide for themselves which objects should be defined, build applications on the platform, and get the field to use them. If they aren't used, they rebuild them the following week. It's easiest to picture the role as combining the functions of a product manager, data engineer, solution architect, and customer success into one person.

The premise for this role to work is having a highly capable foundation like Foundry / AIP. Without that foundation, FDEs would just be a small contracted development team. Palantir's product and engineers only become a competitive advantage as a set.

How do FDEs differ from similar roles at competitors?

Competitors also have roles that go into the field, but their nature changes depending on whether field learnings can be reflected in product specifications.

RoleTypical affiliationMain dutiesReflection into product
FDE (Palantir type)Product companyObserving customer operations, app implementation, adoptionField learnings feed directly into the product roadmap

The differences come down to three points. First, contracts are framed around "problems to solve" rather than "things to build." Second, implementation leadership sits with the FDE. Third, gaps found in the field lead to improvements in the platform itself. Because of this cycle, even with the same headcount, the speed of learning differs.

Under what conditions do FDEs stop working?

In environments where there is no one with authority to change business processes and data permissions are not granted, FDEs spin their wheels.

The FDE model is not a panacea. If the customer has no counterpart with the authority to change business processes, no matter how good the screens you build, operations won't change. Also, in organizations where it takes months to obtain data access permissions, the FDE's greatest weapon—the cycle of "showing something working in 1-2 weeks"—cannot turn. Those considering adoption should check whether their company can meet these two conditions before comparing tool features.

What criteria should be used to distinguish Palantir from its competitors in the Japanese market?

The starting point of selection is to first determine whether you want to change decision-making or build out a data platform.

In the Japanese market, beyond product feature differences, relationships with existing SIers and the approval (ringi) process heavily influence selection outcomes. We will organize this in the order of decision criteria, Japan-specific constraints, and PoC design.

What decision criteria should be used for selection?

The decision criteria are five: where the problem lies, in-house talent, speed requirements, data sovereignty, and existing contracts. The top priority is where the problem lies.

  • Where the problem lies: If it's a break in decision-making or operations, a Palantir-type; if the analytics platform is underdeveloped, a Databricks/Snowflake-type.
  • Depth of in-house talent: If your company has a full team of data engineers, an open platform is advantageous. With limited talent, an accompaniment-style like FDE is more realistic.
  • Speed requirements: Do you want business screens running within weeks, or a company-wide platform built over years?
  • Data sovereignty and confidentiality: Can it meet domestic region requirements and regulations in defense and critical infrastructure sectors?
  • Contracts with existing SIers: Can it be designed to proceed in parallel without breaking relationships with current vendors?

Of the five, the one where internal opinions diverge most is the first: "where the problem lies." If you proceed to product comparison while this remains ambiguous, each person's evaluation criteria will differ, and the comparison table will become useless for decision-making.

How should Japan-specific constraints be incorporated?

In the Japanese market, contracts with existing SIers and the approval (ringi) process influence selection outcomes more than product performance.

As a fact about the Japanese market, Palantir Technologies Japan was established in 2019 as a joint venture with SOMPO Holdings. Palantir's business development in Japan has centered on this joint venture. Domestically, wariness of vendor lock-in and long-term contracts with SIers that run core systems tend to be barriers when introducing a new platform. Excluding business companies that own their own data products like VACAN, most companies cannot avoid the step of "evaluating alongside proposals from existing SIers."

To add a practical observation, proposals tend to pass approval more easily when framed as "improving a specific business process" rather than "platform renewal." This is because a grand vision of a company-wide data platform makes it hard to explain return on investment.

What are the keys to designing a PoC that doesn't fail?

PoCs are less likely to fail if you narrow the focus to a single business KPI and design them so success or failure can be judged within 6 to 8 weeks.

In comparative evaluations, "side-by-side evaluation" where multiple vendors run the same PoC tends to be chosen. However, because Palantir-type and Databricks-type solutions have different evaluation axes, even the same problem won't yield comparable results. The recommended approach is to align evaluation targets on business KPIs such as "man-hours reduced" or "lead time to decision," and leave the implementation method to the vendors. Detailed checklist comparisons of technical requirements can be deferred at this stage.

How does a career change for engineers who understand the competition?

Understanding the competitive landscape leads to being valued in the market not as a specialist in a particular product, but as a professional in business implementation.

The competitive landscape between Palantir and its rivals may continue to change. That is precisely why a combination of skills not dependent on a specific platform, and the habit of articulating your results, lead to career stability.

What skill combinations increase market value?

Engineers with both data engineering skills and an understanding of business domains have high market value.

The skills required of an FDE don't need to be outstanding individually: SQL and Python, data modeling, API integration, and "the ability to talk with business owners and draw out requirements." People with this combination are valuable not only at Palantir but also as Databricks solution architects or in data product development at business companies. Conversely, if you focus only on operating a specific platform, your market value tends to drop when the product landscape shifts.

What should you learn in the first few months to build a foundation?

As a guideline, spend several months acquiring, in order, the basics of data modeling and SQL, pipeline construction, and business understanding (the duration of each stage varies depending on the learner's experience).

StageLearning themeConcrete output
Stage 1Data modeling and SQLDesign a normalized schema using public data and write aggregation queries
Stage 2Pipelines and orchestrationBuild ETL with dbt, Airflow, etc., and be able to explain the lineage
Stage 3Business understanding and application buildingInterview operational departments and build a simple app that improves a business KPI

The biggest differentiator is the "interviewing operational departments" in Stage 3. Even just getting 30 minutes with someone in sales or logistics, hearing their daily pain points, and diagramming them is training in FDE-style thinking.

What career pitfalls are easy to overlook?

The FDE's on-site work style makes results hard to see from the outside, and without the habit of documenting in words, you cannot convey your value.

Because FDE-style work is completed within the client's environment, it can be difficult to build a portfolio you can show externally. The habit of recording, within the bounds of confidentiality, an abstracted account of "what business problem you solved, with what design, and over what period" pays off when changing jobs. Also, some say that going too deep into a client dulls your sense of product development. You will need to proactively create opportunities to regularly return to product-side discussions.
