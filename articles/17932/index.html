Scope Definition Playbook: Turning "We Want Everything" into "Which One First"

Original title: 需求范围界定 Playbook：把「全都要」变成先做哪一个

FDE中国 · 2026-09-04 · 6 min read

Summary

This FDEChina playbook addresses the common tug-of-war where clients want everything at once. It lays out eight steps: break vague requests into a scenario register, score business value and frequency with client-signed ratings, assess feasibility and hidden costs, then place scenarios in a value-cost matrix that forces an explicit, justified "won't do" category. It stresses three written artifacts—scenario register, scope baseline, and requirement pool—plus a change gate with weekly reviews and milestone-based drift tracking, tailored to Chinese client environments where verbal agreements often go undocumented.

Key points

  • Eight numbered steps from scenario breakdown to drift review.
  • Scores must be client-signed or scope cuts get overturned.
  • The matrix turns "won't do" into a justified formal decision.
  • A change gate handles on-site "just add this" requests.
  • Three artifacts: scenario register, scope baseline, requirement pool.

This article addresses the tug-of-war FDEs most often face early in a project: the client wants everything, and you can only build part of it first. The step most easily misused is scoring: scores must be signed off by the client—scores entered by the engineer will be swept aside with a single "we never said this wasn't important" when it's time to cut scope. Compared with generic prioritization methodologies, this article emphasizes three written deliverables—a scenario register, a scope baseline, and a requirements backlog—and, addressing the reality on the ground in China of "nodding in meetings but never putting it in writing," adds two mechanisms: a change gate and drift retrospectives. After reading, do one thing: take the pending requests in your current project, fill them into a scenario register one by one, and send it to your counterpart for written confirmation. — FDEChina Editorial Team · Practical Field Notes

When the client says "we want all of these features," saying yes outright will drag the project to its death, and saying no outright will cost you trust. The essence of scope definition is not cutting requirements, but turning a vague blob of "we want it all" into a list that is ranked, has conclusions, and is confirmed in writing. This article lays out eight numbered steps, from scenario decomposition to scope drift retrospectives, each covering what to do, the deliverable, and common pitfalls. The earlier you do scope definition, the less rework every subsequent step requires. It assumes you have already completed requirements discovery and have a batch of raw requests to organize; if the client's expectations are themselves unbalanced, read expectation management first, then come back to do the ranking.

Step 1: Translate "we want it all" into a scenario list

What to do: Extract every request that appears in kickoff meetings, requirement documents, group chats, and emails, and rewrite each one in the format "who does what under what circumstances, and how success is judged." One request, one scenario per entry—prefer splitting too finely and merging later over squeezing three things into one line like "improve operational efficiency." A rule of thumb for judging whether the split is fine enough: each scenario must be able to independently answer "done or not done"; if a record needs supplementary explanation to judge, it still mixes multiple things. For each record, note the requester, the channel it came through, and the original wording—the original wording is the only credible evidence when disputes arise later.

Deliverable: A scenario register with at least the following fields: ID, scenario description, requester, request channel, and the original request text.

Common pitfall: Only recording requests made by senior client stakeholders in meetings while missing the real problems raised by frontline operators in private conversations; the latter are often more frequent and more specific.

Step 2: Tag each scenario with business value and usage frequency

What to do: Together with the client's counterpart, answer three questions for each scenario: who uses it, how often, and what it saves or earns after use. Score value and frequency each as high, medium, or low, and record the rationale in the client's own words alongside. Note that two types of scenarios follow different ranking logic: low-frequency but extremely high per-use value (e.g., quarterly audit data pulls) and high-frequency but low per-use value (e.g., daily report drafts) cannot be compared directly on the same scale; for the former, look at the weight of the decisions it unlocks; for the latter, look at the cumulative hours it saves. Try to complete the scoring within a single meeting—dragging it into multiple rounds of scoring will exhaust the client's patience and land you back at the starting point of "everything is important."

Deliverable: A scenario list with value scores, frequency scores, and rationale columns.

Common pitfall: Engineers scoring on the client's behalf. Your judgment can be offered as a suggestion, but the signed-off scores must come from the client; otherwise, when you cut scope later, a single "we never said this wasn't important" can overturn the entire ranking.

Step 3: Tag technical feasibility and data readiness

What to do: Check three things for each scenario: whether the input data exists, its quality, and whether it can be legally obtained; whether the dependent systems have open APIs and who to ask for access; and whether there is a manual fallback if the scenario fails. Assess feasibility separately from value—don't downgrade a scenario's value just because "it's hard to build." Hard-but-worthwhile items should land in the "do later" quadrant, not be quietly dropped.

Deliverable: Three new columns on the list: data readiness, external dependencies, and fallback method.

Common pitfall: Treating demo-environment data quality as production-environment data quality. Running one extraction with real data on the client's site exposes readiness problems better than ten pages of assessment documents.

Step 4: Estimate the effort and hidden costs of each scenario

What to do: Roughly estimate each scenario's implementation effort in person-days, and list the hidden costs: data cleaning, edge-case handling, post-launch false-positive handling, and ongoing maintenance. Give ranges, not point values—the width of the range itself reflects uncertainty; the less familiar the domain, the wider the range, and this information is valuable to the client too.

Deliverable: An effort column added to the list, plus a one-page estimation note stating the basis, assumptions, and which costs were excluded.

Common pitfall: Reporting only first-implementation costs and ignoring maintenance costs. For AI-type scenarios, maintenance costs (bad-case fixes, prompt adjustments, regression validation after model upgrades) are often no lower than the initial implementation; following the approach in eval baseline building, include regression costs in the estimate up front.

Step 5: Draw a value-cost matrix and sort scenarios into four categories

What to do: Plot all scenarios with value on the vertical axis and cost on the horizontal axis, yielding four categories: do first (high value, low cost—usually the core of the first-phase validation), do later (high value, high cost—put on the roadmap with agreed trigger conditions), discuss again (low value, low cost—leave to the client to decide), and won't do (low value, high cost—state clearly that it won't be done and why). The point of the four quadrants is not a pretty chart, but turning "won't do" into a formal, reasoned conclusion rather than an unresolved ambiguity. When placing scenarios, tolerate and record disputes: if the client insists a particular scenario is high value, move it per the client's judgment and note that this is the client's call—the ranking is negotiable, but the accounting must be clear.

Deliverable: A four-quadrant chart with every scenario placed and tagged with its disposition.

Common pitfall: Deliberately omitting the "won't do" category in communication. Unspoken requirements don't disappear; they come back under a different name at acceptance time.

Step 6: Align the ranking with the client and confirm in writing

What to do: Hold a scope alignment meeting, bring the matrix and your recommended ranking, and give the client multiple-choice questions (do A or B first) rather than open-ended questions (what do you think we should do). Walk through the rationale for each "won't do" and "do later" item in the meeting; allow the client to adjust individual conclusions, but record the reason for each adjustment on the spot—every conclusion changed in the meeting must be traceable in the minutes to who changed it and based on what. Within twenty-four hours after the meeting, send a confirmation email stating clearly what will be done this phase, what is explicitly not being done, and under what conditions "do later" items will be triggered.

Deliverable: Alignment meeting minutes, the confirmation email, and the updated scope baseline document.

Common pitfall: Nodding in the meeting but never putting it in writing afterward. The pattern of "everyone understood in the meeting, then everyone went back and told their own story" is especially common with clients in China; written confirmation is not distrust—it saves both sides from a dispute over memories three months later.

Step 7: Set up a scope change gate

What to do: Agree with the client that all newly emerging requests go into the requirements backlog, get re-evaluated and re-ranked through the same process as Steps 2 through 5, and are reviewed once a week at a fixed time. When asked face-to-face on site, "can you just add this while you're at it," the standard answer is one sentence: "Noted—next review will give you a conclusion." Neither agree nor refuse on the spot. Brief your counterpart on this line in advance so it becomes a process rather than your personal attitude; otherwise, every instance will be taken as a fresh rejection.

Deliverable: A client-visible requirements backlog and weekly change review records.

Common pitfall: Agreeing on the spot to "small requests" to preserve the relationship. Scope creep is never caused by one big compromise; it accumulates from twenty instances of "just this once." The rhythm of the gate matters more than its strictness.

Step 8: Use milestones to review scope drift

What to do: At the end of each milestone, compare the actually delivered scope against the scope baseline, count how many items were added, removed, or changed, and write the drift amount into the weekly report so both sides see it. Drift itself is not bad—out-of-control drift is; make drift visible, and the client will manage it with you instead of assuming that adding requirements is free.

Deliverable: A scope drift record, reviewed together with the milestone review.

Common pitfalls: Running forward without looking back, letting drift accumulate until the acceptance stage—by then, any explanation sounds like an excuse.

Summary

The core of these eight steps is three written deliverables: the scenario register, the scope baseline, and the requirements pool. Whether scope definition is done well isn't judged by how pleasant the meeting was, but by whether both parties can produce the same document three months later. It connects upward to the scope confirmation step in the project lifecycle, and downward provides the basis for acceptance boundaries in the PoC acceptance checklist; if multiple stakeholders are pulling priorities in different directions, combine the four-quadrant alignment meeting and confirmation email mechanisms with the stakeholder tiering in expectation management.
