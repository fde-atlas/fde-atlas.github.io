After 17+ Hours of In-Depth Conversations with 19 Practitioners: The Path for FDE Implementation in China Is Becoming Clear

Original title: 17+小时 深聊19位实践者之后：FDE在中国的落地路径 正在变得清晰

亿邦动力 · 2026-11-22 · 5 min read

Summary

Based on 16 projects, 17+ hours of interviews with 19 practitioners, and a CIO survey, this Yibang Power article maps how Forward Deployed Engineers (FDEs) are taking shape in China. It argues the Chinese FDE emerged organically—"practice first, recognition later"—rather than copying the Silicon Valley role, filling a responsibility gap between general model capabilities and business outcomes. The core tension is depth versus scale, and the viable path is a productized FDE model that turns field experience into reusable assets. 73.3% of surveyed CIOs accept FDE services, favoring industry-specific solutions, small pilots, and stage-based acceptance. The article offers advice for brands, factories, service providers, and platforms, warning against repackaged traditional delivery and linear headcount-driven businesses.

Key points

  • Chinese FDEs grew organically from market demand, not from copying Silicon Valley templates.
  • FDE fills the responsibility gap between model capability and business results; it can be a role, capability, or cross-team mechanism.
  • The depth-versus-scale tension is resolved via productized FDEs that convert field experience into reusable assets.
  • 73.3% of CIOs accept FDE services, preferring industry solutions, small pilots, and stage-based acceptance.
  • Beware traditional delivery teams rebranding as FDE for premium pricing without changing how they work.

Full text

Based on 16 projects, in-depth interviews totaling over 17 hours with 19 practitioners, and a targeted CIO survey, this article outlines the core logic behind how FDEs are taking root in China. Key takeaways:

1. The Chinese FDE is not a copy of the Silicon Valley job template. It exhibits a "do first, get recognized later" pattern: after enterprises began deploying AI, they found their existing organizational divisions could not own responsibility for business outcomes, and this new way of working emerged organically;

2. The FDE is essentially not a new standalone role, but a piece of outcome accountability that fills the gap between general-purpose model capabilities and enterprise business results. It can be a role, a capability, or a cross-departmental joint mechanism;

3. The core tension in FDE adoption is depth versus scale. A viable direction in China is the productized FDE, which distills frontline experience into reusable assets to avoid degenerating into a linear-growth labor business;

4. The survey shows that 73.3% of CIOs accept FDE services, and favor an adoption path based on industry-specific offerings, small pilot projects, and phase-by-phase acceptance.

The development of FDEs provides brand owners with a clear action reference for deploying AI and building AI-related businesses. Key takeaways:

1. When brand owners deploy AI, their existing division of labor typically has sales closing deals, pre-sales producing proposals, product teams building generic features, and implementation teams only handling go-live—leaving a gap where no one is accountable for the final AI business outcomes. They can draw on the FDE approach, filling this accountability gap by forming an independent team or a cross-departmental joint group;

2. If brand owners offer AI-related services externally, they should neither directly copy Palantir's labor-intensive model abroad, nor simply rebrand traditional delivery as FDE to cash in. They should pursue a productized FDE route that balances deep value with scalable growth;

3. According to the survey, 73.3% of customer CIOs accept FDE services. When expanding their market, brand owners should build industry-specific solutions, start with small-scale, short-cycle projects, adopt phase-by-phase acceptance, and clarify ROI and boundaries of responsibility, which makes it easier to win customer trust.

This article reveals new growth opportunities and risks in the AI deployment space and offers clear guidance for sellers building AI-related businesses. Key takeaways:

1. On the opportunity side: in today's AI deployment process, there is a clear accountability gap between model capabilities and business outcomes. The survey shows 73.3% of enterprise CIOs accept external FDE services, with 33.3% willing to try them soon. Market demand has already emerged, making this a new growth track worth entering;

2. On the implementation direction: do not copy the labor-intensive model from abroad; instead pursue a productized FDE route. Deploy a small number of frontline staff to work deep inside customer sites to uncover needs, then distill that experience into eval sets, industry templates, and product components, eliminating repetitive work through productization;

3. On risk warnings: avoid falling into the labor-business trap. If customer growth requires proportional headcount growth, and similar projects repeatedly consume the same amount of staffing time, scalable profitability is impossible and industry trust gets eroded. At the same time, structure engagements into verifiable, exit-able phases that match customer needs.

The development logic of FDEs offers important insights for factories advancing digital and AI transformation and exploring new business opportunities. Key takeaways:

1. When factories deploy AI, their existing division of labor mostly also suffers from the problem that no one is accountable for actual production business outcomes after AI features go live. They can draw on the FDE approach, filling the outcome-accountability gap by cultivating hybrid capabilities or forming cross-departmental joint teams, thereby increasing the real value of AI deployment;

2. Factories deploying AI must resolve the tension between depth and scale. For the personalized needs of different production scenarios, they should promptly distill validated deployment experience into standard workflows and reusable components, avoiding starting from scratch on every new project, improving deployment efficiency and reducing costs;

3. On the business opportunity side: many manufacturing enterprises currently need industry-savvy FDE services for their AI deployments. If a factory has accumulated mature AI deployment experience, it can extend related services to peers, starting with small and mid-sized pilot projects, which makes it easier to win customer trust.

This article clarifies industry development trends, core customer pain points, and viable solutions in the FDE space, and is highly valuable for AI enterprise service providers. Key takeaways:

1. Industry development trend: the Chinese FDE is a product forced by market demand, not a copy of foreign models. The form of the role may change in the future, but accountability for model deployment outcomes will only shift, never disappear, and the overall direction will move toward productization;

2. Core customer pain point: what CIOs need today is not a new job title called FDE, but a service that understands their business, converts vague requirements into working systems, proves return on investment, and clarifies boundaries around data, permissions, and responsibility. Traditional delivery models cannot meet this need;

3. Solution: pursue an industry-specific, productized FDE route—build trust with peer cases, start with small-scale, short-cycle projects, adopt phase-by-phase acceptance, distill frontline experience into standardized templates and components, balance deep value with scalable growth, and avoid degenerating into a labor business.

This article maps market demand for platforms in the AI deployment era, pointing out the direction for platforms building FDE ecosystems and the risks to avoid. Key takeaways:

1. On the demand side: the core tension in FDE development today is depth versus scale. Both service providers and customers need platform support to help distill personalized deployment experience into reusable assets, filling the gap between general-purpose models and enterprises' individualized needs;

2. Viable platform approaches: build experience-capture infrastructure for FDE practitioners, launch standardized industry templates and product component libraries to help service providers lower the cost of scaling deployments, and guide providers toward service models of small pilot projects with phase-by-phase acceptance that match customer acceptance habits;

3. Risk avoidance: be wary of traditional delivery teams merely rebranding as FDE to charge premiums without changing their underlying production methods. In vendor recruitment and operations, platforms should guide providers toward productization, assess project experience reuse rates, and promote healthy industry development while avoiding erosion of market trust.

Based on large-sample in-depth interviews and targeted surveys, this article reveals new industry trends, new problems, and new business models in China's AI deployment process, and is highly valuable for industry research. Key takeaways:

1. New industry trend: in China's AI deployment process, a new organizational form—the FDE—has emerged organically, exhibiting a "do first, get recognized later" pattern. It is not a copy of the Silicon Valley model but a product genuinely forced by market demand, filling the accountability gap between general-purpose model capabilities and enterprise business outcomes—a new species in AI industry development;

2. New problem: the core tension in the current development of FDEs in China is depth versus scale—going deep prevents scaling, while scaling prevents delivering sufficient value. This is a new problem in AI enterprise services that warrants in-depth research;

3. New business model: the article proposes the productized FDE business model, in which frontline staff uncover personalized needs and the resulting experience is distilled into standardized, reusable assets, with productization eliminating repetitive work. This both solves the problem of generic products failing to meet personalized needs and resolves the pain point of custom services being unable to scale—an innovative business model in AI enterprise services.
