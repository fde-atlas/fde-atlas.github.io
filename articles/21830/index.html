Summary

This Japanese article by Shohei Tokumitsu, CEO of DeFactory, explains the role of Development Strategist: a professional who synchronizes corporate strategy with system development to maximize ROI on technology investment. It distinguishes the role from PdM, Tech Lead, IT consultants, Solutions Architects, and FDEs, and identifies three core skills: architecture combined with business design, resource optimization and roadmap planning, and stakeholder management. It also covers avoiding failed PoCs through predefined KPIs and exit criteria, rebuilding products as AI-native, and a path to freelancing as a technical advisor earning over 1.5 million yen monthly by serving multiple companies in parallel. The piece ends with promotion of DeFactory's services.

Key points

  • A Development Strategist aligns corporate strategy with development to maximize technology investment ROI.
  • Unlike PdMs or Tech Leads, the role spans both business and technology at a company-wide level.
  • Core skills: architecture plus business design, resource optimization and roadmaps, stakeholder management.
  • Avoiding 'PoC death' requires predefined KPIs, exit criteria, and a path to production.
  • The author claims freelance advisors serving multiple firms can earn over 1.5 million yen monthly.

A Development Strategist is a role responsible for synchronizing business strategy with system development and maximizing business value through technical decision-making.

"We have excellent engineers, yet the features we develop don't lead to business results"—companies struggling with this "disconnect between development and business" are rapidly increasing. The role gaining attention as a bridge to close this gap is the "Development Strategist." It is a role that serves as the junction of technology and business, synchronizing business strategy with system development and reliably converting technology investments into business value.

In this article, drawing on my experience supporting multiple development organizations as a technical advisor, I thoroughly and systematically explain the definition of the Development Strategist, essential skills, and how the role differs from other positions. Furthermore, I dig deep into why companies seek external development strategists, and even "the path to going independent as a freelancer or technical advisor and earning over 1.5 million yen per month," incorporating first-hand information and real-world insights. Please use this as a practical guide for career design that AI summaries cannot provide.

A Development Strategist is a professional responsible for synchronizing business strategy with system development and maximizing business value through technical decision-making. While the job title is not yet fully standardized in Japan, its importance is rapidly being recognized alongside growing demand for professionals who "understand technology and can also speak to business." Their mission is neither simply implementing technology nor merely drawing up strategy, but connecting the two and "converting technology investments into business results."

With the advancement of DX and the rise of generative AI, we have entered an era where technical decisions directly influence management. In this context, the presence of development strategists who bridge the gap between technology and business is growing.

FDE project information, exclusively for Forward Deployed Engineers currently in operation

This is a DeFactory in-house opening where a pair of at least two people—a consultant (PdM) and an engineer—join the client company and handle everything from identifying issues to implementation and establishing in-house capabilities. Hourly rates are 5,500 yen and up for the engineer role and 6,000 yen and up for the PdM role, with fixed billing (hourly rate × estimated utilization).

In conclusion, a Development Strategist is a specialist who synchronizes "the strategy drawn up by management" with "the systems built by development teams," eliminating discrepancies between the two to maximize the ROI of technology investments.

The core roles of a development strategist are as follows.

  • Technical translation of strategy: Translating business strategy into feasible system requirements and development plans
  • Optimization of technology investments: Directing limited resources toward development with high business impact
  • Control of technical debt: Designing the balance between development speed, quality, and maintainability
  • Bridging decision-making: Leading technical decisions between management and development teams

As a professional opinion, the background behind the attention this role receives lies in the critical shortage of "management talent that understands technology" and "technical talent that understands business." In many companies, management doesn't understand technology, and engineers don't understand the business, with each operating in a divided state. This division is the root cause of failures such as developed features going unused and technology investments failing to pay off. Development strategists are rare professionals who can bridge this divide. For related roles, please also see What Is a Solution Architect? Roles, Essential Skills, Salary, and the Path to High-Rate Independence.

In conclusion, a Development Strategist differs clearly from a PdM responsible for the product, a tech lead who leads the team, and an IT consultant who handles proposals, in that they are responsible for the most upstream and cross-functional perspective of "synchronizing business strategy and development."

Here is how the role differs from positions it is often confused with.

PositionMain missionPerspectiveCharacteristics of involvement
Development StrategistSynchronizing business strategy and development, maximizing ROIBusiness × Technology (cross-functional)Bridging strategy and the field
PdM (Product Manager)Product successProduct and customersResponsible for a specific product
Tech LeadDriving the team's technologyTeam development progressLeading while hands-on
IT ConsultantProposing and solving IT issuesBusiness improvement and proposalsProposal-focused (implementation is outsourced)
FDEAI implementation and problem-solving at client sitesField and implementationOn-site at the client, implementing

As a real-world insight, while a PdM focuses on "how to make a single product succeed," a development strategist operates with a broader perspective on "how to convert a company's entire development investment into business results." While IT consultants often stop at making proposals, the strength of development strategists lies in drawing up strategy with an understanding of technical feasibility. In conclusion, while a Development Strategist plays the role of "synchronizing management and development and drawing up the overall strategy," an FDE (Forward Deployed Engineer) plays the role of "embedding into the customer's frontline, implementing AI solutions, and delivering results." The collaboration between the two is the key to ensuring that strategy doesn't remain a pie-in-the-sky plan.

Below is a summary of the differences between the two roles and how they work together.

Main roleSynchronizing business strategy and development; overall designOn-site AI implementation, operation, and adoption

As a professional opinion, when the link between strategy and implementation breaks down, you end up with situations like "there's an impressive strategy, but it never gets implemented on the ground" or "the frontline is active, but it's out of sync with the business strategy." The FDE brings to life on the ground the strategy drawn up by the development strategist. Only when this upstream (strategy) and downstream (implementation) mesh together do technology investments bear fruit as business results. The collaboration of the two is the ideal model for technology-driven business going forward. For details on the FDE role, see What is an FDE?.

What a Development Strategist needs are three core skills: "architecture × business design," "resource optimization and roadmap planning," and "advanced stakeholder management." Neither technical skill alone nor strategic planning ability alone is enough; it is the combination of these that determines market value. This section explains each skill and the specific work involved.

In conclusion, the first core skill is the ability to make technology choices and design architecture in a way that maximizes business ROI while properly controlling technical debt. What is required is not just judging whether a technology is good or bad, but being able to determine what is "optimal for the business."

Examples of decisions made with this skill are as follows.

Decision axisConsiderations
Build or buyWhether to develop in-house or leverage existing SaaS
Tolerance for technical debtWhether to prioritize speed and accept debt, or invest in quality
Architecture styleMonolith or microservices (depending on business phase)
Investment prioritiesWhich development will generate the greatest business impact

As real-world criteria for technology selection, an excellent development strategist does not make "cutting-edge technology" or "elegant design" the goal. During a startup's launch phase, they may deliberately accept technical debt to prioritize speed. Conversely, during the scaling phase, they invest in paying down that debt. The essence of this skill is the judgment to determine "what is the right answer right now" according to the business phase. For controlling technical debt, What is a Technical Architect? Role, Essential Skills, and How to Go Independent at High Rates is also a useful reference.

In conclusion, the second core skill is the ability to optimally allocate limited development resources and formulate a roadmap while visualizing return on investment. It means deciding "what to build, when, and in what order" from the perspective of business value.

The key perspectives to emphasize in roadmap planning are as follows.

  • Prioritization: Judging development priorities based on business impact and implementation cost
  • Visualizing ROI: Quantifying the value each development effort generates and presenting it to management
  • Resource optimization: Allocating limited engineer hours to where they will have the greatest effect
  • Flexible revision: Updating the roadmap nimbly in response to market and business changes

As a common failure seen in the field, there are cases where teams prioritize "features the development team wants to build" or "requests from the loudest customers," wasting resources on development with little business impact. An excellent development strategist sets priorities based on data and business strategy rather than intuition, and makes clear "what not to do." This act of selection is the very core of resource optimization.

In conclusion, the third core skill is advanced negotiation ability: translating management's abstract vision into concrete requirements that the development team can implement, and moving both in the same direction. This presupposes fluency in both the languages of technology and management.

Examples of the required translation and negotiation are as follows.

Management's words (abstract)Translation into development requirements (concrete)
"We want to improve the customer experience""Improve the high-drop-off payment flow with ◯◯"
"We want to leverage data""Build a data platform and connect it to ◯◯ decision-making"
"We want to boost competitiveness with AI""Incorporate LLMs into ◯◯ operations to reduce workload"
"We want to speed up development""Set up CI/CD and systematically pay down technical debt"

As a professional opinion, this translation is where a development strategist's value shines most. Management speaks abstractly about "what they want to achieve," while the frontline thinks concretely about "how to build it." There is a large gap between the two, and if left unaddressed, it leads to "building something that doesn't match management's intent." People who understand both languages and can bridge the gap are rare, and that is precisely why they command high market value.

In conclusion, the problem of "the divide between development and business" that many companies face—namely, "what gets built doesn't translate into business results"—can be solved through the involvement of a Development Strategist. Avoiding development without purpose, adapting to the AI era, and a shift in the professionals' own perspective are the keys. This section explains concrete solutions.

In conclusion, the biggest failure caused by the divide between development and business is "build it and be done"—the so-called "PoC death." To prevent this, it is essential to clearly define "KPIs (success metrics)" and "exit criteria" before starting development.

Concrete measures to prevent "PoC death" are as follows.

MeasureDescription
Clarifying purposeDefine "what this development is for" based on business goals
Setting KPIs in advanceDefine numerically "what constitutes success"
Setting exit criteriaAlso decide in advance "when to stop"
A path to productionPlan the production rollout from the PoC stage onward

As a common failure in the field, companies frequently churn out vaguely purposed feature development and PoCs with a "let's just build something" mindset, wasting budget and man-hours without any of them translating into business results. Development strategists align with management and field teams before development begins on "what does success look like" and "when do we pull the plug if it fails." This discipline prevents wasteful development and turns investment into results.

In conclusion, the rise of generative AI (LLMs) has created a need to fundamentally redesign existing products with "AI-native" as the premise. Development strategists rebuild roadmaps with this major shift in view.

Considerations for the transition to AI-native are as follows.

  • Re-evaluating existing features: Identify which features can be replaced or enhanced by AI
  • AI-first experience design: Place AI at the core of the experience, not as an add-on
  • Building the data foundation: Advance the preparation and integration of data that supports AI
  • Phased migration: Don't rebuild everything at once; migrate with clear priorities

As a professional view, the emergence of generative AI has a "rebuild-level" impact on many products. If companies limit their response to "adding a bit of AI to existing features," they risk being outcompeted by rivals designed AI-native. Development strategists draw the big-picture roadmap of "how to rebuild this product with AI as the premise" and protect the company's competitiveness.

In conclusion, what senior engineers and PdMs need most to step up to Development Strategist is a shift in perspective from "how to build" to "why build and what drives the business." They are required to elevate their viewpoint from technology and product to management and the business as a whole.

The required shifts in perspective are summarized below.

Previous perspectiveDevelopment strategist's perspective
How to build (implementation)Why to build (business purpose)
Success of the product in chargeOptimization of the company's overall development investment
Technical correctnessBusiness impact and ROI
Optimization within the teamCompany-wide optimization across management and field

As a professional view, the better the engineer or PdM, the more they obsess over "technical correctness" and "product completeness." However, development strategists are sometimes required to make the hard call that "even if it's not technically the best, this is the right answer for the business." This shift in perspective is not easy, but when people with a technical foundation acquire a management perspective, they become extremely rare and highly valuable. For career development paths for engineers, also see Backend Engineer Career Paths and Salaries.

In conclusion, Development Strategists are extremely high-value talent in the market—those who go independent as freelancers or technical advisors can aim for over 1.5 million yen per month. Their rarity in being able to speak to both technology and business, and the demand for an external, neutral perspective, support these high rates. This section explains the real market value and the path to independence.

In conclusion, the reason operating companies seek external development strategists lies in the pressing need that "talent who can bridge technology and business is extremely difficult to develop or hire internally, so we want to leverage external expertise and neutrality only when needed."

The background behind the demand for external talent is as follows.

  • Difficulty of hiring: Talent combining technology and business is rare, making full-time hiring extremely difficult
  • Burden of full-time employment: Many companies cannot afford to hire CTO-level talent full-time
  • Neutral perspective: They want objective technical judgments free from internal politics
  • Diverse expertise: They want to leverage the broad experience of external talent who have seen multiple companies

As a professional view, many companies face the dilemma of "we don't need to hire a full-time CTO, but we want someone to support technical decision-making." By bringing in an external development strategist as a technical advisor, they can leverage the expertise of rare talent they could never hire, for exactly the period needed. The growing demand for technical advisors and external CTOs is driven precisely by this structure.

Similarly, the decision framework of "develop internally or leverage external help" is also common to the "Business Architect" talent type defined by METI's Digital Skills Standard. For details, see What Is a Business Architect? Roles, Skills, and the Framework for Developing vs. Leveraging External Talent.

In conclusion, the reason the high rates of development strategists (over 1.5 million yen per month) are justified lies in "neutral architecture design" free from dependence on specific vendors, and the magnitude of the business impact it generates.

As a professional view, when consulting vendors or SIs, there is a bias toward "wanting to sell our own products," but a neutral external development strategist can judge solely by the standard of "what is truly best for this company." This neutrality is what justifies rates exceeding 1.5 million yen per month. Since a single technology selection decision can affect costs or revenue on the scale of tens of millions to hundreds of millions of yen, the high rate as compensation for this is entirely reasonable.

In conclusion, the most income-efficient style of independence for development strategists is "parallel work," concurrently serving as CxO support or technical advisor for multiple companies. By not dedicating themselves to a single company and supporting multiple firms, they can achieve both risk diversification and income maximization.

The benefits of parallel work are as follows.

  • Income maximization: Earning compensation from multiple companies raises total income
  • Risk diversification: Avoiding dependence on a single company spreads the risk of contract termination
  • Synergy of expertise: Experience across multiple companies raises the quality of support for each
  • Flexibility of workload: Workload can be flexibly designed, such as 1–2 days per week per company

As a real-world perspective, the value of a development strategist lies in "providing sound technical judgments with just a few hours to a few days of involvement per week." Since they don't work on-site full-time writing code, they can serve multiple companies at once. For example, by serving as a technical advisor to several startups one day a week each, you could earn 300,000–500,000 yen per month from each, achieving a high total income. This way of working favors those with more experience. For a broader comparison of freelance work in general, see The Benefits and Cautions of Going Independent as an IT Freelancer.

In conclusion, for freelance development strategists to maximize their income, two defensive measures are essential: "contract design that prevents unpaid consulting outside the scope" and "rate negotiations based on business contribution."

Here are concrete measures to prevent scope creep and secure fair rates.

Defensive measureDetails
Clearly document the scope of workSpecify "support scope and working days" in the contract and SOW
Set a cap on working daysDefine an upper limit such as "up to X days per month," with additional compensation for excess
Make results visibleShow how much the business has advanced through your involvement
Negotiate based on business contributionJustify your rate by citing "cost reductions and revenue gains generated by your technical judgments"

A common failure pattern is when small "quick consultations" pile up, the scope of work gradually expands by default, and the effective hourly rate becomes unprofitable. Professionals don't simply "refuse"—they draw the line constructively by saying, "That's outside the scope, so I'll take it on as additional work." And in rate negotiations, they justify high rates by quantitatively demonstrating the business value their technical judgments have created (reduced development costs, faster release cycles, avoidance of technical debt, etc.). People capable of such strategic technical judgments have high market value and are well positioned to win high-rate projects as freelancers. Here are answers, from a practitioner's perspective, to frequently asked questions from those aiming to become Development Strategists.

A: You don't need to keep writing code at a professional level, but a deep understanding of technology is essential. A development strategist's main duties are strategy planning, technology selection, and decision-making, so there are few opportunities to write production code yourself. However, judging the feasibility and difficulty of technologies requires deep understanding backed by hands-on development experience. Someone who "talks about technology but can't write code" won't earn the trust of engineers, and their strategies tend to remain armchair theory. What matters more than current implementation skills is "being able to make business-oriented judgments while understanding the practical realities of implementation."

A: The biggest difference is that while a Solution Architect is responsible for "designing technical solutions," a Development Strategist plays a more upstream and cross-functional role of "aligning business strategy with development." Here is a summary of the differences between the two.

RoleMain missionPerspective
Solution ArchitectDesigning the optimal technical architecture to solve problemsTechnical solutions
Development StrategistAligning and optimizing overall business strategy and development investmentBusiness × Technology (cross-functional)

While an SA designs "how to solve this problem with technology," a development strategist works from a perspective closer to management: "how to convert the company's overall development investment into business results." SA experience is one promising stepping stone toward becoming a development strategist.

The IT Strategist Exam in particular is directly relevant to the fundamentals of a development strategist, since it tests knowledge that connects business strategy with IT. However, certifications are only an entry point; your track record of "what business results you actually achieved through technology" ultimately determines your market value.

A: They are in wide demand wherever technology determines business competitiveness, such as companies driving DX, businesses leveraging generative AI, and startups in growth phases. Specifically, demand exists in the following careers and situations.

  • Technical advisor / external CTO for startups: A role that complements technical decision-making
  • DX promotion at enterprises: A key change agent bridging management and development
  • Launching new businesses: A role that shapes technology strategy and leads development
  • Generative AI projects: The designer of AI-native businesses and products

Their value is highly regarded not only as full-time CxOs or VPoEs, but also in ways of working that support multiple companies as freelancers or technical advisors.

However, the strategies a development strategist draws up are executed as actual projects. Therefore, understanding the essentials of management—schedules, costs, and resources—enables you to create more feasible strategies. Even without PM experience, you can succeed if you have a foundation of technical skill and business perspective, but it's recommended to deepen your understanding of how projects actually proceed.

This article has comprehensively covered the role of the Development Strategist, required skills, differences from other roles, solutions for bridging the divide between development and business, and the path to going freelance. Finally, let's review the key points.

  • What a Development Strategist is: The "junction of technology and business" that aligns business strategy with system development
  • Differences from other roles: A cross-functional role that goes beyond PdM, tech leads, and IT consultants, converting overall development investment into business results
  • Three core skills: Architecture × business design / resource optimization and roadmap planning / stakeholder management
  • Divides solved: Avoiding "build it and forget it" (PoC death), rebuilding for AI-native, and shifting perspective
  • Market value: Over 1.5 million yen per month as a technical advisor or freelancer, with income maximized through parallel work

Development Strategists are rare professionals whose demand never dries up in an era where technology directly determines a business's competitiveness. People with the rare combination of "understanding technology and being able to talk business" command high market value, and if they go independent as freelancers or technical consultants, they are well positioned to win high-rate projects while supporting multiple companies. By layering a management perspective on top of a foundation of technical skill and aiming to become one of a kind, great possibilities open up ahead in both career and income.
