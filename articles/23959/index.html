What's the Difference Between FDE and Consulting? Comparing Roles, Skills, Suitability, and Career Transition Paths

Summary

This Japanese article, aimed at individuals choosing between careers, compares FDEs (Forward Deployed Engineers) with consultants using an "endpoint of involvement" framework: consultants stop at recommendations, while FDEs carry work through implementation and on-site adoption. It compares roles, deliverables, daily work, and skill sets, arguing both share problem structuring and client dialogue skills, but FDEs additionally need hands-on implementation ability (e.g., Python) and high agency, citing Anthropic's FDE job posting. It offers distinct transition paths for consultants versus engineers, discusses English proficiency and company size, and includes a self-check list for deciding which role fits.

Key points

  • Core divide: consultants end at recommendations; FDEs own implementation and adoption.
  • Consultants deliver reports; FDEs are judged by whether their software is actually used.
  • Shared skills: problem definition and client dialogue; FDEs add coding and high agency.
  • Consultants need building experience; engineers need problem-definition and client-facing skills.
  • A self-check list helps readers pick between advisory and build-oriented work.

What's the difference between an FDE and a consultant?

Using the FDE Research Institute's comparison axis of "endpoint of involvement," an FDE is responsible through implementation and adoption, while a consultant is responsible through recommendations.

Consultants analyze problems and make proposals, handing implementation off to clients or development companies. An FDE (Forward Deployed Engineer) takes on the same problem all the way to "working software." Palantir describes FDEs as engineers who embed themselves in the customer's field and personally handle everything from workflow design to implementation and testing (Palantir official blog). If you'd like a video explanation, see FDE seminar video (What is an FDE? Differences from consulting and system development).

Many top-ranking comparison articles are written for companies commissioning AI implementation support. The FDE Research Institute targets this article's readers at individuals considering career choices or job changes as consultants or engineers, comparing daily work, evaluation criteria, skills, and transition paths in that order. The scope of responsibility for each phase is as follows.

What kind of role is an FDE?

An FDE is an engineer who, at the customer's site, handles everything from problem discovery to implementation and adoption, alone or as a single team.

An FDE doesn't hand requirements off to someone else to build; they define the problem themselves and work hands-on to deliver results. They turn problems heard in meeting rooms into code on the spot to verify them, and bring the results back to the field. Because there's no telephone game through specification documents, verification cycles are shorter. The close proximity between problem and implementation is the essence of the FDE role. For more detail on the definition of the role, see What is an FDE.

How far does a consultant's responsibility extend?

A consultant is a professional who structures problems, makes recommendations on the direction to take, and drives decision-making.

The core of a consultant's value lies in clarifying "what should be done," and implementation is generally handed off to the client's internal team or a development company. Therefore, the quality of proposals and the communication skills to drive decision-making become the axes of evaluation. Assuming division of labor is not a weakness; it's a structure for focusing on the quality of decisions.

How do the roles of FDE and consultant differ?

The difference in roles between FDEs and consultants comes down to whether the work ends at recommendations or carries working software through to actual use in the field.

Even when facing the same problem, a consultant's role ends at "proposing a solution." An FDE's role includes "implementing the solution and making it work in the field." This difference in boundaries affects everything: deliverables and evaluation criteria.

Where do proposing consultants and building FDEs diverge?

The dividing line between FDEs and consultants lies in where results are placed: consultants treat recommendations as results, while FDEs treat software that gets used as results.

A consultant's results are documents such as analysis reports and strategic recommendations. An FDE's results are the software itself that is actually used in the field. FDEs are evaluated not on "whether the proposal was adopted" but on "whether what they built is used and produces results." Even a correct proposal doesn't count as an FDE's result if it isn't used in the field.

How do differences in deliverables change daily work?

For consultants creating recommendations and FDEs building products, how a day's time is spent and who they interact with differ.

A consultant's day tends to center on information gathering, analysis, and document creation. An FDE's day flows continuously between dialogue with clients and writing code. Conduct interviews in the field in the morning, then build and show a prototype in the afternoon. This way of working is possible because FDEs own the building role themselves.

How do the skill sets of FDEs and consultants differ?

For FDEs and consultants, problem-definition and dialogue skills are a shared foundation; FDEs additionally require implementation skills such as Python and high agency.

The difference is that FDEs additionally require implementation skills such as Python, and high agency—the ability to make decisions and move forward independently even in ambiguous situations. Anthropic's FDE job postings also list strong implementation skills alongside strong communication skills as requirements (Anthropic official job posting).

Why are implementation skills essential for FDEs?

Even if an FDE correctly grasps a problem, they won't reach results without being able to turn it into something that works, so implementation skills are indispensable.

Consultants can deliver value through proposals alone, but FDEs enter the customer's site on the premise that they can "build it." If you enter the field without sufficient implementation skills, you'll end up reverting to a proposal-only role, which diminishes the point of joining as an FDE.

What skills do consulting and FDE work have in common?

What consultants and FDEs share is the ability to organize vague inquiries into defined problems, and the dialogue skills to engage with customer decision-making.

Because of these two skills, consultants and FDEs can work on the same problems in the same meeting room. When someone with consulting experience aims to become an FDE, what they need to newly acquire is implementation ability and high agency, while their foundation can be used as-is. Conversely, even if you only hone your implementation skills, you won't function as an FDE without problem definition and dialogue abilities.

What do consultants and engineers each lack when transitioning to FDE?

For those from consulting, implementation skills tend to be the gap to fill, while for those from engineering, it's problem definition and customer dialogue.

People from consulting already have the foundation of problem definition and dialogue skills, so the shortcut is to add experience building small things that work. Engineers have implementation skills, but if they've spent a long time on the receiving end of requirements, they often lack experience defining problems themselves. The difference in scope of responsibility as a technical professional is compared in detail in The Difference Between FDEs and Regular Engineers.

Differences in role by company size are often things you can't tell from job postings alone. In interviews, confirm whether the FDE handles everything alone from problem discovery to operations, or whether the team divides the work. What do you take on by choosing FDE?

Choosing FDE means accepting the evaluation that your work only counts as a result if it's actually used in the field, even if your proposal was correct.

Consultants can focus on the quality of decision-making and leave implementation to division of labor. FDEs can shorten the distance between problem and implementation, but in exchange, whether what they build gets used becomes their own responsibility. Whether you find this style of evaluation appealing or burdensome is the dividing line in choosing a role.

Who is not suited for FDE?

People who feel the most fulfillment from moving organizational decisions through recommendations are better suited to consulting than FDE.

Moment of feeling a sense of accomplishmentSuited role
When decisions move because of your recommendationsConsultant
When what you built is used in the fieldFDE

People who have no interest in writing code themselves, or who feel strong stress about making judgments and proceeding while problems remain vague, will also find it hard to perform well as FDEs. It's not a matter of which is better; it's a difference in whether you prefer to "solve problems through recommendations" or "solve them by building things yourself."

Which is a better fit: FDE or consultant?

By FDE Soken's decision criteria, people who want to move organizations through recommendations suit consulting, while people who want to build things themselves and deliver them to the field suit FDE.

Both roles involve going deep into customer problems, so people who enjoy problem-solving have aptitude for either. Use the following self-check as a guide, leaning toward whichever side has more items that apply to you.

Self-check itemIf it applies to you
You're good at persuading stakeholders with documents and logicConsultant
You want to leave implementation to specialist teams and focus on decision-makingConsultant
You want to build a prototype on the spot to show the problems you hear aboutFDE
You want to be evaluated on whether what you built gets usedFDE
You can make your own judgments and move forward even in ambiguous situationsFDE

What should you do to move from consulting to FDE?

To move from consulting to FDE, the realistic path is to leverage your problem definition skills while gaining experience building and delivering small things.

You don't need to take on large-scale development from the start. Instead of ending everyday business problems with a recommendation, try turning them into a tool yourself. Through this experience of "building and delivering," your strength as a consultant expands into "the ability to carry through to implementation." If you can talk about what you built together with how it was used in the field, it becomes easier to demonstrate implementation skills even in FDE selection processes. The path from no experience is explained in Can You Transition to FDE Without Experience?.
