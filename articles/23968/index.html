How an FDE Can Handle a Customer CTO Opposing the Technical Solution

Original title: FDE 如何应对客户 CTO 反对技术方案

FDE Times · 2026-11-10 · 6 min read

Summary

This guide advises forward deployed engineers on handling CTO pushback against AI designs. Its core argument: treat objections as information about constraints you missed, not attacks. If the CTO is right, admit it and revise the design with a firm date; for genuinely contested points, log an open question and discuss underlying reasons rather than re-arguing; for operational concerns, ask targeted questions and turn them into design criteria. Drawing on Google's code review guidance, Product Talk, LeadDev, and a Kepler case where a simple viewer ended a year-long data migration stalemate, it argues that listening beats repeating technical arguments.

Key points

  • Admit correct objections immediately and commit to a dated revision.
  • Log contested direction decisions as open questions with fair reasoning.
  • Turn operational concerns into explicit design criteria via questions.
  • A Kepler viewer tool resolved in days what a year of arguments couldn't.
  • Have a client engineer review your design before the CTO sees it.

Behind an objection often lies a constraint you haven't heard yet; asking the right questions resolves disagreements faster than another round of explaining.

Key Takeaways

  • When the CTO raises an objection, first ask yourself whether they're right; if so, acknowledge it immediately and revise right away—don't promise to "fix it later."
  • If a point is genuinely debatable, stop arguing about direction; record it as an open question and discuss the reasoning behind it instead.
  • Repeating technical arguments rarely dissolves objections; at Kepler, a small viewer unblocked an engineer who had been stuck on a data migration for about a year.

Picture this scenario: you're on slide four, the architecture diagram for the AI assistant has just appeared, and the customer's CTO interrupts you: "Why do we need another vector database? Nobody on my team has ever operated one." Most engineers' instinct is to explain again, in more detail, with more numbers.

That's often the moment a consulting session starts to go off track. The people across the table don't need you to be more persuasive. They're telling you about a constraint your architecture diagram didn't account for.

For a Forward Deployed Engineer (FDE), these meetings matter more than they appear. In an article on hiring FDEs, First Round Review treats being on-site with customers as central to the role. You're there precisely to hear these kinds of constraints, not to defend your slides.

Treat objections as information, not attacks

Google's engineering guide on handling disagreements in code review contains advice worth taping to your monitor: if the other person is right, tell them so, and then let the point go. It sounds simple, but it requires you to stop and ask yourself before rebutting.

Swap "code" for "the customer's systems," and this advice applies even more to CTOs. They know which team is on call at night, which system has failed before, and which regulations require data to stay on their own infrastructure. And you've only been there a few weeks.

The same guide also warns that letting people "clean it up later" is a common path to a codebase gradually getting worse. The same applies at customers: once you acknowledge a point is correct, change the design immediately rather than pushing it onto a "later" list nobody will ever open.

Don't let the CTO be the first person to read the design

Before walking into the meeting room, have someone else read the design first. A guide on getting feedback on design documents from Refactoring English argues that fixing mistakes with one reviewer beats having ten people trip over the same one.

At the customer, the ideal first reader is one of their engineers, ideally someone you've worked with for a few weeks. They can point out things you couldn't possibly know, like the platform team having just rejected a new service. Every mistake they catch is one fewer question the CTO will ask.

A consulting session, handling objections one by one

Suppose you're proposing an assistant for an insurance company to help claims adjusters review case files. The architecture includes document storage, an embedding step, a vector database, an LLM called through an external API, and an evals suite for scoring the answers. The following three objections are fairly typical.

Objection one: "The claims files contain personal data; it can't be sent to an external API." There's nothing to argue about here. The right response is: "You're right, I didn't account for that constraint. I'll revise the architecture diagram so the model runs on your infrastructure, and send you the new version by Friday."

Objection two: "Adding a vector database means one more system to keep on call." Don't rush to defend, and don't rush to give up. In an article on finding common ground with engineers who dislike a feature, Product Talk describes this situation: an engineer is stuck on an implementation problem, but it comes across as opposition to the feature itself.

So ask a targeted question: "Which data stores does your team operate today? If we reused one of them, what would we lose?" According to Product Talk, simply asking often lets the person raising the objection see for themselves what the real concern is.

In this example, the objection becomes a design criterion: the other team's operational cost.

Objection three: "Why not just fine-tune a model on our data and be done with it?" This point is genuinely debatable. Product Talk suggests that when the two sides disagree on direction, instead of arguing about the direction itself until everyone is frustrated, shift to the reasoning behind it.

You could say: "This is a big decision. I'll record it as an open question, laying out the reasoning for both options, and we'll decide when your team reviews it together." The test of a well-written open question is simple: the person advocating fine-tuning reads your description of their position and still nods in agreement.

Behind every objection lies a different concern

What you hearWhat it usually meansHow to respond
"Data can't leave our systems"A legal or security constraint you didn't know aboutAccept immediately, revise the design, set a date for the new version
"My team can't operate this"Operational cost, who's on call at nightAsk about existing infrastructure, make operations a criterion
"Why not use X for this?"An approach they've weighed or believe inRecord as an open question, present both options fairly
"Great, let's do it"Enthusiasm, but not necessarily readinessBefore committing, verify the data, people, and processes

The last row is the easiest to overlook, because it doesn't look like an objection. But a CTO nodding enthusiastically may not have clean data, or a team to operate what you just drew.

When explaining a third time still doesn't work

The most telling example of the limits of argumentation doesn't come from a CTO. Vinoo Ganesh tells how, at Kepler, the objection came from a data quality engineer: she blocked a migration of data from CSV to Parquet for about a year. Explaining the technical benefits over and over did nothing to dissolve her objection.

The real constraint was how she worked. She checked data by opening CSV files directly, and Parquet had no reading tool that let her do that. What broke the deadlock was a viewer that preserved her ability to inspect the data; within days, the migration was running.

A year of arguing is worth less than a few days fixing the thing that actually needed fixing. The roles differ, but the mechanism is worth bringing into meetings with CTOs: a recurring objection is usually protecting some habit or risk that your slides never touched.

An article from LeadDev on building consensus with objectors suggests finding the deeper concern and addressing it at that level, rather than getting stuck in endless argument. The question worth asking is "What would it take for you to feel comfortable?"—not how to phrase your argument more elegantly.

Mistakes that ruin consulting sessions

The most common mistake is treating the presentation as a sales pitch, continuing to flip through slides even after the CTO raises a constraint that overturns half the architecture.

The second is settling every controversy in the meeting, making absent people feel sidelined and object afterward. The third is promising changes without setting a date for the new version, so "you're right" sounds like mere politeness.

In terms of career development, this skill is easier to demonstrate than you might think. If an FDE job posting mentions serving as a technical advisor to customer leadership, it means you should be ready with a story about how a customer once objected to your design.

That story should have three parts: what you proposed, what problems the other side pointed out that you had missed, and how the final design ended up better because of it.

An exercise for your next CTO meeting

Before the meeting, copy the four-row table above onto a sheet of paper. For each row, write down what objections you expect the CTO to raise about your own architecture, and how you plan to respond: accept and set a date, ask follow-up questions, or record it as an open question.

After the meeting, mark which predictions were right and which surprised you. Next time, bring those surprises to your first reader.

What makes a CTO willing to invite you back is not winning every argument, but leaving the meeting room with a different design because you listened carefully.
