Can LLM Agents Serve as FDEs: Requirements Discovery and Delivery Evals

Original title: LLM 智能体能当 FDE 吗：需求发现与交付评测

FDE Times · 2026-11-04 · 3 min read

Summary

A study benchmarked LLM agents against a real four-month text-to-SQL deployment by a forward deployed engineer. In requirement discovery, the strongest models uncovered over 80% of deliberately hidden requirements, while open-weight models found only 40–50%. Delivery was far weaker: across eight coding agent configurations, just 25% of runs met all acceptance criteria, with 40% of hidden requirements missed or misstated. Baidu's independently built FDE-Bench, using 49 real customer cases, reached a similar conclusion, with a best average delivery score of 0.5510 out of 1.0. The authors recommend human review of requirement lists and monitoring of agent plans, suggesting FDEs shift toward oversight rather than replacement.

Key points

  • Strongest models found over 80% of hidden requirements; open-weight models only 40–50%.
  • Only 25% of runs across eight agent configurations passed all acceptance criteria.
  • 40% of hidden requirements were missed or misstated, causing delivery failures.
  • Baidu's FDE-Bench independently found a best delivery score of just 0.5510/1.0.
  • Authors recommend human review of requirements and monitoring of agent execution plans.

Key Takeaways

  • The strongest models discovered over 80% of hidden requirements, while open-weight models only found 40–50%.
  • Across eight coding agent configurations, only 25% of runs met all acceptance criteria; 40% of hidden requirements were missed or misstated.
  • Baidu's FDE-Bench found the same gap: the best average delivery score was only 0.5510 out of 1.0.

When sent to interview enterprise customers, LLM agents performed better than many expected: the strongest models discovered over 80% of deliberately hidden requirements. But when asked to deliver a working system, results were far worse, with only 25% of runs passing all acceptance criteria.

These are the two core numbers from the paper "Can LLM Agents Be Forward-Deployed Engineers?", a study on requirement discovery and delivery for enterprise text-to-SQL systems, submitted to arXiv on November 2, 2026. The first author is Marianne Menglin Liu, with co-authors including Dan Roth and Sujith Ravi.

For those working as or aspiring to be Forward Deployed Engineers (FDEs), this study carries a very practical lesson: a long list of requirements doesn't mean communication with the customer is over. Even a requirement that has been recorded can cause delivery to fail if its meaning is captured incorrectly.

The Benchmark: Four Months of One Engineer's Work

The authors didn't run experiments on toy problems. Their reference point was a real deployment: an FDE spent four months putting a text-to-SQL model that generates Oracle-dialect SQL into production at a large enterprise.

The agents had to retrace the two phases this engineer went through: first discovering requirements together with the customer, then delivering a system that passes acceptance.

In the first phase, the team tested 15 models. The strongest exceeded 80%, while open-weight models only discovered 40–50% of hidden requirements. Looking only at the best results, one could easily conclude that requirement discovery, the most "human" part of the job, is nearly automatable.

Why 80% Doesn't Translate into 80%

The delivery phase was a different story. Across eight coding agent configurations, only a quarter of runs met all criteria. The authors attribute the primary bottleneck to incomplete requirement discovery: 40% of hidden requirements were missed or misstated.

These two numbers are not contradictory. The 80%+ figure belongs to the strongest models, while open-weight models stalled at 40–50%. More crucial is the phrase "misstated": a requirement may count as discovered yet fail to match the customer's actual intent.

Take a hypothetical example. The customer says "quarterly revenue," but at this company, revenue must be net of returns, and the fiscal year starts in April. The agent records "quarterly revenue," but the SQL it writes gets both points wrong, and this misunderstanding carries all the way into acceptance testing.

Another Benchmark Reaches a Similar Conclusion

This finding is not an isolated case. FDE-Bench, released separately by the Baidu AI Cloud team with no co-authors in common with the study, contains 49 cases drawn from real customer projects, where agents must uncover requirements that no one mentions initially.

Across 18 agent configurations, the best average delivery score was only 0.5510 out of 1.0. The Baidu team's conclusion: the agents tested cannot yet reliably complete end-to-end delivery at FDE level.

Two independent studies with two different datasets both found that agents fall short at the delivery stage. It is therefore hard to dismiss the 25% figure as a fluke of one experiment.

Two Tasks Still Need Human Oversight

Liu and her co-authors' recommendations are specific: have humans review the list of requirements the agent discovers, and monitor the agent's plan during execution. Under this view, at least for now, the FDE shifts toward a supervisory role rather than being replaced.

The skill worth practicing right now is tracing a single requirement from the customer meeting all the way to acceptance testing, and writing down the business definition behind every term the customer uses. The authors believe human review should be placed exactly there.

When reading job postings, watch for roles where "requirements gathering" or "acceptance criteria" appear alongside "LLM agents." On your resume, a project that states how many requirements you discovered, how many acceptance criteria there were, and how many were met is more persuasive than any adjective.

Agents have learned to ask questions. Checking whether the answers are correctly understood remains the engineer's job.
