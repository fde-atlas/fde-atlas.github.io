Build an FDE Team or Buy Delivery Services: A Dividing Line

Original title: 自建 FDE 团队还是采购交付服务：一条分界线

FDE中国 · 2026-09-04 · 8 min read

Summary

This article addresses the build-versus-buy decision for FDE capability after demand is confirmed. The author, writing from a delivery manager's perspective, proposes three criteria—scenario specificity, iteration frequency, and data sensitivity; hitting two or more favors building an in-house team. For most companies, a middle path is recommended: an external vendor handles initial delivery while you hire one or two client-side FDEs to absorb and validate the work, with absorption depth determining how much capability stays in-house. The piece also covers pitfalls of three procurement forms (model vendors, system integrators, consulting firms), five hidden costs of building in-house including salary inversion and poaching risk in the Chinese market, and five governance clauses to negotiate before signing. It closes with three questions to settle your position, to be revisited every six months.

Key points

  • Three criteria, two or more met, favor building in-house.
  • Middle path: vendor delivers first phase, client-side FDEs absorb results.
  • Don't let a project manager double as the absorber—different skill sets.
  • In-house costs include salary inversion, hiring cycles, and poaching risk.
  • Five governance clauses should be negotiated before contract signing.

Assuming you have already confirmed the need through the judgment framework (see Does Your Enterprise Need an FDE: A Judgment Framework), the next decision—building the capability in-house or procuring the service—is the fork where most enterprises truly get stuck. This article gives practitioners an answer in the form of a dividing line, plus a decision comparison table. The author writes from the perspective of a frontline delivery manager: fewer concepts, more pitfalls. For the complete method of calculating ROI, see An ROI Framework for FDE Teams; this article focuses on the logic of choosing an organizational form. Whichever path you choose, you must ultimately answer the same question: after delivery is complete, what remains in your organization.

First, clarify the two options

Building in-house means you, as the employer, recruit or internally transfer staff to form an FDE team: people are accountable for outcomes, capability accumulates on your side, and on-site work means your own employees working at the client's or business's site. Procurement means buying delivery services externally, mainly in three forms: model vendors' on-site delivery teams (according to openai.com, OpenAI announced in 2026 the formation of a dedicated delivery organization; according to thenewstack.io, Google Cloud is also hiring FDEs at scale); system integrators' project-based delivery; and consulting firms' newly created FDE-type service roles (according to job tracking on fdeinterviews.com, Deloitte, BCG X, EY and others have all posted FDE-type positions; according to thenewstack.io, ServiceNow and Accenture jointly launched an FDE program in 2026). The two choices are not mutually exclusive, but only by first drawing each one's boundaries clearly does the middle path make sense.

A dividing line: should this capability grow on you

The dividing line comes down to one question: does the delivery capability for this scenario constitute your future core competitiveness. Answer it with three criteria. First, scenario specificity: if delivery outcomes depend heavily on your unique business logic and data, the external vendor has to relearn everything at every engagement—the learning cost of building in-house amortizes across projects, while with procurement you always pay full price. Second, iteration frequency: for scenarios requiring continuous iteration monthly or even weekly, an external team's commercial processes cannot keep up with the business rhythm. Third, data sensitivity: under the hard constraint that data cannot leave your domain, the external team must go through approval and authorization again at every engagement, and the cost is paid repeatedly.

If two or more of the three points apply, lean toward building in-house; if none apply, hand the problem to the market; in between, procurement is usually more economical. One industry fact also supports "don't force it": according to analysis cited by joinplank.com, AI projects co-built with partners are about twice as likely to reach production as purely in-house builds, while deployment accounts for only about 20% of an AI system's total lifecycle cost. Sentiment about building in-house cannot substitute for delivery reality; handing non-core components to partners is itself a rational decision.

There is another often-underestimated criterion: the maturity of market supply. According to The New Stack (thenewstack.io), in 2026 both leading vendors and consulting firms are rapidly expanding FDE-type services, which means supply on the procurement side is improving and prices are becoming rational; but conversely, the more generalized the service, the harder it is to go deep into your specific scenarios. Improved supply lowers procurement costs for generic components, while the scarcity of specific scenarios rises relatively as a result—this dynamic is worth re-evaluating every six months.

Three forms on the procurement side and their respective pitfalls

Model vendors' on-site teams. Highest capability ceiling and strongest brand endorsement, suitable for breaking through the hardest scenarios first. Two pitfalls: their objective function is promoting their own model stack, so solutions may not be friendly to your existing systems; and top vendors' on-site resources are scarce—according to The New Stack (thenewstack.io), OpenAI received over $4 billion in initial funding when forming its delivery organization, showing the model has been validated by capital but also that bargaining power sits with the seller, so you need explicit acceptance clauses as a hedge. The countermeasure is to write your existing systems inventory and integration constraints into the procurement requirements document, and require key members of the on-site team to pass your organization's technical review, keeping the right to confirm personnel with the client side.

System integrators. The most mature ecosystem domestically, rich experience in private deployment and Xinchuang (IT application innovation) adaptation, clear project-based boundaries, and the easiest budget management. The pitfall is co-creation capability for scenarios: most integrators' processes are designed for "freeze requirements, then develop," and when facing AI scenarios where requirements clarify as work proceeds, change management consumes enormous energy across all three parties. Lei Feng Network's (leiphone.com) research on large-model all-in-one machine deployments provides a footnote: hardware in place does not mean deployment; the gap lies in business logic and data governance—precisely the parts hardest to stipulate in integrator contracts. The countermeasure is to narrow the requirements scope of a single contract, replace one big contract with multiple small ones, and let change management happen between contracts rather than within one, so each renewal is a recalibration.

Consulting-firm FDE services. Strong in methodology and executive-level dialogue, suitable for strategic project initiation and blueprint planning stages. The pitfall is delivery depth: engineering execution from blueprint to production environment varies widely, so acceptance criteria in the contract must be written to be measurable; the on-site PoC Acceptance Checklist can be used directly as a negotiation draft. Another countermeasure is to require that core members of the delivery team have verifiable production-environment delivery experience, rather than judging only by methodology credentials and case narratives.

The real cost checklist on the build-in-house side

The cost of building in-house goes far beyond salaries; in practice there are at least five items. First, compensation and internal equity: according to 36Kr (36kr.com), monthly salary bands for FDE positions at leading domestic companies are significantly higher than for same-level R&D roles; an analysis by the Tencent Cloud Developer Community (cloud.tencent.com) points out that salary inversion is real—you need to be prepared in advance to answer internal equity questions. Second, hiring timeline: qualified candidates are scarce (reports of dozens-fold growth in job postings over two years at xhby.net), and hiring the first batch often takes quarters; see How to Hire Your First Batch of FDEs. Third, management cost: frontline teams are far from headquarters, and goals, metrics, and feedback mechanisms all need dedicated design; see Managing an FDE Team. Fourth, talent attrition risk: according to 36Kr (36kr.com), enterprises widely worry that FDEs will be scouted and poached by clients or competitors during on-site delivery—delivery assets leave with the people, a risk that the procurement form does not have. One addition beyond the checklist: building in-house is not a one-time decision; a two-step approach of "validate via project-based engagement first, then establish permanent headcount" is recommended to minimize irreversibility. Fifth, diseconomies of scale: a team of three to five cannot sustain a complete methodology and toolchain, unless you accept a long maturation period. The pragmatic approach is to first share methodology assets with ecosystem partners, and only after the team exceeds five people and scenarios exceed three cases, complete your own toolchain.

The middle path: start with procurement, take over in-house

Most enterprises ultimately take the middle path rather than choosing one of the two. The recommended structure is: an external vendor handles the delivery of scenarios requiring heavy initial investment, while you simultaneously hire one or two "client-side FDEs" responsible for takeover and acceptance—they understand the technology, can read the vendor's proposals, and have permission to access the business and data, and they are responsible for truly digesting the external team's output and keeping it within the enterprise. The depth of acceptance by this role directly determines the retention rate of the external delivery.

There are three key points for implementation. First, before signing the contract, use the PoC acceptance checklist to agree on acceptance criteria, translating "meets performance targets" into measurable terms. Second, when the vendor exits, knowledge transfer should be executed item by item according to the on-site handover checklist: documentation, data, eval assets, and outstanding items—none can be missing. Third, give the client-side FDE a clear dual positioning—internally as the acceptance owner, externally as the business interface for the project—to avoid becoming a mere messenger. The cost structure of the middle path falls between the two extremes, but it avoids the biggest risks of both: with pure procurement, knowledge never accumulates; with pure in-house building, cold start is too slow. One common execution deviation deserves mention: if the client-side FDE on the middle path is a concurrent role held by an existing project manager, the takeover will usually fail—project managers are good at managing milestones, while takeover requires the technical judgment to understand solutions and data, and these are not the same capability. It is better to set up a half headcount specifically for this than to let the takeover responsibility fall through.

Contracts and governance: whichever path you take, five things should be put in writing

Beyond the choice of form, governance terms determine the quality of the collaboration. The following five things should be put in writing—for the procurement route, in the contract; for the in-house route, in the team charter. First, acceptance criteria: performance metrics, test datasets, and judgment procedures, all three in place, with terms negotiated as an extension of the PoC acceptance checklist. Second, knowledge transfer: documentation inventory, ownership of source code or configurations, and the handover period before exit, written out item by item per the handover checklist. Third, personnel terms: key-person lock-in and approval for replacements—on the procurement side, this guards against "Team A negotiates, Team B shows up"; on the in-house side, it guards against key-person single points of failure. Fourth, data terms: scope of authorization, anonymization requirements, and data disposal after exit, directly citing the conclusions of your compliance review. Fifth, iteration cadence: the review mechanism and response deadlines for requirement changes—in AI scenarios, requirement changes are the norm, not the exception; putting this in writing saves effort for all three parties. The negotiation timing for all five items is before signing, not after problems arise in the first delivery phase.

Three closing questions. Question one: Is the scenario exclusive and in need of high-frequency iteration? If yes, build in-house or take the middle path; if no, procure. Question two: Are there hard commitments that must be delivered in the first year? If yes, use procurement as the foundation with in-house building in parallel—don't gamble delivery milestones on organizational learning. Question three: Are there people internally who can take over the results? If not, every form will leak—solve the takeover problem first, then the sourcing problem. The answers to the three questions should be written into the decision document, with the validity period of the assumptions noted, and reassessed in six months; when the context changes, the conclusions will change too. Summary and next steps

One closing sentence: the dividing line is not an opposition between in-house building and procurement, but a boundary line of "which capabilities must be internalized." Capabilities that are scenario-exclusive, high-frequency, and data-sensitive should grow within your organization; standardized, one-off, and methodologically generic segments are more cost-effective left to the market, freeing up management bandwidth for true differentiation. Recommended next steps: first calibrate your position with the three closing questions, then use the ROI framework for FDE teams to calculate the costs and benefits of both forms side by side; if you decide to build in-house, see How to hire your first batch of FDEs for the hiring route and FDE team design for team structure; if you decide to procure, preparation for vendor management and acceptance is best started with the PoC acceptance checklist.
