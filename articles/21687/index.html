How FDEs map a sales process before deploying an AI agent

FDE Times · 2026-10-26 · 6 min read

Summary

Using Outreach's job posting for Forward Deployed Engineers on its AI Revenue Agents team as a starting point, this article argues that deploying a sales AI agent requires mapping the customer's sales process first. Failures usually stem from the agent reading stale data or appearing at the wrong moment, not from bad prompts. The author walks through interviewing reps, Marketing, and RevOps across four stages (prospecting, discovery, pipeline management, deal review), building a process map, treating training and change management as part of deployment, and delivering structured feedback to Product. It closes with four common mistakes and advice for showcasing these skills to recruiters.

Key points

  • Outreach's FDE posting lists two non-coding duties: understanding sales processes and client change management.
  • Process maps matter most where teams' actual behavior diverges from documentation.
  • Training, documentation, and hands-on support are deployment work, not afterthoughts.
  • Feedback to Product should include observation, frequency, impact, and a proposal.
  • Common mistakes: interviewing only managers, trusting onboarding diagrams, one-off training, anecdotal feedback.

Sales teams can abandon an email-drafting agent within two weeks, and an Outreach job ad names two tasks, neither involving code, that help prevent it.

In brief

  • Outreach's FDE posting for AI Revenue Agents asks for LLM experience and sales-process knowledge, plus client training and change management
  • A process map is most valuable where it shows the sales team working differently from the documentation, because that is where the agent must read the right data
  • Training and feedback to Product are part of the deployment, not clean-up after go-live

Two weeks after go-live, the logs show that most of the agent’s drafts were deleted unread. Take a team that has just handed over an AI agent that drafts outreach emails for the sales staff of a B2B software company. The demo went smoothly, the emails read well and the executives nodded along. This case is hypothetical, but the pattern is common.

The fault is rarely in the prompt. It lies in where the agent sits within a process its builders have never seen first-hand. A job posting from Outreach describes, quite precisely, the work that helps avoid this.

Outreach’s Forward Deployed Engineer posting for its AI Revenue Agents team requires experience with AI/ML systems, LLMs or prompt engineering. Yet the same posting lists two tasks that involve no code. The first is working with Sales, Marketing and RevOps to understand the client’s business processes in depth.

The second is preparing the client’s team to adopt a new tool and change how they work, through training, documentation and hands-on support. Anyone who wants to be an FDE on AI products can start practicing both skills this week.

Why must the process come before the prompt?

PostHog describes the FDE as the person who closes the gap between what a product can do and what a customer actually needs. Pragmatic Engineer argues that LLM integrations and AI products are a particularly good fit for the role.

For a sales agent, that gap sits in three places: the prompt, the data the agent reads, and the moment it appears in a seller’s working day.

The prompt is under your control. The data and the timing are set by the client’s process. That is why Outreach explicitly asks for “practical understanding of sales processes”, covering prospecting, discovery, pipeline management and deal review. Those four stages also make a good frame for building the map.

Example: mapping the four stages

Go back to the company from the opening. Schedule three separate interviews: one with a few reps who sell directly, one with Marketing and one with RevOps.

Do not ask “what is your process?”, because that question only prompts people to recite the slides. Ask about a specific occasion: “The last time you prepared for a discovery call, which tabs did you open, and in what order?”

With Marketing, the question is which criteria qualify a lead to be passed to sales, and who is allowed to change those criteria. With RevOps, ask to see the CRM configuration and ask directly: which fields are actually filled in, and which are almost always empty? After three sessions you can assemble a table like this.

Stage Who does it Input data Where an agent could help
Prospecting Reps, Marketing Lead lists, lead qualification criteria Draft the first email from the lead’s context
Discovery Reps Call notes, interaction history Pre-call summary, suggested questions
Pipeline management Reps, RevOps CRM stage field Flag deals with no recent activity
Deal review Sales managers Pipeline reports Prepare a risk summary for each deal

The table is only a draft. Its real value lies where the accounts from different teams do not match. Say RevOps reports that the stage field is updated continuously, while the reps say they only edit it just before the weekly deal review. An agent that reads stage midweek is then working on data several days old.

A finding like this changes the design. You might move the deal nudges to just after the review, or have the agent read email history as well rather than relying on the stage field alone. Writing prompts alone would never surface this detail.

Training is deployment too

Outreach files training, documentation and hands-on support under change management, and that is the right place for them. In the company above, if the new tool slows reps down in the first week, they may well drop it, even if it would help them in the long run.

A simple plan can start with a small group of reps trying it first. Each stage on the map gets a short page of documentation answering three questions: what the agent does, what the rep does, and when to ignore the agent’s suggestion. The third matters most, because users trust a tool only when they are allowed to disagree with it.

Hands-on support means being present while reps use the tool for real, not just at the launch webinar. It matches the spirit Palantir describes for its FDSEs: working alongside customers to quickly understand their hardest problems. For a sales agent, “alongside” means sitting next to a rep for a morning of prospecting.

That morning will teach more than any dashboard. Track the share of drafts that are sent after the rep edits them, because that number shows whether the agent really sits inside their workflow.

Taking the lessons back to Product

Pragmatic Engineer defines FDEs as engineers who rotate between sitting with customer teams and working with the core product’s engineering team. Outreach’s posting turns that rotation into a concrete duty: delivering structured feedback to Product and Engineering based on what is learned from customers.

Here, “structured” means more than a complaint left on Slack. Each feedback item should have four parts: the observation, how often it occurs, the impact on the customer, and a proposal.

For the stale stage field, the item might say the agent needs a source of activity signals beyond stage, because at many customers that field is only updated on the meeting cycle.

Four mistakes that make the map useless

The most common is interviewing only the head of sales. Managers describe the process as it should run; reps tell you how it actually runs. The next is copying a diagram from the client’s onboarding documents and treating it as fact.

Also common is reducing training to a single session just before go-live, when habits form only over weeks of support. The last is sending feedback to Product as anecdotes. Without frequency and impact, the product team has no basis for setting priorities.

Putting the skill on your CV

When reading FDE postings, watch for phrases such as RevOps, change management, enablement or “business processes”. They signal that the company wants someone who can stand between the software and the way customers work, rather than someone who only integrates APIs. If you have never worked in sales, study the four stages above until you can explain how a deal review runs.

The best proof is a concrete artifact: a process map you built from real interviews, with a one-page training plan and one feedback item for Product. A recruiter who reads those three pages will understand you faster than from a line claiming “strong communication skills”.

A good prompt decides what the agent can write. The process map and the training plan decide whether anyone uses what it writes.
