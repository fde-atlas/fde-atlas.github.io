Over the past two months, FDE has suddenly become the hottest job title in Silicon Valley. OpenAI and Anthropic have successively set up joint ventures with investment banks for enterprise deployment, and Google has built its own FDE team. But what exactly is an FDE? Is it just a new disguise for outsourced on-site staffing, or the true value node of the AI era?

This episode features Jove, who is building an FDE team from scratch at Cresta—he has declared he wants to build an FDE team better than Palantir's. This conversation offers an answer far deeper than "on-site engineer": the FDE is a translator who converts enterprise know-how into AI-executable context, the last mile between model intelligence and the messy real world, and perhaps even the prototype of the next generation of industry software companies.

01 Starting from Palantir: why the term FDE suddenly went viral

The FDE originated from Palantir's practice over a decade ago, essentially to handle scenarios where "requirements are too complex to be spelled out in documents"; but how much of Palantir's success is actually attributable to FDEs is hard to disentangle.

The conversation begins with a prop—Jove deliberately brought a sign reading "Forward Deployed Engineer" to the table. Translated literally, the term means "Forward Deployed Engineer": sending engineers to the customer's battlefield.

Its origin is that "mysterious company" Palantir. Palantir's clients are mainly governments and militaries, and many requirements are so sensitive that even after signing a contract, you can't write down in documents what exactly needs to be done—someone has to be on site, with the client telling you face to face. Requirements this complex, impossible to deliver through pure software and clear documentation, combined with Palantir's unique Ontology system, made the FDE a highly effective solution. Palantir lay dormant for ten years, but its stock price suddenly took off in recent years, so everyone is asking: can Palantir's success be replicated by copying the FDE model?

Jove offers a clear-eyed demystification of this. He has interviewed many people from Palantir and admits that Palantir's success factors are too complex—whether you can meet with top military brass, whether you can place people inside those institutions, very few companies in the world can do that. FDEs were indeed effective for Palantir, but how much of it came down to "relationships" is hard to say. Moreover, much of what Palantir does, such as Data Engineering and Digital Twin, isn't actually AI.

This is precisely where Jove's confidence lies: the FDE team he is building at Cresta is optimized entirely for AI, succeeding on merit in a purely software market with no mysterious relationships and full competition. Early this year he made a bold statement: he would build the world's best FDE team, better than Palantir's. The team now has 30 people and may grow to 50 or even 100 this year; Jove reports directly to the VP of Engineering, who reports directly to the CEO.

02 Defining the FDE: not outsourcing, but "reliable AI experts + product improvers"

The FDE is best defined in the context of AI agents—a group of reliable AI experts who, leveraging their own product's strengths, stay close to customers, build production-grade systems, and at the same time make their own product better and better. The second half is the essential difference between FDEs and outsourcing.

Many people's first reaction is the same as the question Indigo raised: isn't this just outsourcing? In Japan it's called a staffing agency—send people to the client, finish the work, and leave. When Jove was first contacted by Cresta's CEO (his college classmate, who had previously worked on Contact Center at Google), he too was "both attracted and scared"—after looking around, his first reaction was: isn't this just a Solutions Engineer?

After nine months of doing the job in depth, he offered his own definition, in his own words: "An FDE is a group of highly reliable AI experts who, leveraging the strengths and features of your own product, stay close to the customer, build them a production-ready system, and make your own product better and better." He added: that sentence is long, but every part of it matters.

Why don't customers do it themselves? Those big clients have hundreds or even thousands of programmers on hand. The answer is that AI makes mistakes too easily. Building a demo isn't hard, but going live directly facing end users—for example, building passenger-facing voice experiences for Alaska Airlines or United—means a poorly built system leads to complaints, customer churn, and miscalculated charges. This requires sufficient experience with the fine-grained texture of models and deep familiarity with the Cresta platform itself, and customers have neither such experts nor the willingness to spend three to six months learning.

What truly distinguishes FDEs from outsourcing is the flywheel of feedback. Jove and many people on his team spend 10% to 30% of their time improving the product itself—not fixing bugs, but identifying common patterns after working with several clients and distilling those patterns into the product. Indigo used a precise metaphor: this is like distillation—using human intelligence at the front end to understand customers' various frictions, feeding the friction logs to the machines at the back end, then going back to improve the models and the product. Problems FDEs were painfully struggling with three months ago may already have become part of the product, freeing the team to tackle new problems and new scenarios. The next deployment takes less time.

This also explains the FDE business model: customers must be able to afford it. Cresta's contracts start in the six figures, serving Fortune 100 and Fortune 500 companies. But Jove also revealed the team is exploring downward—small restaurants, dental clinics, recruiting agencies, real estate brokerages. These SMBs without any call centers likewise want an AI assistant integrated with their calendars and order systems to screen calls, filter intents, and book appointments. This is a new market that is opening up.

03 Ontology: helping enterprises build a "super Wiki" is also training a super god

Palantir's Ontology is essentially a super Wiki of enterprise Know-How—a Company Model. All the work of FDEs ultimately grounds the "almighty god" of AI in reality, and the grounded Know-How can only be obtained by being on-site.

The conversation reached its first theoretical climax here. Indigo gave a plain-language translation of Palantir's ontology system: it helps you organize your enterprise's Know-How into a super Wiki you could never compile yourself—all the knowledge nodes and circulation paths of the company live in this grid, and then Palantir's engineers make the system run on top of that grid. It's essentially building a Company Model for the enterprise. Even more crucial is the second layer: Palantir itself takes the knowledge from this model back home, making the next generation of ontology systems better, so the next time it encounters a similar enterprise it can reuse it quickly.

Jove pushed this logic into the AI era: "All of us are training a super god. AI's IQ is already very high, but it needs to be grounded in reality—only when grounded can it solve real problems." [00:13] And how does it get grounded? Through FDEs, through various productized SOPs (standard operating procedures).

Indigo shared a personal practice to echo this: he runs his daily projects as if they were a small company, switching models the way you'd switch teleprompters. Every time he completes a step or thinks through a key decision point, he has the Agent write records and notes—"this was wrong," "this was right, keep it next time"—doing cognitive-level reflective feedback on the execution process, accumulating into very long Logs and Memory. These things are transferable; they are his personal workflow ontology.

Why not just train a model directly? The two gave the same answer: it's too clunky. Jove gave the example of a gaming company—releasing a new DLC every week or two, you'd have to keep retraining the model, which is laborious and hard to correct when errors appear. Instead, using Markdown, Context, and accumulated documents to carry Know-How is relatively transparent and can migrate across vendors. This brings up a very real enterprise mindset: no one wants to be locked into a single model. Just like the cloud providers of years past, big enterprises standardize on at least two clouds—adopting Google Cloud and AWS simultaneously—so if one goes haywire, you still have bargaining leverage in hand.

04 Model Companies Enter the Arena: Why the OpenAIs Are Spinning Up Separate Companies to Do FDE

The motivation for model vendors forming JVs to do deployment is straightforward—their own researchers are too expensive; hiring FDEs under the OpenAI brand costs three times as much, so it's better to set up a separate entity to keep costs down. As long as the deals close and tokens get consumed, that's all that matters.

The direct trigger for FDE's recent explosion in popularity was that, one week before this episode was recorded, OpenAI and Anthropic established joint ventures (JVs) with investment banks to specifically serve the enterprises those banks invest in—I invest in you, so you use my models, my JV's systems, and my engineers to do your deployment.

Jove had just returned from Propel in San Francisco—the world's largest Professional Services industry association conference, with five to six hundred attendees. The industry's view of model vendors entering the field is "both a good thing and a bad thing": the good is that the term is getting hotter, which helps with recruiting and with explaining to outsiders what they do; the bad is hidden in the motivation.

He relayed a circulating take: OpenAI's and Anthropic's researchers and engineers are themselves very expensive, and making AI projects succeed requires hiring many FDEs, who don't necessarily need to be top-tier. If you recruit under the OpenAI brand, the price is triple; switch to a different entity and the price comes down. Hire slightly lesser people at a lower price—as long as the work gets done and tokens get consumed, it even helps the valuation.

This passage foreshadows the core disagreement later: in the eyes of model companies, FDEs are still, to some extent, an outsourcing channel for "helping me burn through tokens"; in the eyes of application companies, FDEs are part of the product flywheel. This difference in perspective is exactly why Jove insists on not placing FDEs under the Professional Services department.

05 The Deeper Meaning of Organizational Design: Why FDEs Can't Report to Professional Services

FDE should really stand for Forward Deployed Product Engineer—essentially still people who build software and products, just spending a lot of time with customers. Organizational placement determines whether this flywheel can spin.

Jove was an "outlier" at the Propel conference: the room was full of Professional Services practitioners, many of whom were very interested in the FDE model (of course they'd be—it sells for more), but his view was precisely that FDEs should not belong to PS.

The reason lies in the first principles of organizational incentives. If FDEs report to PS, all you think about all day is scheduling projects, counting headcount, and tracking hours; when you find a problem with the product, all you can do is open a Ticket and mention it to the product team, and the fix is indefinitely delayed—you're neither trusted nor supported to change the product directly, because that's another team's job. At Cresta, by contrast, Jove reports directly to the VP of Engineering; the engineers building the interface, the backend, and the features are all Peers with the FDEs, and FDE candidates even go through their first-round interviews. FDEs are first-class citizens of the engineering organization.

The energy released by this organizational design is astonishing: to improve deployment efficiency, the FDE team built their own CLI tools and even built their own IDE. This IDE turns a large body of best practices for system integration into Skills—using Claude Skill to turn processes into documents and scripts, packaged as an Agentic Harness.

Even better, these tools aren't just for internal use. Cresta's customers fall into two categories: one is traditional large enterprises with weak IT capabilities (insurance companies, home security), who only care about results—"give me a phone number, and from now on when customers call in, you have to handle it as well as my own employees would"—with zero interest in how you do it; the other is companies with technical capabilities, who explicitly require that after delivery they can make changes themselves—adding new APIs, adjusting Routines—without wanting to be locked in. For the latter, the FDE hands this IDE over to the customer, so the next time they need to modify an AI workflow, they just point and click at the system.

Skills here work like plugins: there are general-purpose ones (payments, scheduling), industry-specific ones, and packages custom-built for a single customer and restricted to that customer. Without leaking customer privacy, commonalities are continuously abstracted out and fed back to everyone.

06 Skills as the Next Generation of Software: Intelligence in Software

A Skill is the automated sedimentation of an SOP, a kind of dynamically executing software—it's not If-Then-Else program code, but flexibly decides how to execute a task based on context. This shift will rebuild the entire infrastructure industry.

The conversation achieves a beautiful leap in abstraction here. Indigo points out: the content of these Skills is even more valuable than the software itself, because it is the automated sedimentation of SOPs, knowing a company's very detailed business processes and cognition. It is also a kind of software, just software that dynamically executes tasks—the carrier might be some Markdown plus some tables, flexibly judging how to execute based on the context of future new tasks. This is the next generation of software: Intelligence in Software.

He gave an analogy: no matter how clever software used to be, it was just the result of executing program code with If-Then-Else logic, like self-driving code written with handcrafted rules; now it's self-driving trained by neural networks—the two are fundamentally different things. The two also cited Jensen Huang's observation: in the past, code might have been executed 100% by CPUs, because it was pure logic; now the ratio is half CPU and half GPU; in the future, the GPU will do the bulk of the work, with the CPU only handling a small portion of scheduling and computation in between.

Following this thread, the conversation slid toward a conclusion that excites investors: the Agent era will rebuild the infrastructure industry from scratch. Indigo's chain of logic goes like this—Agents work like humans, but far faster and with far greater collaboration bandwidth; all the tools human software engineers have used to collaborate, Agents can use all over again. Why have Claude (Agents) come alive? Because of the file system—they use the file system to share knowledge. Next, efficient caching systems, communication systems, and new databases that are more "Agent-specific" will all be reinvented. Jove added: databases and file systems will be the most important things in the next wave of software, and "the market will hype them."

There is a thought-provoking episode here. Before joining Cresta, Jove spent four or five years as an entrepreneur building real-time databases—Single-Digit Millisecond latency, aimed at scenarios like quantitative trading and cyberattack detection, where data volume is large, complexity is high, and results must be delivered in real time. He joked self-deprecatingly that he was a bit too early, but then pivoted: as AI's demands on infrastructure grow ever higher, that field may heat up again in a few years. His career trajectory is itself a footnote to this infrastructure cycle—born in 1981, graduated from Fudan University, four years at IBM doing enterprise search, four years at EMC, seven years at Splunk doing big data and security, then founding a startup building real-time databases, and finally "reborn in another world and switching careers to FDE" (the title of one of his articles).

The two also left an open question: right now we train AI to work like humans, using human-like tools (for example, someone built a Slack-like collaboration interface for Agents), but this may just be a transitional form—opening hundreds or thousands of channels is nowhere near enough for Agents, whose bandwidth supports tens of thousands of concurrent collaborations. Once AI can Self-Evolve in 2027, no one knows what forms of communication will emerge. Indigo offered his judgment: superintelligence will not be born in a single model, but in a group of models—the emergent result of massive Agent collaboration.

07 Model Stickiness: Why OpenAI Can't Do Cresta's Business

Model companies have always had a stickiness problem—users switch their primary model every two or three months. Real stickiness lies in the accumulation at the application layer: complex APIs, domain-specific RAG, semantic detection, low-latency voice—things model vendors have neither the willingness nor the patience to do.

Jove's take on the ambivalence of model companies entering FDE work comes down to one word: stickiness. He speaks from personal experience—two or three months ago he loved using Claude Code, and in the last month or two everyone feels Codex has come back, "really fast and really good at getting work done." This echoes a point Yao Shunyu (Anthropic) made on the show: early differences were more a matter of model companies' willingness—Anthropic was more willing to do Coding and thus gained an early lead—but once everyone has the willingness, OpenAI is no fool either; it hired great people, and Codex took off.

No matter how strong a model is, if it isn't grounded in reality it can't handle transactional work; facing it, users feel like they're facing "such a big chicken and no idea how to eat it." And OpenAI itself doesn't have a strong desire to build enterprise applications, and hasn't accumulated an enterprise-grade platform or a full suite of APIs—so its attitude is: I don't care how you FDEs do it, just close the deals and use my models.

Cresta is the complete opposite: it is rooted in the Customer Experience domain, with very complex APIs, excellent RAG (Retrieval-Augmented Generation), semantic detection, and extremely fast Summarization—these application-layer accumulations are so complex that it's a hassle for users to learn them themselves, so FDEs handle it for customers while continuously closing deals and polishing the product. The FDE's role in this model is twofold: both closing the deal and making the product better—"it's not outsourcing-type work."

Indigo added a jab from an investment perspective: if a model company recruits deployment engineers under its own name, the financials look terrible—the Margin on selling Tokens is so high, while the Margin on doing deployments is so low. Using another entity as a JV both lets it quickly enter the enterprise market and gets its Tokens consumed better within enterprises. At the end of the day, on a model company's chessboard, an FDE company is a channel that helps it consume Tokens.

08 The Huge AI Gap: From Chatbot to Agent Is Far Harder Than Imagined

Most companies and bosses are still mentally stuck in the Chatbot era of two years ago. Going from "knowing how to ask questions" to "being able to abstract your own workflow" is a hurdle most enterprises cannot cross—and that is precisely why FDEs exist.

The conversation shifted from an industry perspective back to a more general observation. Indigo said that having engaged with many small and medium-sized businesses and individuals, he feels the AI Gap is now enormous: since Agents took off, most company leaders and bosses are still thinking with the Chatbot mindset of two years ago and find it hard to shift their thinking to Agent workflows. Consuming Tokens with a Chatbot is too slow; switching to an Agent is roughly ten to a hundred times more—but this transition is much harder than learning to ask questions.

What's hard about it? With a Chatbot, you just need to know how to ask questions; to get an Agent to work, you first have to abstract your own workflow. And reality is brutal: many enterprises can't even articulate their own goals clearly, can't sort out their SOPs—the boss does it one way on a whim today and another way tomorrow. Jove told a piece of dark humor from the front lines: he often encounters client supervisors whose SOP in their heads is a completely different thing from the real operators' SOP—the supervisor imagines the scripts of a perfect world, but Jove randomly samples 5% of calls and takes a look: sorry, even your best operators don't talk like that at all. The real world is extremely Messy.

Chat-and-search style AI usage was only the first step into the AI world, and that era has passed. Now, to actually get work done, it involves real-world intent, standardized workflows, understanding intent, executing workflows, delivering results, and accepting results—an Agent is essentially an employee you hire, and you have to learn how to make good use of this person and how to accept their results.

What about companies that lack clear goals, where the boss changes his mind every day, and where the top thinks one thing while the bottom does another? Jove's answer was blunt: without introducing an external force like an FDE, things will only fall further apart.

09 IT Housekeeping for a New Era: Declutter First, Then Talk AI

FDEs are like housekeeping services—helping enterprises tidy up their messy APIs, SOPs, and knowledge bases first, because enterprises that aren't Ready can't adopt AI. This is also the form the new generation of consulting firms takes: instead of producing Slides, they produce usable AI systems.

When Indigo was on his speaking tour in the Bay Area, he proposed a concept: the new IT housekeeping. This metaphor was explored more and more deeply by the two during the conversation. Just as Japan has services specializing in home organizing and decluttering, many enterprises need to bring in an FDE company to help the company do a digital decluttering.

Jove gave their rough criteria for screening customers: the comfortable ones are AI Native or AI Ready—already using Salesforce, with data models in place, knowledge bases in ServiceNow, just not knowing how to build a good voice agent (after all, things like latency are genuinely complex). For these "good students," success is fairly easy: just pair them with a good model and build a harness. The troublesome ones are the other kind: APIs built casually in-house or by some students, extremely unstable, with even the error codes being wrong, documentation consisting of tons of screenshots and archaic formats—extremely AI-unfriendly.

For the latter type of customer, the FDE's role is to tell them "in a fairly polite yet professional way": you should first fix your API into good shape, straighten out your SOPs, and organize your database and knowledge base—only with that foundation can you do AI automation—otherwise the AI won't even know whether what it did this time was right or what the next step is.

Why are enterprises willing to listen? Because nobody dares to miss this wave. Some companies skipped the cloud wave and skipped the mobile wave, but everyone feels they can't afford to miss AI—if you don't adopt AI, investors don't believe in you, customers don't believe in you, and your peers are all competing fiercely. Adopting AI isn't up for debate; the problem is that many companies simply aren't ready for it yet.

Indigo elevated this insight into an industry-level judgment: the FDE is the consulting firm of the intelligence era. Consulting firms of the last era would produce some slides, produce a deck, the boss would be delighted, employees would applaud—and then what? The slightly stronger consulting firms would tie themselves to HR systems, help you swap out senior and middle management, restructure the company, and then send people in to implement. Today's "consulting firm" doesn't need to replace your senior leadership—just send in FDEs with agents, have them help you reorganize everything, effectively replacing the middle layer with agents.

10 The Disappearance of the Middle Layer Battlefield Transparency and Organizational Extraction

As AI intelligence grows higher, company organizations will extract into a "core decision-making layer + edge executors," with message-passing and summarizing middle managers replaced by agents—because agents make the battlefield completely transparent to commanders.

The phrase "replace the middle layer with agents" opens up a line of organizational reasoning. Indigo's judgment is: the more advanced AI becomes, the more the whole company will extract out a core decision-making layer, and the middle layer will gradually be eliminated. The logic is simple: agents communicate with extremely high efficiency and can handle massive concurrent message passing—whatever happens on the front line, the agent reports back immediately, and the boss knows everything—the battlefield becomes transparent. If the middle layer only passes messages and summarizes, there's no reason for it to exist; leadership can directly command edge employees to do the work. And the edge executors can be humans, robots, or agents—"if humans are needed, humans do it; if not, something else does it."

Jove corroborated this from the angle of management bandwidth: previously one manager managed 10 people; now they manage 20 or 30, and "soon managing 50 will be perfectly normal."

This segment also included a commentary on an industry case: Elon Musk's xAI had low GPU utilization and couldn't sell tokens, and eventually resold its idle GPUs to Anthropic. Jove's diagnosis: they also lack FDEs. The model side is extremely competitive, while on the application side, apart from coding where demand is clear, generating images and videos is "really just fluff—people think it's just for fun." By contrast, customer experience is low-hanging fruit yet extremely concrete: nail it once for United Airlines or Marriott, with tens of thousands of calls coming in daily, billed by volume, consuming enormous tokens, with very substantial revenue.

11 Virtual Jove Live Demo The 20 Models Behind a Single Phone Call

A seemingly simple voice agent has behind it 20 different models, tens of thousands of tunable parameters, and extensive testing and guardrails. Model companies do "unification," while application companies tame complexity into experience.

The show did a live demo here. Jove dialed his own Virtual Jove—a voice agent cloned from his voice (a rough clone using just two minutes of recordings), loaded with his blog, interviews, and hiring process. The use case is very practical: people on LinkedIn often want to chat with him for ten-plus minutes, so he has them talk to his virtual counterpart first, and they get to experience Cresta's product at the same time.

The demo was quite impressive. After the call connected, the AI first made a compliance statement (call recording, used for improvement), then Indigo asked questions under the name Tom. Does Canada hire FDEs?—Yes, including fully remote positions, with a Forward Deployed Engineer opening for Canada currently posted. How many FDEs are in Canada?—It honestly said the data doesn't break down to Canada, but there are about 20 globally at the moment. What's the interview process?—Online application, resume screening, online coding test, a video chat with a recruiter. It answered every question, didn't fabricate what it didn't know, and the information was accurate in real time (Jove added: they've now hired 27 people, and next week it'll be 28—the data updates accordingly).

Indigo's live reaction was: this is not something a simple Q&A bot could do. You can imagine restaurants and dentists using it—what are the latest deals, which doctor is in, cancel an appointment for me—you wouldn't even need a front desk anymore.

So why can it grasp context so precisely? Jove broke down the engineering behind it. The data belongs to the customer and needs to be organized into several layers: static knowledge (knowledge base, company culture, history, who the boss is), dynamic information (inventory, when each doctor is available), and a grasp of tone—if building for a bank, RBC's style should be rigorous, while VanCity can be a bit lighter, which involves selecting voice timbre and speaking pace, even whether to deliberately add verbal fillers like "um" and "uh." If the customer has one or two years of call history, it can be used as samples or for training, closely fitting a real person.

The most crucial numbers are here: model companies pursue unification—eventually just one model that you never see; you just chat with it. But Cresta's system involves roughly 20 different models in the middle, with 10,000 tunable parameters. You not only have to make it work by writing prompts and subagents, you also have to run extensive testing to prove the agent won't make mistakes—won't chat about politics or sensitive topics, can brake, and redacts credit card information. "If you just spend money buying OpenAI, no one helping you won't do. And OpenAI isn't happy doing it itself, so it needs to find an FDE to do it." [00:45]

12 SaaS Is Dead, Customization Is Immortal The Era Where More Customization Is Better

Standardized offerings have lost their appeal; AI has for the first time made "bespoke customization" economically viable. The FDE's value lies precisely in the mess—the messier the house, the more you want to hire a housekeeper.

Indigo, drawing on his own experience in content moderation, remarked: everyone now says SaaS is dead, precisely because overly standardized things have lost their appeal—the more customized, the better. A nearly fully customized product can let enterprises hire far fewer people and deliver faster and better.

Jove shared real-world texture from implementations. Interestingly, "solid IT metrics" doesn't mean "easy project": some clients are extremely wealthy and can afford the best APIs and the best platforms, but a single meeting can involve 20 or 30 people, held every couple of days, endlessly aligning on how to "handle the conversation"—with big disagreements and frequently changing requirements, so the FDE's implementation cycle actually drags on much longer. On the other end, clients with terrible APIs and documentation that's all screenshots are a headache, but Jove takes a dialectical view: "If everyone's APIs were excellent, our value would be lower. The messier my house, the more you want to hire a housekeeper." [00:48] They have good "grease-removal methods" and Best Practices, and the most painful clients are often the ones only they can handle.

He told a particularly vivid industry case: dentists in North America. Dentistry is extremely profitable (he cites personal experience: when starting his business he bought the cheapest insurance, and the dentist treated him with indifference; back at a proper company with a two-to-three-thousand-dollar dental budget, the clinic tried every way to get you to use it all—cleanings, X-rays, everything), but dentists' IT capabilities are actually not great. Cresta helps many dentists with scheduling appointments and insurance claims consultations, and then you encounter scenarios that would never appear in a textbook: a patient whose tooth has fallen out calls in and can't speak clearly—you need the AI to roughly understand where it hurts and whether he wants an appointment next Thursday or the Thursday after. "You'd never know these things exist unless you did it yourself," and you have to go back and tune the voice model: hearing this sound might mean that thing, or let the AI think a bit more.

Jove summed up the unique joy of the FDE job: the closer you get to the world, the better you can fix the last mile. And as an FDE you don't need to change jobs four or five times a year to get hands-on entry into an industry four or five times a year—national parks have their own insider knowledge, dentists have theirs, insurance has its own. This felt sense of "so this is how the world actually works" is a gain beyond the knowledge level.

13 Anthropic's Open Strategy: Managed Agents Are the Next-Generation Model

The next-generation model is no longer a weight file that can be deployed standalone, but a managed agent deeply integrated with cloud infrastructure—context management, memory, and continual learning all live in the cloud. Once an enterprise chooses it, it gets locked into a deep binding like "Microsoft Office + AWS."

The second half of the conversation produced the most forward-looking judgment of the entire episode. Indigo laid out the industry layering: model companies increasingly resemble operating systems; the application layer is no longer standard SaaS (because much of the functionality can be implemented directly with model capabilities), but instead requires people who deeply understand enterprise needs to go in, turn processes into context, and turn context into agent workflows the model can execute.

Executing these workflows requires new cloud infrastructure. Indigo specifically mentioned Anthropic's newly launched Managed Agents platform—managed agents running in the cloud, equipped with context management, memory, and even "dreaming" (Dreams, self-reflection) capabilities. The key point: these things can only live in the cloud; no on-premise deployment.

Why design it this way? Indigo offered his interpretation: once the next-generation model comes out, it's no longer a simple model—a pile of weights you can deploy standalone—it must be integrated with the infrastructure. The managed agent is effectively the next-generation model. Anthropic built a particularly lean model, not very large, but with excellent reasoning ability and intuition, and then relies on infrastructure to reinforce its context, give it memory, and give it continual learning. Once your enterprise chooses this model, you're completely locked into its cloud.

Jove confirmed this logic with a business-minded remark: of course we hope everyone uses it more, instead of constantly comparing me to open source—"Comparing benchmark scores and parameters is meaningless and exhausting."

Indigo pushed the reasoning one step further: as large numbers of enterprises use it, after model companies get massive feedback from front-end FDEs, Anthropic and OpenAI are effectively building their own ontology support systems. This ontology can be handed to front-end FDE companies to use, while the model itself gradually accumulates more know-how, locking customers in even deeper. For enterprises, the endgame looks like—Microsoft Office plus AWS.

Following this structure, the historical positioning of FDE companies becomes clear: the next generation of industry SaaS, or industry ontology companies. If you know an industry's know-how, you can do rapid implementations for that industry; or conversely, the people in an industry who understand IT best and have played in it for years come out and found such companies to serve their own peers.

14 The Compounding Returns of Know-How

Skill Is a Transmission System for Skills

An FDE distills one client's know-how into a Skill, and the next similar client reuses it directly—what used to be two months of work becomes two days, or even a single command-line entry. This is the "root" that pure outsourcing and consulting firms will never have.

This section answers a pointed question: will clients mind that you learn their stuff and then teach it to others?

Jove's answer has three layers. First, PII (personally identifiable information) must never be leaked—that's the bottom line; second, the company must be clear about which parts are the client's secret sauce, and not touch those; third, industry common sense—things everyone does without saying so—is not a secret to clients, and once the FDE masters it, it's extremely useful for the next deployment.

Indigo pointed out the essence of this mechanism: you turn their know-how into a capability of your software, into a Skill; another similar client comes along and uses that Skill directly—effectively transmitting one person's skills to another through a system.

The compounding effect is very concrete in practice. Jove says he has several FDEs who have already worked with three or four similar companies, and when doing the fifth, the client says my API is like this and the process is like this, and the FDE can respond: I can do this, but it could be better—based on my experience, skipping this step and going straight to the next one would be more natural. The client will believe you have experience. "Even when we work with restaurants, we may have more experience than typical restaurant people on how to improve table turnover." For people from consulting backgrounds (Jove has hired people from Accenture and McKinsey), this is a completely different work philosophy: the consulting model is that when a client has a project you go over, solve it, and leave—"there's nothing of your own that needs to be presented," and after two or three years you feel it was just so-so; whereas an FDE's roots are planted in the product, "and this tree grows bigger and bigger"—a case that used to take two months now takes two days, or even just one command-line entry.

The conversation also touched on a counterintuitive sociological observation. In the previous era, SaaS software constrained and standardized the processes of small and mid-sized companies—you had to follow the process the software was designed around, even if it felt awkward. Now, models plus Skills plus FDE deployments have loosened those processes: every enterprise can fine-tune to its own needs, more personalized and better suited to itself. Indigo's judgment is that this personalization will become a new source of stickiness—whether you're a barber or a coffee shop, the more you are yourself, the more people buy into you.

15 Will FDEs Be Automated by AI? Trust Is Humanity's Last Bastion

The past year or two have been hard. AI can do the work, but building trust can only be done by people—coffee, chatting about kids, small talk after turning off Zoom. Humanity's place is at the edge: connecting emotions, connecting things together.

Indigo pressed on a question no FDE practitioner can avoid: if models get better next year, will the FDE job be automated?

Jove's answer was candid: how many years out, no one knows, but the next year or two will be tough. He offered supporting evidence—AI SDRs (automated sales development, the kind that sends cold emails and makes calls) look like the easiest jobs for AI to replace, yet real-world reception has been lukewarm; and how well AI DevOps can perform is also hard to say.

The more structural reason lies in the parts of FDE work that are "beyond technology." Cresta's FDEs actually rarely stay on-site long-term (no one stays more than a week)—usually it's just two days: skimming through and discussing things at a high level, aligning on API details—but the real purpose is building trust. People drink coffee and have drinks together, turn off Zoom and chat about kids and hobbies, and once that trust is established, the inevitable friction down the line (your API doesn't work, your API changed again) becomes much easier to handle. "This is something AI simply can't do."

This creates an interesting picture of human-machine division of labor: once trust is established, the Agents do the work—recording meetings, taking notes after conversations, with AI bringing the context back and acting on it itself. But having AI cultivate trust is too hard. Indigo summed it up with a spatial metaphor: humans are at the Edge, making connections—tying together all sorts of disparate things—while AI does the work in the middle. This also explains why people are pursuing embodied intelligence: to get closer to the world rather than living only in digital channels.

16 Advice for Programmers: Switch to FDE, or Build an OPC?

The FDE profile is "a reliable AI expert + a mini CTO"—fast, precise, and ruthless, ideally with startup experience. An OPC (one-person company), by contrast, is essentially a media business, suited to people who naturally attract attention. These are two completely different paths.

The episode closes by returning to the question every practitioner cares about most: what should ordinary engineers do?

Jove receives hundreds of resumes every month, and his observation is: nowadays everyone calls themselves an AI Engineer—if you're an Engineer, of course you use AI; not using AI would be the problem—but few people truly know how to build AI agents. Prompts, context engineering, tests and evals, voice, the many details of call centers—there are real technical barriers here. His advice to engineers is threefold: first, you can't just know how to code; you need to know how to build AI agents. Second, if communicating with people isn't something you particularly hate, you should do more of it, because AI can't replace that—even if you're a nerd, that's fine; a Tech Lead who is equally nerdy will actually resonate with you more easily, and it's the slick talkers who put people off. Third, think like a mini CTO, figuring out how to combine the customer's needs with your own product to deliver results—there are many trade-offs along the way, and you also need to know how to say no.

He distills the requirements into three words: fast, precise, ruthless. Fast means quick to act and quick to learn—when you take on an insurance project, you need to get up to speed quickly on base contracts, APIs, and so on. Precise means accurate judgment—a project often gets only two people, which is why he doesn't hire juniors: "Juniors are highly motivated, but they may not make very good decisions." You still need seasoned veterans—"their model is bigger, so they can make a good decision." Ruthless means being hard on yourself—overtime and travel are inevitable, and you need to be able to argue with people and carry the load yourself. He especially likes hiring people who have started companies: "Ideally someone like me who failed at it; succeeding at it is fine too"—because people who've started companies know that getting things done is hard, and it doesn't happen just by following the playbook.

Why work this hard? Because this is the era of seizing high ground. OpenAI and Anthropic are fighting for the high ground of models, and several companies are fighting for Customer Experience—once you've captured the biggest airline and the biggest hotel chain, it's very hard for them to leave. Without moving fast and leveraging a good platform, it's hard for one person to make a big impact alone.

The conversation ends with a sobering reality check on the OPC (One Person Company). Jove is not so optimistic about OPCs: FDEs actually avoid the hardest parts of entrepreneurship—customer acquisition, PMF, dealing with VCs, making cold calls. "My job is basically when the deal is nearly closed, figuring out how to finish it in the best way." Indigo offers a sharper framework: producing software is easy now, but acquiring customers is hard, because attention is expensive and intelligence is cheap. So OPCs are best suited to self-media types—people who naturally attract attention; once you have attention, you can sell anything. Code is media, content is media, video and text are all media; if you can both create content and use AI to carry out products with a strong personal stamp that are sharp, serving your fan base in a niche market, then the OPC works—he cites the example of Xiaohongshu creator Peter, who has built more than 40 software products by himself. This kind of OPC is essentially a KOL—celebrity software.

What if you don't have that gene? The two agree: becoming an FDE at a good platform is a bit simpler and far more realistic than building an OPC.

Further Reflections

FDE is "deployment," but even more, it's a migration in the form of knowledge. The deepest undercurrent of this conversation is that the carrier of knowledge is changing generations: from experience in human brains, to documents and wikis, to the fixed processes embedded in SaaS software, and now to today's Skills—a kind of "living software" carried by Markdown and context, dynamically interpreted and executed by models. What Palantir once did with ontologies is being redone in the AI era at a hundred times the scale and one percent of the cost. Whoever masters the ability to "translate messy reality into context" stands at the tollbooth of this migration.

The industry structure is being reshuffled into three layers. Model companies build the operating system (and try to swallow the infra along with it using managed agents); vertical application companies build domain expertise (Cresta for Customer Experience); FDEs handle the last mile of translation and implementation. What's interesting is the tension among the three layers: model companies want FDEs to be merely channels for token consumption, application companies treat FDEs as a product flywheel, and FDE companies themselves may grow into the next generation of industry SaaS. How this three-body structure evolves in the coming years—especially whether products like Anthropic Managed Agents, which integrate "models + infra," will squeeze the middle layer—is worth continuous tracking by every investor.

The rehearsal in organizational theory has already begun. "Replacing the middle layer with agents" is not just rhetoric. When the battlefield is fully transparent to decision-makers and management bandwidth expands from 10 people to 50, the disappearance of relay-and-summarize roles is almost a mathematical inevitability. Humanity's new position is at the Edge: building trust, connecting emotionally, making judgment calls that require accountability. The implication for individuals may be more urgent than for companies—the closer your work is to "trust" and "judgment," the farther it is from being automated.

Key Takeaways

1. The definition of FDE (Jove's version): a group of highly reliable AI experts who, leveraging the strengths of their own product, stay close to customers, build systems fit for production, and make their product better and better—the second half is the dividing line between FDE and outsourcing.

2. Organizational placement determines destiny: put FDEs under Professional Services and they will degrade into headcount-based outsourcing; place them within the engineering organization, reporting directly to the VP of Engineering, and only then can you form the distillation flywheel of "customer friction → product improvement."

3. Skills are the next generation of software: Not static If-Then-Else code, but Intelligence in Software that executes dynamically based on context. Once Know-How is distilled into Skills, it compounds across customers—two months of work becomes two days.

4. Enterprises don't want to be locked in by models: Cross-vendor Context accumulation (Markdown, documents, Skills) is more transparent and flexible than training proprietary models—just like how big enterprises used to standardize on two clouds.

5. Hosted agents are the next-generation models: Models are no longer standalone deployable weights, but services deeply integrated with cloud Infra (memory, continuous learning, Context management). Choosing means lock-in; the endgame looks like Microsoft Office + AWS.

6. The truth about the AI Gap: In the transition from Chatbot to Agent, the hard part isn't technology—it's whether an enterprise can abstract its own workflows. Companies with chaotic SOPs need to hire "IT housekeepers" to declutter first before AI transformation is even possible.

7. Humans belong at the edge: AI does the work in the middle, while humans at the Edge build trust and connect with the world. Trust cannot be automated—this is the fundamental reason FDEs won't be replaced by AI anytime soon.

8. A roadmap for individuals: Being able to build AI agents (Prompt, Context Engineering, Evals) + willingness to communicate + a mini-CTO mindset + fast, precise, decisive = the profile of an excellent FDE. OPC is another path—it's essentially a media business, where Attention is expensive and intelligence is cheap, suited for natural-born KOLs.

9. This is an era of seizing high ground: Win over the biggest airlines and hotel chains, and customers' switching costs become your moat. Move fast, and leverage a good platform.
