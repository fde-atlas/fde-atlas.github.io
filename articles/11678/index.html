Sagas (1987) Explained for FDEs

FDE Interviews · 2026-10-18 · 4 min read

Summary

This breakdown explains Garcia-Molina and Salem's 1987 saga paper for forward deployed engineers. Long-lived transactions hold locks for hours or days, and deadlock frequency grows with the fourth power of the objects touched. A saga splits such work into ordinary transactions that interleave with others; each step has a compensating transaction that semantically undoes it, and the system guarantees either all steps run or completed ones are compensated in reverse order. The tradeoff is lost isolation: others see partial results and are not notified of compensation. The article applies this to AI agents acting across systems without a shared transaction manager: every state-changing tool needs a business-level compensation, compensation must undo meaning rather than restore old values, and irreversible actions like sending payments should come last or require human approval.

Key points

  • Deadlock frequency grows with the fourth power of objects touched
  • Each saga step commits independently with a semantic compensation
  • Compensations run in reverse order; no isolation between steps
  • Compensate the meaning, never write back stale values
  • Put irreversible steps last or behind human approval

The paper in one minute

A long-lived transaction (LLT) takes hours or days: producing monthly bank statements, processing an insurance claim, booking several flights while the customer decides between each one. As an ordinary atomic transaction, it holds locks on everything it touched until it commits. Short transactions queue behind it. Deadlocks become far more likely: in the analysis the authors cite, deadlock frequency grows with the fourth power of the number of objects a transaction touches. And a transaction that runs for hours is more likely to be hit by a crash and aborted.

Hector Garcia-Molina and Kenneth Salem at Princeton observed that many LLTs do not need full atomicity. A booking transaction can let others reserve seats on a flight right after it reserves its own. What it needs is that the whole booking either completes, or any reservations it made are cancelled.

They named the structure a saga: a sequence of ordinary transactions T1 … Tn that may interleave with other transactions. Each Ti except the last has a compensating transaction Ci that undoes it semantically. To undo a seat reservation, Ci subtracts one from the reservation count; it does not write back the count Ti saw, because other transactions may have changed it since. The database guarantees that either T1, …, Tn all execute, or T1, …, Tj execute followed by Cj, …, C1.

There is no isolation at the saga level: other transactions can see a saga's partial results, and when a step is compensated, nobody tells them. A saga is like a nested transaction with only two levels and no atomicity at the top.

The paper then works through the machinery: commands for programs to declare sagas and their compensations, storing saga code reliably, backward recovery (compensate) and forward recovery (resume from a save-point), what to do when a compensation itself fails, how to run sagas on an existing database with tables and a background daemon, parallel sagas, and how to design databases and transactions so that long work can be written as sagas.

Research notes: the problem, approach and results

growth of deadlock frequency with transaction size in the analysis the paper cites, so long transactions are disproportionately costly

compensations run in reverse order of the completed steps on abort

isolation between a saga's steps: others can see partial results and are not told when they are compensated

Long-lived transactions (monthly statements, insurance claims, multi-stop bookings that wait for the customer) hold locks for hours or days. Other transactions wait, deadlocks and aborts multiply, and a long transaction is more likely to be hit by a crash itself.

If the long transaction can be split into a sequence of ordinary transactions that may interleave with others, run it as a saga: each step commits on its own, and each step has a compensating transaction that semantically undoes it. The system guarantees either all steps run, or the completed ones are compensated in reverse order.

A simple mechanism, buildable inside a database or on top of an existing one, that trades isolation for availability: locks are released after each step. The paper covers backward and forward recovery, save-points, failing compensations, parallel sagas and how to design data and transactions so that sagas are possible.

Why it matters to an FDE

An agent that books travel, provisions accounts, places orders or updates records across several systems is running a long-lived transaction across services that do not share a transaction manager. There is no global rollback. Sagas are the pattern that makes such work safe.

Every state-changing tool needs a compensation. For each action the agent can take (create a ticket, reserve a room, charge a card), define the action that undoes it in business terms (close the ticket, cancel the reservation, issue a refund), and when it may run.

Compensate, do not restore. Writing back the value you saw before is wrong whenever anyone else touched the record since. Undo the meaning of your step.

Expect others to see half-done work. Between steps, other users and agents can read your partial results. Design the in-between states so that they are valid, as the paper's "funds in transit" advice does.

Some actions cannot be undone. The paper's example is firing a missile; for agents it is sending an email or a payment. Put irreversible steps last, or behind human approval.
