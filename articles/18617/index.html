What Is an AX Implementation Partner? 7 Criteria for Selection and Evaluation, and How to Avoid Failure

Original title: AX実装パートナーとは？選び方・評価基準7軸と失敗回避

FDX株式会社 · 2026-07-09 · 15 min read

Summary

This article defines the 'AX implementation partner' as an external partner responsible end-to-end for AI strategy, business redesign, implementation, on-site adoption, and operational handover, measured by business KPI improvement and customer self-sufficiency rather than system delivery. Citing MIT NANDA (about 95% of GenAI pilots produce no measurable results) and McKinsey data, it attributes failures to fragmented responsibility across consultants, SIers, and SES vendors. It proposes a 7-axis evaluation framework, a 5-step selection process, and four common failure patterns, emphasizing paid pilots over proposals. FDX's Palantir-style FDE-based offering and a CRM automation case (about 90% input-time reduction) are included.

Key points

  • AX implementation partners own strategy through handover, judged on KPI gains and self-sufficiency.
  • MIT NANDA: ~95% of GenAI pilots yield no measurable business results.
  • Of 7 evaluation axes, implementation, adoption design, and handover support are irreplaceable.
  • Verify capability via a 1-2 month paid pilot, not proposals or demos.
  • Contractually fix exit conditions and knowledge-transfer milestones to avoid lock-in.

What is an AX implementation partner? (3-line summary)

  • An AX implementation partner is an external partner that takes end-to-end responsibility for AX (AI transformation), from strategy formulation through business process redesign, implementation, on-the-ground adoption, and operational handover. It is neither a one-off development company nor an advisor who only gives counsel.
  • Its accountability structure differs from strategy consultants who stop at recommendations and SIers who stop at delivery; its success metrics are placed not on "delivering a working system" but on "improving the customer's business KPIs" and "making the customer's organization self-sufficient."
  • Selection is done using seven evaluation axes (strategy-implementation integration, business understanding, implementation capability, adoption design, handover support, outcome commitment, and executive dialogue), judging by verifiable facts rather than the polish of proposals.

Why "implementation partner" is the choice now

Behind the spread of the term "AX implementation partner" since 2025 lies the structural failure of generative AI investments.

MIT NANDA's "The GenAI Divide: State of AI in Business 2025" reported that roughly 95% of corporate generative AI pilots have not produced measurable business outcomes, with only about 5% of organizations delivering value in production. McKinsey's "The State of AI" (2025 survey) likewise found that while 88% of responding companies use AI in regular operations, fewer than 40% reported company-wide profit (EBIT) impact, and only about 6% are "high performers" who have converted AI into profit. AI adoption has become the norm, but only a tiny minority of companies can convert it into results. That is where we stand as of 2026.

The cause of failure is neither technology nor budget. The typical structure at Japanese companies is as follows.

  • Strategy is drawn up by strategy consultants (implementation is out of scope)
  • Implementation is contracted to SIers (the premises of the strategy are not carried over)
  • Operations are left entirely to IT departments and frontline teams (who don't know the design philosophy)
  • Adoption promotion is nobody's responsibility (no entity owns the KPIs)

Ownership is fragmented across phases, with no entity connecting accountability. The "low-level equilibrium" relationship between user companies and vendors pointed out in METI's "DX Report 2.2"—a structure in which neither the requirements side nor the building side commits to transformation—has been carried over unchanged into the era of generative AI. As detailed in "5 Structural Reasons Why AI PoCs Fail," the structural reason PoCs never reach production is not failed technical validation but the absence of design for post-implementation phases.

What is needed to bridge this fragmentation is an AX implementation partner that carries strategy through implementation, adoption, and handover under a single accountability. For the definition of AX (AI transformation) itself and how it differs from DX, see "What is AX?." This article focuses specifically on how to define and choose the partner to whom you entrust that implementation.

Why neither a consultant nor an SIer

An "implementation partner" differs in accountability structure from every existing external support model. If you outsource while leaving this ambiguous, expectation mismatches are the typical result.

Consultant ≠ implementation partner

A strategy consultant's deliverables are diagnosis and prescription; treatment (implementation) and relapse prevention (adoption and handover) are out of scope. Even when an excellent strategy map is produced, the implementation phase becomes a separate contract with a separate vendor, and the strategy's premises fall apart there. The classification and selection of AI consulting itself is covered in "How to Choose AI Consulting," but what matters from this article's perspective is that consultants and implementation partners are not comparable on the same playing field. The former answers "what should be done"; the latter answers "who is accountable until results are delivered."

SIer ≠ implementation partner

SIer contracting assumes "finalized specifications" and "acceptance." However, generative AI and agent projects involve continuously changing requirements alongside business process redesign, making them structurally a poor fit for specification-freeze contracts. Even if deliverables conform to the specifications, business KPIs don't move. This is the typical outcome of SIer-outsourced AX. Moreover, because knowledge stays with the vendor, every improvement requires contract changes and additional costs.

SES ≠ implementation partner

SES provides person-hours and bears no responsibility for outcomes. Even if excellent engineers arrive, they have neither the authority to redesign business processes nor a channel to make proposals to management, so they cannot be the driving force of AX.

7 Evaluation Axes | How to Verify and Warning Signs

Companies proclaiming "AX" and "AI implementation" are rapidly increasing, and a signboard alone cannot reveal what is actually inside. Quite a few companies have simply rebranded while remaining SES or contract development in substance. Evaluate using the following 7 axes, verifying verifiable facts rather than proposals.

Evaluation axisHow to verifyWarning sign
1. Integration of strategy and implementationAsk them to describe cases where strategy planning and implementation were handled by the same team under the same contract"After the strategy phase, implementation will be handled in collaboration with partner companies"
2. Depth of business understandingAsk how many hours of on-site interviews and business observation they conduct before proposingA proposal produced from documents and a single meeting
3. Implementation capability (production track record)Ask about three or more cases that reached production operation, including technical architecture and business contextOnly demo and PoC cases come up
4. Design capability for on-site adoptionAsk how they designed and measured on-site user usage rates and adoption KPIs"Post-release usage promotion is up to your company"
5. Operations handover and in-house development supportAsk about completed handover track records and evidence that improvement cycles are running on the customer side after handover"We deliver documentation and we're done"
6. Outcome definition and commitmentAsk whether business KPIs, milestones, and exit criteria can be clearly stated in the contract"We will continue supporting until results are achieved" with no deadline set
7. Ability to engage with managementAsk about track record of participating in management meetings and cases where implementation decisions changed management decisionsThe point of contact is only a project manager and you cannot meet executives

It is not necessary for all 7 axes to be perfect. However, the three axes of 3 (implementation capability), 4 (adoption design), and 5 (handover support) cannot be substituted. A partner weak in these three will reproduce "PoC dead ends" no matter how beautiful the proposal. Conversely, 1, 2, 6, and 7 can be compensated to some extent through contract design and organizational design. In practice, score candidate companies on a 5-point scale and compare them using a weighted total emphasizing 3, 4, and 5.

5-Step Selection Process

Here is how to proceed with selection in 5 steps in order to use the evaluation criteria as a tool. The standard duration is roughly 1.5 to 3 months.

Step 1: Provisional decisions on target business and outcome metrics

Before selecting a partner, your own company should hold a hypothesis on "which business, which metrics, and to what degree you want to move." It does not need to be refined, but without it, the comparison axis of proposals becomes only "price and impression." For inventorying target businesses, you can also use external assessments that diagnose AI applicability per business process (FDX offers a free AX diagnosis).

Step 2: Creating a long list and gathering information

List 5 to 10 companies offering "AX support" and "AI implementation," and gather primary information from public case studies, technical blogs, and speaking engagements. What to look for at this stage is not the "number" of track records but the specificity of cases that reached production operation. Check whether business name, technical architecture, and outcome figures are presented as a three-piece set.

Step 3: Shortlisting using the 7 evaluation axes

Score each company on the 7 axes from the previous chapter and narrow down to 2 or 3. In meetings, use the verification methods for the 7 axes directly as questions. Beyond the content of answers, transparency—whether they can candidly disclose failures and withdrawn projects—also becomes an important distinguishing factor.

Step 4: Verify implementation capability with a small order

Implementation capability cannot be judged from proposals and demos. Place a paid pilot of 1 to 2 months with a limited scope, and verify on the ground: (1) how they engage with the field, (2) their speed of adapting to requirement changes, and (3) the operational quality of deliverables (access control, logging, documentation). The few million yen spent here is cheap insurance compared to the loss of a mismatch in the main contract.

Step 5: Main contract | Making handover conditions and exit criteria explicit

In the main contract, clearly state business KPIs, role ratios by phase, knowledge transfer milestones, and exit conditions in the contract document. Grasping from the start "when and under what conditions the partner will step back" is the most reliable means of preventing the perpetuation of external dependency. Cost structure and rate benchmarks by role are detailed in "FDE Cost Benchmark Guide".

4 Common Failure Patterns in Selection

Failure 1: Choosing based on brand recognition and size

Choosing a large company because it feels safe is a typical example of contracting without verifying axes 3, 4, and 5 of the 7 axes. Major strategy firms and major SIers each have their strengths, but end-to-end coverage from implementation through adoption and handover is simply not their business model. Size is not a proxy variable for implementation capability.

Failure 2: Choosing based on the polish of the proposal demo

Generative AI demos can be made to look beautiful by anyone if sufficiently crafted. Between a demo and production implementation lie walls of existing system integration, access control, exception handling, and on-site business workflows, and whether these can be overcome can only be judged by production track record. The paid pilot in Step 4 is the direct countermeasure to this failure.

Failure 3: Choosing the lowest unit price

If a low-unit-price partner leaves no knowledge in-house, the total cost over 18 to 24 months actually grows due to continued external dependency. What should be compared is not monthly unit price but "what remains in hand after the contract ends"—that is, TCO including in-house development. For the break-even point between external use and internal development, refer to "External FDE Use vs Internal Development".

Failure 4: Being unable to see through a mere rebranding under the "AX" label

As the market expands, more companies are calling themselves "AX support providers" while in reality remaining SES or contracted development firms. The way to tell is simple: just ask them, in order, how they handle each of the seven axes. Man-hour-provision-type companies typically get stuck at 4 (adoption design) and 5 (handover support). "Our resident engineers can respond flexibly" is just a rephrasing of man-hour provision, not a statement of accountability for outcomes.

Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ)

Q1. What is the difference between an AX implementation partner and AI consulting?

A. The scope of responsibility and the definition of outcomes differ. AI consulting centers on strategy planning and implementation advice, with deliverables being recommendations and plans. An AX implementation partner takes on implementation, on-the-ground adoption, and operational handover in addition to strategy planning under a single contract, with outcomes defined as improvement in business KPIs and the customer organization's ability to run on its own. Consultants answer "what should be done"; implementation partners answer "who is accountable until results are achieved."

Q2. What is the typical cost range for an AX implementation partner?

A. The mainstream contract structure is a combination of quasi-delegation and performance-based fees. As a rough guide, a senior professional working full-time typically costs 3–5 million yen per month, and a multi-person team around 10–20 million yen per month. However, since costs vary greatly depending on project scale, duration, and specialized area, you should compare total cost—including what remains in-house after the contract ends—rather than unit prices.

Q3. Isn't this model unusable for mid-sized companies?

A. It is usable. In fact, for mid-sized companies that find it hard to hire dedicated AI talent, the structure of launching with a fixed-term hands-on contract and landing on in-house capability has a greater effect. What matters is not company size, but whether you can narrow down target operations, start small, and expand while validating results.

Q4. Isn't the end-to-end model precisely what deepens lock-in?

A. A valid concern, but the structure is the opposite. Lock-in arises from "knowledge remaining on the vendor's side," so division-of-labor or deliverable-based ordering that does not specify handover and milestones in the contract is actually more likely to prolong dependency. An implementation partner whose success metric is self-sufficiency has exit written into the contract as the goal. The way to judge is whether exit conditions and handover track record can be confirmed before signing.

Q5. How long should the partner selection process take?

A. A rough guide is 1.5 to 3 months. Spend two weeks provisionally deciding target operations and success metrics, three to four weeks going from longlist to shortlist, and one to two months on a paid pilot, running contract negotiations in parallel. Rather than rushing selection and signing a large contract first, placing a small order to verify implementation capability before moving to the main contract often shortens total lead time.

Q6. Can we place an order even if we have no AI talent in-house at all?

A. You can, but the design must avoid simply handing everything over. The minimum requirement is to appoint a business owner and have them attend regular meetings with the partner. In addition, nominate one or two candidates for in-house capability building and have them work alongside the partner, so operational capability accumulates in-house during the contract period. If you order while leaving a zero-talent state unaddressed, there is a high risk that the whole mechanism stops the moment the contract ends.

Summary

  • An AX implementation partner is an external partner that takes on everything end-to-end from strategy planning through implementation, on-the-ground adoption, and operational handover, with success metrics set on business KPI improvement and self-sufficiency
  • It differs fundamentally in accountability structure from consultants who end with recommendations, SIers who end with delivery, and SES firms that provide man-hours
  • Selection should be done using the seven evaluation axes, among which implementation capability, adoption design, and handover support are the three irreplaceable axes
  • The selection process has five steps. Verify implementation capability in the field through a paid pilot, not through proposals and demos
  • There are four failure patterns—"choosing by brand recognition, choosing by demo, choosing by lowest price, and failing to see through rebranding"—all of which can be structurally avoided using the seven-axis verification method
  • Grasping exit conditions and handover milestones before signing is the most reliable way to prevent external dependency
