Prologue: When Technology Arrives at Your Door

Original title: 序章 技术到门口以后

pafa.github.io · 2026-05-11 · 9 min read

Summary

This is the prologue of a book about Forward Deployed Engineers (FDEs). It opens with a fictional repair-services company: an AI demo works well, but the customer's real pain—waiting time—may occur in support, documentation lookup, or parts logistics, so a demo alone cannot justify choosing between buying tools, building in-house, or co-building with vendor engineers. The book argues for placing problem understanding close to software change, while stressing FDE is not always the answer: mature products with clear configuration may suffice, and what matters is leaving capability the client can maintain. It proposes reading case evidence as a ladder—contract signed, pilot done, people using it, measured improvement—and offers three reading paths for executives, product/tech leads, and aspiring FDEs. Seven parts cover the role, organization, delivery, industries, countries, careers, and vendor selection, returning to the opening decision in the conclusion.

Key points

  • A repair-company scenario frames the book: a good demo doesn't reveal where customer waiting actually occurs.
  • FDE's value lies in bringing problem understanding and software change closer, but it isn't always the right choice.
  • Case evidence should be graded: contract, pilot, adoption, and measured improvement are different claims.
  • Three reading paths serve executives, product/tech leads, and aspiring FDEs.
  • Seven parts span the role, organization, delivery, industries, countries, careers, and vendor selection.

Let us first put a decision on the table. This is the teaching scenario that runs through the book from beginning to end; it does not correspond to a real project at any particular company.

You run a repair services company. Your team has built an AI demo: it can read fault descriptions, search repair documentation, and generate a clear summary within seconds. The business lead wants to roll it out to all service locations, and the vendor suggests sending an engineering team to build it together. You must decide what the next investment should buy.

The difficulty is that what the business really wants to shorten is customer waiting time. The waiting may occur when customer service staff try to understand the fault, when technicians search through documentation, or when spare parts simply never arrive. The same elegant summary can change the first two tasks, but not necessarily the last one. Right now, no one can pick the answer for you based on the demo alone.

Buy a mature tool and configure it, build it in-house with your own team, or bring in vendor engineers to build it together? Each of the three paths has its rationale, and they can be combined. If you have not even figured out where the waiting occurs, the faster you commit to one organizational model, the more likely you are to spend money on something that is easy to showcase but not the part that most needs to change.

Real projects encounter this kind of gap too. In August 2026, Cohere engineers documented an anonymous meeting-briefing program: the instructions referenced tools that did not exist at all, and the Forward Deployed Engineer later rebuilt the program and modified the accompanying tools. 1 A model being able to write summaries does not mean the other conditions needed for the task already exist. The fix for this particular fragment is traceable, but the cost and final outcome were not fully disclosed.

What this book aims to help you decide is precisely the decision facing the repair company: which problems are worth having engineers step into the customer's work, what should be changed once they get close, and how do you know whether this investment is useful? The epilogue will return to the same teaching company and fill out a decision record complete with costs, alternatives, and items pending verification. The answer is not presumed to be "adopt the FDE model."

Why Someone Still Needs to Get Closer

FDE stands for Forward Deployed Engineer, translated in this book as 前线部署工程师. What we focus on is a type of role that stays close to customers' real work and takes on substantive engineering delivery, while also tracking how customer problems change the product. Different companies assign it different responsibilities; sometimes the emphasis is technical, sometimes it requires more requirements discovery and organizational coordination.

In the repair company, business staff know why customers are anxious, and engineering staff know how the systems run, but the two sides may not yet have explained the problem well enough to act on. Requiring the business side to write out all requirements first may miss difficulties that only surface in actual use; requiring engineering to build all features first may cement a wrong understanding even earlier.

If every discovered problem requires re-explaining across departments and waiting for the next scheduling round, someone needs to continuously walk this distance: able to revisit the problem together with users, and able to make testable changes in the system.

This is what makes the FDE worth studying. It attempts to bring understanding the problem and modifying the software closer together. Whether the value holds depends on whether this closeness helps teams discover misunderstandings faster, deliver more reliably, and leave behind capabilities that those who come later can continue to use.

Sometimes the answer is yes, sometimes not. A mature product plus clear configuration may already be enough; an internal team may be better suited to holding long-term critical capabilities; and a task with no business owner in the first place will not automatically find one just because external engineers work hard.

An Engineering Investment, and Also a Business Choice

When this book gathered its material, OpenAI's FDE job posting linked requirements discovery, scoping, system design, building, and production rollout together. It describes job requirements, not the results every customer has already received. 2 In 2026, the dedicated delivery arrangements at OpenAI, Anthropic, and other companies pushed customer-proximate engineering investment to a much larger organizational scale. Chapter 5: Models and Delivery Changes will compare these arrangements, avoiding the mistake of adding up news-reported investment, headcount, and customer benefits into a single number. 34

Understanding this job requires looking both at which step the engineer changed and at who has the authority to change the process, who bears the cost, and whether the customer's problem made it into a public product. Looking at only one side easily leads to misjudgment: a customer-facing role does not necessarily have the authority to make changes, a successful custom project does not necessarily produce a general-purpose product, and a year of growing product sales does not necessarily prove that one team model is better.

Therefore, this book will preserve records that explicitly use the FDE name, and will also use similar delivery mechanisms for comparison. The former kind of material lets us track the role; the latter helps us test whether the mechanism truly belongs to this job title. Sections on education, public procurement, and similar areas contain many adjacent practices; they serve as points of comparison and must not be summed up into a global count of FDE projects.

Following Time, and Following the Work

When understanding a popular concept, it is easy to reason backward from its recent buzz: the year a company was founded gets written down as the year the role was born, similar early work gets retroactively renamed FDE, and a single product launch gets treated as the date the entire model completed its transformation.

This book will take these things apart. When the name first left a traceable record is one question; how people solved similar difficulties before the name existed is another; and how a particular company formed its own organizational approach requires specific material to answer.

Tracing forward, we will enter Palantir's early records and public organizational descriptions, observing how field engineering and the core product influenced each other. We will then look at how commercial systems and AI applications changed the work, and how a growing number of companies adopted different co-delivery arrangements.

Expanding horizontally, we will enter hospitals, factories, banks, retail, logistics, energy, professional services, education, and research. Industry differences show up in more than just terminology: some systems only need to make suggestions, some change frontline work, and some must leave decisions to those qualified to bear responsibility. Whether software runs is the starting point; how it enters these concrete arrangements is what constitutes practice.

Different regions also need to be understood separately. Local language, procurement procedures, internal technical capabilities, and partnerships can change how a project moves forward. A vendor having an office in a place cannot substitute for evidence of what customers actually use; one case in one country is not enough to represent an entire market.

Therefore, "global" in this book means actively comparing core practices and their boundaries, and honestly leaving material-scarce places on the map as blank. We will not fill in internal details not covered by public information with a smooth story.

How to Weigh a Case

When you see "project success," first ask what those two words actually refer to.

It may only mean a contract was signed, or a pilot may have been completed; there may be a group of people using it, or it may prove that some piece of work genuinely improved. In the last case, you still need to keep asking how the improvement was measured, who provided the data, and whether the costs were counted.

This book reads material along such a ladder. When a company claims what it achieved, the company's identity is preserved; when a customer discloses results, the basis of those figures is explained; mechanisms explained by researchers are kept separate from actual observations. Our own analysis and teaching examples will also be clearly recognizable as such.

When reading, follow the concrete work: how it was done originally, where it got stuck, who changed which step, and what happened afterward. Dates, source locations, and evidence boundaries can be found in the notes.

Readers will also encounter questions without tidy endings. Costs were not disclosed, adoption lacks continuous records, or an effect has alternative explanations. These gaps affect the weight of conclusions but need not render the entire body of experience worthless. Knowing what a piece of evidence can and cannot answer is often more useful than writing it up as an all-conquering success story.

You do not need to finish reading about every industry before you can start making decisions. You can first build judgment along one of the short paths below, then return to the industry and region closest to your own work.

Bring the technology in, and leave the capability behind

A book about the FDE most easily focuses on capable people: engineers who react quickly, have deep technical skills, and get along well with customers. These abilities matter, yet they are not enough to explain why a project can be sustained.

Customers need to genuinely participate, products need someone to maintain them, rule changes need to be understood, and when accidents happen someone needs to take over. A system that remains usable after the people most familiar with it have left often says more about the weight of a delivery than a brilliant demo.

Returning to the maintenance company: the first meeting does not need to immediately decide whether to expand procurement. First pick one category of requests, find out at which step it is waiting, and then compare one specific change with a simpler alternative. In the later chapters you will get the evidence, engineering trajectories, and business accounting needed to make such comparisons.

We will keep reading along the same main question: what problem did the engineer get close to, what capability to change it was gained, and what was left for the next user?
