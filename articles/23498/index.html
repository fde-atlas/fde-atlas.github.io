Key points

  • BPs exchange engineers via SES contracts, as either case providers or talent providers.
  • Deeper contracting tiers mean lower rates; aim for prime or second-tier partners.
  • METI projects an IT talent shortage of up to ~790,000 by 2030, driving BP demand.

An SES partner (Business Partner, or BP) is a collaborating company that exchanges engineers with each other through SES contracts. There are two types of relationships:

PositionDescription
Project-providing BPA company that brings in engineers from other companies for projects it has received from prime contractors or SIers
Talent-providing BPA company that proposes and dispatches its own engineers to SES companies or prime contractors that have projects

Most SES companies move back and forth between both roles—providing projects and providing people—depending on the situation. If they have idle engineers, they provide people; when they have many projects, they seek engineers from other BPs. This mutually beneficial relationship forms the foundation of the SES industry's business.

Summary of the main roles of SES partners (BPs):

  • Supplementing engineers in technical areas the company cannot cover itself
  • Stabilizing the utilization rate of in-house engineers (having people placed into open project slots)
  • Serving as a counterpart for information gathering and exchange to acquire new projects
  • Acting as an intermediary in the business flow to shorten the distance to end companies

Related article: If you want to learn more about how SES contracts work, also read What Is an SES Contract? A Thorough Explanation of the Differences Between Quasi-Delegation, Contracting, and Dispatch.

The business flow in the SES industry is layered as follows:

End company (client)

↓ First tier

Major SIers and prime contractors (NTT Data, Fujitsu, SCSK, etc.)

↓ Second tier

Mid-tier SIers and semi-major SES companies

↓ Third tier and beyond

Small and medium SES companies (business partners)

↓

Freelance engineers

The following terms are used within this structure:

TermMeaning
End companyThe company that ultimately orders system development. Banks, manufacturers, retailers, etc.
Prime contractorSIers and major IT companies that receive orders directly from end companies
Second-tier contractorA company that receives projects from the prime contractor. Up to this level, the business flow is considered "shallow"
Third-tier contractor and beyondCompanies that receive projects from second-tier contractors. The deeper the business flow, the lower the rates
BP (Business Partner)A term for SES companies that mutually provide projects and talent to each other

The goal in SES sales is to partner with BPs as close to the top of the business flow as possible—that is, companies close to prime contractors or second-tier contractors. The further down the chain (third, fourth tier), the more margins accumulate for the intermediaries, and the rates that can be paid to engineers decline.

There are three main reasons why SES companies focus on developing partners.

1. Addressing the engineer shortage

According to a survey by the Ministry of Economy, Trade and Industry, Japan is projected to face a shortage of up to approximately 790,000 IT professionals by 2030. It is realistically difficult to secure enough engineers through in-house recruiting alone, making it essential to have a system for receiving engineers from other BPs.

2. Expanding technical coverage

IT projects span a wide range of technical areas: infrastructure, web, embedded systems, AI/machine learning, and more. If BPs can fill in the areas a company cannot handle itself, the range of projects it can take on expands significantly.

3. Stabilizing utilization rates

When in-house engineers have idle time, receiving project referrals from BPs can shorten the waiting period. Conversely, when projects pile up, supplementing staff from BPs prevents missed opportunities.

In the SES industry, telemarketing is the easiest and fastest-acting prospecting method. Since the engineer shortage has persisted in the IT industry for a long time, appointments are easier to secure than in other industries.

Why telemarketing works:

SES sales representatives often think "we always want more partners," so they are willing to listen even to calls from unknown companies. Some in the field say that once you get the hang of it, you can secure appointments at a 60–70% rate.

Sample telemarketing script (basic structure):

1. Greeting and self-introduction:

"Hello, this is [Name] from [Company] Co., Ltd.

I am calling after looking at your website."

2. State your purpose concisely:

"We run an SES business providing IT engineers and

are looking for partner companies we can collaborate with."

3. Propose a schedule (immediately):

"Would it be possible to set up an information exchange meeting next week?

I would appreciate about 30 minutes of your time."

Tips for telemarketing success:

  • To reach the right person, ask for "the person in charge of partners" or "the person in charge of SES"
  • Keep your company introduction minimal and propose a meeting first
  • Don't try to explain everything upfront; maintain an "let's discuss in person" stance
  • Manage your lists with a CRM or spreadsheet to avoid duplicate outreach

Points to note about email sales:

Emails get fewer replies than cold calls and are often ignored without being read. That said, you can increase the open rate by crafting your subject line and attaching a concrete skill sheet. Sending emails as a follow-up after a call, combined with telemarketing, tends to be effective.

Effective subject line examples for BCP prospecting emails:

  • "[React/TypeScript Project Introduction] Partner inquiry"
  • "2 Senior Java Engineers | Project and talent proposal"
  • "Request to share our engineer profiles | [Company] Co., Ltd."

One of the BP development methods with a particularly high closing rate in the SES industry is referrals. When an introduction comes from an existing BP with whom you already have a trusting relationship or from your own engineers, discussions start from a position of established trust, and the path to a contract is much faster.

Approaches for referral-based development:

  • Regularly ask existing BPs, "Do you know any other companies we could collaborate with?"
  • Ask your own engineers to introduce SES companies they know from previous jobs or client sites
  • After business meetings, when the other party asks, "Do you know anyone else looking for engineers like this?", build a culture of referrals

Tips for building relationships that generate referrals:

  • Never skip thank-yous and follow-ups with existing BPs (thank-you emails after a project is secured, monthly status sharing, etc.)
  • Maintain the stance of being "a provider of information" rather than "a receiver of information"
  • Even for deals that fall through, continuing courteous follow-up builds trust over time

A BP with many projects looks attractive, but if none of them match your engineers, the number means nothing. If engineers join projects without matching technologies, they cannot perform well, the client's evaluation drops, and no further projects come their way. The first thing to look at when choosing a BP is not the number of projects but whether they align with your own talent.

Skill match verification checklist:

  • Have you shared skill sheets before making proposals and identified engineers available for interviews?
  • Do the project's technologies, frameworks, and infrastructure environments match your engineers' experience?
  • Does the project's difficulty level (upstream design to implementation to testing) match the target engineers' level?
  • If the client site requires industry or domain knowledge, do the engineers have similar experience?

Common examples of skill mismatches:

  • Proposing an engineer with only Java experience to a Python AI project
  • Sending an engineer with no infrastructure experience to a cloud construction project
  • Proposing an engineer without English skills to a project requiring English (a foreign-owned end client)

Among these, the depth of the transaction chain directly affects rates. Even engineers with the same skills can see monthly rates differ by 100,000–200,000 yen or more between projects received directly from a prime contractor and those received as a third-tier subcontractor. It's not uncommon. By asking "how many of your projects are lower-tier subcontracted work" before starting a relationship, you can avoid later discovering that no profit is being made.

In SES transactions, things don't end once a project is secured. Rather, the communication that follows lasts longer. Sharing engineers' availability, negotiating rates, updating skill sheets—contact continues for months. If the other party is slow to respond or hard to communicate with, your project sites suffer every time.

Points to check for communication quality:

  • Speed of response to initial information-exchange emails and calls
  • Accuracy of skill sheets (whether work history and tech stack are specific)
  • Whether they clearly communicate "our challenges" and "benefits to the other party" during meetings
  • Whether they provide post-placement follow-up (monthly availability checks, sharing engineers' status)

Criteria for judging whether they are a "trustworthy BP":

  • They don't resist signing contracts or NDAs
  • They continue sending information even when no deal is closed
  • They report engineer issues (health problems, early departures) promptly without hiding them
  • They make proposals that consider the other party's circumstances, not just their own profit

In SES sales, it's said that building deep relationships with a small number of BPs leads to better results than shallow, broad relationships with many.

What is the "Principle of Focusing on 5 Companies":

The idea is that if you keep exchanging information weekly with 5 trusted BPs, stable project-and-talent matches will emerge every month. Connecting thinly with 100 companies yields fewer actual deals than connecting deeply with 5.

How to choose your 5 focus BPs:

  • Companies with at least one closed deal within 2–3 months of the initial information exchange
  • Companies that proactively send project information weekly or biweekly
  • Companies whose technical domains complement your own
  • Companies where you've built a personal-level relationship of trust with the contact person
  • Companies holding projects with shallow transaction chains (up to second-tier subcontracting)

To maintain a relationship with BPs, it's effective to keep proactively providing something yourself rather than waiting to receive information.

Practical actions for maintaining relationships (by frequency):

FrequencyAction

Biweekly to monthlyCheck in by phone to understand both parties' situations
QuarterlyDeepen the relationship through information-exchange meetings (in person or online)
As neededSend a thank-you message the same day a deal is closed

How to write an effective skill sheet:

A skill sheet is a sales document aimed at BPs. If the following information is clearly written, it becomes easier for the other party to pitch to their internal project managers.

  • Name (anonymized is acceptable), age, nearest station, and available start date
  • Specific listing of strong technologies (languages, frameworks, cloud, databases)
  • Overview of recent projects (scale, role, phases handled)
  • Desired rate, work arrangement (on-site/remote), and available on-site areas

The "throwing jabs" communication strategy:

Rather than proposing an engineer right away, it's easier to get proposals accepted if you first ask, "Do you have anyone who could move if a project like this comes in?" By gauging the other party's interest before acting, you also reduce the hassle of arranging pointless meetings.

In the SES industry, troubles can arise from dealing with companies that lack knowledge or have bad intentions. Think carefully before forming a BP relationship with a company showing the following signs.

Common characteristics of black BPs:

  • They resist or postpone signing contracts or NDAs
  • They change (lower) rates after placement without permission
  • The on-site client gives direct work instructions to engineers (possible disguised subcontracting)
  • Payments are delayed or invoices go unpaid
  • They propose engineers with falsified or exaggerated skill sheets
  • Their projects are mostly fourth-tier subcontracting or lower, with abnormally deep transaction chains

Legally notable risks:

SES contracts are in principle "quasi-mandate (delegation) contracts," and you cannot give direct work instructions to engineers. If the client gives direct instructions to engineers, it could constitute "disguised subcontracting" and violate the Worker Dispatch Law. Clarify "who gives the instructions" before signing a contract.

Also, dispatching an engineer who is already under a dispatch contract to yet another company (double dispatch) is prohibited by law. The more complex the transaction chain, the higher this risk, so be especially careful with third-tier subcontracted projects and beyond.
