How to Communicate with Customers During an Outage: Incident Notification Template

Original title: 故障期间如何与客户沟通：事故通报模板

FDE Times · 2026-11-02 · 7 min read

Summary

This guide teaches forward deployed engineers how to communicate with customers during outages before the root cause is known. It recommends assigning a single communication owner, using four status labels (Investigating, Identified, Monitoring, Resolved) and a six-line template covering status, impact, progress, customer actions, next update time, and owner. Key rules: send the first notice immediately, promise a next update time instead of guessing a recovery ETA, and close incidents with impact windows measured by the customer's clock. A worked retail connector outage example shows six notices, plus common phrasing mistakes and a practice exercise.

Key points

  • Send the first notice immediately without waiting for root cause.
  • One person owns communication; handovers must be explicit and confirmed.
  • Promise next update times, never guessed recovery ETAs.
  • Close incidents with impact start and end times from the customer's perspective.
  • When fixes depend on the customer's team, still keep a single voice.

Key takeaways

  • Send the first notification immediately; don't wait until the cause is identified.
  • Only one person owns communication, with explicit, face-to-face handoffs every time the owner changes.
  • Commit to the next update time, not a guessed recovery time; when closing the incident, state when the impact started and ended.

A customer's orders suddenly stop flowing into their warehouse system. Three minutes after you open the logs, a question pops up in the shared chat channel: "What's going on? When will it be fixed?"

At that moment you know neither the cause nor when it will be fixed. Engineers typically react in one of two ways: either stay quiet until they feel confident, or throw out a time to reassure the customer. Both are best avoided. Silence leaves the customer unable to explain things to their own team, while a thrown-out time is a promise you have no basis to keep.

As a Forward Deployed Engineer (FDE), you may be fixing the incident while also being the person the customer contacts directly, as in this scenario. The good news is that writing incident updates during a fire drill doesn't require good prose—just one owner, four statuses, and a few prepared templates.

When the whole team is fixing the incident, who talks to the customer?

Google's SRE book draws a clear line between two roles. The incident commander holds the overall picture of the incident; the communications lead speaks for the team externally, with the duty of proactively sending regular updates to the team and stakeholders rather than waiting to be asked.

There are practical reasons for this division of labor. PagerDuty notes that proactively updating stakeholders lets the core team focus on handling the incident. Customers don't have to chase for answers, and whoever is watching the logs doesn't have to stop to reply to messages.

Only two people on the team? One fixes, one writes. If you're alone at the customer site, immediately ask a colleague back at your company to take over writing updates; at minimum, set an alarm for each send time so you don't forget.

When the owner changes, don't let the handoff happen silently. Google requires the outgoing commander to say it explicitly: "You are now the incident commander, correct?" Customer communication should work the same way: state it face-to-face, wait for confirmation, then tell the customer the new owner's name.

Four words to tell the customer where the incident stands

incident.io's templates use a one-line status to show progress, with four values: Investigating, Identified, Monitoring, and Resolved. These four words let customers see how far the incident has progressed without needing to understand your architecture.

Investigating means you know there's a problem but not yet the cause. Identified means the fault has been located and a fix is underway. Monitoring means the fix is live and you're watching whether it holds. Resolved means the customer is no longer affected.

Every update uses the same six-line skeleton: status, impact, progress, what the customer needs to do, next update time, and owner. This structure is adapted from incident.io's internal update template; per Statuspal's advice, write only facts in each line.

Statuspal also puts it bluntly: you don't need to wait until the problem is identified to communicate. The first update only needs to confirm that a problem has been detected, which parts are affected, and who is handling it.

OneUptime warns that inventing an estimated time to resolution (ETA) amounts to making a promise the team has no basis to keep. The next update time, however, is something you can honor—as long as you make clear it is not a fix time.

On update frequency, incident.io sets an upper limit: during a customer-impacting incident, never go more than an hour without an update. If the customer's business is down, choose a shorter interval, such as 30 minutes.

One incident, six updates

Imagine a retail customer. You deployed a connector for them that pushes orders from the sales portal to the warehouse. At 14:05 new orders stop arriving at the warehouse; at 14:12 you send the first update.

Status: Investigating (14:12) Impact: Since around 14:05, new orders have not been entering the warehouse system; orders already in the warehouse are unaffected. Progress: Checking the connector; cause not yet clear. What you need to do: Nothing for now. Next update: 14:40, or sooner if there's a major change. Owner: [your name]

No cause yet, no lengthy apology, but the customer's operations lead already knows enough to notify the warehouse team. At the agreed 14:40, you know more.

Status: Identified (14:40) Impact: The warehouse system has been rejecting authentication since 14:05; 340 orders are queued and waiting, none lost. Progress: Obtained a new key and preparing to re-run the queue. What you need to do: Please have IT issue a new API key; in the meantime, the warehouse can manually enter urgent orders. Next update: 15:10. No recovery time yet. Owner: [your name]

At 14:55 you discover that when the queue is re-run, manually entered orders will be pushed to the warehouse again. OneUptime advises sending an update immediately when the impact expands, a workaround becomes unsafe, or the recovery assessment changes significantly. Don't wait until 15:10.

Status: Identified (14:55) Impact: When the sync is re-run, manually entered orders may be duplicated. Progress: Waiting for the new key and preparing to remove those orders from the queue. What you need to do: Stop manual entry immediately and send us a list of the orders already entered. Next update: 15:10. Owner: [your name]

At 15:10, the new key has just arrived and isn't fully in effect yet. You send the update anyway, because that's the time you promised the customer.

Status: Identified (15:10) Impact: New orders are still queued and have not reached the warehouse. Progress: The new API key has been received and is being configured. What you need to do: If there are any remaining lists of manually entered orders, please send them over as well. Next update: 15:20. No recovery time yet. Owner: [your name]

Ten minutes later, the key takes effect and the sync is running again.

Status: Monitoring (15:20) Impact: The sync is running again, but some queued orders have not yet reached the warehouse. Progress: The new key took effect at 15:12; processing the remaining queue and continuing to monitor—the incident is not yet closed. What you need to do: No further action needed at this time. Next update: 15:50. Owner: [your name]

At this point you might want to write "everything is fixed." Hold off. The key has been replaced, but the customer's warehouse is still missing orders—for them, the incident isn't over.

Status: Resolved (15:50) Impact: From 14:05 to 15:31, a total of 86 minutes; 412 orders were delayed in reaching the warehouse, with none lost and none duplicated. Progress: The sync has returned to normal; the engineering team is completing the root cause analysis and will send it to you once done. What you need to do: Nothing. Owner: [your name]

Statuspal requires the closing update to state the impact window, including start and end times. In this example, the end time is 15:31, when the last order reached the warehouse—not 15:12, when you finished swapping the key. Go by the customer's clock, not the deployment's clock.

When the fix depends on the customer's IT team, who speaks?

This is exactly where FDE work differs from ordinary on-call duty. At 14:40 in the example above, you've already located the fault but can't fix it yourself, because the API key is issued by the customer's IT team. The operations lead will still come to you, because you're the person they see every day.

If you reply "IT can probably fix it in half an hour," you've made a commitment on behalf of a team you don't manage. If IT also sends their own message, the operations lead will receive two versions of the same incident.

So immediately find your IT point of contact and settle it in one sentence: "I'll still be the one sending the shared updates to operations. Let me know when the key is ready and I'll report it as-is, okay?" Just like with handoffs, wait for confirmation.

If the client wants their own team to send the message, step back and switch to feeding that person facts. It doesn't matter who writes it—what matters is that only one person does.

The "what needs to happen" line should also state who owns the next step, without blaming anyone: "Your IT team ([contact name]) is issuing the key; completion time pending IT confirmation." This way the operations lead knows whom to ask, and you don't become the guarantor of another team's progress.

Set it up before the fire starts

When you arrive at a new client, the first questions to ask are: in an incident, who needs to know, through which channel, and if the broken part belongs to their team, who sends the message? Lock in these answers in the first week of the deployment, and at the same time designate your own primary sender and backup.

When a failure happens, use the six-line skeleton to send an "investigating" update immediately. Set an alarm for the next update time, and send it the moment it goes off—even if all you can say is "no new progress yet, still investigating X." Before each send, ask yourself: what changed for the customer? Not what changed in the code.

This skill should also show up in your job application materials. When reading FDE job descriptions, watch for phrases like incident response or customer-facing. In interviews, walk through one incident using a specific update timeline, rather than vaguely claiming you have "strong communication skills."

Words to delete before hitting send

Most mistakes come from a few habitual phrases, and the fix is always the same: replace speculation with observed facts.

What slips outWhat you should send
Probably fixed in 30 minutesNo recovery time yet. Next update at 15:00
The warehouse team may have changed the configThe warehouse system has been rejecting authentication since 14:05; investigating the cause
The system has a small issueNew orders are not reaching the warehouse; existing orders are unaffected
All fixed!Service restored; affected window was 14:05 to 15:31

Three more mistakes have nothing to do with wording. First, having two people reply to the customer with two different versions. Second, staying silent because "there's nothing to say yet"—which is exactly when the customer is most anxious. Third, writing "no data loss" without verifying; when unsure, write "verifying."

Rewrite an old incident this week

Pick an incident your team has handled and reopen its timeline. Write four updates using the skeleton, with the constraint that each one can only use information the team actually knew at that moment, then hand the first update to a colleague who wasn't involved.

If within 30 seconds they can tell you what broke, who's on it, and when the next update is coming, the update passes. When a real incident arrives, you won't be leaving the customer waiting while you learn on the fly how to write.
