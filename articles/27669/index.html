Today's Judgment

Today's most important signal comes from the judicial system: the Supreme People's Court has officially issued the "Opinions on Adjudicating AI-Related Dispute Cases in Accordance with the Law", with 5 parts and 24 articles, covering torts, intellectual property, technology contracts, data use, evidence review, and criminal offenses.

This document means far more to enterprise AI practitioners than "just another policy": it elevates what FDEs emphasize daily—"logs, versions, data provenance, test records, limitation disclosures, and human review"—from engineering best practices to liability and evidence assets in potential disputes. Whether or not you did these things will now affect not only delivery quality, but potentially the outcome of a lawsuit.

Industry signals from the same period echo the liability theme: the KubeCon China agenda features a China Merchants Bank talk on "scaling digital employees in production environments," and there is a new reference point for overseas FDE team staffing models.

Key Signals

Supreme People's Court's 24-article opinions: judicial checkpoints for enterprise AI contracts and delivery evidence

  • Source: Supreme People's Court | Original link
  • On September 7, the Supreme People's Court issued Fa Fa [2026] No. 10, the "Opinions on Adjudicating AI-Related Dispute Cases in Accordance with the Law." Rules directly relevant to enterprise AI delivery:
  • Fault determination will consider the specific scenario, degree of autonomy, transparency, potential risks, preventive measures, and the user's ability to foresee and control
  • AI product liability will focus on whether applicable scenarios, inherent limitations, and foreseeable risks were truthfully disclosed and clearly warned about
  • Technology contract breaches will be assessed based on contractual terms and whether the developer made reasonable efforts
  • Evidence requirements: in intellectual property cases, developers may need to substantiate claims with training data provenance, training process records, and model operating modes; courts will focus on the authenticity and integrity of electronic data; AI-generated legal materials must be verified and their use disclosed before submission
  • Why it matters: this is the first time China's judicial system has systematically established an adjudication framework for AI disputes. The direct impact on FDEs is that the deliverables checklist needs a new "judicial dimension": data provenance and authorization, model/prompt/rule versions, operation logs, applicable limitations and warnings, human review records, infringement notice handling, exception escalation, and liability allocation—these eight categories of evidence should be built into contract annexes and acceptance packages upfront, not patched together after an incident. The principle that "the higher the degree of autonomy, the more detailed the liability review" also reaffirms the necessity of keeping human sign-off on high-risk actions. Note: this is a judicial guidance document; specific contract revisions still require lawyers to review them against project scenarios.

KubeCon China agenda signal: China Merchants Bank to share "scaling digital employees in production environments"

  • Source: Linux Foundation | Event page
  • KubeCon + CloudNativeCon China will be held in Shanghai September 7-9, and the official agenda lists a China Merchants Bank talk: "Scaling Digital Employees in Production Environments: A Controllable AI Agent Approach."
  • Why it matters: a leading bank is beginning to share "production environment + controllable" agent experience at an international technology conference—note the combination of these two keywords: not a PoC, not a lab, but a production environment; not fully automated, but controllable. This is consistent with banks' earlier semi-annual report statements that "high-risk credit decisions remain under human oversight." The consensus forming in the financial industry is that scalability presupposes controllability. Currently only the agenda and question setup are available; after the event, it's worth tracking the talk materials for deployment scale, permission design, and human takeover mechanisms.

Overseas FDE team staffing reference: cross-functional delivery units

  • Source: AHEAD Careers | Careers page
  • The FDE careers page of US IT services provider AHEAD showcases a team staffing model worth referencing: FDEs are placed within enterprise internal AI transformation teams, forming cross-functional delivery units with business stakeholders, transformation consultants, and adoption leads, end-to-end responsible for discovery, design, build, launch, optimization, and ongoing support.
  • Why it matters: the triangle of "FDE + transformation consultant + adoption lead" answers a common question—why do solo FDEs often deliver systems without achieving adoption? Because engineering, change management, and usage promotion are three different disciplines. When domestic service providers build FDE teams, this staffing model is more worth emulating than "hiring only engineers." Note: the job page proves the role structure, not team size or client outcomes.

Implications for FDE Practitioners

  • Turn judicial evidence requirements into contract annex checklists: data provenance and authorization, version records, operation logs, applicable limitations and warnings, human review, infringement notice handling, open-source licenses, evidence preservation, exception escalation, and liability allocation—write each item into contract annexes and have lawyers review them. This is both risk defense and a professionalism selling point.
  • Explain to clients that "degree of autonomy is proportional to liability": courts will consider the degree of autonomy and the user's ability to control—this provides a judicial basis for "keeping human sign-off on high-risk actions," so persuading clients no longer relies on engineering intuition.
  • Learn the "triangle model" for team staffing: the FDE handles engineering and the evidence chain, the transformation consultant handles organization and process, and the adoption lead handles usage and promotion—these three roles can be three people, or in a small team one person wearing three hats, but all three responsibilities must be covered by someone.
