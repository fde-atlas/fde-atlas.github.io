Should You Avoid FDE? 5 Reasons It's Said to Be Tough, Its Drawbacks, and Who Isn't Suited for It

Original title: FDEはやめとけ?きつい・デメリットと言われる5つの理由と向かない人の特徴

Summary

This article examines five reasons the FDE role is often called grueling or advised against, verifying each against primary sources such as Palantir and OpenAI job postings, The Pragmatic Engineer, First Round Review, and a16z. It argues that travel/on-site work and ambiguity with end-to-end accountability are inherent to the role and openly stated in postings, while three other concerns—work not becoming personal assets, drift toward sales support eroding market value, and Japan's nominal FDEs resembling SES—depend on company design and can be screened before applying. It closes with traits of suitable candidates and interview questions.

Key points

  • Palantir's Tokyo FDSE posting requires 25–75% travel; OpenAI's Tokyo FDE role involves mainly domestic travel.
  • The Pragmatic Engineer reports only about 10% of engineers want FDE roles, with many newer postings skewing toward sales support.
  • Whether FDE work feeds back into the product and how success is measured depends on company design, not the role itself.
  • In Japan, nominal FDEs can be spotted by lack of an own product and per-person-month contracting similar to SES.
  • First Round Review lists five traits for FDEs: independent thinking, grit, high technical bar, building, and business curiosity.

Written and reviewed by: FDE Talent Editorial Team/Primary sources such as official job postings and official announcements from each company are cited

Voices like "avoid FDE," "FDE is tough," and "FDE is awful" include both well-founded claims and those that simply project the image of SES (contract-based staffing) onto the role. Looking at actual job postings and interview articles, burdens such as travel and on-site client work, ambiguous requirements, and accountability for results are explicitly stated by the companies themselves—they are not hidden issues. On the other hand, whether that burden becomes "tough" or "rewarding" depends greatly on the company's design and the individual's aptitude.

This article organizes the reasons why FDE (Forward Deployed Engineer) is said to be "something to avoid" into five points, and verifies each one against primary sources such as official job postings from Palantir and OpenAI, The Pragmatic Engineer, First Round Review, a16z, and interview articles and technical blogs from Japanese companies. Each reason includes "how to assess before applying" and "how to prepare after joining," and the article concludes with the traits of people who are and aren't suited to the role, plus a list of questions to ask in interviews. Negative personal anecdotes are clearly labeled as "individual posts" when quoted.

Key Points of This Article

  • Among the burdens that make FDE "tough," the ratio of travel and on-site work is explicitly stated in job postings: Palantir's Tokyo FDSE posting states "25–75% travel," and OpenAI's Tokyo FDE posting states "travel expected (mainly domestic)" (as of October 2026).
  • The core of the "avoid FDE" argument is that at some companies, the work shifts from software engineering toward sales support; in 2026, The Pragmatic Engineer reported that hiring companies estimate engineers seeking FDE roles make up only about 10% of the market.
  • Most of FDE's drawbacks vary greatly depending on a company's design (whether it has its own product, mechanisms for feeding field insights back into the product, and evaluation metrics), so you need to assess each company individually rather than judging by the job title alone.
  • According to First Round Review, the traits of people suited to FDE are five: thinking independently and delivering results, enduring difficult situations, a high technical standard, continuously building, and curiosity about how business works. People who don't fit these traits will find the burden heavier even in the same role.

Organizing the 5 Reasons FDE Is Said to Be "Something to Avoid"

Negative assessments of FDE can be broadly divided into five. The first two (travel and on-site work, ambiguity and accountability for results) are burdens inherent to the FDE role itself and exist to some degree at any company. The latter three (results rarely becoming your own asset, concerns about market value, and FDE in name only) are burdens that vary greatly depending on the company's design and can be avoided through careful assessment before applying.

Reason 1: Burden of Travel and On-Site Client Work (Ratios Stated in Job Postings)

Since FDEs work deeply on-site with customers, travel and work at client sites account for a certain proportion of the job. Many companies state this ratio explicitly in their job postings. Palantir's "Forward Deployed Software Engineer - Japan Government" (Tokyo, as of October 2026) lists among its requirements: "Ability to travel 25–75% required. Varies by location and team." The work style is hybrid, and the description of the company culture also mentions "often working on-site with customers."

OpenAI's "Forward Deployed Engineer - Tokyo" (as of October 2026) is hybrid with three days per week in the office, and states: "Travel is expected in this role, mainly within Japan." The company's Tokyo FDE Manager posting is more specific: "travel up to 40%, although mainly local." An article in The Pragmatic Engineer from 2025 that examined job postings from various companies also reported that Palantir assumes about 25% of working hours are spent at customer sites, and healthcare AI company Commure estimates up to 50%.

How to assess it and prepare for it

  • Read the travel percentage in the job posting. When there is a range like "25-75%", ask in the interview for the actual figure of the team you would be assigned to (Palantir's own job posting says it "varies by location and team")
  • Check the scope of travel. If it says "mainly domestic" or "mainly short-distance", overnight travel will be limited. For government and manufacturing clients, also ask whether you might work in environments isolated from external networks
  • People who assume full remote are a poor fit for FDE. Going to customer sites is itself the source of this role's value, so it is not something you can negotiate down
  • If family circumstances impose a cap on travel, state that cap before accepting an offer. The wider the range in the travel percentage, the more room there is for adjustment depending on the team

Reason 2: Ambiguity of requirements and accountability (the job posting's phrase "responsibilities similar to a CTO")

The second burden of FDE work is starting from a state where requirements are undefined and being accountable for outcomes. Palantir's Tokyo FDSE posting says: "As an FDSE your responsibilities look similar to those of a startup CTO: you'll work in small teams with minimal supervision and own end-to-end execution of high stakes projects." Among the qualities sought, it also lists "being comfortable with rapidly changing environments, dynamically shifting goals, and iterating with users."

Colin Jarvis, who leads the FDE organization at OpenAI, said in an interview with The Pragmatic Engineer: "FDEs work in a ton of ambiguity, and often what the customer describes in scoping doesn't match the data/system reality on the ground." OpenAI's Tokyo FDE posting also requires "making trade-off decisions on scope, speed, and quality and adjusting plans to meet deadlines" and "demonstrating composure and judgment in high-stakes situations," and explicitly states that success is measured by "production adoption, measurable business impact, and feedback-based evaluation."

Ryuta Sakamoto, who leads FDEs at Loglass, explained in an interview with Levtech LAB that the unique difficulty of the FDE role is "simultaneously carrying two goals that are normally in trade-off: individual optimization for the customer and scalability of your own product." You must neither merely satisfy the customer's requests nor merely protect the product; you need to find a solution that makes both work yourself. This is the biggest reason why people accustomed to "building to spec" find FDE work tough.

How to assess it and prepare for it

  • Check whether the job posting defines "what success looks like." Companies like OpenAI that state success is measured by production adoption and business metrics have clear accountability, and correspondingly clear evaluation criteria. For companies that say nothing, be sure to ask in the interview "what are the evaluation metrics for FDEs?"
  • Reverse the question in the interview and ask how ambiguity is handled. The answer to a question like "when a customer's description differed from the reality on the ground, how did past FDEs handle it?" reveals whether the company has mechanisms to support FDEs (validation phases, escalation paths, collaboration with product teams)
  • As preparation, make sure you can write about experience defining requirements yourself in your resume. Experience at an SIer or in contracted work of "moving a project forward when the spec was undefined" is direct evidence of FDE aptitude
  • "Minimal supervision" is the flip side of having few people to turn to when you are stuck. If you are vulnerable to isolation, check the size of the FDE team and whether multiple people are assigned to the same customer

Reason 3: Frustration that code and outcomes remain on the customer's side and rarely become your own assets

The deliverables of an FDE are systems that run in the customer's environment and business outcomes for the customer. Unlike a product development engineer, it is hard to achieve the form of "a feature I built being used by many users," and customer-specific implementations remain with the customer. A 2026 article by The Pragmatic Engineer titled "Is the FDE role losing its appeal?" points out that some FDE positions have become a state of "merely integrating software built by product teams, with limited contribution to the platform and little beyond genuine software engineering."

However, this is not an inherent fate of the FDE role but a problem of how the company is designed. Palantir describes software engineers (Dev) as "one capability, many customers" and FDEs (Delta) as "one customer, many capabilities," premised on a structure where problems FDEs solve at customer sites flow back into product features. According to The Pragmatic Engineer, at Palantir, after Foundry's release in 2016, many FDEs returned to Foundry development and worked as software engineers. At companies where customer-site knowledge flows back into the product, FDE outcomes become company assets, and the individual's career record retains "which features they originally built."

OpenAI's Tokyo FDE job posting also includes in its duties "turning field patterns into tools, playbooks, and components so others can use them" and "communicating to Research and Product where models succeed and where they can be improved." Whether this feedback loop mechanism is written into the job posting is the first clue for spotting the burden described in Reason 3.

How to assess it and prepare for it

  • Check whether the job posting includes duties to the effect of "feeding field insights back into the product." If not, ask in the interview, "What product features emerged from FDE proposals in the past year?" Companies that cannot give concrete examples likely have a structure where FDE results only remain on the customer side.
  • Check the distance from the product team. Whether FDEs can commit code to product repositories or attend roadmap meetings reveals the reality of the feedback loop.
  • As preparation, make a habit of extracting "generalizable parts" yourself even from customer-specific implementations. If you leave them as internal tools or playbooks, they become your own assets even when customers change.
  • On your resume, write customer outcomes (improvement of business metrics, scale of production adoption) rather than deliverables. An FDE's market reputation is determined by what they changed, not what they built.

Reason 4: Concern of becoming sales-support-oriented and losing market value (The Pragmatic Engineer's argument)

The heaviest criticism in the "don't become an FDE" argument is a piece published by The Pragmatic Engineer in 2026. Citing Wall Street Journal reporting, the article notes that Indeed job postings grew more than tenfold year-over-year in 2025, and that listed companies mentioning this role in earnings calls rose from 8 to 50 according to AlphaSense data, while an executive at recruiting firm Betts Recruiting stated that "although everyone wants them, only about 10% of engineers in the market want this role."

The author, Gergely Orosz, analyzes that the FDE image he depicted in his 2025 coverage (someone handling both software engineering and platform engineering) was realized at OpenAI and Ramp, but many of the FDE postings that appeared afterward have the reality of "significantly less software engineering and even less platform engineering." He points out that many FDE roles function closer to sales engineers supporting deal closures, solution engineers, or consultants. The article concludes: "If there's a role in high demand at considerable salaries but with low engineer interest, there is always a reason."

The article quotes a post from an engineer who took an FDE role: "This FDE job had the typical mindset of an IT services company. The company wanted to use me on the sales lead side, and software development was nearly zero. This was not what I had agreed to, and I couldn't come to like the workplace atmosphere or culture either. I quit after four weeks." While this is an individual post and does not represent facts about any specific company or the industry as a whole, it shows that the concern of "being labeled FDE but being something different" is actually occurring overseas as well.

On the other hand, a 2025 article by The Pragmatic Engineer comments on Salesforce's senior FDE posting, which lists ten responsibilities, as "similar to what is implicitly expected of experienced software engineers at senior level and above." The true FDE is not a role that lowers the engineering bar, but rather one that demands more on top of it. In a First Round Review article, Tiffany Siu, who handled recruiting at Palantir, states that FDEs were held to the same interview standards as software engineers, with no compromise on technical ability. Market value drops not "because you became an FDE" but "because you took an FDE role where you don't write code."

How to assess it and prepare for it

  • In the interview, ask specifically, "What percentage of an FDE's week is spent writing code?" If the answer is vague, or "mainly building relationships with customers," treat it as a sales engineer role regardless of the title in the job posting.
  • Ask whether the evaluation metrics are "deal size and number of sales conversations" or "production adoption and business outcomes." The former means a sales-oriented design; the latter means an FDE design.
  • Look at whether there is a technical interview and its level. FDE postings without coding or system design interviews may not be seeking engineering standards.
  • As preparation, keep updating your technical portfolio even after joining. Experience designing and evaluating (Evals) production systems incorporating LLMs remains valuable on the product development side even if you leave FDE work.

Supplement: The FDE model is also a heavy burden for companies

To understand the FDE burden, knowing the company-side situation improves your ability to assess. In its "Services-Led Growth" piece, a16z argues that implementation services including FDEs are a competitive advantage for AI companies to penetrate complex enterprise operations, while acknowledging the critical view that "reliance on professional services limits scalability, and low gross margins reflect the product being a commodity." a16z counters this with examples such as ServiceNow improving its gross margin from 63.2% at IPO to 79% in 2024, but the fact that companies with many FDEs face pressure on gross margin and scalability is a shared premise within the industry.

This pressure feeds directly back into how FDEs work. If a company sees FDEs as "staff for generating revenue," it moves toward Reason 4; if it sees them as "staff for growing the product," it moves toward resolving Reason 3. According to a16z, of the 311 job postings OpenAI had published at the time, 22 were implementation and deployment roles. Companies investing in both product and deployment are likely to treat FDEs as product-side staff.

Reason 5: In Japan, "FDE in name only" may mean SES

When people in Japan say "don't become an FDE," it is often the concern that "isn't it the same as on-site SES work?" The concern itself is legitimate: when a company without its own product calls itself "FDE," the structure is no different from SES. Shirai Suguru, who leads the FDE group at LayerX, stated in a Levtech LAB interview that the decisive difference from SES or technical consultants is "having a product," explaining that FDE is closer to a business model than a role.

Sansan's engineering blog describes the company's FDE role as "connecting the three layers of business, data, and the field, creating a state where AI continues to deliver value," and lists three qualities it looks for: technical skill, business understanding, and drive. In other words, the definition is adapting the company's own product to customers' operations and accompanying them until value is realized. Judged against this definition, if a job posting has the following characteristics, the role may be called FDE but end up resembling SES work.

Who is and isn't suited to FDE, even so

The burdens discussed so far do not weigh equally on everyone. Based on interviews with people who built FDE organizations at Palantir, Ironclad, Zeta Global, and others, First Round Review summarizes five qualities of people suited to FDE.

  • 01 Don't bring in someone else's playbook; think for yourself and deliver results. Shilpa Balaji of Zeta Global describes an FDE as "a results-oriented, independent thinker who believes they can solve any problem in front of them," and says she hired people closer to new graduates rather than those long accustomed to fixed ways of working at big tech companies
  • 02 Endure difficult stretches (grit). Balaji cites "a willingness to embrace pain," saying that the ability to believe the seemingly impossible can be achieved is the defining trait of an FDE. Jake Stauch describes the FDE as a position like a "mini founder"
  • 03 Meet a high technical bar. James Honsa of Ironclad says the early lawyer-engineers did code reviews with the CTO every week, and Tiffany Siu, who handled hiring at Palantir, says she applied the same interview standards as for software engineers
  • 04 Keep building. Balaji describes the best FDEs as "compulsive builders who can't help making something"
  • 05 Have deep curiosity about how businesses work. Honsa points to being genuinely interested in customers' operations themselves, such as legal and sales

In the same article, Honsa also says that FDE is "not a panacea," reflecting that FDE work involves a lot of "necessary but not strategic work," and that scope creep was a persistent challenge. Siu notes that founders tend to expect FDEs to be all-powerful figures who "build the product, deploy it, train people, customize it, and satisfy customers," but that this becomes unrealistic at scale. These show how the burden of Reason 2 (ambiguity and accountability) is amplified by insufficient design on the company's side.

A technically correct solution is not necessarily one a customer organization can readily accept. Whether you find this adjustment "tedious" or "interesting" completely changes how heavy the same job feels.

Summary: FDE isn't "avoid it" — it's "vet it first"

Among the reasons FDEs are called "tough" or "you should quit," the burden of travel and on-site work at customers, and the burden of being accountable for results under ambiguous requirements, are inherent to the role. These are clearly stated in job postings: Palantir mentions "25–75% travel" and "responsibility similar to a startup CTO," while OpenAI mentions "travel required (mainly domestic)" and "success measured by production adoption and business impact." On the other hand, the three concerns — results not becoming your own asset, drifting toward sales support and losing market value, and being no different from SES — can be avoided depending on how the company is designed.

As The Pragmatic Engineer wrote, "If engineers' interest is low, there is always a reason," and it is true that shallow job postings are mixed in among the surge of FDE openings. That's why you should not judge by the job title alone; before applying, confirm whether the company has its own product, whether there is a mechanism for field insights to flow back into the product, the evaluation criteria, and the proportion of time spent writing code. If you match the five qualities listed by First Round Review and find a well-designed FDE role, this is one of the few jobs where you can directly touch customer outcomes while maintaining your standard as an engineer.

FAQ

Frequently Asked Questions

Q: Is being an FDE really that tough?

Travel and on-site work at customers, along with being accountable for results under ambiguous requirements, involve a certain level of burden at any company. Palantir's Tokyo FDSE job posting explicitly states 25–75% travel and responsibility similar to a startup CTO, while OpenAI's Tokyo FDE posting states mainly domestic travel and success measured by production adoption (as of October 2026). Whether you perceive this as "tough" or as "rewarding" depends on whether you enjoy pushing things forward yourself when requirements are not yet defined.

Q: Why do people say "avoid becoming an FDE"?

The heaviest criticism is that job postings are increasing where, despite the FDE title, the substance leans toward sales support or consulting with a low proportion of software engineering. In 2026, The Pragmatic Engineer reported that hiring companies estimate only about 10% of the market consists of engineers who want FDE roles, noting that roles with high demand but low interest have a reason. However, this is a matter of company design and can be avoided by confirming time spent writing code and evaluation criteria in interviews.

Q: How much travel is involved as an FDE?

It varies greatly by company and the customers you handle. According to job postings as of October 2026, Palantir's Tokyo FDSE states "25–75%, depending on office location and team," OpenAI's Tokyo FDE states "travel required, mainly within Japan," and the same company's FDE Manager states "up to 40%, mostly short-distance." According to The Pragmatic Engineer's research, Palantir assumes about 25% of working hours and Commure up to 50% are spent at customer sites. When there is a range, ask in interviews about the actual figures for the team you would be assigned to.

Q: Does becoming an FDE lower your market value?

Not if it's an FDE role where you keep writing production-quality code. The Pragmatic Engineer notes that the responsibilities required in Salesforce's Senior FDE posting are equivalent to what is expected of a senior-or-above software engineer, and Palantir applies the same interview standards to FDEs as to software engineers. Market value drops only if you take an FDE role centered on sales support where you barely write code.

Q: Isn't an FDE ultimately the same as SES?

It differs depending on whether the company has its own product. Mr. Hidei Shirai of LayerX states that the decisive difference from SES or technical consultants is "having a product." A posting with no in-house product, quasi-delegation contracts with on-site work, and compensation based on per-person-month pricing is structurally SES even if labeled FDE. Check the product name and contract type in the job posting.

Q: What are the characteristics of people not suited to being an FDE?

People who want to act only after specifications and instructions are complete, who want to avoid customer interactions and focus purely on technology, who want to assume full remote or a fixed work location, and who want to delegate implementation to others and act as coordinators tend to feel the FDE burden heavily. The qualities of well-suited people listed by First Round Review are five: thinking for yourself and delivering results, enduring difficult situations, high technical standards, continuously building, and curiosity about how business works.

Q: What should I check before applying for an FDE role?

Six points: What is the company's own product deployed to customers? Is the contract a quasi-delegation agreement or a product usage agreement? What are the FDE evaluation criteria? What proportion of a week is spent writing code? What product features were born from FDE proposals in the past year? What is the actual travel ratio of the team you would join? Companies that can answer these concretely and immediately operate FDE as a deliberately designed role.
