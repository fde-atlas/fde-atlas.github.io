Summary

This Daily Economic News feature interviews Chinese FDE practitioners about a role that originated with Palantir's on-site delivery model and surged after Anthropic and OpenAI backed enterprise AI deployment. Hiring platform Maimai reports FDE job postings grew over 1,500% in the first seven months of the year, with average advertised salaries of 408,000 yuan and top firms offering around one million. Practitioners describe doing miscellaneous technical work, embedding in stores to find use cases, facing vague requirements and organizational resistance, and small teams struggling to collect final payments amid chaotic pricing and dubious training courses. Interviewees predict companies will eventually build in-house FDE capability, with external firms possibly shifting to equity-based models.

NBD reporter Zheng Xinwei, NBD editor Wei Guanhong

This summer, Wei Quyun attended an FDE (Forward Deployed Engineer) industry conference. During those three hours, the entire audience kept asking the same core question: What exactly is an FDE? Even when the conference ended, there was still no answer.

Wei Quyun at the FDE industry conference. Image source: interviewee

But that hasn't stopped it from becoming the most sought-after job in the AI (artificial intelligence) circle.

The spark originated more than 20 years ago with Palantir's on-site delivery model. This May, world-leading AI companies Anthropic and OpenAI simultaneously established companies betting on enterprise AI deployment, and the flame of FDE has "spread" all the way from Silicon Valley to China.

A report released by the job-hunting platform Maimai shows that in the first seven months of this year, FDE job postings on the platform grew 1522.73% year over year, topping the list of expanded AI technology positions. According to statistics from Liepin Big Data Research Institute, the average annual salary for FDE-related positions is 408,000 yuan, with 28.25% offering more than 500,000 yuan. Tech giants like Tencent, ByteDance, and Meituan offer annual salaries as high as around one million yuan.

Behind the high salaries and expansion lies the complex reality of frontline implementation. Recently, reporters from the Daily Economic News (hereinafter referred to as NBD reporters) spoke with multiple FDE practitioners, glimpsing the true picture beneath the glamour of the boom: they handle miscellaneous technical tasks at companies, talk with CEOs to uncover requirements, dive into frontline stores shaking milk tea to find implementation scenarios, and some small teams struggle to collect 70-80% of final payments after taking on orders...

All-purpose outsourcing, "makeshift teams", niche domain experts... The complex nature of FDE

Wei Quyun once served an AI startup as an FDE. His work was highly varied: helping the company build marketing automation workflows, iterating the product's AI capabilities, and connecting AI monitoring systems for stores—anything touching hardware or software. "In a not-so-mature company, an FDE is essentially an all-purpose technical outsourcer."

This is not an isolated case. Multiple interviewees candidly admitted that they are essentially "technical outsourcers." Zhou Ziyao, an FDE entrepreneur and founder of Yaohan Digital Technology, told NBD reporters that a large number of FDE teams have emerged in the market this year, and people in the industry often self-deprecatingly call themselves "makeshift teams"—everyone is feeling their way across the river.

With the rise of the FDE role, questions like "What exactly does an FDE do?" and "How does it differ from product managers, pre-sales Solutions Engineers, after-sales customer engineers, and other roles?" have not reached a consensus. In the view of the multiple practitioners interviewed by NBD reporters, FDE work does indeed overlap with many traditional roles, but the greatest common denominator they offer is: someone who can translate AI capabilities into business results for enterprises and integrate AI into real workflows.

Gan Yifan, co-founder of Rolling AI, doesn't even view FDE as a specific job title, but rather as a "capability package." "Just like electrical engineers in the electrical age, whose core was replacing human physical labor with electricity-driven machines, the core of FDE is replacing repetitive intellectual labor with AI," he explained to NBD reporters.

Gan Yifan broke down this "capability package" using Palantir's Echo-Delta pairing system (mainly referring to a pair of collaborative roles in the FDE model): Echo stays close to the business, finding opportunities on the frontline where AI can amplify value and making it run in both new and old workflows; Delta handles technical implementation, using various engineering methods to turn opportunities into operational systems. Additionally, he proposed that Chinese enterprises need a third capability role—the "knowledge governance officer"—because Chinese companies generally lack the organization and consolidation of explicit knowledge, processes, and expert experience.

Rolling AI's team is very flat; Gan Yifan also goes on-site and writes code himself. Everyone on the team is an "all-around super individual" with different emphases on the above capabilities. Several people form groups to serve enterprises, with a middle-office team providing underlying technical support behind them.

"There are two thresholds for companies hiring FDEs," Gan Yifan told NBD reporters. First, being a niche expert in a certain field who understands how the industry operates; second, having experienced transformation projects in the industry and knowing what happens when new productivity enters an enterprise. Compared to technical experts, business-savvy experts are more critical: "Business experts are the '1,' and technology is the '0's that follow—without the '1,' nothing can be added."

Two weeks on-site, interviews with 12 departments, 80% "useless information"—"gaining trust" is even harder than "uncovering requirements"

Familiarity with industry business is indeed a widely valued quality for FDEs. Wei Lei, an FDE entrepreneur and founder of OpenFDE, said in an interview with NBD reporters that to sustain an FDE career, one must focus on a specific vertical—in the end, it comes down to "understanding the customer." And "reading the customer" is often the first challenge FDEs encounter after entering a company.

Although many business leaders are anxious about AI applications, they don't clearly understand the actual effects and pathways of AI implementation, so requirements are often vague, making consulting and research the first step after entering.

Gan Yifan gave an example: the first thing his team does upon entering a company is to reach consensus with the client from a management consulting perspective on issues like the boundaries of AI capabilities, application expectations, the company's financial situation, and business goals. After aligning understanding, they go to the frontline: for fast-moving consumer goods, they follow along on channel visits; for milk tea chains, they get health certificates and go to stores to shake milk tea. They spend two to three weeks on the business frontline formulating hypotheses about how AI can optimize systems and improve processes, then two to three weeks building prototypes and testing and evaluating them. After confirming feasibility, they iterate quickly, promote during iteration, and expand from a small scope to the entire company within two months.

This process—from consulting research to prototype design, implementation trial and error, and iterative maintenance—is the FDE work path described by most interviewees, but this long chain is not easy from the very beginning.

Wei Quyun's on-site workplace. Image source: interviewee

After the National Day holiday, Wei Quyun and five colleagues entered a subsidiary of a state-owned central enterprise for an FDE project. The team spent two weeks interviewing employees from 12 departments, compiling 800 to 900 issues. "You could feel that employees were more venting emotions than making constructive suggestions," Wei Quyun said. The interviews were mentally exhausting because 80% to 90% was useless information that couldn't help the team analyze the company's pain points or find the next direction.

"The core of FDE on-site work is becoming an expert who understands the customer's business," Wei Lei shared his experience serving a company with 7 billion yuan in annual revenue: "Including in-depth business discussions with the CEO, thorough communication with all levels of the company, uncovering requirements, and gaining trust."

"Gaining trust" can be even harder than "uncovering requirements." Organizational resistance is an obstacle consistently mentioned by interviewees. Gan Yifan's team once did AI-based store scheduling for a company, trying to save labor costs through refined operations. The results were significant, but implementation was hindered by declining employee income: "Without incentives, store managers simply won't push it forward."

Gan Yifan said that AI applications can't just run in an enterprise by simply building technical tools; organizational resistance is inevitable, requiring deep work within the organization on coordination and incentives. "AI has difficulty understanding emotions and context between people. Even changes that are perfectly reasonable on paper may fail to move forward—a certain business department may be underperforming, but since it's been with the boss since day one building the business, it has to stay."

To counter reform resistance, the determination of the "top leader" is crucial. Multiple interviewees said they only take orders where they can "reach agreement with the top leader," because "without very strong determination from the boss, nothing can be pushed forward." Wei Quyun was deeply impressed by the kickoff meeting on his first day at the central enterprise subsidiary: "All mid- and senior-level staff gathered—it was quite grand. The chairman spoke personally, requiring every department to cooperate with us."

Long chains, heavy business: payment collection pressure, pricing disorder, and training chaos

"Real FDE implementation services are extremely delivery-heavy and results-oriented," Zhou Ziyao said. Developing a single pain-point scenario takes one to two months, while deeper transformation requires a year or even longer of accompaniment. "We do all the dirty, tiring work and earn hard-earned money, and clients often don't understand." He had just quoted 200,000 yuan for the first phase to a semiconductor company, and the young boss, who had originally been very determined, wavered a bit after hearing it.

Currently, the market has no unified pricing standard. Different teams serve different clients, in different forms, with different client standards, resulting in varied pricing systems. Zhou Ziyao's team mainly serves mid-sized companies with annual revenue between 50 million and 300 million yuan. On-site pricing varies by city, averaging 1,800 yuan per person-day (a common workload measurement unit in the software and IT services industry).

Rolling AI client case. Image source: screenshot from Rolling AI's official website

RollingAI targets private enterprises with annual revenue of over 500 million yuan. Clients subscribe monthly to a small team of several FDEs for services such as data analysis and causal inference, along with knowledge assets accumulated from previous cases, such as AI employees and industry know-how. "A single enterprise's annual subscription is roughly in the range of several million yuan," said Gan Yifan, adding that the team typically accompanies an enterprise for at least six months.

Gan Yifan has observed that this "heavy business" is facing real difficulties of varying degrees: more and more small teams and even individuals are taking on contracts as FDEs. Winning contracts is not hard, but "70 to 80 percent never collect the final payment." Several interviewees confirmed this problem. "The issue often lies in misaligned expectations—the enterprise cannot articulate its needs, and FDEs lacking capability are unable to judge this or make excessive promises just to sign the deal, leaving both sides unable to back down."

Gan Yifan expressed his concern: many people rush into the profession after seeing only the surface-level hype around FDEs. Without familiarity with an industry, they may actually use AI to amplify their own misconceptions when delivering services, producing the opposite of the intended effect. "Having reverence for an industry is a basic standard of professional service ethics."

The market's chaos does not end there: pricing systems are chaotic. Practitioners without real-world case studies either charge exorbitant prices or undercut competitors with low bids, while the services they actually deliver do not match.

More consistent criticism is directed at "fleecing-the-sheep" style training courses. FDE entrepreneur and OpsGo AI founder Xie Yao categorizes these training courses into three types: those targeting laid-off technical workers in their thirties and forties who want to become FDEs, claiming "enroll and we guarantee you a job"; those selling FDE certificates to fresh graduates, even though the certificates have nothing to do with actual work; and "AI executive classes" for all kinds of business owners, where the instructors themselves have no hands-on implementation experience and the tools they promote are hard to put into practice.

"Like a Confinement Nanny at the Company": Enterprises Must Learn to "Raise the Child" Themselves

"There are indeed some rather hasty moves in the market, and some low-quality FDE services have damaged the industry's reputation. But in an environment where corporate management in China is not standardized, FDE will still be a fragmented market with enormous room," Wei Lei told NBD reporters.

"AI transformation is like teaching people to use computers and Office 20 years ago," Xie Yao said. "Companies founded ten years from now will be born natively into a business environment adapted to AI and won't need anyone to teach them how to use AI at all."

Gan Yifan also believes that FDE services that purely sell "person-days" or deliver by project cannot become a sustainable business model in China. In the future, every enterprise will have native FDE teams that grow out of its own business. "They may not even be physical organizations—it could just be that certain people possess an FDE capability package and become the key players driving AI transformation within small business units. This will happen on a large scale in the next six months." In his vision, external FDE companies in the future may no longer live on service fees, but instead ride the capital-driven logic of asset transformation, working with venture capital firms to transform companies and industries with AI capabilities and earning a share of equity appreciation.

What kind of enterprise can truly succeed at AI transformation? Gan Yifan's answer: openness, willingness to learn, and initiative.

"An FDE's role in a company is very much like a confinement nanny," he said by way of analogy. The company has given birth to a child called AI, which is extremely "disruptive" and upends many household routines. A good "nanny" must have professional skills and also be able to read the room—knowing who makes decisions on what matters and who can cooperate. Moreover, the "nanny" will not stay forever; the goal is to teach the family to raise the child themselves.

For the "parents," what matters more is proactively learning how to raise the "child," not dumping everything on the "nanny." Gan Yifan has met quite a few owners of companies worth tens of billions of yuan. "We also 'force' them to learn cutting-edge tools, so they can form an accurate judgment of AI's effectiveness," Gan said. "But business owners who can truly make AI implementation succeed are always those with a sense of ownership."

A "confinement nanny" who grows up alongside the "parents"—this may be the truest portrait of the FDE business at its current stage: what it sells is not a technical tool, but a stretch of time spent accompanying an enterprise through the transition of AI productivity.
