What is Palantir? What kind of company it is, its four products, the relationship with FDEs, and its expansion into Japan

Original title: Palantirとは？何の会社か・製品4種・FDEとの関係と日本展開

FDE総研 · 2026-09-02 · 20 min read

Summary

This article profiles Palantir Technologies: founded in 2003 by Peter Thiel, Alex Karp and others, it listed via direct listing in 2020 and reported roughly $4.48 billion revenue for fiscal 2025, up 56% year over year. Its four products—Gotham (government/defense), Foundry (commercial, built on an ontology), AIP (connecting LLMs to business objects, launched April 2023), and Apollo (continuous delivery)—share common data and business-model layers. The piece explains that the FDE role emerged because Palantir's customers' operations were too complex for requirements documents, so engineers work on-site from problem definition through implementation. In Japan, expansion relies on the SOMPO joint venture (2019) and a Fujitsu partnership (2020). The article compares FDEs with SIer, consulting, SWE and PdM roles, and outlines career entry points for Japanese engineers.

Key points

  • Palantir was founded in 2003, listed directly in 2020, and earned about $4.48B in fiscal 2025 (+56%).
  • Its four products share common data and ontology layers rather than being separate tools.
  • The FDE role exists because requirements documents failed for complex customer operations.
  • Japan expansion runs through the SOMPO joint venture and a Fujitsu partnership.
  • FDEs differ from SIer, consultants and PdMs by owning outcomes through actual usage.

What kind of company is Palantir, and what does it sell?

Palantir is a US company founded in 2003. It provides a platform that integrates fragmented data to support decision-making, along with the talent to implement it.

The core of Palantir Technologies' business is putting data scattered across multiple systems at government agencies and large enterprises onto a single platform, and connecting it to daily decisions and business operations. The company name comes from the "seeing stone (palantír)" that appears in J.R.R. Tolkien's works.

As explained later, Palantir's product Palantir AIP is a product for connecting large language models to existing business environments. In other words, Palantir is not a company that builds generative AI models; it is a company on the side that connects AI to data and business operations. Japanese engineers most often learn Palantir's name through stock market buzz or as the company that created the job role of "FDE." However, Palantir's business is closer to rebuilding customers' business operations themselves than to selling analytics tools. Keeping this premise in mind will make the later discussion of products and FDEs easier to understand.

When and by whom was Palantir founded?

Palantir was founded by Peter Thiel and others in California, USA, in 2003, and went public via a direct listing in 2020.

The founding members are Peter Thiel, Alex Karp (current CEO), Joe Lonsdale, Stephen Cohen, and Nathan Gettings. In its early days, the company received investment from In-Q-Tel, the CIA's venture capital arm. Starting its business in the public and national security sectors has strongly shaped Palantir's character as a company.

The company was headquartered in Palo Alto, California for a long time, moved to Denver, Colorado in 2020, and in February 2026 announced a relocation of its headquarters to Miami, Florida. On September 30 of the same year, it went public via a direct listing rather than a traditional IPO. Its ticker symbol is "PLTR," and in November 2024 it moved its listing from the New York Stock Exchange to Nasdaq (Source: Palantir Investor Relations https://investors.palantir.com/).

What are Palantir's revenue structure and scale?

Palantir's business rests on two pillars, government and commercial, with revenue of approximately $4.48 billion for the fiscal year ending December 2025 (up 56% year over year). For the fiscal year ending December 2024, revenue was approximately $2.87 billion. In recent years, the commercial segment has been driving growth.

Palantir discloses its results in two segments: Government and Commercial. In September 2024, it was also announced that the company would be added to the S&P 500. Customer counts and employee numbers are disclosed in investor information, but since they change over time, this article does not include those figures. Palantir has disclosed that it continues to post high growth rates in subsequent quarters as well. Always verify the latest figures in the primary investor information sources.

How does Palantir differ from a "software vendor"?

Palantir provides not only its products but also implementation talent embedded in customers' operations as a set. This is the biggest difference from typical SaaS.

With typical SaaS, post-deployment utilization is handled by the customer itself or by partner SIers. Palantir has sent its own engineers into customers' workplaces, taking on everything from defining business challenges to implementation and operational adoption in-house. The job role of FDE (Forward Deployed Engineer) emerged from this structure.

For this reason, comparing Palantir with other products on license fees alone tends to miss the essence. This is strongly felt in actual adoption evaluations as well. How to interpret its pricing is covered in the related article "What is Palantir's pricing structure?".

What are the differences between Palantir's main products Gotham, Foundry, and AIP?

Gotham handles government and defense, Foundry handles commercial operations, and AIP handles AI utilization. Apollo is the foundation that supports the distribution and operation of all of these products.

Palantir's products are organized on its official website into four categories: Gotham, Foundry, Apollo, and AIP (Source: Palantir official website "Platforms" https://www.palantir.com/platforms/). At first glance, they appear to be separate tools, but in reality they share a data integration layer and a business model layer. It is closer to the truth to see them as a structure where the entry point is divided according to target customers and use cases. What is Palantir Gotham for?

Palantir Gotham is a product that integrates scattered information to support situational awareness and decision-making in the government and defense sectors.

Palantir Gotham handles data spanning multiple information sources under a common model. It has been used to enable analysts and commanders to grasp the situation on the same screen. It is designed to link and track entities such as people, organizations, places, and events, with an emphasis on access control and audit logs in highly sensitive environments.

Gotham is a product that has continued since Palantir's early days. The prototype of Palantir's culture of "building on-site" is also thought to have been shaped through the development of Gotham. The differences from Foundry are compared in detail in "Differences between Palantir Gotham and Foundry."

What is new about Palantir Foundry and its ontology?

Palantir Foundry is an integrated platform for private companies. At its core is an ontology that defines business concepts and their relationships.

Palantir Foundry connects core systems, equipment logs, spreadsheet files, external data, and more. On top of that, it defines business objects such as "equipment," "customers," and "projects," along with their relationships. This business model is the ontology (Source: Palantir official website "Foundry" https://www.palantir.com/platforms/foundry/).

From a technical perspective, the ontology is a concept close to data catalogs and semantic layers. Foundry's distinguishing feature is that, on top of the same model, it can consistently handle not only analysis but also applications, access control, and write-back to business systems. A representative commercial example is "Skywise," an aviation industry platform announced by Airbus in 2017 (Source: Skywise official website https://www.skywise.com/).

What roles do Palantir AIP and Apollo play?

Palantir AIP is an LLM connection layer announced in April 2023. Palantir Apollo is the foundation that distributes and updates all products across diverse environments.

Palantir AIP (Artificial Intelligence Platform) is a product that connects large language models to existing Foundry and Gotham environments. It enables AI to be used in business operations while maintaining access controls and auditing. The biggest difference from generic chatbots is that the objects the model references and operates on are business objects defined in the ontology. Since 2023, there have also been more opportunities to engage with customers through "AIP Bootcamp," a format for launching actual use cases in a short period (Related: What is Palantir AIP Bootcamp?).

Palantir Apollo is the foundation for continuously distributing and updating these platforms to various environments, such as cloud, on-premises, and classified environments. Although it is rarely visible to customers, it underpins the premises of Palantir adoption (Related: What is Palantir Apollo?).

How do Palantir and FDE (Forward Deployed Engineer) connect?

The FDE is an engineering role that Palantir institutionalized as its implementation approach. At customer sites, FDEs are responsible for everything from problem definition to implementation and adoption.

The term Forward Deployed Engineer became widely known through Palantir's recruiting pages and engineering blog (Source: Palantir official website "Careers" https://www.palantir.com/careers/). "Forward Deployed" derives from the military term for forward deployment. It expresses an attitude of going out to the customer's site and getting involved from the point of defining the problem, rather than waiting for specifications to arrive at headquarters.

Why did Palantir need the FDE role?

Because customers' operations were too complex, and handoffs via requirements documents did not produce results in Palantir implementations.

What Palantir has handled—such as intelligence agency analysis work and aircraft maintenance planning—are domains that outsiders cannot understand from documents alone. The people on-site are the ones who can articulate what the problem is, but they do not speak the language of data models or implementation. The FDE, who connects business understanding, data modeling, and implementation in one person, became necessary to bridge this gap. As an explanation of why the FDE was born, this is the most practical and convincing one.

It is also known that, as an organization, Palantir has operated by separating engineers who build products from engineers who deploy them to customers. FDEs fall into the latter category. What is important is that the system is designed to include a feedback loop that returns insights gained in the field to the product side.

What does an FDE actually do?

An FDE handles everything in one continuous stream: defining problems through on-site observation, ontology design, implementing business applications, and supporting adoption.

What engineers accustomed to Japanese contracted development find confusing at first is that no scope is given to them. An FDE's work often starts not with designing the platform, but with identifying "who decides what, and when." In a factory, that means understanding what the production planning staff look at every day; in logistics, the dispatch coordinator. From there, they work backward to determine and connect the data that is needed.

The right-hand column of the table looks appealing. But the flip side is that if what you build isn't used, your results are evaluated as zero. Much of the reason FDEs are said to have a heavy workload lies not in technical difficulty but in the harshness of this evaluation.

Is the term FDE spreading beyond Palantir?

It is. In recent years, the job title FDE has also been used in job postings by AI and SaaS companies both in Japan and abroad.

FDE, originally an internal Palantir job title, has spread to companies that need to integrate complex products into customers' business operations. In Japan as well, the title Forward Deployed Engineer is now appearing in job postings from companies offering data platforms and AI products.

However, even when the title is the same, the actual role ranges from "implementation support engineer" to "product developer embedded at the customer." When considering a career move, it is more practical to check the following two points rather than the job title.

  • Breadth of authority: Can you decide the scope yourself?
  • Evaluation criteria: Are you evaluated on delivery, or on whether your work is actually used?

The overall picture of the FDE role is summarized in "What is an FDE?".

How does a Palantir-style FDE differ from SWEs, IT consultants, PdMs, and SIers, and who is it not suited for?

Compared using the three axes of the FDE Research Institute—"starting point, deliverables, and measure of success"—the Palantir-style FDE differs from other roles in that it takes responsibility for the outcome of being used.

For roles similar to FDE, it is clearer to look at "where the work starts and what counts as success" than at job titles or technologies used. The FDE Research Institute fixes the axes of comparison to three: "starting point," "deliverables," and "measure of success." This allows comparison without being misled by job titles in postings.

How do roles similar to FDE differ across the three axes?

Whereas traditional roles split between those who write requirements and those who build, the Palantir-style FDE takes on both roles alone.

In Japan's IT talent market, those who write requirements (consultants and business departments) and those who build (SIers and development companies) have been clearly separated. Who is not suited to being a Palantir-style FDE?

People who want to focus on technology with a fixed scope, or who want to close out work at delivery, are likely to feel significant stress as Palantir-style FDEs.

From everything above, what separates fit from misfit is not technical skill but assumptions about how you work. If any of the following strongly applies to you, another role may suit you better than FDE.

  • People who prefer being given a scope: FDE is a role that often starts from a state where no requirements have been written.
  • People who want closure at acceptance and delivery: An FDE is not evaluated if what they build isn't used.
  • People who want to avoid talking directly with frontline staff or executives: FDEs interact directly, without going through a liaison.
  • People who want to avoid verbal discussion in English (if aiming for Palantir itself): Palantir is a global company, so the ability to work in English is virtually a given.

How is Palantir expanding in Japan, and what does it bring to Japanese engineers?

In Japan, Palantir has expanded primarily through its joint venture with SOMPO and its partnership with Fujitsu. As a result, FDE-style job openings and talent demand have emerged.

Palantir's expansion in Japan has not taken only the form of direct large contracts with customer companies as in the US. It has also built a foothold through joint ventures and partnerships with major Japanese companies. For Japanese companies to adopt an overseas platform, procedures are required such as review by information systems departments, meeting security requirements, and sorting out relationships with existing SIers. It is natural to see the aim as having domestic partners handle these procedures.

What structure does Palantir's Japanese entity use to run its business?

The domestic point of contact is a joint venture established with SOMPO Holdings in November 2019. In 2020, it also formed a partnership with Fujitsu.

In November 2019, SOMPO Holdings established the joint venture Palantir Technologies Japan with Palantir and announced its policy of promoting data utilization, including in the nursing care and insurance fields (Source: SOMPO Holdings https://www.sompo-hd.com/). SOMPO Holdings subsequently also announced an investment in Palantir.

In June 2020, Fujitsu announced a strategic partnership with Palantir and has been advancing the domestic provision of Foundry and its application to its own operations. In September 2026, it announced a deepening of the partnership, strengthening a structure that covers everything from providing Palantir AIP and Foundry to implementing them in business operations (Source: Fujitsu https://pr.fujitsu.com/jp/news/). The sequence in which a vendor first masters the product internally and then extends it to customers tends to carry persuasive power in the Japanese market.

What changes for engineers in Japan?

As Palantir products spread in Japan, demand and market value are rising for engineers who can also conduct business requirements interviews.

Not only Palantir itself, but also domestic partner companies and adopting companies are increasingly seeking people who can do both business understanding and data modeling. As seen in the previous H2 section, in Japan the side that writes requirements and the side that builds have been clearly separated.

FDE-type roles presuppose crossing this boundary. The rising market value of "engineers who can also conduct business interviews" can be seen as a structural change.

What are the constraints unique to the Japanese market when adopting Palantir?

The three barriers most likely to arise in the Japanese market are dispersed decision-making, sorting out roles with existing SIers, and building consensus with data-owning departments.

Dispersed decision-makingApprovals are split among business divisions, IT departments, and management, so a single division can neither stop nor advance the projectMap out the approval routes of the relevant departments early on

The third constraint, building consensus with data-owning departments, cannot be solved by technology. It must be handled as a discussion of permission design and governance. All of these are issues that arise regardless of whether you choose Palantir. Companies considering adoption will find that checking these three points in advance keeps the product comparison discussion from drifting.

How should you choose a career involving Palantir?

Choose from three entry points—Palantir itself, domestic partner companies, or in-house departments at operating companies—whichever fits your current position.

Consultations of the form "I want to work with Palantir" actually split into three different careers. Because the experience required and the rewards differ, sorting them out at the start reduces confusion.

What is evaluated when aiming for Palantir itself?

In Palantir's own selection process, what is evaluated is not product knowledge but experience in independently defining problems in ambiguous situations.

Palantir's hiring requirements list foundational technologies such as SQL, Python, data modeling, and API integration. However, what is probed deeply in interviews is your experience with "deciding what to build in a situation where no one writes the requirements for you."

Also, in practice, the dividing line tends to be whether you can not only read and write but also discuss verbally in English with customer site staff. The realities of the selection process are covered in detail in "How Hard Is It to Get a Job at Palantir?".

How can you get involved with Palantir from a partner company or operating company?

Even if you cannot touch Palantir products directly, the shortcut is to accumulate experience at your current workplace of "listening, building small, and getting people to use it."

Even if you don't have immediate access to Palantir products, you can replicate the FDE-style approach. Narrow the target business process down to one. Go into the field and define the problems. Build something small and have people use it. Design the write-back into business systems as well. This sequence doesn't depend on any particular product.

In fact, even when job postings list product names as requirements, what often gives you a strong edge in interviews is being able to talk about this kind of experience.

What should you start building from today?

Rather than broadening your technical range, it's more realistic to prioritize creating one deliverable you can say was actually "used."

  • Choose one target business process — Processes that occur daily, have clearly defined decision-makers, and rely on spreadsheets and personal experience are good candidates.
  • Interview field staff directly — Ask not about the requests they receive, but about their actual work procedures and exception handling.
  • Deliver something tangible in two weeks — Prioritize a state where the field can give feedback, rather than completeness.
  • Measure usage — Record who used it and how many times, and eliminate the reasons it isn't being used one by one.
  • State explicitly what you won't do — The decision to narrow scope is the most highly valued part of FDE-style work.

Whether or not you join Palantir, this accumulation of experience is transferable. Products will eventually change, but the ability to "translate the language of business operations into data models and drive adoption through to actual use" will remain. The essence of why the FDE role is attracting attention is not any particular vendor's technology. I believe it lies in the fact that the market has begun to put a price on this ability.
