How many remote FDE positions are there? How can I check the travel requirements?

Original title: FDE 远程岗位有多少？出差要求怎么看

FDE Times · 2026-10-11 · 5 min read

Summary

This article examines how rare remote FDE roles actually are. Rung found about 19% of 402 job postings (October 2026) support remote work, while FDE Pulse counted only 9% of 1,086 postings. Even remote-labeled roles require customer-site travel: Cohere asks for 20%–40% of time on site, and some Palantir teams up to 50%. The reason is that FDE value comes from embedding with customer teams, plus technical constraints like air-gapped networks. According to Valletta Software, underestimated travel requirements are the most common reason FDEs quit in their first year, so candidates should ask for actual travel numbers before accepting an offer.

Key points

  • Only 9%–19% of FDE postings allow remote work, depending on the tracker.
  • Remote roles still require travel: Cohere 20%–40%, some Palantir teams 50%.
  • FDE value lies in embedding with customer teams; air-gapped networks force on-site work.
  • Understated travel requirements are the top reason first-year FDEs quit.
  • Engineers outside the US can target hubs like London or Singapore, or accept limited remote roles.

Key takeaways

  • According to Rung, about 19% of 402 FDE job ads are marked as remote-friendly; FDE Pulse counted only 98 out of 1,086, or 9%.
  • Even companies that support remote work, like Cohere, require up to 20%–40% travel time to customers, because the job itself is about being embedded in customer teams.

In October 2026, Rung collected 402 Forward Deployed Engineer (FDE) job ads from public applicant tracking system (ATS) pages, and only about 19% were marked as remote-friendly. In the same dataset, 80% of the ads mentioned travel or on-site work.

Both numbers could easily appear in the same ad. If you want a remote FDE role, you first need to understand why.

For people outside the US, or in cities without offices of major AI companies, whether a role can be done remotely determines whether they can apply at all. Such roles do exist, but they are few. And even when a role is remote, you still have to go to customers.

Every tracking organization has its own numbers

There is no widely accepted figure for the remote share of FDE roles. Rung puts it at about 19%, roughly 76 out of 402 ads. Another tracker, FDE Pulse, counted 1,086 open FDE ads in April 2026 and found only 98 that allowed remote work, or 9%, with the rest being on-site or hybrid.

The two figures differ by a factor of two, which shows the share depends on which dataset you look at. Rung's data is a single snapshot, sourced from Greenhouse, Lever, Ashby, and Workday. The two organizations counted six months apart, covered different ads, and likely defined "remote" differently.

Whichever number you believe, the conclusion holds: fewer than one in five ads allows remote work. Job ads are also concentrated in a handful of countries. About 42% of the ads in Rung's dataset are in the US, followed by the UK, Singapore, Japan, France, and Germany.

The data does not say whether remote roles are restricted by country, so if you are elsewhere, read each ad's location requirements carefully before applying.

"Remote" means not going to an office, not avoiding customer sites

Reading ads one by one is more informative than looking at overall percentages. Cohere explicitly supports remote work but has offices in Toronto, London, and New York. Its FDE Agentic Platform role requires up to 20%–40% of time working on-site at customers and partners.

Translated into a work calendar: with five working days a week, 20%–40% means one to two days at the customer. With roughly 20 working days a month, that is 4 to 8 days away from home. Such roles still carry the "remote-friendly" label.

Comparing five job ads side by side reveals a range of arrangements, but none lets you stay home all the time:

Databricks has the lightest travel requirement: customer visits roughly every 4–8 weeks, averaging about 6 to 13 trips a year. The trade-off is that the role is firmly tied to London.

Factory sits at the other end: five days a week in the office, plus frequent customer visits. Palantir notes that the 25%–50% figure varies by team, meaning that on some teams you would spend half your time at the customer.

So when comparing two offers, do not just look at the "remote" or "on-site" label. Calculate how many days per month you must be in an office or at a customer site.

Why customers still need FDEs on site

The answer lies in the definition of the job. According to Paraform, Palantir's "Delta" model places engineers directly inside customer teams, spanning government, financial services, and enterprise operations.

FDE Pulse's explanation for the low remote share follows the same logic: being physically present with customers is often part of the FDE's value itself.

Beyond business reasons, there are technical constraints. Valletta Software notes in its 2026 FDE job description template that if a customer's network is air-gapped, engineers must be on site.

Imagine deploying an agent for a bank whose internal network is fully isolated: you cannot SSH in, you cannot pull logs onto your own laptop, and every debugging session has to happen in front of their screen.

It follows that the more security-sensitive the customer, the less work can be done remotely, no matter how lenient your own company's policy is.

Misreading an ad has a cost

Valletta warns that job ads understating travel requirements are the most common reason FDEs quit within their first year. Imagine someone who thinks they have landed a remote job, only to discover months later that they spend more nights in a hotel near the customer's factory than at home.

Valletta's guide also recommends verifying, when reading a job ad, whether "remote" really means little travel. Ask directly in interviews: how many days last month did the FDEs on the team spend at customers? What industry are the main customers in? Are there air-gapped environments?

A number like "20%–40%" says almost nothing without context, because 20% and 40% are two very different lives.

If you are far from the hiring hubs

For engineers outside the US, there are two realistic paths. The first is to target cities with many openings, such as London or Singapore, and accept office-centric roles with light travel, like Databricks. The second is to seek out the few remote-friendly roles, like Cohere, provided you are genuinely willing to travel one or two days a week.

Whichever path you take, your resume should prove the part the "remote" label leaves unsaid: that you can get things done sitting across from a customer. Write about a time you handled an incident on site, or installed a system on a restricted internal network. Then state clearly in your cover letter how much travel you can accept.

On the days you are not at customers, build your asynchronous collaboration skills: write deployment docs others can follow without help, and report progress without meetings.

Remote FDE jobs do exist, but they are a way of arranging travel, not a substitute for being with customers. Those who understand this before signing the contract are the ones who land a job they can sustain long term.
