What's the difference between an FDE and a Customer Success Engineer?

Original title: FDE 和客户成功工程师有什么区别

FDE Times · 2026-10-11 · 5 min read

Summary

This article distinguishes the Forward Deployed Engineer (FDE) role from the Customer Success Engineer (CSE). An analysis of roughly 1,000 FDE job postings found that 55% mention direct customer work, yet none assign revenue responsibility. The core distinction: FDEs come in early to make the first technical deployment succeed, while CSEs keep customers using the product effectively after launch. A practical test: if a contract is signed but deployment stalls, you need an FDE; if customers churn after going live, that is a retention problem. The two roles are measured differently—FDEs by operational outcomes, CSEs by adoption, NPS, and renewals. The author advises reading job descriptions for verbs and metrics rather than titles, and writing resumes around what you built and its production results.

Key points

  • FDEs make the first deployment succeed; CSEs sustain usage after launch.
  • None of ~1,000 FDE postings assign revenue responsibility, though 55% mention customer work.
  • Stalled deployments call for FDEs; post-launch churn is a retention issue.
  • FDEs are judged on operational outcomes, not NPS or renewals.
  • Seed-stage self-serve products need retention hires; FDEs matter from Series A.

This dividing line separates the FDE from the Customer Success Engineer: one gets the first deployment done, the other keeps the customer using the product over time.

Key takeaways

  • FDEs get involved early and are responsible for getting the first technical deployment done; CSEs get involved later and are responsible for keeping customers using the product effectively, and rarely write large integrations.
  • If the contract is signed but the deployment is stalled, you need an FDE; if customers churn after go-live, that is a retention problem.
  • FDEs are measured on operational outcomes, not NPS, adoption rates, or revenue. Both reading JDs and writing resumes should be benchmarked against this metric.

An analysis of roughly 1,000 job postings for Forward Deployed Engineers (FDEs) found that working directly with customers was the most frequently mentioned responsibility, appearing in 55% of postings. However, not a single posting listed revenue accountability as a core responsibility.

At first glance, these two numbers seem contradictory: the people with the most customer contact are not responsible for keeping the contract. Customer success roles, by contrast, are typically tied to whether customers renew. This difference explains why FDEs and Customer Success Engineers (CSEs) are two different jobs, even though both are close to the customer.

Conflating the two is not just a naming error. It determines what you do every day, and which numbers you are evaluated on.

The Pragmatic Engineer noted in May 2026 that demand for FDEs at Google, OpenAI, and Anthropic is very strong. Strong demand is good news, but precisely because of that, you should read every job posting carefully before applying: the job title alone does not tell you what the day-to-day work looks like.

Both are close to the customer, but they enter at different times

The simplest way to tell the two apart is to look at which stage each one starts working from. FDE Academy writes that FDEs help get the first technical deployment done; CSEs help customers keep using the product effectively and usually do not take on large deployments or custom integrations.

Paraform summarizes it as a handoff chain: the Solutions Engineer convinces the customer of what the product can do, the FDE turns it into a working system, and once the deployment is stable, the Customer Engineer maintains it over time.

Companies use different names for the post-launch stage (FDE Academy uses Customer Success Engineer, Paraform uses Customer Engineer), so this article uses CSE as an umbrella term for this type of role.

Palantir's job description shows why the FDE must be in the building stage. The company wants people who work directly with customers to solve their most pressing problems, delivering custom applications, LLM-based workflows, and production-grade solutions tailored to each customer's specific situation.

This is writing new software, not providing support for a packaged product.

Look at the symptoms, and you know what the role is

Paraform offers a memorable way to judge: if the contract is signed but the deployment is stalled, that is FDE work; if customers churn after the product is up and running, that is a post-sales technical support problem, in other words, retention.

Imagine a startup selling inventory forecasting software that signs 10 contracts in one quarter. Scenario one: 6 customers have not gone live yet because their data sits in legacy ERPs that the product cannot connect to for now.

There is no retention issue to speak of here, because the customers have not received any value yet. The company needs someone to go on-site and write connectors and normalize data: that is, an FDE.

Scenario two: all 10 customers have gone live, but three months later a few of them leave because the bugs they submitted were not addressed promptly. Writing more custom code will not solve this. The company needs someone to monitor customer health, handle incidents, and help customers use the product more deeply.

From the company's stage of development, the conclusion is the same. According to Paraform, seed-stage startups with self-onboarding users should prioritize hiring for retention, because users leave when they hit problems and no one provides technical help.

By Series A, deployments become complex and require close collaboration with each customer, and that is when the FDE starts to make sense. For job seekers, this signal is worth noting: if a seed-stage company selling a self-onboarding product is hiring an "FDE," the job is likely skewed toward retention.

Don't turn the FDE into an account manager

Precisely because FDEs are so close to customers, many customer success teams want to use them as Customer Success Managers (CSMs). Chad Horenfeldt, who writes about customer success, calls this a trap. In his view, getting bogged down in solution details eats up the time a CSM needs to understand what outcomes the customer actually wants.

He also states plainly that he considers the FDE closer to services, or even technical support, than to a strategic, revenue-linked customer success function. FDEs may not like this categorization, but it matches the hiring data: not a single FDE job posting assigns revenue responsibility.

A blog post from software company Jestor looks at the same issue from the other side. Traditional customer success focuses on adoption and retention.

In the FDE-style approach, success is measured by operational outcomes, not NPS or feature usage. Horenfeldt and Jestor evaluate FDEs from different angles, but both point to the same thing: these two roles are measured with different types of metrics.

Metrics determine what kind of professional you become

For career development, the "how success is measured" row in the table above says more than the job title does. You get strong at whatever you are evaluated on. If you are evaluated on NPS and renewal rates for three years, you develop customer management skills.

If you are evaluated for three years on whether the customer's system runs in production, you develop the ability to build software.

So when reading a JD, don't just look at the job title; look at the verbs and the metrics. If the posting talks about build, deploy, integrate, production, and operational outcomes, that is FDE work.

If it talks about adoption, health score, renewal, and expansion, that is customer success, even if the job title says Forward Deployed. Neither role is better than the other, but you should be clear about which one you are choosing.

In interviews, you can borrow Paraform's method of judgment and ask a question: are most of the team's customers stuck before or after go-live? The answer will tell you whether most of your time will go to writing connectors or handling tickets.

Resumes should follow the same metrics. A statement like "supported 30 enterprise customers" fits a customer-success-oriented resume. An FDE-oriented resume needs to say: where the customer was stuck, what you built, and how the operational outcomes changed once the system ran in production.

If you don't have external customers yet, an internal project that you pushed from stuck to running in production demonstrates exactly the same ability.

To figure out what a role really is, don't ask what it's called—ask what numbers the company uses to measure its success.
