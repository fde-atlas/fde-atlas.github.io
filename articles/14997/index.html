FDE interview pass rates: why no one publishes them

FDE Interview Prep · 2026-09-25 · 13 min read

Summary

This article investigates widely circulated figures about FDE interviews—pass rates for specific rounds, round weights, and loop timelines—and finds that no employer publishes any of them. Tracing the numbers backward, they originate from prep sites like explainx.ai and are recycled across Perspective AI, dev.to, and Substack posts with no underlying data; one citation points to an Aced guide that doesn't contain the figure. The author offers a three-step check (who could know, follow the chain, verify the final page) and advises preparing for real rounds and asking recruiters directly instead of trusting unsourced statistics.

Key points

  • No FDE employer publishes pass rates, round weights, or loop timelines.
  • Circulating figures trace back to prep sites citing no data.
  • A dev.to post credits an Aced guide that lacks the cited figures.
  • A three-step check tests any interview claim's sourcing.
  • Ask recruiters directly; practice real rounds instead of trusting figures.

You have seen the same pass-rate figure on three prep pages this week, and none of them showed you the data behind it. It sits beside the case or , next to that round’s supposed share of the hiring decision, and now you are more afraid of that round than of the rest of the loop. It is one of several loop claims that the FDE interview guide checks round by round, and it falls apart faster than most. Here are the figures, exactly as they circulate.

The short answer: no employer in our research publishes an FDE interview pass rate. None publishes how its rounds are weighted, how long its FDE loop takes from end to end, or what its interviewers write down. The figures that circulate come from prep sites and blog posts that cite no underlying data, and one of them credits a guide that does not contain the figure. Why is there no official FDE interview pass rate?

Working out a round’s pass rate takes every candidate’s result for that round at one company, and no one but the employer holds those results. So a real figure could only come from an employer, and none publishes one. The figures above come from prep sites instead. Follow them backward and the trail is short.

Look at the other side too. None of the candidate reports or hiring-leader statements in our research gives a pass rate. The figure lives on prep pages, not in any account of a loop in our research.

Why an unsourced figure spreads

It is specific, so it sounds measured. It is frightening, so it gets clicked. It is short, so it repeats cleanly from one post to the next. None of that makes it data.

A real figure would not help you either

Suppose an employer did publish a pass rate for one round; it still would not tell you your odds. It would count everyone who sat that round: people who prepared and people who did not, every level, every team. Your result in the round depends on what you say in that hour, and that is the part you control.

So a table of round shares cannot tell you where to spend your preparation hours. Your own gaps can. That is the idea behind the double bar: measure yourself on the engineering side and the customer side, then spend your time on the weaker one.

How long does the FDE interview process take?

Timelines work the same way.

An Aced prep guide from May 2026 gives a range of stages and weeks for an FDE loop, a shorter range for AI startups and a longer one for Palantir, OpenAI and Anthropic, and cites no underlying data for any of them. Source 6Forward Deployed Engineer Interview: The Definitive 2026 Guide (FDE)PublisherAced (formerly Exponent)Source typeinterview prep site It credits the startup and big-company ranges to what most candidates report, but it shows none of those reports, so you cannot check them. That fails step 2 of the check below: the chain ends at nothing you can open.

The nearest thing to a first-party figure is a company-wide page. Google’s general hiring page gives a length, in weeks, for its hiring process. Source 10Our hiring process - Google CareersPublisherGoogleSource typecompany hiring page The same page covers every role Google hires for, not FDEs in particular, so it describes Google’s hiring as a whole, not your loop. Source 10Our hiring process - Google CareersPublisherGoogleSource typecompany hiring page

Candidate reports give you single dated accounts. One person’s dates tell you what happened in one loop, at one level, in one month. The post on reading FDE reports on Reddit and Blind covers how to weigh them.

The one timeline that matters is yours, and your recruiter has it. Ask right after the first call:

“Could you walk me through the stages left and roughly when each one would happen? And is there a date your team is aiming to decide by?”

It is a logistics question, so it costs you nothing, and it gives you dates to plan your preparation around. If you are in another process, say so plainly. Never invent one to speed things up; the one-week offer deadline question shows why.

What do FDE interviewers and recruiters write down?

The last kind of claim is about what happens behind the scenes.

The same Aced prep guide asserts, without citing a source, that the recruiter is calibrating how difficult your later rounds will be, and that FDE hiring managers screen for “I did” rather than “we did” language. Source 6Forward Deployed Engineer Interview: The Definitive 2026 Guide (FDE)PublisherAced (formerly Exponent)Source typeinterview prep site No employer in our research publishes what its interviewers write down or what recruiters’ notes contain. The nearest is Palantir, whose early-talent timeline says the hiring manager interview is customized based on your onsite feedback. Source 11Students | Palantir Careers (timeline image: RecruitingTimeline_V3.jpg)PublisherPalantirSource typecompany hiring page That says feedback moves forward. It does not say a recruiter is setting the difficulty of your rounds.

Run the check: who could know? Only the employer. Does the guide cite the employer? No. So the claim is unknown.

That does not make the advice wrong. Separate the claim from the advice, and judge the advice on its own reasoning:

  • Treat the recruiter screen as a real round. Not because of hidden notes, but because it is where your level and your reasons for the role come up first. Prepare the level question before the call, with the scope of what you owned, not your years.
  • Say “I” for what you did. Not because a manager is screening for a word, but because the follow-up to any team story is “what did you decide?”, and “we” leaves you with nothing to say. Our reasoning, not a published rule.

A three-step check for any interview claim

This check is our method. It is not a standard anyone publishes, and it works on any claim about a loop: a figure, a round, a rule.

Step 1: ask who could know

No one outside an employer can compute a pass rate, because it takes every candidate’s outcome for one round at one company. The same goes for how rounds are scored against each other, and for what interviewers write down. Only the employer holds that data. If the page making the claim is not the employer’s, it is passing something along at best, so find out what.

Step 2: follow the chain to its end

Click the source. Then click that page’s source. Stop when you reach one of four things:

  • the employer’s own page, posting or blog;
  • a dated, first-hand account of one loop, even if the poster is anonymous;
  • a dataset with its method stated;
  • nothing.

The circulating figure ends at nothing: as the trail above shows, no prep site in it shows data. Before you believe a loop claim

  • Who could know this, and is it them speaking?
  • Where does the chain of sources end?
  • Does the last page contain the exact claim?
  • Which lane: published, reported or unknown?

What to prepare instead

The figures were never the useful part. The rounds they were pinned to are real, and that is where your time goes.

  • Map your loop from evidence. For your target company, list what it publishes, what candidates report and what is unknown. The evidence map is free and walks you through it.
  • Practice the round the figure was about. The decomposition round exists and candidates describe it, whatever its share of the decision. Work a prompt out loud against a clock, such as a manufacturer losing margin to unplanned downtime, whose framework is free. The decomposition interview guide covers the round end to end. Then read what the decomposition round tests.
  • Find your weaker side. Run one customer case and one timed coding question in the same week, and compare how each felt and scored. The double bar turns that into a plan.
  • Prepare the recruiter screen. Have your level answer and your reasons for the role ready before the first call.
  • Send your unknowns in writing. One email, sent after the first call:
Thanks for the call today. A few questions so I can prepare well:
- Which kinds of interview are in the loop for this role?
- Is the coding round algorithm-style or a practical task?
- Which level is this loop for?
- Roughly when would each remaining stage happen?

A recruiter can answer every line of that. No prep site can.

Spend ten minutes on the round, not the figure

A figure you cannot source tells you nothing about your hour in the room. Practice does.
