The Phoenix Project Book Review: Theory of Constraints and the Three Ways of DevOps

Original title: 凤凰项目书评：约束理论与 DevOps 三步工作法

FDE Times · 2026-10-21 · 4 min read

Summary

This review of The Phoenix Project (Gene Kim, Kevin Behr, George Spafford; IT Revolution, 2013) targets forward deployed engineers. The novel opens with an unauthorized change taking down Parts Unlimited's payroll system, and shows how Brent, a talented but overloaded engineer, becomes the organization's bottleneck. Drawing on the Theory of Constraints and the Three Ways, the author distills three lessons: limit work in progress at bottlenecks, learn the customer's change-approval process before deploying anything, and feed incident learnings back to the product team. It also suggests reading The DevOps Handbook and The Goal afterward, and advises writing concrete accomplishments on resumes.

Key points

  • The novel opens with an unauthorized change causing a payroll outage.
  • Overloaded engineer Brent shows that adding tasks to a bottleneck only lengthens queues.
  • Uncontrolled changes return as unplanned work that crushes the bottleneck.
  • FDEs should learn the customer's change process before deploying anything.
  • Recommended order: The Phoenix Project, then The DevOps Handbook, then The Goal.

Key Takeaways

  • The book opens with a payroll system outage caused by an unauthorized change, and gradually introduces the Three Ways and the Theory of Constraints.
  • Three things worth remembering: don't keep piling work onto the bottleneck; control changes to reduce unplanned work; bring the lessons from incidents back to the product team.
  • As an FDE, read this one first, then The DevOps Handbook and The Goal.

The book opens with employees not getting paid on time. An unauthorized change has taken down Parts Unlimited's payroll system. Anyone who has lived through a production outage on-site at a customer will recognize this scenario.

The Phoenix Project: A Novel about IT, DevOps, and Helping Your Business Win was written by Gene Kim, Kevin Behr, and George Spafford, and first published by IT Revolution on January 10, 2013. It is written as a novel, but what it describes is how operations work actually functions.

The reason a Forward Deployed Engineer (FDE) should read it is simple. Most of your time on-site is spent working inside someone else's systems, with someone else's ops team, under someone else's change process. This book helps you understand why they are slow, and what you can do to help.

One Good Engineer Can Stall an Entire System

The protagonist, Bill, is an IT manager. The CEO gives him 90 days to clean up the mess, or the entire department gets outsourced.

The publisher positions the book as an introduction to the Three Ways and the Theory of Constraints, two foundational principles of DevOps. John Willis has called it a modern version of The Goal, Goldratt's novel about the Theory of Constraints.

This article covers only the First and Second Ways, the two an FDE encounters most often on-site; the Third Way is out of scope. The three takeaways below are also not the same as the Three Ways: two of them build on the first two steps, and the third comes from change control.

The most memorable character in the book is Brent. IT Revolution's official summary describes him as a brilliant but overloaded engineer. Because he is always pulled in to fight fires, Brent's expertise becomes the bottleneck for the whole organization.

The character Erik offers this advice: reduce work in progress (WIP) to improve efficiency and throughput. Applied to Brent, the advice becomes concrete. If every task has to go through him, then every new task added just joins the queue in front of him, and nothing gets done any faster.

So reducing WIP means no longer feeding work in faster than the bottleneck can process it. This is exactly the spirit of what Gene Kim summarizes as the First Way: look at the performance of the whole system, not the performance of individual silos. Sandor Dargo, an engineer who wrote a review of the book, also restates this lesson from the factory floor: keep the amount of work in progress low.

Imagine this scenario: on the customer's data team, only one person understands the legacy data pipeline, and there are ten tickets waiting for that person.

Opening an eleventh ticket for your integration will only make the queue longer. Do the opposite: document what that person knows, and take on yourself the parts of the work that don't require them.

Where Does Unplanned Work Come From?

The publisher's page highlights four types of work. According to Dargo, the first two are business projects and internal IT projects. They generate changes, and changes are usually tracked in a ticketing system.

The fourth type is unplanned work, defined as urgent, reactive work that crowds out planned work and slows progress.

The payroll incident shows where unplanned work usually starts: an unauthorized change. Bill then forms a change advisory board (CAB) to review and approve changes to IT systems.

The causal chain worth remembering is this: out-of-control changes come back as unplanned work, and that work lands on people like Brent, the already-overloaded bottleneck.

For an FDE, there is one thing worth doing early. In your first week on-site, before deploying anything, ask about their change approval process. If the next payroll incident is your fault, the customer will stop trusting you.

Feedback Must Flow Back to the People Who Write the Code

The Second Way is about creating feedback loops from right to left, that is, from operations back to development. The FDE sits exactly at this junction. You can see how the product breaks in the real world; the product team back at headquarters cannot.

After an incident on-site is resolved, don't stop at the moment the system is back up. Write a short note to the core team with three parts: which change triggered the incident, how long it took to detect, and what should be fixed in the product so the next fire doesn't need fighting.

What Order to Read In, and What to Put on Your Resume

Tim O'Reilly wrote that everyone who has lived through a failed IT project should be forced to read this book. That's a bit strong, but if you've been through a botched project, many scenes in the book will feel uncomfortably familiar.

Read The Phoenix Project first, because novels are easy to read. Gene Kim points out that The DevOps Handbook goes deeper into applying the Three Ways, so it comes second. If you want to understand how the idea of "flow" emerged from manufacturing, read The Goal last.

Don't write vague phrases like "experienced with operations and DevOps" on your resume. Write about specific work, for example: "Wrote a runbook for a legacy data pipeline that only one engineer at the customer understood, enabling their team to handle common incidents on their own."

This sentence shows recruiters that you can spot a bottleneck and eliminate it. If you have real numbers, such as ticket counts or wait times before and after, include those too.

Bill had 90 days to save the IT department. An FDE usually has even less time to earn a customer's trust, and the fastest way is still the same: don't be the one behind that unauthorized change.
