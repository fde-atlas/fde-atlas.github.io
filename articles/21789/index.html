How to Handle CTO Pushback on Your AI Architecture

FDE Times · 2026-11-10 · 7 min read

Summary

This article advises forward deployed engineers on handling CTO objections to AI architecture designs. It argues that pushback usually signals an unknown constraint, so re-explaining rarely works. Drawing on Google's code review guidance, Product Talk, and LeadDev, it categorizes objections into constraints to accept immediately, operational concerns to probe with questions, and genuinely contested decisions to log as open issues. A Kepler case shows a small tool fixed in days what a year of argument could not. It also recommends pre-reviewing designs with a client-side engineer and preparing responses before meetings.

Key points

  • If the CTO is right, admit it and fix the design immediately with a committed date.
  • Log genuinely contested decisions as open issues and discuss underlying reasoning instead of arguing direction.
  • Turn operational objections into design criteria by asking about the team's existing infrastructure.
  • At Kepler, a data viewer unblocked a CSV-to-Parquet migration that had been stalled for about a year.
  • Have a client-side engineer review the design before presenting it to the CTO.

Pushback usually hides a constraint you have not yet heard, and a well-aimed question resolves it faster than another round of explanation.

In brief

  • When the CTO pushes back, first ask yourself whether they are right; if they are, say so plainly and fix it now rather than promising to do it later.
  • On points that are genuinely contested, stop arguing about the direction, log an open issue and discuss the reasoning underneath.
  • Repeating the technical argument rarely removes an objection; at Kepler, a small viewer unblocked a data migration that one engineer had held up for about a year.

The interruption tends to come around slide four. The architecture diagram for the AI assistant has just gone up, and the client’s CTO cuts in: “Why add another vector database? Nobody on my team runs one.” Most engineers respond by explaining again, in more detail, with more numbers.

That is the moment the advisory session starts to go wrong. The person across the table does not need a better pitch from you. They are handing you a constraint your diagram has not accounted for.

For an FDE, this meeting matters more than it looks. First Round Review’s piece on hiring FDEs treats living alongside the customer on site as a core part of the role. You are there precisely to hear constraints like this one, and defending the slides is a distant second.

Pushback is data

Google’s engineering guidance on handling pushback in code review contains a line worth taping to your monitor: if the other person is right, tell them so and let the point go. It sounds simple, but it requires you to stop and ask yourself the question before you argue back.

Swap “code” for “the client’s systems” and the advice applies even more strongly to a CTO. They know which team is on call at night, which system has gone down before, and which regulation keeps data from leaving their infrastructure. You have been there a few weeks.

The same guidance warns that letting people “clean it up later” is a familiar route to a degrading codebase. The same holds on a client site: once you have accepted a point, fix it in the design straight away instead of pushing it onto a “later” list nobody reopens.

Never let the CTO be the first reader

Before you walk into the room, someone else should read the design. A guide on Refactoring English about getting feedback on design docs argues that fixing a mistake with one reviewer beats having 10 people trip over it.

On a client site, the ideal early reader is an engineer on their side whom you have worked with for a few weeks. They can point out things you could not have learned yourself, such as the platform team having just turned down a new service. Every flaw they catch is a question the CTO does not have to ask.

One session, objection by objection

Say you are proposing an assistant that helps claims handlers at an insurer search case files. The architecture has a document store, an embedding step, a vector database, an LLM called through an external API, and an eval suite that scores the answers. The three objections below are fairly typical.

Objection one: “Claims documents contain personal data. They cannot be sent to an external API.” There is nothing to debate here. The right answer: “You’re right, I hadn’t accounted for that constraint. I’ll change the diagram so the model runs inside your infrastructure and send you the new version before Friday.”

Objection two: “Adding a vector database means one more system to be on call for.” Do not rush to defend it, and do not rush to drop it. Product Talk, writing about finding common ground with engineers who dislike a feature, describes cases where an engineer is stuck on an implementation problem but voices it as if they object to the feature itself.

So ask a pointed question: “Which data stores does your team run today? If we reused one of them, what would we give up?” According to Product Talk, simply asking often lets the person objecting see for themselves what their real concern is.

Here, the objection becomes a design criterion: the operational cost to their team.

Objection three: “Why not just fine-tune a model on our data and be done with it?” This one is genuinely contested. Product Talk advises that when two sides disagree on direction, they should stop arguing about the direction itself until both are frustrated, and move to the reasoning underneath.

What to say: “This is a big decision. I’ll log it as an open issue, set out the case for both approaches, and we can settle it when your team reviews it together.” The test for an open issue is simple: the person who wants to fine-tune reads your paragraph on their position and still nods.

Behind every objection sits a different worry

What you hear What usually lies behind it How to respond
“Data cannot leave our systems” A legal or security constraint you did not know about Accept it at once, fix the design, commit to a date for the new version
“My team can’t operate that” Operational cost, who is on call at night Ask about existing infrastructure, make operations a criterion
“Why not do it with X?” An approach they have weighed or strongly believe in Log an open issue, present both approaches fairly
“Great, let’s just build it” Enthusiasm, not necessarily readiness Check data, people and process before promising anything

The last row is the easiest to overlook, because it does not sound like an objection. Yet a CTO nodding enthusiastically may not have clean data or a team to run what you have just drawn.

When the third explanation still fails

The clearest example of the limits of argument does not involve a CTO. Vinoo Ganesh recounts that at Kepler, the holdout was a data quality engineer who blocked the move from CSV to Parquet for about a year. Explaining the technical benefits again and again did nothing to lift her objection.

The real constraint lay in how she worked. She checked data by opening CSV files directly, and for Parquet she had no reader that let her do the same. What broke the deadlock was a viewer that preserved her ability to look at the data, and within a few days the migration was running.

A year of argument achieved less than a few days of fixing the right thing. The job title differs, but the mechanism is worth carrying into a meeting with a CTO: an objection that keeps coming back is usually protecting a habit or a risk your slides never touched.

A LeadDev piece on building consensus with detractors advises finding the deeper concern and addressing it at that level instead of sinking into endless debate. The question worth asking is “What would make you comfortable with this?” A more polished explanation rarely gets you there.

The mistakes that sink an advisory session

The most common mistake is treating the presentation as a sales meeting, pressing on through the slides after the CTO has raised a constraint that knocks out half the architecture.

The second is settling every dispute at the table, which leaves the people who were absent feeling sidelined, so they object later. The third is promising changes without committing to a date for the new version, which makes “you’re right” sound like mere politeness.

For your career, this skill is easier to demonstrate than you might think. If an FDE job posting mentions acting as a technical adviser to client leadership, that is a signal to have a story ready about a time a client pushed back on your design.

The story should have three parts: what you proposed, what they pointed out that you had missed, and how the final design ended up better.

An exercise for your next CTO meeting

Before the meeting, copy the four-row table above onto a sheet of paper. For each row, write down the objection you expect the CTO to raise about your own architecture, and how you plan to respond: accept and set a date, ask a follow-up question, or log an open issue.

After the meeting, mark the objections you predicted and the ones that caught you off guard. Next time, take those surprises to your early reader first.

The person a CTO invites back is not the one who wins every argument, but the one who leaves the room with a different design because they listened.
