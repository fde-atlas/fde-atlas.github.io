How to Set Kill Criteria for AI Pilots: An FDE Scorecard Guide

Original title: AI 试点止损标准怎么定：FDE 记分卡指南

FDE Times · 2026-10-25 · 6 min read

Summary

This guide argues that FDEs should decide kill criteria for AI pilots before work begins, since S&P Global data shows companies abandon 46% of AI proofs of concept on average, often because no one predefined what success means. It recommends signing a scorecard on day one with business metrics, thresholds, and response rules, while writing detailed error rubrics only after reading real logs. A hypothetical logistics customer-service agent case shows a 74% accuracy rate improving to 89.5% by fixing a missing tracking-number input, versus only 7 errors a stronger model could fix. The article distinguishes reviving (fixing one bottleneck) from restructuring (redesigning around workflow gaps), requires a binary scale-or-stop decision at day 90, and warns against scoring too early, reflexively switching models, and letting pilots drift indefinitely.

Key points

  • Companies abandon 46% of AI PoCs, usually because success was never defined upfront.
  • Day-one scorecard: set metrics, thresholds, and response rules; write error rubrics after reading logs.
  • A 74% accuracy case rose to 89.5% by fixing inputs, beating a model swap that could fix only 7 errors.
  • Choose revive (fix one bottleneck) or restructure (redesign for workflow fit); day 90 is scale or stop.
  • Avoid premature scoring, reflexive model switching, and indefinite pilot extensions.

Key Takeaways

  • Killing a pilot is normal: S&P Global found that companies abandon an average of 46% of AI proofs of concept before they reach production.
  • Result thresholds and response rules must be set on day one; error scoring rubrics should be written only after reading real logs.
  • Before switching models, count how many errors actually come from data, product, or workflow.

It's week six of the pilot, and you're in a meeting with the client. The AI agent gets roughly three-quarters of questions right. The head of operations asks: "So is that good or bad?" No one answers, because no one ever wrote down what counts as "good."

This scenario is more common than you'd think. A survey by S&P Global Market Intelligence found that companies abandon an average of 46% of AI proofs of concept (PoCs) before they reach production. The share of companies abandoning most of their AI projects jumped from 17% to 42% in a single year.

So the question a Forward Deployed Engineer (FDE) should ask is not how to ensure the pilot never fails, but: when results fall short, is the next step already decided, clear, and signed off by both parties? That's what separates people who run pilots well from those who are only good at demos.

Pilots fail not just because of insufficient quality, but because of missing definitions

A practical guide from SoftwareSeni puts it bluntly: pilots often stall because no one agreed on what "usable" means before starting. Without a definition, everyone interprets the number 75% differently. The engineering team sees progress, finance sees risk, and the project sponsor sees exhaustion.

Gartner predicted that by the end of 2025, at least 30% of generative AI projects would be abandoned after the proof of concept, citing four reasons: poor data quality, inadequate risk controls, rising costs, and unclear business value.

The last one is entirely within your control. Value is "unclear" only because no one wrote down how to measure it.

Anthropic's official documentation on model evals lays out the basic principle: replace "performs well" with specific, measurable metrics, and define those metrics before building the evals.

The same document also warns that goals should not exceed the model's current capabilities. Setting a 99% threshold for a task where benchmarks only reach 85% is writing your failure report in advance.

What to set on day one, and what to leave for later?

Many people get this wrong. Hamel Husain, an evals expert who has worked at Airbnb and GitHub, warns that writing scoring rubrics before looking at the data is a very common mistake. In his view, you must first analyze logs to understand what errors actually occur, and only then build evals.

These two pieces of advice are not contradictory. What you set on day one are business outcome metrics, thresholds, and response rules; the detailed scoring rubrics for each error category should be written after reading real logs in the first few weeks.

Response rules are the most commonly forgotten part. SoftwareSeni recommends agreeing in advance on how to respond before the scorecard signals failure, and dividing course corrections into two categories.

Revive means keeping the current solution and fixing a solvable bottleneck. Restructure means redesigning the solution. The final decision on day 90 is deliberately designed to have only two possible answers: scale up, or stop.

What does a day-one scorecard look like?

Imagine a hypothetical pilot: building an AI agent that answers invoice questions for the customer service team of a logistics company. Before writing the first line of code, you and the head of customer service sign a one-page scorecard like this.

CheckpointQuestion to answerAgreed threshold and action
Day 30What do the real errors look like?Read at least 100 logs, categorize the errors, and write scoring rubrics based on them
Day 60Is progress on track?Accuracy of 85% or above: prepare to scale up. 70–85% with errors concentrated on one fixable cause: revive. Below 70%, or errors scattered and caused by the workflow: restructure or stop
Day 90Scale up or stop?Only two options; the decision-makers who sign off are designated on day one

The 85% and 70% here are illustrative. In practice, they should be set based on a small preliminary experiment and the current efficiency of staff handling tasks manually, which also aligns with Anthropic's emphasis on "achievable" goals.

Now it's day 60. On 200 real questions, the AI agent answered 148 correctly, or 74%, landing in the middle band. No one can jump to conclusions. You open the 52 incorrect answers and label them one by one.

The result: 31 errors occurred because the asker didn't provide a tracking number, so the AI agent couldn't retrieve the correct invoice; 14 errors came from the AI agent consulting an outdated price list that hadn't been updated; only 7 were cases where the model reasoned incorrectly despite complete data.

This is exactly the kind of failure Husain describes: the user's question is too vague and lacks sufficient context, so the model never had a chance to answer correctly.

If adding a required tracking number field fixes those 31 errors, accuracy rises to 179 out of 200, or 89.5%.

By contrast, switching to a stronger model would fix at most 7 errors.

According to the signed rules, the decision is revive: keep the current solution and fix a specific bottleneck. As a result, the day-60 meeting lasted only fifteen minutes, because no one needed to argue about what "good" means.

When should you redesign rather than patch?

Suppose the labels paint a different picture: the errors are evenly distributed, and customer service staff don't use the AI agent because it isn't in the tools they use daily. That's the signal to restructure. MIT NANDA's report "The GenAI Divide" argues that generic tools repeatedly struggle in enterprises because they don't learn from real workflows or adapt to them.

The report puts the failure rate of enterprise AI solutions at 95%, but the sample is quite small, so this figure should be taken only as a reference.

What's worth remembering is its diagnosis: the root cause lies in the gap in workflow integration, not in model quality. In this case, restructuring means putting the AI agent directly into the interface employees already use to do their work.

The same report also found that the success rate of in-house built solutions is only one-third that of solutions purchased from specialized vendors or built through partnerships. So when restructuring, seriously consider buying rather than building—even if you're the one writing the code.

Mistakes that ruin good decisions

The first mistake is scoring too early. Gartner's Rita Sallam points out that the impact of generative AI often doesn't appear immediately but emerges gradually over time. If your primary metric is the team's overall processing time, don't measure it in week two while employees are still learning the tool.

The second mistake is reflexively switching models. As the example shows, most errors may stem from an input form or a price list, and no model can fix outdated data. Before opening the model comparison page, count the error labels first.

The third mistake is letting the pilot drag on indefinitely. A pilot without a day-90 checkpoint easily turns into an endless "one more month." It slowly burns through costs, and S&P Global ranks cost alongside data privacy and security as the top obstacles.

Turning this skill into a career advantage

Don't just write "deployed an AI agent for a client" on your resume. Write clearly how you set up the scorecard, how many logs you read, and which numbers drove your choice between revive and stop.

When recruiters read this, they'll see that you know how to drive a pilot to a clear conclusion rather than stopping at a demo.

This week's exercise: pick a pilot you were involved in, even one that's already ended, and rewrite the scorecard it should have had from day one. If you can't write out its day-60 thresholds, you'll understand why that meeting went silent.
