What Implementation Teams Need From an AI Customer Success Platform

Quivly · 2026-11-01 · 6 min read

Summary

This Quivly buying guide evaluates AI customer success platforms from the implementation team's perspective, including onboarding managers and forward deployed engineers (FDEs). It argues that implementation produces most of the context platforms rely on later, so requirements should be defined before go-live. The guide lists must-have requirements across onboarding, adoption, renewals and expansion, plus AI-layer needs like approval thresholds, evidence trails and security, and flags red signals to watch for in vendor demos. Quivly's own product is presented at the end.

Key points

  • Implementation-phase context shapes adoption, renewal and expansion outcomes.
  • Onboarding needs project management: milestones, dependencies, input collection, readiness gates.
  • Adoption requires one account record, automated handoff summaries and two-sided technical alerts.
  • Renewals need kickoff goals retained; expansion signals should come from delivery work.
  • AI layer must have approval thresholds, evidence on proposals and full audit trails.

Most buying guides for AI customer success platforms are written from the CSM's point of view: health scores, playbooks, renewal forecasts. Implementation teams usually join the evaluation late, if at all. That is a problem, because the implementation phase produces most of the context the platform will depend on for the rest of the customer's life. The goals named at kickoff, the integrations that were hard to stand up, the stakeholders who engaged and the ones who did not all shape adoption, renewal and expansion.

This guide sets out what to look for in an AI platform that covers onboarding through expansion, written from the needs of the implementation team: onboarding managers, implementation leads and forward deployed engineers (FDEs). It is a requirements list you can bring to an evaluation, not a ranking of vendors. Quivly, the AI workforce for post-sales, was built around these requirements, and we describe how it fits at the end.

Why the Implementation Team Should Write Part of the Requirements

A customer success platform that only starts working at go-live inherits a blank page. It does not know why the customer bought, what was promised or which parts of the rollout are fragile. CSMs then rebuild that context by hand, usually by asking the customer questions they already answered months earlier.

When the implementation team helps define requirements, the platform is chosen to capture context at the source and carry it forward. That shortens time to value during onboarding, because work and inputs are tracked in one place, and it protects the renewal later, because the account history is complete. The requirements below are grouped by lifecycle stage, with the implementation team's needs stated first in each.

To use the list, mark each requirement as must-have or nice-to-have for your own team before any vendor conversations begin. Then ask every vendor to show each must-have working on a realistic account rather than describing it on a slide. A requirement the vendor can only explain, not demonstrate, should be treated as missing until proven otherwise. Keep the implementation lead and a CSM in the same evaluation sessions, so both sides see how context moves from one stage to the next. To keep the pilot itself from stalling on one person’s calendar, see how to run a post-sales platform pilot without becoming the bottleneck.

Onboarding: Run the Work, Not Just Track It

For technical products, onboarding is a project with dependencies, customer-side tasks and integration work. A platform that treats onboarding as an email sequence and a checklist will not serve an FDE team.

Must-have requirements

  • Project templates with milestones, owners, dependencies and dates, so every rollout starts from a repeatable plan.
  • Customer input collection for files, credentials, screenshots and answers, attached to the project and followed up automatically.
  • Conversation-to-task capture that proposes tasks, risks and blockers from calls, emails and Slack, with the source attached and a person approving each one.
  • Readiness gates tied to technical checkpoints, so go-live depends on the customer being ready rather than on a date.

Nice to have

  • Planned versus actual effort per project, so you can see which rollouts run over and why.
  • A customer-facing view of the plan, so the customer sees the same status your team does.

Our guide to running customer implementations as projects covers this stage in more depth.

Adoption: Inherit the Implementation, Then Watch Activation

Adoption is where implementation context pays off or gets lost. The implementation team needs confidence that what they learned will reach the CSM, and that technical problems after go-live will come back to them with enough detail to act.

Must-have requirements

  • One account record that holds the project history alongside product usage, support tickets, conversations and commercial data.
  • An automated handoff summary generated from the project, covering goals, configuration decisions, open risks and key contacts.
  • Activation tracking against milestones you define, such as first integration live or first value event, with alerts when an account falls behind.
  • Two-sided technical alerts that give the FDE full context, such as error logs and affected integrations, and give the CSM a plain-language summary.

To set those milestones well, see how to define and track activation events.

Renewals: Keep the Original Goals in View

Renewal conversations go better when the team can show progress against the outcomes the customer named at kickoff. That only works if those outcomes were captured during implementation and are still visible a year later.

Must-have requirements

  • Kickoff goals and success criteria stored on the account, not buried in a closed project or a slide deck.
  • Risk signals that include implementation history, so a rollout that slipped or a sponsor who disengaged during setup still counts toward renewal risk.
  • AI-drafted renewal preparation, such as a summary of value delivered and open issues, that a person reviews before it reaches the customer.

Our guide on spotting churn risk during implementation explains why early signals matter so much here.

Expansion: Spot Growth Grounded in Delivery Evidence

Expansion opportunities often appear first in implementation work: a second team asking for access, a new region in scope or a request that sits outside the original project. The implementation team is usually the first to hear about them, and the least likely to log them anywhere commercial.

Must-have requirements

  • Expansion signals from delivery work, such as new use cases or out-of-scope requests flagged from conversations and routed to the account owner.
  • Follow-on projects on the same account record, so a second rollout starts with everything learned in the first.
  • Recommendations that explain their evidence, so the team can see why an account is flagged before anyone contacts the customer.

For more on this stage, see our guide to expansion signals.

Requirements for the AI Layer Itself

Across every stage, implementation teams have specific needs for how AI behaves. FDEs work with customer systems and credentials, so mistakes carry real cost.

  • Approval thresholds you control: internal actions can run automatically, while customer-facing messages, scope changes and date changes wait for a person.
  • Evidence on every proposal: each suggested task, risk or message links to the call, email or signal it came from.
  • A full audit trail of what the AI did and who approved it.
  • Access to the same context in the tools your team already uses, such as Slack, rather than a separate interface that engineers have to remember to open.
  • Clear security answers on data access, retention and permissions. Our security review guide lists the questions to ask.

Red Flags to Watch For in a Demo

Scripted demos tend to show the smoothest path through the product. These signs suggest a platform will not serve the implementation team well.

  • Onboarding is shown only as an email journey or a checklist, with no dependencies or customer-side tasks.
  • The vendor cannot show what a CSM sees about an account's implementation six months after go-live.
  • Implementation work has to live in a separate tool connected by a sync.
  • AI output arrives as alerts with no drafted next step, or as actions with no evidence and no approval step.
  • Integration health is invisible, so technical problems only surface when the customer complains.

Ask the vendor to walk through a single account from kickoff to its first expansion conversation. Gaps in that story are gaps your team will fill by hand.
