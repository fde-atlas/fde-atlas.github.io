What's the Difference Between SES and FDE? A Thorough Comparison of Working Styles

Original title: SESとFDEは何が違う？働き方を徹底比較

日本Fde協会 · 2026-06-21 · 6 min read

Summary

The head of the Japan FDE Association compares SES (contract-based resident engineers) with FDEs, drawing on his own shift from SES-style work. His core argument: SES provides labor billed by hours under client direction, while FDEs are paid for outcomes, define problems themselves, and join decisions from tech choices to business judgment. A manufacturing client asked for an inventory system, but field observation revealed the real issue was cross-department information sharing. He outlines three steps for SES engineers to move toward FDE-style work and stresses SES is a solid early-career foundation, not a dead end.

Key points

  • SES sells labor hours; FDEs sell problem-solving outcomes.
  • FDEs discover and define the client's real problem themselves.
  • A client's inventory request masked a real-time information-sharing problem.
  • Three steps: question tasks, learn business basics, make small proposals.
  • SES experience transfers well to FDE work; 3-5 years is often enough.

SES and FDE—both appear to be engineers who work on-site at client companies. However, their purpose, roles, and how they deliver results are fundamentally different. As the Representative Director of the Japan FDE Association, I have experienced SES-style on-site work at a major engineering company early in my career. Days spent completing assigned tasks versus an FDE-style way of working where you identify problems yourself and solve them with technology. In this article, I will explain the "essential differences" that I can speak to precisely because I have experienced both.

By reading this article, you will accurately understand the differences between SES and FDE and be able to judge which working style suits your career. If you want to learn the basics of FDE first, please also see "What is FDE? A Practitioner Explains the Differences from SE and SIer".

The Decisive Differences Between SES and FDE—Organized in a Comparison Table

To grasp the overall picture first, let's organize the differences between SES and FDE in a table.

Comparison ItemSESFDE
PurposeProviding engineer laborSolving client problems with technology
Contract TypeQuasi-mandate contract (mainly time-based billing)Results-based contracts such as outsourcing or advisory agreements
Evaluation MetricsWorking hours, attendance, responsiveness to instructionsProblem-solving results and business impact
Problem DefinitionInstructed by the client or PMDiscovered and defined by oneself
Involvement in Decision-MakingLimited (implementation level)From technology selection to business decisions
Relationship with ClientIn a position of receiving directionEqual partner
Career DirectionDeepening technical expertise or managementTechnology × business specialist or independence

In short, SES is "providing labor" while FDE is "providing problem-solving." Even sitting at the same client site, the nature of the work is completely different.

The Shift from "Waiting for Instructions" to "Defining Problems"

The Ceiling I Felt in an SES-Style Working Style

What I first experienced at the engineering company was SES-style on-site work. I would go to the client company's office and complete tasks assigned by the PM. Technically, it was rewarding work, but I was always "the one waiting for instructions." No matter how good my ideas were, the scope in which I could propose them at my own discretion was limited.

The turning point came when I was appointed PM of a system project worth around 100 million yen. In this project, with a development team of 8 and more than 20 stakeholders, I had to handle not only technical decisions but also coordinating interests between departments and reporting to management. It was then that I first experienced a working style of "running both technology and business in the field," and we achieved 180% of our profit target.

The Difference Is "Whose Problems Are Defined, and by Whom"

In SES, problems are already defined. Work comes down in the form of "we want you to build this part of this system." FDE is different. You enter the client's workplace, observe business processes, and start by finding out for yourself "what is the problem that really needs to be solved."

When I joined a manufacturing client, they requested "we want to revamp our inventory management system." However, when I walked around the workplace, the real problem was not inventory management but the fact that information sharing between departments was not happening in real time. If I had built the inventory management system as instructed as an SES, the fundamental problem would not have been solved.

How SES Engineers Can Move Toward an FDE-Style Working Style

Step 1: Always Consider "Why Is This Task Necessary?"

There is something you can start doing right away even in an SES environment. That is to develop the habit of digging into "why is this necessary" for the tasks you are assigned. Before writing code, understand what business problem the system solves. Just this alone will dramatically change the quality of your work.

An engineer I worked with on a SaaS company project was someone who made exactly this shift. At first, they quietly worked through API implementation, but at some point they asked, "Will customers actually use this feature?" That one question stopped unnecessary development and saved three weeks of work. FDE-style thinking can start today, even without a special title.

Step 2: Learn Beyond Technology

The most effective way to move closer to FDE is to learn the basics of business. How to read financial statements, how to calculate ROI, understanding the structure of the client's industry—these can all be self-taught without special qualifications. I myself gained "the ability to talk about business with numbers" through my experience managing funds worth 5 billion yen as a trader at a financial institution.

Step 3: Start with Small Proposals

Even in an SES environment, proposals like "this could be improved" are often welcomed. Speaking from my experience of building an organization from scratch at a startup, small improvement proposals from the field are the first step toward FDE-style behavior. Finding manual tasks that can be automated and writing scripts, improving how reports are delivered—accumulating these actions builds the trust that "this person doesn't just wait for instructions."

This Is Not a Rejection of SES

Everything I have said so far is not meant to imply "SES is bad." SES has its own strengths. You can experience diverse workplaces, develop a wide range of technical skills, and it is a relatively stable form of employment. Building a foundation of technical skills through SES early in your career and then transitioning to an FDE-style working style is a very sensible path.

What matters is being clear about what you are aiming for. Do you want to master technology, or do you want to solve business problems using technology as your weapon? If the latter, knowing about FDE as an option has great significance.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q. How many years does it take to switch from SES to FDE?

A. It's hard to say definitively, but if you have 3 to 5 years of practical experience in SES and a strong desire to understand business, transitioning to an FDE-style working style is entirely possible. Since you already have a foundation of technical skills, you can focus on acquiring business skills.

Q. How much of a salary difference is there between SES and FDE?

A. FDE often uses a results-based compensation system, which tends to have more upside potential compared to the hourly-rate model of SES. However, since compensation is directly tied to results, you need to understand both the risks and the rewards.

Q. Do I have to go independent to work as an FDE?

A. Not necessarily. Some people take on FDE-like roles while remaining employed by a company. What matters is not the employment arrangement, but the essence of this way of working: "solving customer challenges from both technical and business perspectives."

Q. Does SES experience help with becoming an FDE?

A. It helps a great deal. Having experienced a variety of worksites, being accustomed to communicating at client sites, and having worked with various technology stacks—all of these become strengths as an FDE.
