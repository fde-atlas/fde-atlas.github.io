Chapter 4 · The Report Is Done, So Why Is No One Taking Action

Original title: 第 4 章 · 报表做完了，为什么没人行动

FDE 的来路 · 5 min read

Summary

This chapter examines the recurring gap between seeing a problem in reports and acting on it. Dashboards can flag anomalies, but judgment, authority, execution, and accountability sit in different departments, so data rarely drives action by itself. The author argues that inaction is often a rational choice under local incentives, that metrics tied to compensation get gamed, and that BI requirements should start from decisions rather than pages. It closes by noting that AI agents sharpen these authorization and liability questions, setting up the role of FDEs.

Key points

  • Reports deliver problems to the door; who judges, approves, executes, and bears risk is not in the data model.
  • Inaction is often rational under local incentives, echoing Pfeffer and Sutton's knowing-doing gap.
  • Once metrics enter compensation, people adapt to them, reshaping the data itself.
  • BI interviews should ask what decisions someone repeatedly makes, not what pages they want.
  • Agents can act, but authorization and liability questions become acute—this is where FDEs come in.

Once a report flags an anomaly, who assesses it, who decides, who executes, and who is accountable? This chapter breaks down the recurring gap between seeing a problem and taking action in enterprise digitalization.

At the business review meeting, the big screen makes the problems very clear.

Revenue in one region has declined for three consecutive months; the turnover days for a certain type of inventory keep getting longer; a certain channel looks like it's selling well, but after deducting promotions and returns, it's actually losing money.

The data team finishes presenting, the leaders nod, the business departments take photos. The meeting ends, and the next agenda item begins.

A month later, the same chart appears again.

The numbers are a bit redder.

When data people encounter this kind of scene, it's easy to doubt their own work: Is the analysis not deep enough? Are the alerts not timely enough? Did the charts fail to highlight the key points?

So they add another layer of drill-down, build a forecasting model, and attach colors and message notifications to anomalies.

These improvements may help. But in many cases, the problem is not "whether it was seen."

The problem is what happens after it's seen.

A Report Has No Hands or Feet

Excess inventory is a fact; dealing with inventory is a chain of actions.

Someone has to judge whether the demand forecast was wrong, whether too much was purchased, whether the product isn't selling, or whether supplier lead times forced the company to stock up early. After the judgment, they still have to decide whether to cut prices, transfer stock, return goods, stop purchasing, or keep waiting. Every action involves different departments and hurts someone's metrics.

A report can only deliver the problem to the door.

Who is behind that door is often not written into the data model.

The person responsible for watching inventory may not have the authority to stop purchasing; the person with that authority may worry about stockouts; sales wants to keep more inventory on hand; finance wants to reduce tied-up capital first. Everyone is looking at the same table, but the accounts behind them are not the same.

That's why "data-driven" is most easily misunderstood as: as long as the data is good enough, the right actions will naturally happen.

In reality, data rarely drives anything by itself. It needs to enter a concrete structure of accountability: who judges, who approves, who executes, and who bears the consequences of mistakes.

Missing any one of these links, a report is just a rather sophisticated notification.

Not Acting Is Sometimes a Rational Choice

Data departments often interpret "no action" as the business not valuing data.

Sometimes that's true; sometimes it's too easy an explanation.

A regional manager seeing profits decline doesn't necessarily not know he should shut down inefficient outlets. He may know it better than headquarters—it's just that closing stores would make his scale metrics look bad. Procurement seeing excess inventory may also know they should order less, but supplier contracts, minimum order quantities, and stockout penalties all press on the other side.

From the company's overall perspective, acting makes sense. From an individual's position, not acting is safer.

This is also why some companies have many reports, many meetings, and few actions. It's not a lack of knowledge; it's that everyone is making reasonable choices within a set of local incentives.

In 1999, Stanford professors Jeffrey Pfeffer and Robert Sutton called a similar phenomenon the "knowing-doing gap." Companies don't lack knowledge for improving performance, yet they let discussions, plans, and presentations substitute for action.

Data platforms can shorten the time to "knowing," but they can't fill every gap on the way to "doing."

If assessments, permissions, and risk ownership don't change, sending everyone a few more alerts will only make the notification bar busier.

Once a Metric Enters Performance Reviews, It's No Longer Neutral

Many data projects have a simple wish: lay out the facts, and everyone will manage by the facts.

But once a metric is tied to bonuses, promotions, and department evaluations, it is no longer just a fact.

Rank regions by revenue, and some will recognize revenue early; rank by profit, and some will argue about how costs are allocated; rank by customer satisfaction, and some will push difficult customers to other teams. The metric doesn't lie—people adapt to the metric.

This isn't to say there should be no assessments, but to remind data teams: after a metric is published, the business will shape the data in return.

If an alerting system only counts the number of cases handled, teams may prioritize closing easy anomalies; if it emphasizes recovered amounts, they may spend all their time on a few big cases. The most technically accurate metric doesn't necessarily produce the best business actions.

At this point, report design has run into organizational design.

Who will use this metric? What actions can they take? Are those actions aligned with their interests? If several metrics conflict, who decides the priority?

If these questions are only asked late in the project, it's usually too late.

From "What to Display" to "Who Needs to Do What"

When doing BI, requirements interviews often start with pages.

The boss wants an overview, department heads want trends, business staff want details. Everyone discusses filters, drill-down levels, and refresh frequency. The page gets built, and it looks quite complete.

But if you flip the question around, the answers will be different.

Don't start by asking "What do you want to see?" Start by asking:

What is one decision you repeatedly make? What information do you need before deciding? Under what circumstances would you change your current approach? Do you execute this action yourself, or does someone else need to approve it? After it's done, how many days or months until you see the results?

Once you ask these questions, many "must-have" reports start to seem less necessary.

Some data just makes people feel reassured and corresponds to no action; some data is important but only needs to trigger an alert when something is abnormal; and some problems don't lack numbers at all—what's missing is a set of cross-department handling rules.

This is not an argument against dashboards. Dashboards are good for building shared situational awareness, letting teams know where things stand. But if the goal is to change the business, you must keep going down to the level of action.

Data teams have often stopped at "providing information" in the past, and it's not entirely a capability issue. Contracts are accepted that way, and organizations are divided that way. If analysts proactively push into business execution, they risk being accused of overstepping; if business results are poor, someone will turn around and ask what value the data project created.

This awkward position later falls to the FDE.

Actionable Doesn't Mean Automatically Acting

"Actionable insights" was once a very popular phrase in BI.

It was a step beyond "a few more reports," but it still left one thing vague: who acts?

In the age of AI agents, technology finally seems able to supply the hands and feet. When the system detects an inventory anomaly, it can generate handling suggestions; when it detects customer churn risk, it can draft follow-up scripts; when it finds inconsistent orders, it can prepare tickets.

But once it actually starts to act, all the old problems come back—and sharper.

Who allows it to stop purchasing? Who approves it sending messages to customers? If two metrics conflict, whose side does it take? If an action causes losses, is the responsibility on the model, the engineer, the business owner, or the approver?

In the past, a report not leading to action was a regret. Now, an AI agent acting rashly could be an incident.

So moving from BI to FDE is not as simple as adding a button to a report. The company has to take the judgment, authorization, and accountability previously hidden inside people and the organization, and rewrite them explicitly.

And this step immediately runs into another old question:

Is what the customer says they need really the work they do every day?
