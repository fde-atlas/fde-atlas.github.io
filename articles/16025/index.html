The Weekly Client Check-In: A Template for Forward Deployed Engineers

HCL GUVI Blog · 2026-09-28 · 7 min read

Summary

This HCL GUVI blog post is a practical guide for forward deployed engineers on running weekly client check-ins as decision-making meetings rather than status calls. It covers preparation, opening summaries, outcome-focused progress reporting, distinguishing blockers from risks, targeted feedback questions, short product demonstrations, and closing with owned action items. It includes a reusable nine-part agenda template and a real-world example where feedback redirected work from new features to fixing outdated documentation.

Key points

  • Every check-in should answer: are we solving the right problem, is progress on track, what comes next.
  • Report completed capabilities and their customer impact, not individual engineering tasks.
  • Separate current blockers from future risks so customers know what needs immediate action.
  • Ask targeted feedback questions instead of a generic request for comments.
  • End each meeting with actions, owners, deadlines, and written notes for continuity.

Done well, the meeting becomes a decision-making tool rather than another status call.

Quick Answer

A strong weekly client check-in should cover what changed, what was completed, what is currently blocked, what the customer needs to review, and what happens next. Keep the discussion focused on customer outcomes rather than a long list of engineering tasks. Document decisions and assign owners before ending the meeting.

Why Should FDEs Run Weekly Client Check-Ins?

1. What Does the Meeting Actually Achieve?

FDE projects can change quickly. Requirements evolve, integrations behave differently in production, users provide new feedback, and technical constraints can appear unexpectedly.

A weekly check-in creates a predictable point for reviewing those changes and deciding what needs attention next.

2. Is It Just a Status Meeting?

No. A useful check-in should answer three questions: Are we solving the right problem? Is the implementation progressing as expected? What needs to happen next?

That makes the conversation useful for both technical and nontechnical stakeholders.

What Should You Do Before the Meeting?

1. What Should the FDE Prepare?

Review the previous week’s action items, recent deployments, open technical issues, customer feedback, and upcoming milestones.

Prepare a short list of decisions or questions that require customer input. Avoid using meeting time to discover basic project information that could have been reviewed beforehand.

2. What Should You Bring?

Keep a simple project update containing:

  • Completed work
  • Current work
  • Blockers
  • Risks
  • Customer decisions needed
  • Next milestones

The exact format can change depending on the project.

How Should the Weekly Check-In Start?

What Should You Say First?

Begin with a concise summary of the project’s current state. Mention the main achievement since the previous meeting and the most important issue requiring attention.

For example: “This week, we completed the API integration and tested the primary workflow. The remaining blocker is access to the production dataset.”

This immediately gives the customer context.

What Progress Should You Discuss?

Should You Explain Every Engineering Task?

No. Focus on work that affects the customer outcome.

Instead of listing every code change, explain what capability was completed, what workflow it affects, and whether the result is ready for customer validation.

For example, “The document retrieval flow is now connected to the customer’s knowledge base” is more useful than listing individual implementation tickets.

How Should You Discuss Blockers?

1. What Makes a Good Blocker Update?

Explain three things: what is blocked, why it is blocked, and what is needed to move forward.

If the customer needs to provide credentials, approve a workflow, or confirm data requirements, state that clearly. Do not hide blockers until they become urgent.

2. What About Technical Risks?

Separate current blockers from potential risks. A blocker is preventing progress now, while a risk could create a problem later.

This distinction helps customers understand where immediate action is required.

How Should You Collect Customer Feedback?

What Questions Should You Ask?

Ask targeted questions instead of simply saying, “Any feedback?”

Useful questions include:

  • Does the current workflow match how your team actually works?
  • Is anything producing unexpected results?
  • Which part of the solution is most useful so far?
  • What still feels difficult or inefficient?
  • Has anything changed in your requirements?

These questions can uncover problems that technical monitoring cannot show.

How Should You Review the Product or Deployment?

What Should the Customer See?

If there is a working prototype, new feature, dashboard, integration, or AI workflow, demonstrate the relevant change.

Keep the demonstration tied to the customer’s workflow. Explain what changed, how it should be used, and what feedback you need.

A short working demonstration can create more useful discussion than a long slide presentation.

How Should You Discuss Next Steps?

What Should Be Decided Before Ending?

Review the next milestone and identify the actions required to reach it.

For every important action, clarify:

  • What needs to happen?
  • Who owns it?
  • When is it due?
  • Does another task depend on it?

This prevents vague follow-ups such as “we will look into it.”

What Should the FDE Document?

Why Are Meeting Notes Important?

  • Document decisions, open questions, action items, owners, and deadlines.
  • Meeting notes also provide continuity when multiple engineers, customer stakeholders, or product teams become involved.
  • A short written summary can prevent disagreements later about what was discussed or promised.

💡 Did You Know?

The weekly check-in can also become an early-warning system. Repeated customer concerns, missed milestones, or declining engagement may reveal project risks before they become major deployment problems.

Real-World Example

Imagine an FDE working with a customer on an AI support assistant. During the weekly check-in, the FDE reports that the core integration is complete but customer testing shows inconsistent answers.

Instead of immediately promising another feature, the FDE asks which questions fail most often. The customer identifies outdated documentation as the main issue, so the next sprint focuses on improving the knowledge source rather than adding unnecessary functionality.

What Is the Weekly Client Check-In Template?

A Simple Agenda FDEs Can Reuse

1. Project status: What changed since the last meeting?

2. Customer outcome: Are we still solving the intended problem?

3. Completed work: What meaningful capabilities were delivered?

4. Current work: What is being built or tested now?

5. Blockers and risks: What could delay progress?

6. Customer feedback: What is working, and what needs improvement?

7. Decisions: What requires customer confirmation?

8. Next steps: What happens before the next check-in?

9. Action items: Who owns each task and when is it due?

How Can FDEs Make Check-Ins More Effective?

1. Keep the Meeting Outcome-Focused

Do not let the conversation become a detailed walkthrough of internal engineering activity. Customers generally need to understand progress, impact, risks, and decisions.

2. Create a Consistent Rhythm

Using the same basic structure each week makes meetings easier to prepare for and easier for customers to follow. The discussion can change, but the core questions remain consistent.

Conclusion

A weekly client check-in gives FDEs a structured way to maintain alignment throughout a customer engagement. The strongest meetings focus on outcomes, meaningful progress, blockers, feedback, decisions, and clearly owned next steps.

The meeting should not simply report what the engineering team did. It should help both sides understand where the project stands, whether the solution is working, and what needs to happen next. Consistency turns the weekly check-in into a practical tool for successful customer delivery.

FAQs

1. What should an FDE discuss during a weekly client check-in?

Discuss progress, customer outcomes, blockers, risks, feedback, decisions, upcoming milestones, and action items.

2. How long should a weekly client check-in be?

Keep it focused on the project’s complexity and current needs. A concise meeting is preferable when there are few decisions or blockers.

3. Should FDEs discuss technical details with customers?

Yes, when those details affect the customer’s workflow, decisions, risks, or deployment. Avoid unnecessary implementation-level detail.

4. What should an FDE do if there are no major updates?

Use the meeting to validate priorities, collect feedback, review risks, and confirm upcoming work instead of creating artificial updates.

5. How should FDEs handle customer feedback?

Clarify the issue, understand its impact, determine whether it changes requirements, and agree on an appropriate next step.

6. Why should every action have an owner?

An owner makes responsibility explicit and reduces the chance that important follow-up work gets forgotten.

7. What makes a weekly client check-in successful?

The customer and FDE leave with shared understanding of progress, current risks, decisions, and the specific actions required before the next check-in.
