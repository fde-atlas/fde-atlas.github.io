How to Study Full Stack Open: An FDE Learning Path

Original title: Full Stack Open 怎么学：FDE 学习路线

FDE Times · 2026-10-09 · 4 min read

Summary

This article explains how to use the University of Helsinki's free Full Stack Open course as a learning path for forward deployed engineers. The course spans 14 parts, from React and Node/Express to TypeScript, CI/CD, and containers, mirroring the layers FDEs encounter in client systems. The author recommends completing exercises in order, skipping GraphQL and React Native until needed, and showcasing a deployed project with tests and CI as resume evidence. It also notes the text-only format suits FDE work and warns about Creative Commons licensing for commercial use.

Key points

  • Full Stack Open is free, with 14 parts covering React through CI/CD and containers.
  • Most content is by Matti Luukkainen; TypeScript, CI/CD, and container parts come from outside contributors.
  • Text-and-exercise format with no videos; not suitable for complete beginners.
  • For FDEs, the value is understanding how data flows through each layer of client systems.
  • Use a deployed repo with tests, CI, and a Dockerfile as primary resume evidence.

Key Takeaways

When the course launched in March 2019, Matti Luukkainen of the University of Helsinki gave a very pragmatic reason: "full stack" was the most frequently mentioned term in job ads. He also said that people who complete the course can do the work of a junior developer.

Seven years on, Full Stack Open is still free. The course now has 14 parts, from Part 0 to Part 13.

For Forward Deployed Engineers (FDEs), the reasons for taking this course differ slightly from those of people hunting for junior roles. On client sites, you rarely get to choose the technology yourself.

You need to be able to read their React frontend, call their Node API, modify a TypeScript file, and figure out why their CI pipeline went red. This course teaches all of these layers, and teaches them in the order they stack on top of each other.

Who built the course, and what does it teach?

The course's full name is Full Stack Open, with the subtitle "Deep Dive Into Modern Web Development," and its official website is fullstackopen.com. The course was jointly created by the University of Helsinki and Houston Inc. In June 2019, the university released the English version, aiming to bring Finnish university education to international learners for free, with Luukkainen serving as coordinator.

Parts 0 through 8 and Part 13 were written by Luukkainen. The remaining parts were contributed by people outside the university: Part 9 (TypeScript) came from developers at Terveystalo, Part 11 (CI/CD) from a developer at Smartly, and Part 12 (containers) from Jami Kousa.

The core of the course is building single-page applications with React, with Node/Express, testing, GraphQL, React Native, and relational databases built around it.

According to the About page, the course is free, offers certificates, and can earn European credits (ECTS) from the University of Helsinki. All the materials are hosted in a public GitHub repository with thousands of forks.

What FDE skills does it build?

Imagine your first week on a client site. The client wants an internal dashboard that pulls data from an existing API, with a demo for management on Friday. You need to build a React app, write a thin Node service to collect and clean the data, add TypeScript types to make sure schema changes don't break things, and finally package it into a container so the client's IT team can run it.

Every task on that list maps to some part of Full Stack Open. What you really practice isn't a particular framework, but understanding how data flows through each layer and where errors surface when one layer breaks. That's exactly the ability you need when debugging someone else's system in the middle of the night.

Who is it for, and in what order should you take it?

This course is not for beginners. The About page requires solid programming skills and a grasp of web and database fundamentals. In its 2026 roundup of full-stack courses, Scrimba recommends it to frontend developers looking to move into full-stack work, but explicitly says people who can't yet program should not treat it as their first course.

Scrimba also points out the cost: the course is mostly text and exercises, with no videos to fall back on. A learner on the freeCodeCamp forum who completed most of the content in 2020 gave it high marks, but still recommended having some React experience first, since the text is quite lengthy.

If you're a backend engineer looking to transition into an FDE role, start by working through the first few parts in order—React, Node/Express, and testing. Don't skip the exercises; it's the exercises that force you to truly understand. Then go straight to Parts 9, 11, and 12.

All three of these parts come from outside contributors. As soon as your code has to run on someone else's infrastructure, types, pipelines, and containers are layers you can't avoid. GraphQL and React Native can wait until a specific client needs them.

Three things worth remembering

First is the course's origin. At launch, Luukkainen tied it to job ads: full stack was the most-mentioned term. You can do the same when reading FDE job descriptions: list the technologies mentioned, mark the ones you already have evidence for, and then learn specifically to fill the gaps.

Second, the text-only format may sound like a drawback, but it actually fits the job well. FDEs spend their days reading documentation, READMEs, and other people's code. Spending a few weeks grinding through a lengthy, dry set of materials is exactly the training this skill needs.

Finally, the licensing. The materials are under a Creative Commons license and may not be used commercially without permission. If you plan to use this content to train a client's team in a paid engagement, apply for permission first, or write your own materials.

Turning the exercises into a resume

A 2019 article on I-Programmer noted that at the time, credits, exercise submissions, and the final exam were only open to University of Helsinki students, while outsiders took the unsupervised public version. Regardless of how much weight the certificate carries today, you should treat the code repository as your primary evidence, because recruiters can open it and verify it themselves.

Put a complete, deployed application on your resume, with tests, a passing CI pipeline, and a Dockerfile, plus a one-sentence explanation of how you made data flow through each layer. That's almost a snapshot of an FDE's week.

Luukkainen says people who finish the course can work as junior developers. For FDEs, the payoff is being able to walk into a client's codebase without getting lost.
