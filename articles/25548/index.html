Paden Gayle, who is writing The Forward Deployed Engineer for O'Reilly, published a guide to what the role is, what hiring checks for, and six steps to get in.

Why it mattersA team hiring forward deployed engineers gets a four-part job description and a scoping interview to copy, and an engineer gets a short list of gaps to close first.

Engineers keep seeing job posts for forward deployed engineers (FDEs) and cannot tell which part of their experience the role tests. Paden Gayle, who is writing The Forward Deployed Engineer for O'Reilly, has published a long guide on LinkedIn that answers it: what the job is, the three questions he says come up in hiring, and a six-step plan for getting in.

By his own account, Gayle worked at Google, Bloomberg, Scale AI and Accenture before he moved into the role. O'Reilly's site lists him as the instructor of a one-hour live event on the role.

What the job is, in his words

Gayle defines an FDE as a software engineer placed inside a customer's company to make a product work there. The FDE often owns the outcome, the deadline and the delivery relationship, while the data, the systems, the security review and the people belong to the customer. He links the title to Palantir and says AI companies have taken it up to make their products work on customers' real data, permissions and older systems.

He splits a typical week into four parts. Discovery and scoping, where he writes the statement of work and the success criteria. Building: agents, integrations, retrieval over the customer's documents and evals the customer will believe. Shipping and checking the result through the customer's security review and change process, with their numbers. And reporting what he learned back to his own company's engineering and product teams. His test for telling the role apart from software engineering and solutions engineering is how much of the customer's outcome you own, and for how long.

Three questions, and the smallest useful project

The three questions Gayle says keep coming up in hiring: can you write production code in Python or TypeScript, can you run a discovery conversation with a customer and refuse a wrong scope, and have you shipped something with a language model that a real person used. He says many of these roles do not ask for a computer science degree, and that he has a business degree.

His six steps run from choosing the kind of employer, through listing the customer work you have already done, to closing your largest gap with the smallest finished project. His two examples are adding evals to something you already built, and wrapping an internal tool in an MCP server and getting two people to use it.

The round where strong engineers struggle

Gayle describes four interview rounds: a recruiter screen, a technical round, a take-home where the README counts as part of the work, and a scoping round with a mock customer. In the scoping round, he writes, strong engineers struggle because they propose an architecture before they ask what the customer is trying to do. His advice is to ask why, ask it again, and then propose the smallest thing that solves the problem.

He gives no pay figure. He presents his one timeline, two to three months for engineers with two or more years of experience, as an observation of people he has worked with.

For a team that hires FDEs, or that sends its own engineers into customer environments, the four-part week reads as a job description and the scoping round as an interview worth copying. An engineer thinking about the move can start with Gayle's cheapest step: one small, finished project that two real people use.
