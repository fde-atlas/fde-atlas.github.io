Evaluating Business AI: Design | How to Build Accuracy and Acceptance Criteria

Original title: 業務AIの評価設計｜精度と合格基準の作り方

日本Fde協会 · 2026-09-26 · 7 min read

Summary

This article by the Japan FDE Association addresses evaluation design for business AI, arguing that many deployments stall because teams cannot judge whether outputs are usable. It distinguishes tasks with verifiable answers (measured via accuracy, precision, recall, with per-field weighting) from open-ended tasks (scored by humans across accuracy, appropriateness, and safety dimensions). Pass thresholds should be derived from business risk—how noticeable and costly errors are—rather than a universal percentage. The article also stresses separating scoring and approval roles, and keeping records of test cases, outputs, judges, and dates for auditability and re-evaluation after updates.

Key points

  • Evaluation design gaps are a common reason business AI projects stall.
  • Verifiable tasks use accuracy metrics; open-ended tasks need multi-dimension human scoring.
  • Pass thresholds must be back-calculated from error impact, not fixed percentages.
  • FDEs translate business requirements into measurable evaluation criteria.
  • Evaluation records (cases, judges, dates) enable audits and smooth handovers.

You've introduced AI into your operations, but you can't judge whether it's actually usable, and the project stalls. One of the most common blockers we at the Japan FDE Association see in the field is the absence of AI evaluation design. An FDE (Forward Deployed Engineer—an engineer who embeds themselves at the customer's site and solves problems with technology) doesn't just build something that works; they also set up a mechanism to measure whether its output meets business standards. By reading this article, you'll be able to design for yourself what accuracy metrics to measure for your company's business AI and what level counts as passing, and you'll also get a starting point for keeping records of who judged it and when. Rather than covering the entire implementation process, let's dig deep into this one point: evaluation.

AI Evaluation Design Starts with "What to Measure"

Even if we simply say "accuracy," what should be measured differs completely depending on the business. The first thing to grasp is dividing AI outputs into "tasks that can be checked against a correct answer" and "tasks where there is no single correct answer." If you mix these together, the evaluation criteria themselves break down.

Metrics for Verifiable Tasks

For tasks with a defined correct answer, such as extracting fields from invoices or categorizing inquiries, you can use metrics like accuracy, precision, and recall. For example, in back-office document processing, extracted amounts and dates can be checked against the original documents, so field-level accuracy becomes the primary metric. What matters here is not looking only at overall accuracy. You need to bring weighting per field into the evaluation—for instance, errors in the amount field have a large business impact.

Metrics for Tasks Without a Defined Correct Answer

For tasks where output isn't a single fixed answer, such as generating customer support responses or summaries, the notion of accuracy doesn't fit. In this case, break down the perspectives—does it contain anything factually incorrect (accuracy), does it answer the question (appropriateness), does it contain expressions that violate internal policies (safety)—and create an evaluation sheet where humans score each one. When we did this design for a SaaS company's inquiry handling, we initially scored with a simple "good/bad" binary, but judgments varied widely between evaluators and it didn't work. The moment we split the perspectives into three levels each, scoring stabilized.

Derive Acceptance Criteria Backward from Business Risk

Typical evaluation guides tend to stop at how to calculate metrics. But what's really difficult in the field is "what percentage counts as passing." We don't set this line based on accuracy scores alone. We derive it backward from what happens when a mistake occurs.

Change the Line Based on the Impact of Errors

Even at the same 90% accuracy, the meaning is completely different between generating drafts of internal memos and recording amounts sent to customers. In the former, humans can easily fix minor errors; in the latter, a single error damages trust. So classify errors along two axes—"can humans easily notice it?" and "how much damage results if it slips through unnoticed?"—and raise the acceptance line for tasks with more errors that cause large damage and are hard to notice. Since the numbers differ by business, there is no universal guideline for what accuracy percentage is correct.

Translate Requirements into the Language of Evaluation Criteria

This is where FDEs show their skill. Business requirements gathered through field interviews can't be used for evaluation as-is. A request like "we want invoice processing to be easier" is translated into a measurable criterion such as "judge the three fields—amount, date, and counterparty name—against the original, targeting zero amount errors and no more than one date error per month." When Representative Director Maekawa supported shipping and receiving document processing for a manufacturing client, the first thing he did was work with the field staff to identify "which fields, if wrong, would halt shipping." By making the fields that halt operations the highest-priority evaluation targets, the acceptance criteria gain persuasiveness. If you skip this step of converting requirements into criteria, you end up with AI that scores high on accuracy but goes unused in the field.

Design Evaluation Including "How to Keep Records"

Evaluation isn't a one-time task. Only when you record who judged acceptance, when, and against which criteria, and keep the process running within operations, does it become a state that can withstand governance and audits. This is an area that most AI evaluation guides on the market barely touch, and it's also the point most valued in corporate settings.

Place Judgment Within Job Responsibilities

What's easily overlooked in evaluation is "who decides acceptance." If the person who built the AI declares it passing themselves, objectivity isn't guaranteed. Separating judgment between the developer and the business owner turns the approval flow directly into a form of governance. Record who judged and who approved. This idea of separation of duties is a basic principle of internal controls regardless of whether AI is involved, and the same thinking is effective for AI evaluation.

Keep Evaluation Records as Evidence

What should be kept: the data used for evaluation (test cases), the outputs at that time, the scoring results, the judgment date, and the judge. Keeping these along with version information lets you explain later "why it was judged passing at this point." When you update the AI or prompts, re-evaluate and accumulate records in a form that shows the differences from the previous round. Since his days handling systems at financial institutions, Representative Director Maekawa has treated documenting the reasons for judgments as fundamental to keeping operations running. For AI evaluation too, whether this evidence exists makes a huge difference in how smoothly handovers go when the person in charge changes. Note that what's described here is the general concept of internal controls and does not constitute a guarantee of compliance with any specific laws or regulations.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q. Is measuring accuracy scores alone insufficient for AI evaluation design? Measuring accuracy scores is only the starting point. Only when you establish a system that determines the passing percentage from business risk, records who judged and when, and re-evaluates with every update does the evaluation withstand real operations. If you stop at calculating scores, you tend to end up with AI that scores high on accuracy but goes unused in the field.

Q. What percentage accuracy should we aim for as the passing threshold? There is no universal benchmark. Even with the same accuracy rate, the meaning changes depending on whether humans can notice errors, and how much damage would result if an error slips through unnoticed. We recommend deciding the threshold individually for each task, working backward from the principle that the greater the impact of errors and the harder they are to notice, the higher the standard should be.

Q. Can the person who created the evaluation also make the pass/fail judgment? From an objectivity standpoint, we recommend separating these roles. If scoring and approval are divided between the development lead and the business owner, the approval flow itself becomes a form of governance, and it also becomes easier to explain the reasoning behind decisions later. Recording the judge, judgment date, and results also makes handovers smoother.
