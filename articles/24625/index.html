FDE English Client Meeting: Phrases for Clarifying Requirements and Pushing Back on Scope Creep

Original title: FDE 英语客户会议：澄清需求和拒绝加需求的句子

FDE Times · 2026-10-25 · 5 min read

Summary

This guide targets non-native English-speaking forward deployed engineers, arguing the real difficulty in client meetings is not grammar but lacking ready-made sentences for three moments: clarifying vague requirements, declining scope creep, and answering "when will it be done?" Drawing on advice from PostHog, FDE Academy, Talaera, and Babel Group, it provides sample phrases and a practice dialogue. Key techniques include restating the client's point and offering two concrete interpretations to choose from, using conditional agreement to protect scope, and deferring commitments with a specific follow-up time. It closes with common mistakes and tips for building a personal phrasebook and practicing early speaking in meetings.

Key points

  • The hard part is three moments, not grammar.
  • Clarify by restating and offering two concrete interpretations.
  • Decline extra requests via conditional agreement to protect scope.
  • Defer dates and follow up on time; reliability beats confidence.
  • Build a short phrasebook and speak early in meetings.

Key Takeaways

  • The difficulty rarely lies in grammar; it lies in not having ready-made sentences for the three moments of clarifying, declining, and deferring commitments.
  • When a client uses a vague word, offer two concrete interpretations for them to choose from, and ask which decision the answer will affect.
  • Clients remember people who keep their word longer than people who speak confidently, so "let me confirm and get back to you" is a powerful sentence, not a sign of weakness.

Ten minutes into a meeting, the client's operations lead says: "We need the dashboard to be more flexible." Everyone looks at you. You understood every word, yet you have no idea what they actually want.

For many engineers who didn't grow up speaking English, the instinctive reaction is to nod and say "Sure, we can do that." It sounds polite, but the cost could be two weeks spent building the wrong thing, or a deadline your team never agreed to.

The difficulty rarely lies in grammar; it lies in not having ready-made sentences for the three moments when you're most likely to be caught off guard: when you need to clarify requirements, when you need to say no, and when someone asks "when will it be done." Below are sample sentences for these three scenarios, along with a dialogue you can practice with.

You Don't Need to Sound Polished—You Need to Ask the Right Questions

PostHog defines one of the core competencies of a Forward Deployed Engineer (FDE) as: reading vague or overly specific requirements and finding the real problem behind them. The company's FDE handbook also requires FDEs to solve the customer's actual problem, not the one that's easiest to write up as a ticket.

So you don't need to sound polished; you need to ask the right questions. FDE Academy recommends that when taking on a new client, you ask more "why" and less "what" before writing the first line of production code. PostHog offers a very useful question: before starting work, ask which decision the answer will affect.

In English, it's simple: "Before we dig in, what decision will this help you make?" No advanced vocabulary needed—just say it as smoothly as introducing yourself.

How to Follow Up Without Seeming Like You Weren't Listening

Business English platform Talaera outlines four steps for requesting clarification: soften your tone first, acknowledge that you didn't fully understand, restate what you heard, then ask a specific question. The third step is key. When you restate the client's point, what they see is that you're listening carefully, not that you missed something.

On softening your tone, Babel Group suggests using phrases like "Can I just ask you…?" when interjecting, and "would like" instead of "I want" when making suggestions. Without these buffers, your questions can easily sound like an interrogation.

The technique most worth practicing is offering two interpretations. Talaera's example: when a client says "flexible timeline," ask whether they mean pushing back the deadline or accepting scope changes. By offering two concrete options, the client has to make a choice—and that choice is the real requirement.

A Sample Meeting, Three Sentences to the Rescue

Imagine the meeting from the beginning of this article continuing. The client is a delivery company, and you're building a dashboard to track late orders. The dialogue below can be practiced directly, with the technique used marked at each turn.

Client: "We need the dashboard to be more flexible."

You (softening tone, acknowledging you didn't fully understand): "Can I just ask you something? I'm not sure I've got it fully."

You (restating, offering two interpretations): "When you say flexible, do you mean filtering by more fields, like region or driver, or changing the layout per team?"

Client: "Filtering. Our regional leads can't see their own late orders."

You (confirming understanding): "So, to confirm: filters by region for the regional leads, not a new layout?"

Client: "Exactly."

You (asking about the decision): "Got it. What will the regional leads decide once they see that?"

After a few rounds, "flexible" has become a verifiable requirement: filtering late orders by region for regional leads, confirmed by the client in their own words. That's work that can be estimated and delivered, replacing an adjective no one could measure.

Then the client adds: "Should we also send them a daily email report?" This is the second scenario, and where many FDEs stumble.

Saying No Without Closing the Door

PostHog puts it bluntly in its handbook: push back on every "should we also…" type of suggestion. The same handbook also stipulates that work items that double in size count as a new quote, not an informal add-on. This principle gives you footing: you're declining the client not because you want to do less work, but because the scope was already agreed upon.

FDE Academy recommends replacing unconditional agreement with "conditional yes": agree first, then explain how the work will affect the timeline. In the sample meeting, you could say: "We can, and it'd add work beyond what we scoped. Happy to scope it separately so the filters still ship on time."

Note the structure: instead of flatly saying "no," you first acknowledge the value of the proposal, then explain the cost, and finally offer a next step. The client still hears something positive, and the scope is protected.

"Let Me Confirm" Is a Powerful Sentence

The third scenario comes near the end of a meeting. FDE Academy points out a common mistake new FDEs make: committing to deadlines or features in a meeting before asking the team whether it's feasible. The way to avoid this is to defer the commitment while stating when you'll respond, as in the final exchange of the sample meeting:

Client: "So can we have it by Friday?"

You: "I'd like to give you a date I can keep. Let me check with my team and confirm by tomorrow noon."

This tactic only works if you actually reply on time. FDE Academy's reasoning: clients remember people who keep their word far longer than people who spoke confidently in the meeting room.

How to Practice Until It Becomes Second Nature

Start by preparing a short phrasebook organized by the three scenarios: clarifying, declining, and deferring commitments. For each category, you only need three or four sentences you can actually say out loud—no need to copy a hundred. Read them aloud repeatedly until you can say them without mentally translating.

Then practice in real meetings, starting with speaking up early. Babel Group recommends that non-native English speakers speak at the very beginning of a meeting, without worrying about their English not being perfect. The longer you stay silent, the harder it becomes to say the first sentence.

This skill should also go on your resume. Instead of writing a vague "strong communication skills," describe a time you turned an ambiguous requirement into concrete work, or pushed back on extra requests and held the line on scope. If the hiring process includes a customer role-play, the dialogue above is ready-made practice material.

Common mistakes that derail meetings

The costliest mistake is nodding when you haven't understood, because you'll end up building exactly what the customer doesn't need. Close behind is the generic "Can you repeat that?"—the customer will simply repeat the same vague sentence word for word. The right approach is to paraphrase their point and offer two interpretations.

The third mistake is translating directly from your native language, producing sentences like "I want you to send the data" that sound far harsher than you intended. The last two mistakes are saying "no" without offering a next step, and committing to a date on the spot. The previous section has alternative phrasings for both—provided you've practiced them.

Running meetings in English doesn't require you to sound like a native speaker. It requires you to turn a vague "flexible" into actionable work, hold the line on scope, and follow up by the time you promised.
