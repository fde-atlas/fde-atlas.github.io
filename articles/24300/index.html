How an Anti-Money Laundering (AML) Agent Passes Compliance Approval

Original title: 反洗钱 AML Agent 如何通过合规审批

FDE Times · 2026-11-06 · 5 min read

Summary

This guide explains how forward deployed engineers can get an AML alert-review agent through bank compliance approval, arguing that go-live is decided by compliance, not engineering, and that evidence must be prepared from week one. Drawing on the Wolfsberg Group's August 2025 statement, the Co-Investigator AI research, FinCEN's October 2025 SAR FAQs, and the 2026 SR 26-2 model risk guidance, it maps each Wolfsberg pillar to concrete deliverables, shows how to run a pilot on closed alerts and analyze missed escalations with numbers, and lists decisions—narrative templates, logging scope, applicable model policy—that belong to the institution, not the engineer.

Key points

  • The agent only suggests; investigators must make and sign close/escalate decisions.
  • Compliance needs validation evidence, concrete miss-rate numbers, and per-alert explanations.
  • Pilot on already-closed alerts and walk through each missed escalation case, not aggregate accuracy.
  • Narrative templates, logging fields, and governing model policy are the client's decisions—ask before coding.
  • Hold a joint compliance/model-governance meeting in week one and get three written confirmations.

Key Takeaways

  • An AML alert review agent only makes recommendations; decisions and sign-offs remain with the investigator.
  • Compliance needs three things: validation evidence, concrete numbers on missed-detection risk, and an explanation for every recommendation.
  • What to log, which narrative template to use, and which model policy governs the agent are all decided by the institution. Ask before writing code.

You've just been tasked with integrating an agent into a bank's anti-money-laundering (AML) alert queue. The agent reads transaction history, summarizes know-your-customer (KYC) profiles, recommends closing or escalating each alert, and drafts the narrative section of suspicious activity reports (SARs). The demo goes smoothly, and the client's engineering team is thrilled.

But go-live timing isn't decided by the engineering team; it's decided by compliance. If your first meeting with compliance happens in week six, you'll likely redo most of the architecture.

Projects like this are increasingly common. The Wolfsberg Group is an association of major banks that sets standards in the financial crime space. In an August 2025 statement, it noted that financial institutions are progressively incorporating automation, AI, and ML into their suspicious activity monitoring strategies.

So for a Forward Deployed Engineer (FDE), the scarce skill isn't calling an LLM; it's getting the agent through compliance approval.

What does compliance actually need to see?

Wolfsberg's responsible innovation framework has three pillars: validation when switching to new tools; balancing model risk against financial crime risk; and explainability, meaning an outside party can clearly see how much the system covers and how well it performs.

From an engineer's perspective, each pillar is a deliverable, not a slogan.

The second pillar is the most commonly misunderstood. Model risk is the chance the agent makes an error. Financial crime risk is the chance money laundering slips through, either because the agent judged wrong or because the old process was too slow to keep up.

Compliance doesn't require the agent to be flawless. They want to see the two risks compared side by side, with concrete numbers.

Validation during transitionWhat evidence is needed before running in parallel with the existing process?A pilot based on closed alerts, with acceptance thresholds
Balancing the two risksWhat is the maximum acceptable missed-detection rate, and what is it traded for?A table comparing time saved against the number of missed cases

Walking through a pilot with numbers

Suppose you propose having the agent process 200 alerts that investigators already closed last quarter. The outcomes are all known, making this the best eval set you have. Suppose the agent recommends closing 150 and escalating 50.

The engineering team will focus on those 150: three-quarters of the queue can be processed faster. Compliance will immediately ask a different question: of the 150 the agent wants to close, how many did investigators originally escalate?

If the answer is 6, those 6 cases become the focus of the meeting. You need to open each one, explain why the agent missed it, and propose a fix.

That's balancing risk with data. Don't say "the agent is 97% accurate"; instead say: the agent shortens handling time for this type of alert, at the cost of 6 missed cases, which is not yet acceptable at the current threshold, and here's the remediation plan. Faced with that framing, compliance can make a decision.

The same example also shows why the agent shouldn't close alerts on its own. Co-Investigator AI is a 2025 study on an agent for drafting AML narratives; its authors stress that human investigators must remain firmly in the loop. In your design, let the agent write "recommendations" and let a human press the "decision" button.

What gets logged, and how, and who decides?

There are three questions engineers often decide themselves but that should be decided by the client institution. The first is the narrative template. The Co-Investigator AI study notes that SAR narrative quality varies widely across financial institutions. The agent can help standardize, but which template to standardize on must be determined by compliance before you write your prompts.

The second is logging. The same study leans toward full audit logs for every agent. But how much to record depends on what the rules require.

On October 9, 2025, FinCEN and US banking regulators released a set of SAR FAQs to reduce unnecessary compliance work for financial institutions. According to an analysis by the law firm Orrick, the FAQs make clear that the law does not require documenting decisions not to file a SAR, nor re-reviewing after a SAR has been filed.

So what the agent logs is the client's policy choice, not yours. Have compliance determine the required-field list first, then have the agent strictly log that list for every recommendation, rather than defaulting to "log everything" or "log nothing."

The third is which policy applies to the agent. On April 17, 2026, the Fed, FDIC, and OCC issued SR 26-2 on model risk management, replacing the long-standing SR 11-7.

According to Baker Tilly, the new guidance does not cover generative AI; AI will be addressed through separate guidance or rules. So ask the client's model governance team directly: under which internal policy will this LLM agent be approved?

In what order should you do things?

In week one, request a joint meeting with the compliance and model governance teams, bringing the three-pillar table from earlier. The goal is to leave with written confirmation of three things: the agent's scope of responsibility (recommend only, no decisions), the acceptable missed-detection threshold, and the model policy the agent falls under.

Next, request the approved narrative template and the list of data fields that must be logged. Only after you have both should you write prompts and the log schema. Then run the pilot on closed alerts, and bring every missed case to the second meeting, not just an aggregate number.

The four most common mistakes all stem from the same habit: engineers making decisions that should belong to compliance.

  • Building first, then seeking approval.
  • Letting the agent close alerts itself to "save time."
  • Deciding the logging level without checking policy.
  • Assuming existing model validation processes automatically apply to LLMs.

On the career side, if you're targeting FDE roles in finance, master the concepts covered in this article: AML, KYC, SAR, and model risk management.

If you've taken a system through a control function's approval process, write it on your resume with results: you designed the pilot, the control function set a certain threshold, and the system was approved under certain conditions.

When discussing this experience, start with which missed cases you opened and how you handled them, because that shows you understand risk, not just prompt writing.
