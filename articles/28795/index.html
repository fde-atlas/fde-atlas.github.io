FDEs as Product Managers: Medplum's Approach and Its Bottlenecks

Original title: FDE 兼任产品经理：Medplum 的做法与瓶颈

FDE Times · 2026-10-08 · 3 min read

Summary

This article examines Medplum's decision to operate without product managers, having forward deployed engineers (FDEs) triage all feature requests and bugs and serve as the main input to the roadmap. COO Rahul Agarwal admits the weekly rotation system breaks down once the FDE team reaches roughly ten to twenty people, because each ticket carries too much context the presenter lacks. He suggests monthly or quarterly rotations tied to customer ownership, while arguing the deeper problem—preserving context as knowledge passes through many hands—exists at any scale. The piece includes a concrete example of writing tickets that engineers can act on without follow-up calls, plus interview tips for spotting PM duties in FDE roles.

  • Medplum has no PMs; FDEs triage all requests and drive the roadmap.
  • Weekly rotating presenters fail at 10-20 FDEs due to lost context.
  • Agarwal recommends longer rotations tied to customer ownership.
  • The core issue is preserving context across many handoffs.
  • Well-written tickets should preempt engineers' follow-up questions.

Key takeaways

  • Medplum has no PMs: FDEs triage all requests and are the primary input to the product roadmap.
  • As the team scaled, every ticket carried too much context and the process started to break down.
  • Agarwal would eliminate the weekly rotating PM role, but the context handoff problem exists at any scale.

Medplum has no product managers. Co-founder and Chief Operating Officer (COO) Rahul Agarwal says outright that he has never believed a PM should be a full-time role with its own hiring profile.

At Medplum, the PM's job has been handed to Forward Deployed Engineers (FDEs). But Agarwal himself admits that this process "starts to break down at scale."

For developers who want to become FDEs, this reveals a part of the job rarely spelled out in job descriptions: you don't just do deployments for customers, you also carry the customer's voice back into the product.

What does it look like when FDEs act as PMs?

Medplum's blog states that FDEs are the primary input to the product roadmap. The FDE team triages every issue, feature request, and bug from customers and the community — the entire intake process that at other companies is owned by a PM.

The company wants FDEs to help decide what the product becomes, not just help customers use the existing product well.

The mechanism Agarwal describes is fairly simple: each week, requests are gathered into a single spreadsheet, FDEs negotiate a priority ranking among themselves, and one rotating FDE reports to the engineering team.

The problem is context, not headcount

Agarwal's explanation for the breakdown is brief: when the team has ten or twenty FDEs, every ticket drags along too much context. That number, however, shouldn't be read as a hard limit. He uses it to illustrate overload, and says he would change the design rather than treat it as a fixed ceiling.

Imagine the FDE on rotation this week is presenting a ticket that says "needs date filtering." The ticket was written by another colleague after three phone calls with a customer, and the presenter has never interacted with that customer. The engineering team asks: why does the customer need it? How are they coping now? Does anyone else need it? The presenter can't answer.

The following week, someone else is presenting.

Looking back, Agarwal believes the person playing the PM role shouldn't rotate weekly. He suggests rotating monthly or quarterly instead, and tying it to customer ownership, so the presenter genuinely understands the tickets they bring into the room.

The hardest problem for FDEs

Adjusting the rotation only treats the symptom. Agarwal believes the underlying problem exists at any scale: in his view, the challenge FDEs face is preserving context as knowledge passes through hundreds or thousands of hands.

Tandem, a company building tools for FDE teams, sees the same gap: documenting what's learned in the field is real work that competes with delivery for time, and delivery always wins.

Venture firm Northzone looks at the problem through the lens of incentives. FDEs are motivated to win over their own customers; product teams are motivated to build something many people use. When FDEs are tied to outcomes for a single customer, the customer side wins: they stop being product scouts and become account managers.

Northzone uses a brick metaphor: FDEs pick up bricks in the field, but if no one carries the bricks back to the product team, the company doesn't really have an FDE organization.

What to practice

The capability Medplum lacked while scaling is one you can practice right now: write a ticket that someone who has never met the customer can read, understand, and act on.

Back to the date filtering example. The bad version is one line: "Customer X needs date filtering, high priority." The good version is only five sentences, yet it answers the engineering team's questions in advance.

Customer X's operations team can't filter records by date, so every morning they have to search manually. They mentioned this in all three calls, and the call notes are attached. Currently they export the data to Excel and filter there.

Two other customers have raised similar requests, so this isn't one customer's idiosyncratic need. If it isn't built, Customer X will keep relying on an Excel file outside the system. The reader can make a decision without calling you back.

When reading job descriptions, watch for phrases like "triage feature requests" or "input to roadmap." They signal that the company expects you to take on PM work too. In interviews, you can ask how signals from FDEs reach the product team, who reads them, and how often.

Companies eliminating the PM role won't lack engineers who can write code. What they lack are people who know how to package context so information doesn't get lost in the handoff.
