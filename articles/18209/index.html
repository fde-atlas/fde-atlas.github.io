Time to value in customer onboarding: PS guide

Rocketlane · 2026-10-27 · 29 min read

Summary

This Rocketlane guide argues that time to value (TTV) in customer onboarding should be measured from contract signature to the customer's first measurable business outcome, not go-live. Citing PMI (inaccurate requirements cause 47% of project failures) and SPI Research (73.8% industry on-time delivery), it identifies five structural causes of delay, defines five TTV metrics with a simple formula, and recommends segmenting median TTV and tracking leading indicators like day-7/14 customer task completion.

Key points

  • TTV runs from signature to first measurable outcome, not go-live.
  • PMI: inaccurate requirements cause 47% of projects to miss goals.
  • SPI: industry on-time delivery is 73.8%; high performers hit 81.5%.
  • Five delay causes: customer dependencies, handoff gaps, manual setup, late-found integrations, static risk registers.
  • Track five metrics including TTFV and day-7/14 task completion; report median TTV by segment.

Search results for time to value are dominated by product growth content. That framing treats time to value (TTV) as an adoption idea inside a SaaS product. For implementation teams, the TTV definition has to be operational instead.

An operational definition needs three parts. A start point, which is the signature date. An end point, which is a defined value event agreed with the customer. And leading indicators you can track across every project running in parallel. Define TTV that way, and it becomes a managed number rather than a talking point.

The distinction matters across the whole customer journey. Time to value in SaaS sits between the sales promise and the renewal conversation. It shapes what new customers believe about your delivery organization long after go-live.

When the definition stays informal, every person measures something different. One counts from kickoff. Another counts from sign-up. A third counts from the first invoice. Nobody can compare two implementations, so nobody can improve either one.

Consider a typical case. A customer goes live on schedule in week eight, and the project closes green. Their first report does not run until week fourteen, because nobody agreed what that first report needed to contain. The implementation succeeded. The time to value did not.

Time to value is the duration from signed contract to first measurable business outcome, not the date the implementation closes. It is the metric that predicts renewal: customers who reach their value moment on schedule expand and refer, while customers who wait disengage during the wait.

Time to value is a spectrum, not a single number. Five types matter for onboarding and delivery teams, and each answers a different question.

  • Time to first value (TTFV): The earliest measurable signal that a customer is on the path to their outcome. A first workflow run, a first report generated, or a first project tracked in the new system. TTFV is the leading indicator to define before kickoff.
  • Immediate time to value: Value delivered inside the first session, immediately after account creation. This applies to products where new users reach a core benefit without configuration. Immediate value is realistic for self-serve tools and rare in enterprise implementations, which is exactly why the distinction is useful.
  • Basic value, or short-term time to value: The customer's first outcome connected to the reason they bought. First team using the platform, first billing cycle run, first dashboard shared with a client. Most teams call this go-live, but it only counts as basic value if success criteria were agreed beforehand.
  • Long-term time to value: Full return on the investment. Team-wide adoption, process change, and measurable improvement against the baseline captured at deal close. Renewal decisions are made against this number, and delays here affect customer lifetime value directly.
  • Time to exceed value: The point where the customer derives more value than the contract price. This drives expansion and referral, and it is only reachable if the earlier value events landed on time.

Measure at least two of these. Track TTFV as your early-warning metric and long-term value as your renewal metric. Reporting only on go-live tells you when work stopped, not when value started.

At most SaaS companies, revenue recognition is tied to go-live. Finance needs that date for time-to-revenue reporting, so that date gets tracked with real discipline.

The result is a quiet mismatch. The metric that gets measured is go-live. The metric that predicts retention is the value moment. Teams optimize for the one that appears in the forecast.

That is how implementations close on time on paper while the customer is still waiting. It also explains why churn sometimes arrives from accounts that every internal dashboard marked green. Customer expectations were set at signature, and the customer expects progress against those, not against your phase gates.

Implementations run long for five connected reasons. Customer deliverables tracked in email with no accountability mechanism. A sales-to-delivery handoff that loses context. Manual project creation overhead at deal close. Complex integrations discovered mid-project rather than at scoping. And delivery risk kept in a separate document reviewed once a week.

  • Customer dependency bottlenecks: The customer's data migration, access provisioning, and configuration decisions all sit on the critical path. Assigned by email with no due date and no dependency chain, they have no architecture around them. The project manager becomes the tracking system, sending a weekly follow-up that everyone finds uncomfortable.
  • Sales-to-delivery handoff gaps: The project manager starts blank. Scope, stakeholders, integration requirements, and success criteria that the account executive captured over a six-week deal cycle sit in the CRM. The first two weeks get spent rediscovering what sales already knew.
  • Manual project creation overhead: Copying templates and adjusting tasks adds 30 to 60 minutes per implementation at deal close. For a team running 30 concurrent projects, that overhead compounds. The implementation starts late because the project did not exist until someone had time to build it.
  • Complex integrations found late: Multi-vendor integrations, API dependencies, and third-party timelines sit outside your control. That is legitimate complexity. It becomes avoidable delay when the requirements surface in week three instead of at scoping.
  • Delivery risk kept in a separate document: Risk registers reviewed in a weekly status ceremony surface problems after the intervention window has closed. Risk that updates when the project updates gets caught while it is still cheap to fix.

Five time-to-value metrics matter for implementation teams. Time to first value, time to go-live, customer task completion rate at day 7 and day 14, effort hours versus planned, and milestone customer satisfaction. Most teams measure only time to go-live, which means they miss the four leading indicators that would let them improve before a project closes.

  • Time to first value: When did the customer complete the first success milestone agreed before kickoff? This is the TTV metric that predicts renewal. It has to exist as a named milestone in the live project, not as a calculation you run afterward.
  • Time to go-live: The baseline operational milestone. Easiest to measure and least predictive on its own. Necessary for revenue recognition, insufficient for retention.
  • Customer task completion at day 7 and day 14: The strongest leading indicator available. Below 50% at day 7 means the project is already at risk while the intervention window is open. Below 50% at day 14 means delay is likely, and that window is closing.
  • Effort hours versus planned: Actual versus estimated effort by task type. Large variances tell you which templates are wrong, and a wrong template propagates as a scheduling error into every future project that uses it.
  • Milestone customer satisfaction: A short check at each phase milestone rather than only at project close. Post-project customer satisfaction is a postmortem. Mid-project satisfaction is something you can still act on.

None of these five survive manual collection. The moment measuring costs more than the insight returns, the measurement quietly stops. All five have to be recorded as a byproduct of normal delivery work.

The time to value formula is deliberately simple. Time to value equals the date of the defined value event minus the contract signature date, expressed in calendar days. Reporting tools label the same figure time to value, TTV, and time to value TTV, but the underlying arithmetic does not change.

Two rules keep the time to value calculation honest. Always count in calendar days rather than working days, because the customer experiences elapsed time and not your capacity calendar. And always count from signature rather than from kickoff, because the weeks before kickoff are exactly where implementations lose ground.

For portfolio reporting, calculate the median instead of the mean. One 180-day enterprise rollout will drag an average that otherwise looks healthy. Report median TTV alongside the spread, and time-to-value measurement becomes something leadership can act on rather than argue with.

Used this way, the TTV metric works as a genuine time-to-value KPI. It has a formula, an owner, a target per segment, and a leading indicator attached to it. Speed to value stops being a slogan and becomes a number on a dashboard.

A defined value event is the specific, measurable moment that signals the customer has received what they paid for. Four steps make it usable.

  • Confirm the outcome the customer described during the sales process. Not the feature they liked, the business result they expected.
  • Identify the earliest measurable signal that they are on the path to it. This becomes the TTFV milestone.
  • Agree the definition in writing before the kickoff call, not during it. A value moment negotiated live becomes a value moment nobody owns.
  • Enter it in the project as a named milestone with a target date, sitting alongside go-live rather than replacing it.

The test is simple. "The customer is using the platform" cannot be measured. "First monthly utilization report generated and reviewed by the VP of Professional Services" can be. Vague value events are why so many teams cannot measure time to value at all.

Write the definition down as shared success metrics owned by both sides. Prescriptive onboarding works because the customer knows which outcome the plan drives toward. Nobody has to guess what meaningful value looks like when the first report runs.

In most implementations, the binding constraint on time to value is customer task completion, not internal throughput. Customer data migration, access provisioning, sign-offs, and configuration decisions sit on the critical path. Those tasks often live in email, with no dependency chain and no automated reminders. The implementation then waits on the customer while the project manager waits on an answer.

A task communicated by email has no shared record. There is no due date the customer agreed to, no visibility into what breaks if it is late, and no consequence connected to the go-live milestone.

So the project manager becomes the accountability mechanism. They compose the same follow-up every week, each one a slightly more apologetic version of the last. The customer reads it as a nudge rather than a dependency.

Teams diagnose this as customer readiness, as though it were a fixed property of the account. It is a design problem, and design problems have fixes.

Three things have to be true at the same time. Any one missing and the other two stop working.

  • Customer tasks assigned explicitly in a shared portal, with a named owner, a due date, and one line explaining what the task unlocks. Not an email attachment, and not a separate spreadsheet.
  • The dependency chain visible to the customer. Their pending data migration blocks the integration phase, which blocks user acceptance testing, which blocks go-live. That is information, not pressure, and customers who can see it behave differently.
  • Automated reminders are sent by the system at three days before due and again at overdue. The project manager is copied. The project manager is not the sender.

When the system sends the reminder, the follow-up becomes informational. The discomfort a project manager feels writing a chase email disappears the moment they stop being the one writing it.

A project manager running 25 concurrent implementations cannot open each project to find which customer tasks are overdue. The signal has to come to them.

The useful format is a portfolio alert. Six implementations have customer tasks overdue at day 7. Three sit below 50% completion. Without that view, delay gets discovered one project at a time, usually after it has already compounded into a moved date.

Without a structured accountability mechanism, implementation managers spend hours every week composing manual follow-ups across every active project. That overhead displaces billable delivery work, and it spends your customers' time as well as your own.

The go-live date moves by the same number of days the task sat unanswered. The project manager sends an apology. The customer begins to form an opinion about how the relationship works.

Shorten TTV inside the onboarding flow by removing setup work rather than explaining it. Use smart defaults so the product arrives configured. Guide users toward one specific task that delivers the core benefit. Instrument the flow so you can spot friction points where new users drop. The goal is a shorter path to the first value event, not a more thorough tour.

Every product has an aha moment. It is the point where the value proposition stops being a claim and becomes something the user has experienced.

Find it by looking at retained customers rather than at your feature list. Identify the key events that separate accounts still active at 90 days from those that churned. Those value events are what user onboarding should drive toward, and they usually mark the point where a trial becomes a paying customer.

Then remove everything between sign up and that moment. Guiding users through nine configuration screens before the first meaningful outcome is how a strong product produces a weak onboarding experience. Advanced features can wait until the core benefit has landed.

This is where customer experience and commercial outcome meet. Users who experience meaningful value in the first session convert to paid plans at higher rates. They also need less of your customer success team's time later.

Every setting you ask a new customer to choose is a decision they are not qualified to make yet. They have not used the product, so they are guessing.

Smart defaults invert that. Ship sensible default settings based on what worked for similar customers, then let users change them once they understand the trade-off. A customer starts inside a working configuration rather than an empty one, which is the cheapest way to deliver a shorter TTV.

The same logic applies to enterprise implementations. Conditional templates that pre-select phases based on edition, integration count, and user segment do for a project plan what smart defaults do for a product. Both replace a blank page with a defensible starting point.

Sequence the rollout too. Ship basic services first, confirm the core benefit has landed, then introduce advanced features and new features once the habit exists. Trying to shorten TTV and demonstrate depth in the same week usually achieves neither.

Instrument the onboarding flow at the level of user actions, not page views. Track account creation, first session activity, first configuration saved, and the first value event.

Then look for the step where completion falls off a cliff. That is the friction point. Usually it is a moment where users expect one thing, and the product asks for another. Sometimes a required input lives in a system the user cannot access.

Resist the instinct to blame length. Form analytics firm Zuko found that form length alone is not a reliable predictor of completion rate. What matters is whether the person can answer accurately right now, without chasing a colleague, so the useful question is about difficulty rather than field count.

Fix the drop-off before adding new features. A new feature reaches only the users who survived the flow. Improving the flow reaches everyone, and it lifts user engagement across every segment at once.
