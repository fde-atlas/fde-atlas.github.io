FDE Customer Interview Playbook: From Vague Requests to a Verifiable Problem List

Original title: FDE 客户访谈 Playbook：从模糊诉求到可验证问题清单

FDE中国 · 2026-09-04 · 6 min read

Summary

This FDEChina playbook teaches forward deployed engineers how to turn vague client requests like "help us do something with AI" into a verifiable, prioritized problem list. It lays out a seven-step interview process, each with actions, deliverables, and pitfalls. Key moves include restating your understanding for on-the-spot correction, agreeing on next steps before leaving the room, and sending structured minutes within 24 hours. In the Chinese context, minutes should be written so client contacts can forward them internally, since they face reporting pressure. Teams starting out should prioritize the restatement and minutes steps first.

Key points

  • Seven numbered steps cover preparation through post-interview self-check.
  • Write 3-5 hypotheses before the interview to avoid being led by the client.
  • Dig from wants to why-now: process, frequency, quantified cost, timing motive.
  • Restate understanding and get written confirmation of the problem list.
  • A successful interview ends with four artifacts: prioritized list, non-goals, data owners, next meeting.

The problem this piece solves: when an FDE walks into a client meeting, they often have nothing more than "help us do something with AI," and after the interview they walk away with only a vague good impression and verbal agreement. The most valuable takeaway is the seven-step interview process, especially the "restatement alignment" and "exit actions" steps: saying your understanding back to the client face-to-face and scheduling the next step on the spot is the dividing line between an interview being a chat and being work. The most common misuse is turning the interview into a requirements-collection session—writing down everything the client says without probing "why now" and "what counts as success," ending up with a wish list instead of a problem list. Compared with in-house content on the project lifecycle that covers "which phase the interview belongs to," this piece's increment is the execution details of "how to make an interview effective." What must be layered on for the domestic context: clients often face reporting pressure, so your meeting notes should ideally be something they can directly forward internally, which significantly improves their cooperation. After reading, pick an ongoing project and use the notes template from Step 6 to turn your most recent communication into formal meeting notes, and send them to the client for confirmation. — FDEChina Editorial Team · Practitioner's Edition

An FDE's client interviews are different from a product manager's user interviews: you are not looking for "what users like," but for a specific problem that can be verified through engineering means and that the client is willing to jointly accept. Most interviews fail in the same way—the client talks about their vision for two hours, you fill three pages of notes, everyone leaves feeling the conversation went well, but no one can say what to do next. This piece gives a complete method in seven numbered steps, each covering what to do, the deliverable, and common pitfalls, ready to follow directly. It corresponds to the discovery phase of the FDE project lifecycle and is an input to the subsequent project kickoff document.

Step 1: Pre-interview preparation—write down your hypotheses first

What to do: Spend one to two hours before the interview going through the client's industry, public materials, and the roles of your contacts, then write down three to five hypotheses, each in the form "We guess the client's biggest pain point is X, based on Y." Also prepare about ten questions, ordered by "background—current state—pain points—quantification—decision chain." If you have a product demo, prepare content relevant to the client's industry; don't use a generic demo.

Deliverable: A one-page interview outline containing the hypothesis list, the question sequence, and "the three questions this interview must answer."

Common pitfalls: Going in without written hypotheses and being carried along by the client's narrative the whole time; or over-preparing and turning the interview into reading from a script, missing key information the client mentions in passing.

Step 2: The first five minutes—set the rules clearly

What to do: Do three things at the start: state that "today we're mainly here to listen, not to pitch a solution," to give the client a sense of safety; ask them to introduce everyone present and their respective concerns, to identify who the actual users are and who the decision-makers with veto power are; agree on a time structure, e.g., "we'll ask questions for the first forty minutes, and align on conclusions in the last fifteen." If the client mentions internal confidentiality requirements, confirm on the spot what can go into the notes and what stays verbal only.

Deliverable: A list of participants (name, title, concerns, decision role), plus a verbal agreement on the scope of note sharing.

Common pitfalls: Demoing the product at the start—the client will make feature requests based on your demo, and the interview becomes a feature-matching session; or not asking about roles and treating an "enthusiastic but non-deciding" contact as the source of requirements.

Step 3: Digging—from "what they want" to "why now"

What to do: Use chains of follow-up questions to translate wishes into problems. When the client says "we want to deploy an AI customer service agent," keep asking: How is this done today? Which step consumes the most labor? How often does this problem occur, and how long does each instance take to handle? What cost has it caused before? Why does it need to be solved now? The goal is to extract four types of information: the current process, frequency, quantified cost, and time-based motivation. If the client can't answer quantification questions, record it as "data pending"—that's exactly what you'll verify later. When probing, frequently ask "can you give an example": abstract requests reveal their true nature once they land on a concrete case—the client says "responses are too slow," but following one concrete case may reveal the real problem is that a certain type of document fails to parse. Write these examples down; they will later be the best source of eval cases.

Deliverable: Raw notes organized by request, with each request tagged with four fields—process, frequency, cost, motivation—marking missing ones as "pending."

Common pitfalls: Following the client into technical discussion the moment they mention a technical solution, drifting away from the problem itself; collecting only "what they want" and not "what is unacceptable"—which parts of the existing process absolutely must not break often defines the solution boundary better than what they want.

Step 4: Restatement alignment—say your understanding back face-to-face

What to do: Set aside ten minutes to restate what you heard in your own words: "The problem I understand to be top priority is... the scenario where it occurs is... if solved, the criteria for judging success would be... is that right?" Ask the client to correct you on the spot. Focus your restatement on two kinds of content: the items you plan to put into the problem list, and the items you judge to be out of scope for this phase. The latter is especially important—saying on the spot "we understand X is not in scope this time" is far better than having it discovered as deleted in a document later. In online meetings, you can also post the restatement points in the meeting chat and ask the other party to reply with a written "confirmed"—a written confirmation is closer to a commitment than a verbal "yeah, right," and is easy to screenshot for the record.

Deliverable: A preliminary problem list verbally confirmed by the client, each item containing the problem description, occurrence scenario, and rough validation criteria.

Common pitfalls: Sneaking your own agenda into the restatement—quietly mixing in the direction you hope to pursue. The client not objecting doesn't mean they agree; it only means they didn't catch it. The restatement should keep the shape of the client's language; solution vocabulary belongs after the interview.

Step 5: Exit actions—four things that must be done before the meeting ends

What to do: Complete these in the meeting room; don't wait until you're back. First, confirm whether the client accepts the priority order of the problem list. Second, agree on who will provide each piece of "pending data" and when. Third, schedule the next contact and its format, even if it's just a fifteen-minute call. Fourth, ask one closing question: "Is there anything we didn't ask about that you think we should know?" The output of this question often exceeds the previous forty minutes. Also, this process applies to online meetings too; role identification just needs to be more deliberate: in video calls you can't see seating or rank, so at the start, call on each person by name to say one sentence about their concerns—this is even more indispensable than offline.

Deliverable: An action item list (item, owner, date) and a calendar invitation for the next contact.

Common pitfalls: Saying at the end "we'll organize our notes and get back to you"—a next step without a clear date is no next step; the client's priorities will be washed away within a week by their own business.

Step 6: Key points of the notes template—send within twenty-four hours

What to do: Finish writing the same day and send before noon the next day. Write the notes in a fixed structure: 1. Participants of this meeting; 2. The confirmed problem list (sorted by priority, each item with validation criteria); 3. Explicit non-goals (what will not be done this time); 4. Pending data and owners; 5. Action items and dates; 6. Next-step arrangements. In writing, use phrases the client actually said as hooks, so people on the client's side can take the notes directly for internal forwarding. After sending, proactively ask "did I misunderstand anything anywhere," giving them a low-cost chance to correct you.

Deliverable: Structured interview notes—this is the direct source material for the "Background and Problem" section of the project kickoff document.

Common pitfalls: Writing the notes as a meeting transcript, recording who said what, drowning out the conclusions; or sending them only to your own team, so the client never receives the confirmed version, and two weeks later the two sides' understanding of scope starts to diverge.

Step 7: Master list of common pitfalls—cross-phase self-check

What to do: Consolidate the high-frequency errors scattered across the steps into a self-check sheet, and score yourself after each interview: Did you bring hypotheses into the room? Did you identify the decision-making roles? Did you dig out quantified costs? Did you restate and get confirmation? Did you agree on the next step on the spot? Were the notes sent within twenty-four hours? Did the client give feedback on the notes? If you miss more than two of the seven items, make targeted corrections before the next interview.

Deliverable: A team-shared interview quality self-check sheet; accumulated over time, it can also reveal your team's systematic weaknesses in interviewing.

Common pitfall: treating this checklist as a one-time exercise that gets done once and discarded. Its real value lies in cross-project comparison: when the same pitfalls recur, it means the process—not the individual—needs adjusting.

Summary and Next Steps

At the end of a solid discovery interview, you should walk away with four things: a list of issues whose priorities the customer has confirmed, clear non-goals, a list of missing data with assigned owners, and a next step with a scheduled date. Missing any one of these means you're still at "we had a nice chat" rather than "we're aligned." When your team is just starting out, you don't need to implement all seven steps at once: start with the restatement alignment in Step 4 and the 24-hour meeting notes in Step 6—they offer the best return on effort, and the remaining steps can be added gradually through retrospectives. This checklist should then go into the kickoff document and be continuously calibrated throughout the delivery process—the issue list itself also serves as a baseline source for delivery metrics.
