FDE Handover and Documentation | Preventing Personal Dependency

Original title: FDEの引き継ぎとドキュメント化｜属人化を防ぐ

日本Fde協会 · 2026-10-15 · 6 min read

Summary

This article from the Japan FDE Association argues that a working system and a system others can operate are different things, and handover assets must be deliberately designed. Knowledge silos arise because capable FDEs rely on memory, and the most commonly lost information is the rationale behind design decisions. The authors classify documentation into four reader-specific types and recommend starting the decision log on day one, then handing over in order: overview, operations, design intent, and permissions/evidence locations.

Key points

  • A running system is not the same as one others can maintain.
  • Personalization stems from capable engineers deferring documentation.
  • Design rationale, not procedures, is the most critical record.
  • Four reader-specific record types: operations, intent, permissions, decisions.
  • Start the decision log on day one; hand over from overview to details.

Here, FDE refers to Forward Deployed Engineer (a technical professional who works on-site with customers to implement AI and software tailored to their business operations). It is not an abbreviation for Full Disk Encryption. An FDE's job does not end with "building and running" a system. Whether operations continue even after the person in charge leaves the site depends on how the records for FDE handover are designed. By reading this article, you will understand the types and granularity of documentation that should be left on-site and the order of handover, so you can decide which records to leave, at what point, and for whom in your own project, and get started on a handover plan that avoids knowledge silos. The overall picture of skills required for FDEs is covered in The Full Range of Skills Required for FDEs | Explained in 8 Areas, so this article focuses solely on "handover and documentation."

Design handover assets separately from "things that work"

Typical explanations of FDE work stop at "embedding yourself on-site and delivering results," saying almost nothing about designing your exit. However, as far as we have seen in the field, even a successful deployment leaves behind a system no one can touch six months later if the handover is weak. A working system and a state in which others can operate it are two different things. What supports the latter is handover assets, and these will not exist unless they are deliberately designed.

Why knowledge silos happen

Knowledge silos are not the result of laziness; rather, the more capable the FDE, the more susceptible they are to this structure. When you go deep into the field, you reach a state where "you know it in your head, so things work even without writing it down," and record-keeping gets postponed. At one manufacturing client's site, a single engineer had complete command of the shipping and receiving business logic, which led to a situation where changes stalled until that person's schedule opened up. Excellence creates dependency.

What to leave behind: "the reasons behind decisions"

The most commonly omitted element in handovers is not operating procedures but "why it was designed that way." Code and screens can be understood by looking at them, but "the reason this field was deliberately not automated" or "the background behind not expanding this permission" disappears if not recorded. If a successor makes changes without knowing the reasons, they will repeat failures that were previously avoided. Decision rationale is the asset to prioritize above all else.

What to record and at what granularity

More documentation is not necessarily better. If it is too detailed, no one updates it and it quickly becomes obsolete. What we value is deciding the reader first and varying the granularity accordingly. The information needed differs for operations staff, administrators, and the next FDE. Cramming everything into a single document makes it hard to use for any reader.

Four types of records by reader

The table below organizes handover assets by reader. Check which ones are missing in your own project.

Record typePrimary readersContent and granularity to record
Operations manualDaily operations staffStandard operations and initial error response, at the screen level
Design intent notesNext FDE and modifiersWhy that configuration was chosen, and alternatives that were rejected
Permissions and evidence location tableAdministrators and auditorsWho can access what, and where the logs are
Decision logAll stakeholdersWhat was decided, what was deferred, and change history, with dates

For permissions and evidence, stating "where" is enough

When handling permission management and logs in a handover, there is no need to rewrite the design details. What handover assets need is a clear indication of "which accounts can access what" and "where the operation logs are stored and for how long." A map that successors and administrators can follow when something happens is sufficient. The thinking behind permission design itself is covered in Permission Management Design for AI Agents | FDE Best Practices.

The order of handover and how to proceed on-site

Even with documentation in place, handing it over in the wrong order prevents it from taking hold. Trying to write everything at the moment of departure degrades both granularity and accuracy. What we value is the mindset that handover is not a task to do at the end, but something to accumulate little by little from the start of the engagement.

Start the decision log on day one

The most effective habit is to briefly record, every time, what was decided, what was deferred, and what comes next, starting from the beginning of the project. This also helps build trust, and it is the strongest handover asset. In one SaaS company's project, this decision log became the instruction manual for the successor, making verbal handover almost unnecessary. For how to act right after starting, What an FDE Does in the First Week On-Site is also a useful reference.

The order of handover

A practical order for handing over is: overall picture → operating procedures → design intent → permissions and evidence locations. First share "why this system exists and how far its role extends," then daily operations, then the design intent needed for modifications, and finally the permissions and evidence locations for administrators. Starting with the details means the recipient ends up holding fragments without being able to form an overall picture.

The integrity to leave things in a form that lets you let go

From our experience at startups, the more trusted the FDE, the more they aim for "a state where things run without them." Letting go of knowledge silos may look like reducing your own value in the short term, but it is actually the path to being entrusted the longest. When handover assets are in order, customers will reach out again with their next challenge. We also go deeper into establishing operations in Post-AI-Implementation Operational Adoption | The Reality of Accompaniment Support.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q. How detailed should the documentation be? The practical approach is to decide the reader first, then the granularity. Separate it out: screen-level operations for operations staff, decision rationale for the next FDE, and permission and evidence locations for administrators. Cramming everything into one document means it never gets updated and becomes obsolete. Keeping it at a granularity that continues to be updated is more valuable than a meticulous document that gets abandoned.

Q. When should I start preparing for handover? Not at the point of departure, but from your first day on the job. If you build up a decision log from the start, recording what was decided, what was deferred, and what changed with dates, it becomes a handover asset in itself. Trying to write everything at once degrades both granularity and accuracy, so leaving records little by little each day is ultimately easier and more reliable.

Q. Should permission and log information be included in handover assets? They should be included, but there is no need to rewrite the design. It is enough to be able to trace "who can access what, and where the logs are stored." Leaving a map that successors and auditors can follow when something happens provides particular peace of mind in corporate settings.
