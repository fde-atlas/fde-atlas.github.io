AI agent approval gates: what to remove and keep

FDE Times · 2026-10-08 · 4 min read

Summary

  • Data from 300 synthetic projects, 899 runs and 135 repeated runs separates 'correct decisions' from 'reliable execution'; a counterexample shows some gates cannot be decided from the information available.

On September 24 Jeremy Canale posted a paper to arXiv with an intriguing title: “The Last Human Gate”. The content is highly technical: a framework that replaces each task at governance gates with agents, rule engines and evidence services, and the person tasked with wiring it all together is the FDE.

The question the paper asks is the one a client will put to you in the first working session: which steps still need a human signature, and which can a machine handle? Get it wrong one way and the agent stalls in an approval queue. Get it wrong the other way and a risky action runs without anyone noticing.

The paper’s value therefore lies less in the framework itself than in how it pulls apart two things that are usually lumped together.

The evaluation uses DGF-Bench, with 300 synthetic projects and 899 scorable runs, each run being one model on one project. It adds an evidence audit and 135 repeated runs to distinguish correct decisions from reliable execution.

An agent that answers correctly once has not done enough. It has to answer correctly on the 135th run.

Some gates cannot go yet, and that is good news

The most striking detail is a limitation the paper states itself: a documentary counterexample shows that some gates cannot be decided from the information available. At a gate like that, the agent and the rule engine are not at fault. The input data simply is not sufficient to automate the step.

Those gates cannot be removed yet, and it falls to the FDE to identify them before anyone promises a client “full automation”.

Practitioners look at the same problem more simply. Alejandro Rioja, a practitioner writing on his personal blog, reviews the approval queue every month and removes any gate that shows 95% same-day approval with 0% modification.

Rioja also removes a gate when nobody approves within the SLA, replacing it with an audit trail. A gate where the reviewer just clicks through protects no one; it only slows the agent down.

His test for keeping a gate is equally simple: if you would not want a new hire to do the task without checking first, the agent needs a gate. Airia, a vendor in this space whose advice should be read with some caution, puts the same idea as policy: approval authority should match the risk level of the action.

The FDE’s job is turning criteria into code

Where Airia is most right, and where FDE work begins, is that the conditions that trigger a gate must be machine-readable and enforced at the execution layer, rather than living only in a prompt. A line in a system prompt saying “ask a human before deleting data” is no gate.

A gate is code that blocks the action until there is a signature, with a log of who signed and when.

Canale’s paper gives the FDE precisely the job of connecting agents, rule engines and evidence services.

Read with an implementer’s eye, that wiring raises two unavoidable questions: what counts as sufficient evidence for each gate, and whether the agent repeats the same decision consistently. Those are also the two things the paper’s evaluation measures.

A Factory case study on ZenML describes the same shift. FDEs there do not do professional services; they have moved from operating software directly to maintaining and managing the system that produces the software.

For a developer, in Vietnam or anywhere else, who wants this role, prompting is the wrong skill to drill. Take an internal workflow with an approval step, measure the same-day approval rate and the modification rate over a month, then write the trigger conditions as policy code instead of instructions.

On a CV, a line such as “removed three rubber-stamp gates and kept two high-risk gates, with data” says more than any agent certification.

The last gate will not disappear. Only its keeper changes: from the person who clicks approve to the person who decides which gates deserve someone to click.
