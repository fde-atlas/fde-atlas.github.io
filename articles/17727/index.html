FDE Interview Question: How to Prepare for the Problem Decomposition Round

Original title: FDE 面试题：问题拆解轮怎么准备

FDE Times · 2026-10-08 · 4 min read

Summary

This guide argues that the problem decomposition round, not coding, is the decisive part of forward deployed engineer interviews. Citing Educative, techinterview.org, Aced, and job postings from Databricks, Anthropic, and Palantir, it explains that algorithm practice only helps the technical round. The article details four scoring criteria, a sample dialogue showing how to spend the first 5–15 minutes clarifying goals, a recommended practice sequence, and three common pitfalls, stressing that the round tests collaborative navigation of ambiguous requirements.

Key points

  • FDE interviews have four rounds; algorithm practice mainly helps only the technical one.
  • Databricks' 60-minute decomposition round is considered the core of its interview.
  • Interviewers score structure, user empathy, data reasoning, and scope judgment.
  • The most common mistake is proposing a solution before clarifying the goal.
  • Practice aloud with a timer and keep checking direction with the interviewer.

Key Takeaways

  • According to Educative's FDE course, the interview consists of four rounds: problem decomposition, a customer simulation, a technical round, and a behavioral interview. Algorithms only cover part of it.

Imagine this scenario: you've grinded through hundreds of LeetCode problems and walk into a Forward Deployed Engineer (FDE) interview. The interviewer says: "A chain of clinics wants to use AI to shorten patient wait times. Where do you start?" No inputs, no outputs, no complexity requirements.

An FDE interview guide on techinterview.org puts it bluntly: if you only practice algorithms, you're reviewing the least important part of the entire exam. Educative's FDE course divides the interview process into four rounds: problem decomposition, a customer simulation, a technical round, and a customer-facing behavioral interview.

Of these four rounds, grinding algorithm problems mainly helps with only the technical round.

Databricks is the most typical example. Aced's guide to the company's FDE role mentions that the problem decomposition round lasts about 60 minutes, where candidates design a system based on a vague business goal, and this round is considered the core of the entire interview process. Coding is a separate 60-minute interview.

If you want to become an FDE, the most worthwhile thing to practice seriously is problem decomposition.

Why does most of the interview consist of conversation?

The answer lies in the job itself. Anthropic's FDE job posting states that this role involves working directly with strategic customers and traveling frequently, with travel accounting for 25–50% of the time, going to customer sites to build systems alongside them.

The posting also requires candidates to have a strong sense of ownership and to be able to navigate ambiguity in complex organizations.

No customer will hand an FDE a complete requirements document. The guide on techinterview.org explains that the interview simulates exactly this situation, which is why most of the time is spent talking rather than typing code.

The problem decomposition interview format was pioneered and popularized by Palantir, with the aim of seeing whether candidates can find their own way when no one has drawn them a map.

What is the interviewer actually listening for?

The interviewer has a fairly specific scoring rubric. According to techinterview.org, they look at four things: structured decomposition, empathy for the end user, reasoning about data, and judgment about scope. Educative's course summarizes the purpose of this round as: assessing whether you can systematically handle an ambiguous problem.

These four criteria also explain the most common mistake recorded by Aced: jumping straight to a solution. The candidate hears the question and immediately says "I'd use RAG with a vector database." At that point, they've skipped the end user, never asked where the data lives, and haven't narrowed the scope. Before the interview has truly begun, three of the four criteria are already lost.

A complete walkthrough: from "shortening wait times" to a design

Back to the clinic question above. The Databricks guide recommends spending the first 5–15 minutes clarifying the goal before moving into technical design. Below is a hypothetical exchange from the opening few minutes.

Candidate: "Before designing, I'd like to clarify: does 'wait time' mean from booking to the appointment, or from checking in at the front desk to seeing the doctor?"

Interviewer: "It means the time spent sitting in the waiting room."

Candidate: "Who is most affected by this: patients, the front desk, or clinic management? How does the front desk currently decide the order of appointments?"

The second question demonstrates empathy for the end user. You're looking for the people who use this system every day, not just those who sign the contract. Next comes data:

Candidate: "Does the clinic record check-in times, consultation start times, and service types? Is it recorded in management software, or on paper?"

If the answer is "yes, in the management software, about two years of data," you have grounds to reason about a model that predicts consultation duration. If the answer is "on paper," the first phase of the project must be data collection, and you need to say so explicitly.

Finally comes scope, a step many candidates forget:

Candidate: "I suggest the first phase cover only one clinic and only predict consultation duration, so the front desk can tell patients roughly how long they'll wait. Automatic rescheduling goes into phase two, after we've confirmed the predictions are accurate. Does that sound reasonable?"

After about ten minutes of this conversation, you then draw the architecture: data sources, data pipelines, the model, the interface used by the front desk, and how to measure success. By this point, every component in the design has a rationale, and the interviewer has heard all four criteria.

What order should you practice in?

Practice a fixed routine repeatedly until it becomes instinct. First, restate the goal in your own words and ask how "success" will be measured. Then identify the end users and learn how they currently work. Then ask where the data lives, how good it is, and who owns it.

Once you've mastered these three points, propose a small scope that can be delivered within a few weeks, and explicitly state which parts you're deliberately deferring. Only at this point should you draw the architecture, and finally explain how you'll measure results.

Throughout the process, keep checking with the interviewer whether you're heading in the right direction, because this round is inherently collaborative, not a monologue.

If spoken English isn't your strong suit, don't just practice on paper. Practice out loud and with a timer, because this round requires you to think and speak at the same time, without looking at your notes.

Here's a tip when reading job postings: if the posting emphasizes navigating ambiguity and working directly with customers, like Anthropic's, expect the conversational portion to carry heavy weight and spend more time practicing problem decomposition.

Common Pitfalls

The first pitfall is treating clarifying questions as a formality: asking three token questions, then presenting the architecture you had already planned from the start. If the interviewer's answers didn't change your design, they'll notice immediately.

The second pitfall is trying to solve everything. Attempting to solve the entire problem in 60 minutes leaves the "judgment about scope" criterion in techinterview.org's rubric completely blank. The third pitfall is thinking silently.

This round requires you to speak. Every minute of silence is a minute the interviewer has nothing to score.

The coding round still needs preparation, but it only proves you can write code. The problem decomposition round shows whether you know what code to write and for whom. This job requires you to find your own way through a customer's ambiguous requirements, so practice time is best spent here.
