Palantir's Shyam Sankar on "Outcomes": How to Change the Evaluation of FDE Development

Original title: PalantirのShyam Sankarが語る「成果」。FDE育成の評価をどう変えるか

株式会社本質 · 2026-09-17 · 3 min read

Summary

Drawing on Shyam Sankar's essay "The Primacy of Winning" from his time as Palantir's CTO, the author examines how to evaluate trainees in FDE development programs. Rather than crediting a finished app, evaluation should ask whether users' work actually changed and whether that change can be sustained in operations. The author proposes recording target tasks, procedures, and timing, separating verified facts from untested hypotheses, and giving trainees room to make real decisions instead of fully specified exercises. These evaluation designs can also inform how SES and SIer firms pitch their capabilities to customers.

Key points

  • Sankar stresses mission accomplishment and outcomes, balancing problem-solving with product quality.
  • Evaluation should split into business impact and operational sustainability.
  • Track user workflows, timing definitions, and distinguish facts from hypotheses.
  • Give trainees decision space rather than fully specified exercises.
  • The same evaluation framing supports customer proposals by SES and SIer firms.

Full text

In FDE training programs, how should we evaluate the completion of an application? A useful reference is "The Primacy of Winning" by Shyam Sankar, who was introduced as Palantir's CTO at the time of the article's publication. In this article, we introduce part of his argument and consider evaluation methods that can be used in corporate training settings in Japan.

Key points of the original text and short translated quotes

What Sankar emphasizes is mission accomplishment and outcomes. He argues that development organizations need both a perspective for solving real-world problems and a perspective for building high-quality products.

"Building the right thing and building the thing right"

This is our translation of a subheading in the original text. He positions FDEs as roles that solve real problems alongside customers. The original text also contains strong personal views on ways of working and organizational management; the following is our own proposal derived from part of that content.

The main Pirate Wires article page is dated April 8, 2024, and the full-text page on the same outlet used to verify the body text is shown as April 9.

Our core view: Split evaluation into "changes to the work" and "conditions for operation"

In FDE development, we want to add two questions to evaluations that look at the finished product: "How does the user's work change?" and "Can that change be sustained?"

For example, consider a system that creates draft reports from sales meeting notes for sales representatives. This is a hypothetical example for illustration. Even if the text is natural, if the effort to check required fields increases, the work as a whole may not have improved. The evaluation should track not just the generated output, but the entire process through which the person in charge reviews, corrects, and submits it.

For the first evaluation, record the target person, the target work, the conventional procedure, and the procedure after prototyping. If measuring time, also decide what counts as the start and end points. So that trainees don't simply finish by saying "it became convenient," distinguish between verified facts and hypotheses that have not yet been tested.

For the second evaluation, organize how to verify when an incorrect draft is produced, the scope of information available, and who to contact when problems arise. At the prototyping stage, it is not necessary to implement everything. However, trainees must be able to explain what is still missing before the system can be used continuously in actual work.

Training coordinators should provide a scope within which trainees can make decisions

To give trainees experience of being accountable for outcomes, they need room to make choices. With exercises where the subject matter, features, screens, and implementation methods are all specified, it becomes difficult to observe the ability to choose problems to tackle.

On the other hand, if trainees are handed entire customer engagements, less experienced people lack the information needed to make judgments. At first, narrow the target work down to one, provide opportunities to ask questions of the people doing that work, and assign someone they can consult about design. Within that scope, have the trainees themselves propose what features to build and how to validate them.

When reviewing the training, avoid focusing only on the volume of work or presentation skills; instead, look at "whether the trainee could choose the next action based on the evidence obtained." Even if an attempt failed to produce results, if the trainee investigated the reason and re-scoped the target, there is learning that can be evaluated.

For executives of SES and SIer companies, this evaluation design also connects to proposals for customers. Which problems did our people identify, what did they verify, and what could they put in place up to operational use? The goal should be to be able to describe the training outcomes in exactly those terms.
