AI agent liability: Air Canada, Workday and FDE risk

FDE Times · 2026-10-22 · 7 min read

Summary

The article argues that courts and regulators are making AI agent liability an engineering problem that forward deployed engineers inherit. In Moffatt v. Air Canada, a British Columbia tribunal held the airline responsible for chatbot errors, treating the bot like any website content. In Mobley v. Workday, a California federal court allowed claims that Workday could be liable under an agency theory for AI-assisted hiring. Yet standard SaaS contracts push risk onto customers, who struggle to prove breach. The EU's new product liability directive covers software, and missing technical documentation creates a presumption of defectiveness. The author concludes that approval gates, tested thresholds, and contextual logs are becoming evidence, and offers three traps to avoid plus CV advice.

Key points

  • Air Canada ruling: deployers cannot blame the chatbot.
  • Workday case: vendors may face liability under agency theory.
  • Standard SaaS contracts leave agent risk with customers.
  • EU directive presumes defects when documentation is missing.
  • FDEs should build, test, and log approval gates for high-risk actions.

After the Air Canada chatbot ruling and the Workday lawsuit, the approval gates and logs that FDEs build are becoming evidence when disputes arise.

In brief

  • A tribunal in British Columbia and a federal court in California both refused to let AI serve as a shield: the deployer, and even the vendor, can still be held liable.
  • Under standard contracts, the customer bears the risk when an agent misbehaves, and struggles to prove the vendor was in breach.
  • Bristows advises customers to require vendors to provide approval gates for high-risk actions; the EU's new directive turns version and update records into evidence.

Air Canada once tried an argument that many companies using AI have probably considered: its chatbot was a separate legal entity. In February 2024 a tribunal in British Columbia rejected that argument and held the airline responsible for the wrong information the chatbot gave a customer.

The tribunal’s reasoning was simple. According to an analysis by McCarthy Tétrault, the tribunal found it made no difference whether information came from a static page or from a chatbot, and Air Canada was responsible for all the information on its website.

For a forward deployed engineer, the ruling touches daily work directly. The FDE is the one who connects agents to a client’s real systems, sets thresholds and decides which actions need human approval. Law and contracts are shifting in a direction that turns those technical decisions into evidence when a dispute arises.

Courts will not let AI act as a shield

The law firm Torkin Manes draws a general principle from Moffatt v. Air Canada: a business remains liable if wrong information reaches consumers through any AI tool. The agent does not take the blame; the deployer does.

The engineering consequence is fairly clear. If a chatbot is treated like a page on a website, its answers need to be controlled like official content: an eval suite that checks them against the source policy, and a way to detect when answers drift. That work usually sits in an FDE’s backlog.

In the United States, liability can extend to the vendor as well. In Mobley v. Workday, a federal court in California denied a motion to dismiss and found that Workday could be held liable under an agency theory, because customers had allegedly delegated hiring functions to it.

Duane Morris sums up the lesson for vendors: a vendor that uses AI to screen candidates may be liable for discrimination claims. Handing a task to software, in other words, does not make the legal liability attached to that task disappear.

Contracts are pulling the other way

Reading the case law, you might expect vendors to carry more of the risk. The familiar contract templates, however, allocate it differently.

Mayer Brown describes the logic of traditional SaaS terms: the vendor looks after the platform, while the user is responsible for every action taken on it.

This usually comes with the familiar warranty disclaimer that the service is provided as is, with all faults. According to the firm, that logic is being stretched as agents are sold as a service.

The law firm Bristows puts it more bluntly: under standard contracts, the customer bears the risk when an agent behaves unexpectedly or wrongly. Worse, the customer will find it very hard to prove a breach of contract when an agent causes an error, and must also prove causation.

Put the two pieces together and a gap appears. Courts say the deployer cannot blame the AI, while contracts leave the deployer to carry the agent’s actions alone. When something goes wrong, the decisive question will be whether human oversight actually existed and worked.

That is why Bristows advises customers not to accept sole responsibility for oversight where that is inappropriate or technically unfeasible, and to require vendors to provide approval gates for high-risk actions. Such gates do not appear by themselves; someone has to configure them in the client’s system.

In Europe, version records become evidence

The EU’s new product liability directive extends the definition of “product” to software and digital services. Baker McKenzie confirms the scope includes AI applications and systems.

Two accompanying mechanisms make logging critical. Courts can order parties to disclose relevant evidence, and according to HLC, if a defendant fails to provide technical documentation, the product is presumed defective. Manufacturers are also liable if a defect stems from a software update within their control.

Apply those two mechanisms to a specific agent and you need to be able to answer: at the moment the damage occurred, which model, which prompt and which configuration were running, and what was the most recent update? If you cannot answer, you cannot defend yourself, and you risk being presumed at fault simply for lacking records.

Approval gates are FDE work

Take a hypothetical agent that handles refund requests for an e-commerce company. The contract places oversight on the customer. In live operation, though, the support team is overloaded, so every refund is approved automatically.

On paper, the risk already sits with the customer. If the agent issues wrong refunds in bulk, the system cannot demonstrate any oversight step, and on Bristows’ reasoning the customer will also struggle to shift the blame back to the vendor. The two sides will end up arguing over a control that was never built.

Now consider a different configuration. The agent handles small refunds on its own, stops and waits for human approval above a threshold the customer chooses, and every approval is logged along with the agent version that was running. This is the kind of approval gate for high-risk actions that Bristows advises customers to demand.

Three traps to avoid

The most common trap is oversight that exists only on paper: the contract and design documents say “human review”, but in live operation nobody opens the approval queue. Check it with data, for instance what percentage of above-threshold actions last week were actually approved by a person.

The next trap is a threshold that has never been tested. An escalation threshold written into config, with no test case showing the agent stops in the right place, is not yet a control. Add a few above-threshold scenarios to the eval suite and rerun them after every update.

The last trap is logs without context. A log that says “approved” without recording who approved, when, and with which agent version and configuration is hard to use when a court orders disclosure of technical documentation.

On every deployment, ask early in discovery: where does the contract assume oversight happens, and which of the agent’s actions could harm end customers, candidates or buyers?

Put this skill on your CV

Many engineers write a CV line such as “Deployed an AI agent to handle refunds for an e-commerce client”. That line says only what the agent can do, and leaves out the hardest part of the deployment.

A stronger line states which actions must wait for approval, who agreed the thresholds, what the logs capture, and which evals check accuracy against the official source.

When reading FDE job descriptions, look for words such as governance, human review, approval workflow or audit trail, and have a concrete example of that work from your own projects ready. In interviews, if asked about a deployment, explain why you put the stopping point where you did.

A software engineer may treat liability clauses as a matter for lawyers. An FDE cannot: an approval gate written into a contract protects someone only when it actually runs in the client’s system.
