Do FDEs Need English? Language Skills Required for Palantir-Style Roles and How to Learn Them

Original title: FDEに英語は必要か？Palantir型職種で求められる英語力と学習法

FDE総研 · 2026-08-07 · 13 min read

Summary

This Japanese article examines how much English FDEs actually need. At domestic Japanese companies, FDEs work mainly in Japanese with local customers, so English is a bonus rather than a requirement. For Palantir-style roles at foreign vendors, however, reading and writing English are effectively prerequisites since internal discussions run in English. In practice, English use concentrates on reading product documentation and writing GitHub issues rather than speaking. The author suggests CEFR B2 reading (roughly TOEIC 785+) and B1 speaking as sufficient, and proposes a learning roadmap: read official docs in English first, then build writing templates, then tackle listening and speaking.

Key points

  • Domestic-company FDEs work in Japanese; English is only a plus.
  • Palantir-style foreign-vendor FDEs require English reading and writing.
  • Real usage centers on reading docs and writing issues, not conversation.
  • CEFR B2 reading (TOEIC ~785) and B1 speaking are practical targets.
  • Recommended learning order: reading, then writing, then listening/speaking.

The first stumbling block for engineers aiming to become an FDE (Forward Deployed Engineer) is the question of "how much English do I actually need?" FDE is a role established by Palantir, and much of the primary information circulates in English. On the other hand, FDE positions at companies in Japan primarily serve Japanese-speaking client sites. This article organizes the English requirements for FDEs into five areas: whether it is mandatory, in which situations it is used, what level is needed, how to learn it, and whether you can apply even if your English is lacking.

Is English mandatory for FDEs?

English is not mandatory for FDEs in general, but it becomes a de facto requirement for Palantir-style FDE roles at foreign companies. Depending on whether the company is foreign or domestic, English is either a bonus factor or a prerequisite.

FDE stands for Forward Deployed Engineer, a role that embeds itself in the client's business operations and handles everything end to end, from problem discovery through software implementation and adoption. The company that popularized this role's name and function is Palantir, and Palantir's recruiting information lists openings under the job titles Forward Deployed Engineer / Forward Deployed Software Engineer (Source: Palantir Technologies "Careers" https://www.palantir.com/careers/, retrieved 2026-08-07). In other words, primary information about the FDE career path tends to come from the English-speaking world first.

Is English a mandatory requirement in FDE job postings at domestic companies?

FDEs hired by domestic companies mainly handle clients in Japanese, and English often remains merely a bonus factor. FDEs at domestic companies conduct business interviews in Japanese, work with Japanese domain terminology, and persuade decision-makers in Japanese. What is tested first here is not English ability but business understanding and implementation speed.

That said, English is not "unnecessary." Even at domestic companies, the documentation for the data platforms, LLMs, and visualization tools they adopt is almost always in English, and updates that outpace Japanese translations are not uncommon. While not being able to read English rarely causes you to fail the hiring process, it does affect your learning speed after joining.

To what extent is English a prerequisite for Palantir-style FDE roles at foreign companies?

Palantir-style FDEs operate on the assumption of English product specifications and headquarters teams, so English ability becomes a prerequisite for hiring. Internal design discussions, code reviews, and incident response all run in English, so the job cannot function if you cannot read and write English.

At the Japan offices of foreign vendors such as Palantir, a two-layer structure tends to emerge: conversations with clients happen in Japanese, while internal communication happens in English. Since FDEs stand at the junction between client sites and headquarters product teams, an interpreter-like role bridging these two layers arises structurally.

On-the-ground perspective: When aiming for an FDE role at a foreign company, building "experience working hands-on while reading product documentation written in English" seems to be more effective in the hiring process than "getting better at English before applying." The reality is closer to the understanding that what is being evaluated is not English itself, but whether you can convert technical information in English into business value.

In which situations does an FDE use English in practice?

An FDE's use of English is concentrated in reading product documentation and asynchronous written communication with overseas teams. Reading and writing carry far more weight than impromptu spoken English in meetings.

When you break down the situations where English is needed, it becomes clear that the required skill is not "spoken English." The following is a mapping of typical situations where English arises in FDE work and the skills actually tested in each.

In which situations does an FDE read English documentation?

The largest share of an FDE's English use is input: reading product documentation and technical blogs. Primary sources of information that directly affect decisions, such as data platforms, authentication and permission design, and LLM API specification changes, are published in English.

Japanese explanatory articles are convenient, but there is a time lag before publication, and conditions and constraints can be dropped in the summarization process. Since FDEs are in a position where they must answer clients' "can it be done or not" questions quickly, whether they can verify primary sources themselves determines the speed and accuracy of their answers.

How do the situations where FDEs write English differ from those where they speak it?

FDE's English output is mostly written language, where accuracy matters more than responsiveness, so it is less demanding than conversation. The main battleground is asynchronous writing such as Pull Request comments, bug reports, and requests for specification confirmation.

Written English allows time to consult a dictionary and to reread your draft. If you structure it into a standard format (context → observed behavior → expected behavior → reproduction steps), your intent will come across even with a limited vocabulary. English meetings, on the other hand, are information-dense with technical terms and abbreviations flying around, so it makes sense to treat them as an area you can put off for later.

Do FDEs speak English at customer sites in Japan?

Opportunities for FDEs to speak English at customer sites within Japan are limited, and their main role is translating and bridging business terminology. Customers describe their business in Japanese, and the FDE connects it to English product specifications.

This conversion from "Japanese business requirements → English product concepts" is precisely the FDE's added value, and it is a skill distinct from fluency in spoken English. In setups like VACAN Technologies, which primarily support domestic customers, English functions not as a tool for external communication but as a tool for acquiring technical information.

What level of English proficiency is required for an FDE?

As a guideline, the English proficiency required for an FDE is at the level where practical work can begin with reading comprehension at roughly CEFR B2 and conversation at B1. Reading and speaking skills do not need to be at the same level.

CEFR (Common European Framework of Reference for Languages) is an international standard that indicates language proficiency across six levels from A1 to C2 (Source: Council of Europe, "Common European Framework of Reference for Languages" https://www.coe.int/en/web/common-european-framework-reference-languages, retrieved 2026-08-07). B2 is positioned as the stage where one can understand the main points of complex texts in one's field and interact with native speakers with reasonable naturalness.

What reading comprehension level does an FDE need?

The first skill an FDE needs is reading comprehension, and CEFR B2 is enough to follow product documentation without a dictionary. Technical English uses a limited vocabulary and formulaic sentence structures, so it is easier to reach than general English.

Technical documents rarely use subjunctive mood or metaphors, and the same verbs and structures are repeated. Conversely, because product-specific concepts (ontology, pipeline, lineage, etc.) carry the core of the meaning, mastering product-specific vocabulary improves reading speed more than expanding general vocabulary.

What TOEIC score is a good guideline?

For TOEIC Listening & Reading, a score of 785 or above is considered equivalent to CEFR B2 and serves as one guideline for FDE reading comprehension. However, a TOEIC score is proof of English ability, not reading comprehension of technical English itself.

The correspondence between TOEIC Listening & Reading and CEFR is published by IIBC (Institute for International Business Communication), the administering body in Japan (Source: IIBC, "TOEIC Program 各テストのCEFRとの対応関係" https://www.iibc-global.org/toeic/official_data/toeic_cefr.html, retrieved 2026-08-07). The approximate correspondence is as follows, along with how each level relates to FDE work.

CEFRApproximate TOEIC L&R scoreRelevance to FDE work
A2225–545Assumes translation tools. Verifying primary sources is difficult
B1550–780Can read documentation with a dictionary. Practical minimum for domestic FDEs
B2785–940Can follow primary sources independently. Comfort zone for domestic FDEs / entry point for foreign companies
C1945–990Can lead discussions in English meetings. Comfort zone for FDEs at foreign companies

Note that scores and practical ability can diverge. Even with a high TOEIC score, you cannot read documentation without knowing product-specific vocabulary; conversely, someone with a TOEIC score in the 600s may read English documentation in their area of responsibility without any trouble. Such cases often occur in the field.

What level of speaking and writing skills are needed?

An FDE can function with speaking ability at around CEFR B1, compensating for gaps by preparing domain-specific vocabulary and set phrases. Rather than perfect English conversation, the ability to state key points concisely without omitting anything has greater practical value.

In regular English meetings, what you need to say is often predetermined. If you prepare templates in English covering three points—progress, blockers, and next requests—the burden of improvisation drops significantly. The same applies to written English: the more reusable standard phrases you accumulate within your team, the lower the effective level of English proficiency you actually need.

A field perspective: many people who give up on becoming an FDE because of English are actually measuring themselves against "someone who runs meetings in English." But what is required first in practice is reading English documentation and answering customer questions in Japanese. Simply moving where you place the hurdle, I feel, would allow considerably more people to get started.

How should aspiring FDEs study English?

For aspiring FDEs, the order most directly connected to practical work is to progress from reading comprehension to writing, and finally to speaking. Rather than generic English conversation materials, using documentation for the products you want to work with is the shortcut.

Aligning your study order with frequency of use in actual work maximizes return on investment. Below is a study roadmap for the stage of aiming to become an FDE.

StageGoalConcrete actionsMilestone
Stage 1Independent readingRead the official documentation of the cloud/data platform you use in English. Do not switch to the Japanese versionGrasp the overview without a dictionary
Stage 2Consolidating technical vocabularyManage product-specific terms (ontology, lineage, idempotency, etc.) in a list of 50–100 wordsUnderstand frequently used terms without translating
Stage 3Written outputWrite GitHub Issue and Pull Request comments in English. Create standard templatesConvey your intent in 3–5 sentences
Stage 4Listening and speakingWatch product conference videos in English, first with subtitles, then without. Script your statements for regular meetings in advanceCan report progress and make requests in English

Is there a fastest way to improve reading comprehension?

FDE reading comprehension improves fastest not through generic materials but by continuously reading the official documentation of the products you are responsible for, in English. Documentation for the same product repeats vocabulary and sentence structures, so the learning curve is steep.

Specifically, it is effective to fix your browser's language setting to English and cut off any escape route to the Japanese version. Rather than looking up every unknown word on the spot, first infer the meaning paragraph by paragraph, and consult a dictionary only for parts that affect your judgment. This reading approach is the same as the "deliver an answer within the time limit" way of working in actual practice.

How can I develop my English writing skills?

For FDEs, English writing skills can be developed efficiently through templating and actually posting in public settings. Not having to think up text from scratch every time is the key to sustaining the practice.

The following three approaches are easy to put into practice.

  • Create a bug report template: Fix the four items—Environment / Expected / Actual / Steps to reproduce—in English.
  • Post to OSS issues in English: Start with small typo fixes or documentation improvements, and learn expressions from review comments.
  • Write Pull Request descriptions in English internally: Even on Japanese-speaking teams, you can continue this as personal practice, and it places little burden on readers.

When should I start working on listening and speaking skills?

For FDEs, listening and speaking skills can be addressed after reading comprehension and writing skills have stabilized, and this priority still leaves plenty of time. Opportunities to discuss things in English only become necessary at the stage when you actually work with foreign companies or overseas teams.

As learning materials, conference talks and engineer-oriented webinars for the products you are responsible for are suitable. Since you have already built vocabulary through reading, it is easy to follow the content from audio alone, and reinforcing business knowledge progresses at the same time. If you use online English conversation lessons, framing them as "explaining your own project in English every time" rather than free talk connects them to actual work.

Can I apply for an FDE role even if my English skills are insufficient?

You can apply for FDE roles even with insufficient English skills, and FDE job postings at domestic companies often do not place English at the center of selection. What is prioritized is the ability to understand the customer's business and see a working product through to completion.

The weight of English requirements varies greatly depending on the type of job posting. Determining this at the stage of choosing where to apply keeps your preparation focused.

Job typeWeight of English requirementsPoints emphasized in selection
FDE or similar roles at domestic SaaS companies and businessesLow to medium (a plus)Customer relations, implementation speed, business understanding
FDE roles at domestic consulting and DX support firmsMediumProblem-framing ability, commitment to results
FDE roles at Japanese offices of foreign vendorsHigh (a prerequisite)Internal collaboration in English, technical skill
FDE roles hired directly by overseas headquartersVery highDiscussion in English, global experience

How can I compensate for insufficient English skills during selection?

The fastest way to compensate for insufficient English skills in FDE selection is to show concrete examples of turning English primary sources into work outcomes. Facts about your actions, not scores, serve as evidence of learning motivation and practical aptitude.

For example, an experience like "checked a feature with no Japanese information in the official documentation and worked out a configuration that met the customer's requirements" demonstrates reading comprehension more eloquently than a TOEIC score. In addition, being able to describe your ongoing learning as a concrete plan (what, by when, to what level) works as an explanation of your growth potential.

Is it possible to be hired on the premise of improving English skills after joining?

FDE is a role where hiring on the premise of improving English skills after joining can happen, and the work itself becomes an environment for learning English. Because the need to read English documents arises every day, skills take root faster than through self-study.

The core value of an FDE lies in getting into the customer's workplace, discovering problems, implementing solutions, and making them stick. As long as this core is met, English tends to be treated as a variable element that can be filled in on the job. Conversely, even someone fluent in English will not function as an FDE if they have no interest in the customer's business.

On-the-ground perspective: In FDE hiring, what ultimately matters is not an English score but, I feel, "the attitude of not leaving things you don't understand unresolved." The habit of consulting English primary sources is merely that attitude expressed as action. In terms of order, the understanding closest to actual practice is: first, the ability to tackle the customer's problems head-on, and second, the reading comprehension that supports it.

English is not a wall blocking the entrance to FDE work, but an amplifier that determines your growth rate as an FDE. If you aim for FDE roles at domestic companies, start with reading comprehension; if you aim for Palantir-style foreign FDE roles, build up step by step toward the required level with reading and writing as the premise.
