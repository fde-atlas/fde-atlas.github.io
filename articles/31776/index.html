Palantir: Field Problems, and How They Make It into the Product

Original title: Palantir：现场的问题，怎样进入产品

pafa.github.io · 2019-04-08 · 11 min read

Summary

Drawing on Palantir's public records, this article examines how field-discovered problems enter products. It covers the 2013 Phoenix failure at a bank caused by a missing timestamp treated as the 1970 epoch; Shyam Sankar's 2024 recollection of the Nexus Peering redesign, initially rejected until Mark Scianna built a prototype; and Compute Modules (2025), which turned a custom service into a general container interface. It also analyzes the Dev/Delta role split, Apollo's automated delivery creating new coordination seams, and S-1 figures showing early Acquire customers generated $0.6M revenue against a $65.4M contribution loss.

Key points

  • Phoenix failed when a missing timestamp defaulted to 1970, exhausting memory at a bank deployment.
  • Palantir's 2019 material distinguishes Dev (product development) from Delta (forward deployed software engineers).
  • The Nexus Peering redesign was rejected until a prototype gave reviewers something concrete to evaluate.
  • Compute Modules changed who controls operation, letting users run their own programs on the platform.
  • Apollo automated releases but made cross-team coordination of user experience harder, per Sankar.

The software worked fine in the test environment, but at a bank it was brought down by a single blank data field.

Former Palantir engineer Vinoo Ganesh recalled in September 2026 that in 2013 he worked on Phoenix, a trading data storage system. In a later bank deployment, a missing time field caused the program to treat it as the 1970 epoch, request a huge number of time segments, and eventually exhaust memory. The bank was not named and the failure date was not disclosed. He later went to the customer site to fix the problem, working directly with the Forward Deployed Engineers using Phoenix; these engineers then applied it to business that had not originally been in scope, prompting the product team to reconsider the platform's capabilities. 1

This is not a story of "the engineer showed up on site, so the software got better." Testing for abnormal inputs should have been done anyway; if data representing real work had entered testing earlier, some failures might have been caught in the office. The real question worth asking is: who is responsible for bringing the missing facts in, and who can make them change the next delivery?

Palantir's organizational arrangement attempts to carry two outcomes at once: making one customer's work succeed, and enabling the same product to serve more customers later. The two sometimes reinforce each other and sometimes directly conflict. This chapter follows that conflict through three kinds of material: explicit role divisions, recollections by named engineers who raised product objections, and the formation process of a shared capability. They come from different projects and do not add up to a single customer delivery.

One customer's outcome, and one product's responsibility

In April 2019, Palantir publicly described its core software engineers as Dev, assigned to product development, and its Forward Deployed Software Engineers as Delta, assigned to business development. Dev builds a capability for multiple customers, while Delta organizes multiple capabilities to achieve one customer's technical goals. The company also allowed forward deployed teams to contribute code to the core product, though larger requests still had to be coordinated with the roadmap and reviewed by the product team. 2

"Business development" easily evokes sales, but the responsibilities described in this material include actual building. Conversely, being able to write code does not mean the forward deployed team can freely change the shared product. The customer needs their work restored today, but the platform must remain accountable to other customers tomorrow; the two cannot be managed with a single task list.

If a feature meets its specification on its own, but several features combined still cannot complete the customer's work, who handles that gap? If an engineer makes a special modification for the customer at hand, who decides whether it deserves to enter the shared version? The distinction between Dev and Delta lies precisely between these two questions.

A 2020 company interview made this responsibility more concrete. Forward deployed engineer Brian remotely served a U.S. Department of Defense customer during the pandemic, building workflows on the Gotham platform while handling production failure diagnosis, fix deployment, stability monitoring, and customer coordination. A "workflow" here means the actual steps by which software helps people get something done. 3 Working remotely did not prevent him from owning operational outcomes; office location alone does not define the front line.

In 2022 the company introduced the Deployment Strategist, internally called Echo: a role focused on understanding the business, selecting problems, and driving adoption, with duties overlapping Delta's. 4 So what this chapter discusses is never a universal engineer, but how three kinds of attention combine: what the customer actually needs to accomplish, how the system implements it, and which parts deserve to become product in the long run.

There is a difficulty here that departmental charts easily obscure. The field sees new problems first, but does not necessarily have the most power to change the product; the core team has the most power to change the product, but does not necessarily feel the consequences of problems first. The organization needs to let evidence travel across that distance, not merely add more layers of requirement handoff.

When the product team does not accept the field's judgment

In 2024, Palantir's chief technology officer Shyam Sankar recalled one such dispute. Quality engineer Mark Scianna, who had long worked with network-restricted usage environments, proposed redesigning Nexus Peering. The name refers to a capability for sharing and synchronizing data across systems; the company's public product documentation states that when disconnected, data can be queued and transmitted once the connection is restored. 5 This explains why network conditions could become a product problem, but the full design of that year cannot be reconstructed from the later documentation. The proposal initially did not win support from the engineering team, and he and several colleagues then built a prototype. Sankar said the prototype's successor still supports the company's operations. The original text does not give the exact year of the redesign, its full acceptance, or customer outcomes, nor does it describe Mark's position at the time as an FDE. 6

The value of this episode lies not in proving that "bypassing process is always right," but in giving feedback a concrete shape. The dispute was not a customer requesting a feature that headquarters scheduled as ordered; it was a conflict between the situation of field users and the existing product judgment. The prototype gave the discussion one more object that could be examined.

We still cannot conclude from one executive's successful recollection that all similar claims should be accepted. There may have been just as many suggestions that were not adopted, and the prototype may have sacrificed maintainability. The material provides no such comparisons. The narrower takeaway that can be preserved is: when a team suspects the existing product cannot meet real usage conditions, merely putting an opinion into the requirements pool may not be enough to change the judgment; a way to validate the new approach is also needed.

For managers, the key choice is not favoring headquarters or the field, but deciding what evidence they are willing to let change a decision. Field teams must explain where the obstacle occurs, under what conditions it appears, and why the existing solution cannot handle it; product teams must explain their concerns about compatibility, maintenance, or resources. A small prototype can help both sides distinguish "truly impossible" from "not yet verified as worth doing." This part is analysis drawn from the case, not a reconstruction of the meetings or decision processes of the time.

Phoenix and Nexus Peering leave us with different messages. The former shows that customer work relayed indirectly may miss critical inputs; the latter shows that even when information has traveled back, the organization may not be willing to change the product accordingly. Being close to the customer solved the opportunity to discover problems, but did not automatically solve the power to choose which problems matter.

Turning bespoke service into a capability customers can use

Even if a proposal is finally accepted, productization is not over. Making the original project run, and making the next user able to use it independently, are two different doors.

In March 2025, Palantir developers reviewed the formation of Compute Modules: satellite image processing workloads fluctuated sharply, requiring automatic scaling of compute resources. The team had built a bespoke service in 2022, but found it hard for users to run on their own, so they extended the existing capability into a general container interface. A container can be roughly understood as a package that bundles a program together with the runtime environment it needs. The article lists subsequent customer models and uses with Cohere models; at publication, the capability was still marked Beta in cloud environments, meaning testing stage. 7

The most explanatory change here is the change in rights of use and operational capability. Under the first arrangement, the customer depended on the vendor to run a special service; the second arrangement tried to let users bring their own programs into the platform. This is not copying a piece of code to more people, but redesigning what users can control and what the platform still guarantees for them.

The greater the freedom, the more responsibility rests on the interface. A program operated only by the original team can rely on people familiar with the code to catch unsuitable inputs; a shared capability must instead let unfamiliar users know how to connect, how to find errors, and how to choose runtime resources. Some of the problems once solved through working together need to become conditions the product itself can express. Otherwise, so-called openness merely hands the troubleshooting labor over to the customer.

This public record goes one step beyond "field feedback drives product innovation": it identifies the limitations of the original arrangement, explains the direction of the change, and states the subsequent use. But it does not disclose the release date of each version, the customer maintenance effort, or the specific FDE titles of team members. Therefore, it is mechanism evidence of how a shared product took shape; it cannot be used to calculate how much revenue FDEs generated, let alone serve as the subsequent product history of Phoenix.

The three pieces of material can be placed into the same judgment map, while keeping their distinct identities: Phoenix shows us how to discover that an assumption is distorted; Nexus Peering shows us that feedback can be rejected; Compute Modules shows us that even a validated approach may require changing how it is used. No single piece alone proves the complete closed loop from the first field discovery to benefits reaching all customers.

Making releases faster can also create new distance

The more shared products there are, the more one must handle the differing conditions of each customer environment.

In its 2021 annual report, Palantir stated that Apollo was initially used to continuously deliver its own software to customer environments, and began being offered externally as a commercial solution in 2021. A public 2022 demo explained that upgrades and, when necessary, rollbacks must comply with pre-defined conditions. 89 In plain language, a new version cannot simply be delivered; it may depend on another feature being updated first, or it may run into operating conditions the customer cannot change at that moment.

This looks like handing over to the product what engineers repeatedly verified, but automation has another side. In 2024, Sankar recalled that after Apollo allowed teams to release independently, coordinating the overall user experience actually became harder, and the company adjusted its frontend code organization and product responsibilities. 6

One act of productization removes old waiting but may create new seams. Teams finding it easier to hand off their own components does not mean customers find it easier to use the components together. If each team is still measured only on how fast it releases, customer outcomes may once again fall into no one's responsibility.

So shared products and field engineering are not a simple trade-off. Products take on repetitive work, freeing the field to tackle new problems; but new combinations of products may in turn require field validation. What is worth tracking is whether the same problems decrease and whether the remaining problems change—not merely whether the number of field staff rises or falls.

Engineering investment happens first; returns can only happen afterward

This way of organizing carries a bill that cannot be omitted.

Palantir's 2020 listing documents classified customers into Acquire, Expand, and Scale, corresponding to winning, expanding, and scaling respectively. The group of customers classified as Acquire at the end of 2019 generated $600,000 in revenue that year, with a contribution loss of $65.4 million. Contribution loss is, under the company's defined methodology, revenue minus delivery and sales-and-marketing-related expenses; it excludes stock-based compensation and is not net loss. 10

This set of account figures shows that engineering and sales investment can come far earlier than returns, and cannot be treated as the cost of a single FDE or a single project. The Scale category itself requires a certain revenue level and positive contribution, so one cannot infer from this group's performance that all early investments will eventually succeed.

Operators need to judge two things separately: whether the work for current customers is worth continued investment, and whether the current investment leaves behind a capability usable next time. Both can hold at once, or only one. A customer being well served while the supplier accumulates no shared capability may still be a reasonable business—it just needs to be managed as a service investment; a promising platform improvement that current customers cannot yet use cannot be justified to them by future value alone.

Platform maturity, expansion of the customer base, pricing, and customer selection can all change this calculus. Public materials are insufficient to attribute the company's financial changes solely to FDEs. Organization and project economics continue in the business chapter; what must be remembered here is that field insight is not free, and product backflow does not happen on enthusiasm alone.

How to decide at the next product meeting

Below is a short exercise constructed from the mechanisms in this chapter, not an actual decision record of the companies mentioned above.

A customer needs to run their own model. The field team has already connected it with ad hoc scripts, but every update requires the original engineer. The product team must decide: continue as a paid custom service, or build a general-purpose integration capability?

Known or still unknownIllustrative judgmentConditions that would change the judgment
The customer's current work is genuinely blocked; the workaround can restore usabilityFirst clarify the owner and support scope of the workaround to protect existing workIf the workaround cannot meet the customer's permission or operating requirements, usage cannot continue to expand
Only this one customer has made a similar request, and the interface still changes frequentlyIt can remain on the customer side for now, with a scheduled next review, without immediately committing to a shared productOnly when other customers show similar input and control needs is there a stronger reuse case
Multiple customers need the same integration, but under different operating conditionsBuild a trial shared interface and test it against another customer's conditionsIf the underlying code still has to change every time, the shared portion should be re-divided

This answer does not require every request to enter the product. It requires every choice to have a clear destination: customer-side service, shared capability, or stopping investment. Another reasonable option is for the customer to build the interface themselves while the supplier provides stable platform support; as long as the capability and ongoing costs genuinely exist, there is no need to force co-development for the sake of the FDE model.

Return to the blank time field in the bank. One error can end with one patch, or it can lead to more realistic testing, better interfaces, or new product judgment. The difference lies not in giving the troubleshooter a resounding title, but in whether the organization lets those who see problems propose changes, lets the owners of shared products make trade-offs with evidence, and is willing to test whether the next customer truly saves a step.
