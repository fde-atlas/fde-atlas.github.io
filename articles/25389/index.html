How to Approach Legacy System Modernization: 5 Steps and Failure-Avoidance Strategies Practiced by FDEs

Original title: レガシーシステム刷新の進め方は？FDEが実践する5ステップと失敗回避策

FDE総研 · 2026-09-16 · 11 min read

Summary

This FDE Soken article lays out a five-step playbook for legacy system modernization: visualization, prioritization, approach selection, phased migration, and consolidation. Citing METI's DX Report (up to ¥12 trillion annual losses past 2025) and Fowler's Strangler Fig pattern, it argues most failures stem from undocumented exception handling, poor data quality, and absent decision-makers rather than weak engineering. It compares replace, rewrite, and phased migration, and describes how FDEs embed on-site, build prototypes from real data within two weeks, and hand off to in-house teams. It closes with metrics: rollback rates during migration and DORA's Four Keys afterward.

Key points

  • Five steps: visualization, prioritization, approach selection, phased migration, consolidation.
  • Prioritize by business impact, change frequency, and technical risk; leave stable old systems alone.
  • Top failure causes: hidden exception logic, dirty data, and missing business-side decision-makers.
  • Choose approach by downtime tolerance, spec clarity, and differentiation; mix methods per functional block.
  • FDEs observe work, profile real data, and demo prototypes in two weeks to surface hidden assumptions.

How should legacy system modernization be approached?

In practice, the standard approach to legacy modernization is a five-step process: "visualization → prioritization → method selection → phased migration → consolidation."

Modernization projects that start writing code right away almost invariably stall midway. The "DX Report — Overcoming the '2025 Cliff' of IT Systems and Full-Scale Deployment of DX," published by the Ministry of Economy, Trade and Industry in September 2018, warned that leaving legacy systems unaddressed could result in economic losses of up to 12 trillion yen per year after 2025. Modernization is not simply about "rebuilding because it's old"; it begins with identifying where the business is actually bottlenecked.

StepMain ActivitiesTypical DurationCompletion Criteria
1. Current-state visualizationInventory of system assets, data, and business flows2–6 weeksThe inputs, outputs, and data flows of core business processes can be explained in a single diagram
2. PrioritizationNarrow down targets by business impact × technical debt2–4 weeksThe three-way classification of modernize/keep/discard has been approved by decision-makers
3. Method selectionDecide on replace/rewrite/phased migration2–4 weeksRisks and costs for each method are documented
4. Phased migrationFeature-by-feature extraction and parallel operation3–18 monthsDependence on the old system is decreasing feature by feature
5. ConsolidationOperational handover, documentation, talent development1–3 monthsThe field team can handle changes without external support

What should be done in the first two steps (visualization and prioritization)?

In Steps 1 and 2, inventory your assets and assess business impact, then separate what to modernize from what to keep before anything else.

The most valuable outcome of visualization is not the source code but understanding "where data is created and where it gets rewritten." In legacy environments, it is not uncommon to find structures where a department's unmanaged Excel macros reference intermediate tables updated overnight by core-system batches. If you cut over without uncovering these hidden dependencies, you will face unexplained numerical discrepancies after migration.

For prioritization, it is easiest to rank targets along the following three axes.

  • Business impact — Would stopping it halt sales, billing, or shipping?
  • Change frequency — Have modification requests been concentrated in the last 12 months?
  • Technical risk — Does it depend on end-of-life languages, middleware, or hardware?

Among practitioners, it is often said that the decision to "leave low-change-frequency, low-technical-risk areas alone for now, even if they're old" is actually a key success factor. Plans to modernize everything exhaust the field team's focus before the budget runs out.

How should the remaining three steps (method selection, phased migration, and consolidation) be run?

In Steps 3 through 5, decide on the method, extract features one by one, and finish by handing operations over to the in-house team.

The backbone of phased migration is the Strangler Fig pattern, proposed by Martin Fowler in 2004. The idea is to place a routing layer in front of the old system, switch call destinations starting with newly built features, and gradually "strangle" the old system by shrinking it. Compared to a big-bang migration, its greatest advantage is that each stage can be rolled back.

Many plans do not explicitly include consolidation as a project phase, but omitting it creates a second legacy: "the new system runs, but no one can fix it." The completion criterion for handover should be not whether documentation exists, but whether an in-house team member has successfully released a change on their own.

Why does legacy system modernization fail?

Most failures come down to two factors, not a lack of technical skill: estimating work in areas with unknown specifications, and the absence of decision-makers.

What are the technical failure patterns?

On the technical side, undocumented exception handling and data migration quality issues significantly inflate estimated effort.

In systems that have run for around 20 years, the happy-path specifications may be documented, but exception handling often exists only "in the heads of the people in charge." Even reading the implementation, you cannot tell why a particular branch exists. As a result, exception handling gets dropped in the new system, leading to post-migration incidents where only transactions under specific conditions fail to process.

Data migration has the same problem. Old systems have loose input validation, so dirt accumulates: phone numbers in address fields, obsolete category codes still present in active records. The more correctly you implement validation in the new system, the more records get rejected during migration. Projects that plan only one migration rehearsal lose their schedule here.

Failure PatternTypical SymptomsPreventive Measures
Missing hidden exception handlingTests pass, but errors occur only for specific customersReconcile output differences through parallel operation of old and new systems
Unexpected data quality issuesRecords rejected in migration rehearsal are several times the estimateProfile data early and agree on a cleansing policy beforehand
Overlooked performance requirementsOnly the month-end batch fails to completeRun load tests early using peak-period data volumes (month-end, fiscal closing)
Unknown external integration specificationsRework during connectivity testing with partner systemsMake integration-point inventory a mandatory item in the visualization phase

What are the organizational and decision-making failure patterns?

On the organizational side, development proceeds without an authority who can decide business requirements, and the inability to freeze specifications leads to failure.

A modernization inherently includes the work of "deciding what to stop doing." If you try to reproduce all current operations, costs balloon and you merely rebuild the legacy structure with new technology. But the development team cannot make the decision to discontinue business processes. Here, whether a business-side decision-maker who can make decisions weekly is present becomes the turning point for progress.

On the ground this is often expressed as "requirements definition never ends," but in many cases the reality is not that requirements cannot be decided; rather, the person who decides them does not attend meetings, or the structure allows decisions to be overturned later.

How do you choose a modernization approach (replace, rewrite, or phased migration)?

The approach is determined by three conditions: "tolerance for business downtime," "how well specifications are known," and "payback period." For most projects, phased migration is the realistic solution.

Can you compare the three approaches in a table?

The three approaches differ in the balance of cost, risk, and duration; no one-size-fits-all option exists.

PerspectiveReplace (package/SaaS adoption)Rewrite (rebuild)Phased migration (Strangler Fig)
Suitable situationsBusiness can be standardized, not a source of competitive advantageSpecifications are clear, directly tied to business differentiationMission-critical systems that cannot stop, unclear specifications
Initial costMedium (license + implementation)LargeMedium (dual-operation costs for parallel running)
Impact of failureBurden of fitting the business to the packageFull-scale reworkRollback limited to the affected function only
Typical duration6-18 months12-36 months3+ months per function, overall longer term
Main risksCost overrun from customization bloatQuality incidents from specification omissionsMigration stalls midway and dual maintenance becomes the norm

The weakness of phased migration is in the last row of the table. During the period of parallel operation of old and new systems, operating costs temporarily increase. If you start without setting a deadline for "when the old system will be shut down," dual maintenance becomes the default path.

In what order should the selection criteria be applied?

Applying the conditions in the order of downtime tolerance → how well specifications are known → differentiation factors reduces hesitation in selection.

  • Can the business be stopped? — If not, phased migration is effectively a given
  • Can specifications be reconstructed from documents and current code? — If not, a rewrite is high-risk
  • Is the function a source of competitive advantage? — If not, prioritize replacement with a package/SaaS
  • Over how many years is payback evaluated? — The shorter the payback period, the better a finely-granulated approach fits compared to a one-shot modernization

In practice, rather than applying a single approach to one system, a design that varies the approach by functional block is often adopted. For example, accounting and HR are replaced with SaaS, only the areas directly tied to the company's product design are rewritten, and the integrations in between are absorbed through phased migration.

How does an FDE (Forward Deployed Engineer) work in legacy modernization?

An FDE is stationed at the customer's site and, by having one person (or a small team) go back and forth between business understanding and implementation, bridges the divide between requirements definition and development.

FDE is a role established by Palantir, characterized by engineers embedding themselves in the customer's business operations and building software directly while observing the business. Compared to the traditional division of labor where "business consultants write requirements and a development company implements them," information degradation through message-passing is less likely to occur.

What happens in an FDE's first two weeks on-site?

In the first two weeks, the FDE prioritizes business observation and data connections, grasping the current state through real data rather than documents.

There is a certain pattern to how an FDE ramps up.

PeriodFDE's main activitiesDeliverables
Days 1-3Sit alongside the field and observe actual work proceduresRough business flow notes and a list of questions
Days 4-7Connect to production-equivalent data and profile itReport on the actual state of data quality
Week 2Show a minimal working prototype on-siteTouchable screens and query results

The aim here is not to produce a highly polished design document but to surface misalignments in understanding early. When you show something working to the field, information like "that input field, we actually don't use it" comes out—information that would never appear in a document review. This kind of information rarely surfaces in requirements definition review meetings.

How are roles divided between the FDE, in-house SIers, and in-house development teams?

A realistic arrangement, including establishment, is for the FDE to handle business touchpoints and initial implementation while the in-house team handles long-term maintenance.

If the FDE builds everything alone, a black box remains when the contract ends. Therefore, in modernization projects it is desirable to involve in-house engineers as pairs from an early stage and share the background of design decisions.

RoleMain responsibilitiesFocus during modernization phases
FDEBusiness understanding, translating priorities, initial implementationSteps 1-4
In-house development teamContinuous improvement, operations, standardizationSteps 4-5
External vendor (SIer, etc.)Large-scale migration work, investigation of existing assetsSteps 1 and 4

For engineers interested in the FDE role, legacy modernization is a setting that tests "the ability to understand the language of the business and negotiate priorities" even more than technical skill. This aspect likely demands different muscles than a product-development-centered career.

What metrics should modernization outcomes be measured by?

Outcomes should be measured by separating "leading indicators of migration progress" and "post-completion business and operational metrics," maintaining the ability to course-correct midway.

What leading indicators should be watched during migration?

During execution, tracking the reduction in old-system dependency and the rollback occurrence rate allows early detection of signs of delay.

If progress is judged only by "consumption rate of planned effort," things can look healthy even when substantive migration is not advancing. In phased migration, the following metrics capture the actual state well.

  • Ratio of transactions via the old system — Is it declining monthly?
  • Migrated functions / total — Do not change the definition of the denominator midway
  • Rollback occurrence rate — If high, a sign that the units being carved out are too large
  • Number of output discrepancies during parallel operation — Count of mismatches when reconciling old and new results

What business and operational metrics should be watched after completion?

After completion, measure improvements in development speed and operating costs, and verify whether the modernization actually improved business agility.

As a common metric for development productivity, the "Four Keys" proposed by DORA (DevOps Research and Assessment) is often referenced.

MetricDescriptionExpected direction from modernization
Deployment frequencyNumber of production releasesIncrease
Lead time for changesTime from commit to productionShorter
Change failure ratePercentage of releases causing failuresLower
Time to restore serviceTime to recover from failuresShorter

In addition, on the operations side, metrics such as annual maintenance costs, the number of dependencies on products past end of support, and monthly manual operation hours are effective. On the business side, shortening the time from planning a new feature to delivering it is the most persuasive. This is because the purpose of modernization is not simply "making things new," but regaining the speed at which the business can change.

Note that these metrics cannot demonstrate the extent of improvement unless they are measured before the modernization begins. I recommend including baseline measurement before starting work as part of Step 1's visualization.
