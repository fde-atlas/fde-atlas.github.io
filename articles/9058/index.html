FDE Interview: Turning Project Evidence into On-the-Spot Judgment

Original title: FDE 面试：把项目证据转化为现场判断

FDE 成长手册 · 2026-08-19 · 8 min read

Summary

This article presents a preparation framework for Forward Deployed Engineer interviews, arguing that candidates should turn real project evidence into a defensible judgment process rather than memorize scripted answers. It recommends building an evidence matrix from each target job description, then practicing six scenario types: project deep-dives, customer discovery and scoping, system and AI design, coding and debugging, evals/security/operations, and collaboration stories. It provides a three-tier project presentation (90 seconds, 8 minutes, 30 minutes), a one-page project interview card, and a 14-day preparation schedule. The article also frames interviews as two-way due diligence, suggesting reverse questions to assess role fit, and stresses never fabricating metrics or contributions.

Key points

  • Core thesis: convert project evidence into a questionable judgment process, not memorized answers
  • Build an evidence matrix per target JD to expose gaps before applying
  • Six practice scenarios: project deep-dive, discovery, system/AI design, coding, evals/ops, behavior stories
  • Prepare flagship project at 90-second, 8-minute, and 30-minute depths with openable evidence
  • Treat interviews as due diligence; use reverse questions to judge role fit

The most effective preparation for an FDE (Forward Deployed Engineer, also commonly translated as 前沿部署工程师) interview is not memorizing "standard answers," but practicing real project evidence into a defensible judgment process: how to turn a vague business problem into a deliverable scope, how to make technical trade-offs under constraints, and how to use evidence to decide whether to launch, degrade, or stop.

Verification date: 2026-08-19. The responsibilities and hiring process for FDE/FDSE roles vary by company, team, level, region, and time. This page provides a preparation framework based on public job descriptions and does not claim that any company uses a fixed number of rounds or fixed question types. Before interviewing, defer to the target position's current posting and the recruiter's notices.

First complete a portfolio project with complete evidence. The most useful material in an interview is not another script, but a Discovery Brief, ADRs, eval results, threat models, runbooks, and handover records that you can actually open.

Step 1: Build an evidence matrix for the target role

Title "Step 1: Build an evidence matrix for the target role"

Copy the matrix for every application, using only the exact wording from the target role's current page. Do not assume one company's responsibilities are the standard for the entire industry.

Exact sentence from the job postingThe ability it asks you to proveStrongest project evidenceA decision/conflict you can discussGap and remediation action
[Paste exact sentence][e.g., scoping problems with customers][Discovery Brief link][which scope changed][add one interview drill]
[Paste exact sentence][e.g., delivering production systems][deployment, SLOs, runbook][reliability vs. speed trade-off][add a failure drill]
[Paste exact sentence][e.g., cross-team influence][decision records, handover feedback][how alignment was reached][quantify results or clarify boundaries]

The standard for a completed matrix is not that it's full, but that it exposes gaps. If a key capability only has "I've studied it" with no evidence to point to, add a small experiment, document, or drill before applying.

Six categories of capability scenarios worth preparing

Title "Six categories of capability scenarios worth preparing"

The six scenario categories below come from FDE work itself and are suitable for organizing practice. They do not represent any company's interview arrangement, nor is it guaranteed they will all appear.

1. Project deep-dive

Title "1. Project deep-dive"

Prepare three depths for your flagship project:

  • 90 seconds: the user and problem, your scope, key decisions, results on the same metrics, current limitations.
  • 8 minutes: walk through the decision chain from discovery to handover, and be able to open the evidence on the spot.
  • 30 minutes: withstand continuous follow-up questions, including failure cases, alternatives, the boundary of your contribution, and next steps.

Practice these follow-up questions one by one:

  • How did you know this was a problem worth solving, rather than just the feature the customer first asked for?
  • Which assumption was riskiest? What lowest-cost method did you use to validate it first?
  • What alternatives did you consider? Why didn't you choose the one that looked more advanced?
  • What are the metric's denominator, sample, and baseline? Can the results be reproduced?
  • Which users or data performed worst? How did you protect them at launch?
  • Which piece of feedback changed your scope or architecture?
  • What happens to the system if a dependency fails, costs double, or answers become unusable?
  • Which decisions and implementations on the team were genuinely your responsibility?

When answering, separate facts, judgments, and speculation. You can say "I don't know, but I would first check X, then use Y to decide Z"; don't fill gaps in the story with data that doesn't exist.

2. Customer discovery and scoping

Title "2. Customer discovery and scoping"

Find a partner to play the business owner and run a 25-minute drill with a vague request, e.g., "We want to build an internal knowledge assistant for the customer support team within four weeks." Your goal is not to draw an architecture immediately, but to get a verifiable next step.

Proceed in this order:

  • Outcome: who needs to do what better? Which specific task or decision?
  • Current process: where do inputs come from, who operates it, who receives the output, and how are failures discovered?
  • Baseline: how are current time spent, success rate, rework, or risk measured?
  • Constraints: what are the data permissions, sensitivity levels, system dependencies, budget, timeline, and owners?
  • High-risk assumptions: is the data available, will users adopt it, is automation allowed?
  • Thin-slice scope: which user, data source, and task does version one cover? Be explicit about what you will not do.
  • Acceptance and stopping: what results allow expanding the pilot? What situations require rollback or manual handling?
  • Next step: who provides what data when, or confirms which decision?

Finally, spend two minutes restating your understanding and let the "customer" correct you. After the drill, don't just grade yourself on delivery—check whether you actually obtained the baseline, boundaries, owners, and decision timelines. The

templates/discovery-brief.md

in the repo can serve directly as a recording board.

3. System and AI design

Title "3. System and AI design"

Design downward from the business outcome, not upward from the model by assembling components. On the whiteboard, you can use this order:

  • Define users, key tasks, success metrics, and unacceptable failures.
  • Draw system boundaries, trust boundaries, data flows, and human intervention points.
  • Describe offline/online data paths, identity propagation, tenant isolation, and auditing.
  • Choose the simplest baseline first, then explain when retrieval, tool calls, or an AI agent are needed.
  • Define the offline eval set, release gates, online monitoring, and the feedback loop.
  • Estimate capacity, latency, cost, and external dependencies; design timeouts, retries, idempotency, and degradation.
  • Provide a phased rollout, rollback, and handover plan.
  • Make unknowns explicit, and state which experiments to run first to reduce uncertainty.

A common weak point is drawing only "user → LLM → database" with no identity, permissions, error paths, versioning, evals, or operational ownership. Use

templates/eval-plan.md

to check whether the design is truly acceptable.

4. Coding, integration, and debugging

Title "4. Coding, integration, and debugging"

The specific format depends on the target role's notice. General preparation should cover the engineering tasks FDEs frequently face, not just one type of algorithm exercise:

  • Reading unreliable external APIs, handling pagination, timeouts, rate limiting, retries, and idempotency.
  • Cleaning and validating data with missing values, duplicates, or type drift.
  • Writing small, clear interface boundaries, automated tests, and structured logs that make problems locatable.
  • Reproducing failures with incomplete information, forming hypotheses, and eliminating them step by step with observations.
  • Avoid writing tokens, personal data, or full model inputs and outputs into logs.

During practice, keep a terminal record or a brief retrospective: what was the initial hypothesis, which piece of evidence overturned it, and how does the fix prevent regression. This is closer to real delivery than keeping only the final correct code.

5. Evals, security, and operations

Title "5. Evals, security, and operations"

Prepare to answer "on what basis do you allow it to go live":

  • Where does the eval data come from, and does it represent high-value and high-risk slices?
  • How are metrics computed, and who approved the thresholds based on what risk?
  • Is the LLM judge calibrated against humans? How are disagreements adjudicated?
  • How are privilege escalation, prompt injection, sensitive information, and dangerous tool calls tested?
  • Which thresholds are stop-ship, and which can proceed as a pilot with remediation measures?
  • What do you monitor after release, who responds, and how do you degrade, roll back, and update the regression suite?

Don't treat "no errors seen" as reliability. Prepare an example where you proactively blocked a release, narrowed scope, or added human review—this also demonstrates mature judgment.

6. Customer Collaboration and Behavioral Stories

Heading "6. Customer Collaboration and Behavioral Stories"

Prepare at least six real stories, each including the information you had at the time, the actions you took, evidence of results, and your retrospective:

  • Rewriting a vague request into an acceptance-testable problem;
  • Disagreeing with a customer or internal partner over scope, priorities, or risks;
  • Cutting features under time pressure while holding onto key outcomes;
  • A technical solution or launch failing—how you responded and fixed the mechanism;
  • Persuading a non-reporting team to cooperate on data, permissions, or process changes;
  • Completing a handover so the system runs without depending on you personally.

Avoid writing every conflict as "I explained and they agreed." Strong stories explain the other party's legitimate goals, shared evidence, the concessions you made, and how the final choice was validated.

A Project Interview Card

Heading "A Project Interview Card"

Write only one page per project, for quick review before the interview:

项目：用户与关键任务：原流程与同口径基线：我的个人责任边界：最大的不确定性：我比较过的方案：关键决策及代价：交付的最小范围：质量 / 业务 / 延迟 / 成本 / 安全结果：最重要的失败样本：一次范围或方案变化：上线、降级、回滚与交接：仍未解决的限制：可以现场打开的 3 个证据链接：

The card is a fact index, not a script. In mock interviews, have a partner pick any line and follow up three times in a row to check whether you can return to evidence.

14-Day Preparation Rhythm

Heading "14-Day Preparation Rhythm"

DayTaskEvidence of Completion
Day 1Break down the target JD, build an evidence matrixEvery core responsibility has evidence or a strengthening plan
Days 2–4Fix your flagship project and prepare three levels of explanationLinks accessible; 90-second and 8-minute versions don't go stale
Days 5–6Two customer discovery role-playsDiscovery Brief and partner scoring
Days 7–8Two system/AI designsInclude permissions, evals, failure handling, launch, and trade-offs
Days 9–10Coding, integration, and debugging practiceTests, logs, and retrospective notes
Day 11Deep dive on evals, security, and operationsCan explain thresholds, failures, and stop-ship conditions
Day 12Organize six collaboration storiesFacts, actions, results, and reflections all withstand follow-ups
Day 13One full mock interview, recorded and reviewedQuestion list and prioritized fixes
Day 14Light review and environment checkLocal/online demos, backups, links, and time zone all verified

Two weeks is a sprint schedule, not a promise of skill development. If your portfolio still lacks L2 evidence, go back and fill the gaps in your projects first—don't use repeated mock interviews to mask material gaps.

Judging in Reverse Whether the Role Fits You

Heading "Judging in Reverse Whether the Role Fits You"

Interviews are also due diligence. Choose questions naturally based on the conversation; you don't need to ask all of them:

  • What results should this role deliver in the first 90 days? Who judges success?
  • How are decision rights divided among FDEs, product, sales, research/model teams, and customer engineering teams?
  • What approval thresholds do projects typically pass through from discovery, pilot, to production?
  • When customer speed demands conflict with security or reliability, how does the team decide?
  • Who is responsible for on-call, long-term maintenance, and handover of production systems?
  • What are the actual expectations around on-site customer work, travel, time zones, and parallel projects?
  • What was the reason for the team's most recent scope reduction or project shutdown?

Concrete answers help you judge the role far better than "the pace is fast and the impact is big." Immediately after the interview, record new facts, unanswered questions, and evidence gaps you exposed, then decide whether to continue and how to strengthen your case.

Final Checks

Heading "Final Checks"

  • I can distinguish real customers, pilots, peer testing, and simulated scenarios.
  • I will not fabricate metrics, user feedback, production scale, or personal contributions.
  • For every key number, I know its definition, sample, baseline, version, and limitations.
  • I can show failure paths, not just one successful run.
  • I can go from user outcomes down to code, and from failures back up to business impact.
  • I know when to refuse automation, narrow scope, or require human review.
  • My questions help both sides judge ways of working, rather than reciting content findable on the official website.

When interview retrospectives expose gaps in project evidence, return to the learning path to pick the corresponding stage and strengthen it; when you already have a complete evidence chain, keep practicing with this page's project interview card—no need to keep piling on new projects.
