When to hire your first FDE? A guide to building your Forward Deployed Engineer team

Original title: 第一个 FDE 何时招？前线部署工程师团队搭建指南

FDE Times · 2026-10-09 · 5 min read

Summary

Drawing on sources including Phosai Labs, Rocketlane, Tandem, and First Round Review, this guide explains when startups should hire their first FDE, what to measure, and when to add a lead. The trigger is a third customer requesting an integration the founders have already built twice. Spending over 20% of time on pre-sales signals drift toward sales support, and teams of 5-15 FDEs need an internally promoted FDE Lead managing 2-4 people.

Key points

  • Hire the first FDE when a third customer requests the same integration built twice.
  • Over 20% of time on pre-sales means the role has drifted into sales support.
  • FDEs must return repeated work to the product team or the company drifts toward consulting.
  • At seed and Series A, FDEs should report to engineering, not sales.
  • At 5-15 FDEs, add an FDE Lead managing 2-4 people, ideally promoted internally.

Key takeaways

  • When to hire your first FDE: the founders have already built the same integration twice, and a third customer asks for exactly the same thing again.
  • Watch two metrics: the share of time spent on pre-sales (over 20% means the team has drifted off course), and the ratio of one-off work to reusable work.
  • When the team reaches 5 to 15 people, you need an FDE Lead managing 2 to 4 FDEs, ideally promoted from within the FDE team.

A third customer requests the exact integration the founders have already built twice. Phosai Labs, a services and consulting firm, writes in a hiring guide that this is precisely the right signal to hire your first Forward Deployed Engineer (FDE), and it's far more specific than "the customer needs a lot of technical support."

Now that many people treat FDEs as a cure-all, clear signals like this are all the more valuable. James Honsa, who built the legal engineering team at Ironclad, told First Round Review that FDEs are being packaged as a universal solution, when reality is far more complicated.

In the same article, practitioners emphasized that this model only fits specific stages of a company's development, and it takes a great deal of deliberate design to actually benefit from it.

For developers looking to transition into an FDE role, this directly affects career choices. How a company decides when to hire, whom to hire, what to evaluate, and when to add a lead can tell you whether the job is real engineering or sales support with a new name.

Is repeated demand a hiring signal or a red flag?

There seems to be a contradiction here. Phosai Labs treats multiple customers requesting the same integration as grounds for hiring an FDE. Rocketlane, in discussing whether to hire FDEs, cautions: if many customers want the same feature, the work most likely belongs in the product.

The two views don't actually conflict; they describe the two halves of the same job. The first FDE is hired to take over work the founders keep doing repeatedly. After that, this person must also identify which parts have been repeated enough and hand them back to the product team, rather than manually rebuilding them a fourth time.

If you skip the second half, every new customer becomes a manual project, and the company gradually looks more like a consultancy than a product company.

Take a logistics software startup as an example.

The founder wrote a connector for customer A's ERP system, then rewrote it for customer B. When customer C asks for the same thing, the first FDE should own C's connector. At the same time, this person should document which parts of the three builds were identical, so the product team can turn them into shared modules.

The first FDE: does everything, but must be technically strong

Recruiting from Scratch, a recruiting firm, describes the first FDE as a generalist: running workshops, writing code, maintaining customer relationships, and bringing feedback back inside the company. At this stage no one can share the workload, so a specialist strong in only one area will get stuck.

That said, a generalist doesn't mean weak at everything. Phosai Labs writes that communication skills are just the entry bar; technical depth is what sets people apart. The logic is simple: to identify repetition and move it into the product, you need to understand the architecture well enough to know which parts can be extracted.

The hiring manager must also decide whom this FDE reports to. Phosai Labs recommends that at the seed and Series A stages, FDEs should report to engineering, with a dotted line to product. It also warns against folding FDEs into the go-to-market and sales team, turning them into Solutions Engineers with a new title.

What to evaluate when there's no standard KPI

The easiest number to use is the 20% threshold. According to Phosai Labs, if an FDE spends more than 20% of their time on pre-sales activities, the function has drifted off course. On a 40-hour week, that's 8 hours.

If by Wednesday you've already spent 10 hours doing demos and answering technical questions for unsigned deals, your team is sliding into sales support.

Rather than setting fixed KPIs, Rocketlane proposes a set of questions covering attribution, time, and most importantly: which customer requests are one-off work and which are reusable patterns.

This question tests whether the FDE is doing the second half of the job. If a quarter goes by and the list of repeated work keeps growing while nothing makes it into the product, the FDE is doing the product team's job.

The third metric is workload. Tandem found in its research that FDEs typically handle 3 to 8 customers simultaneously under normal conditions. This is a common range, not a hard cap, but a first FDE juggling 12 customers at once likely has little time to notice what's repeating.

When do you need an FDE Lead?

Recruiting from Scratch puts the milestone at roughly 5 to 15 FDEs. At that size, the team needs structure: career paths, a clear ladder from FDE to Senior FDE to FDE Lead, and a layer of management.

By its description, an FDE Lead manages 2 to 4 FDEs on large or complex customers and develops deployment methodology.

Take a 6-person FDE team. Per Tandem's data, if each person handles 3 to 8 customers, the whole team carries 18 to 48 customers. At that scale, if everyone does their own thing, 48 customers get 48 different deployment approaches. Establishing a unified methodology is exactly the lead's job.

The lead must also decide how work is divided within the team. Tandem found no standard FDE team structure, but in its research, the fastest-growing teams separated pioneering work from replication work.

The question a lead must answer early is: who goes to new customers to experiment, and who carries proven practices to the next customer.

Where should the lead come from? Recruiting from Scratch believes FDE managers should almost always be former Senior FDEs or FDE Leads who are willing to develop people, rather than external hires.

As the team keeps growing, Tandem found that leads managing 15 or more people need capacity planning tools more than management features.

How to read job descriptions and write your resume

When reading an FDE job description, look at three things before the salary. The most telling is the reporting line: reporting to engineering is a good sign, while reporting to sales or customer success warrants follow-up questions.

Second, check whether the description frequently mentions demos, RFPs (requests for proposal), or deal support—often enough that it could exceed 20% of your time. Finally, look for how many customers each person is expected to handle.

In the interview, you can ask directly: "When was the last time something an FDE built for a customer made it into the product?" If no one can answer, the repeated work likely never got handed back to the product, and you'll end up manually building the same thing over and over.

Don't just write on your resume that you're good at communicating with customers—that's merely the entry bar. Instead, describe an experience: you built a feature for one customer, discovered a second customer needed it too, and turned it into a shared component.

This kind of experience demonstrates technical depth, which is exactly what a first FDE team needs most.

The working methods you document starting from your first project may later become the foundation of a deployment methodology you teach others to follow.
