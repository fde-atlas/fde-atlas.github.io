NEC Solution Innovators: The Full Picture of Our Hands-On Generative AI Training

Original title: NECソリューションイノベータ様 生成AI実践型研修の全貌

FDX株式会社 · 2026-09-17 · 16 min read

Summary

This FDX interview case study covers NEC Solution Innovator, an AWS serverless-focused integrator serving automakers, which had to decline generative AI proposals (RAG, AI agents) due to lack of in-house expertise. After comparing four vendors, it chose FDX's flexible, hands-on training built around Dify and AWS Bedrock, where participants built working tools from real field problems. Post-training, the quality of client conversations improved and the team can now proactively pursue AI inquiries within the NEC group. The article also discusses the SIer dilemma of billable-hours revenue shrinking as AI boosts efficiency, the engineer's role in guarding AI risks, the FDE concept of discovering problems inside client operations, and a shift from partial AI adoption toward redesigning whole processes.

Key points

  • Lack of generative AI talent forced NEC Solution Innovator to decline RAG and AI agent proposals.
  • It picked FDX over package-style vendors for flexibility and hands-on case-study training.
  • Training used Dify and AWS Bedrock, with participants building working AI tools from real problems.
  • After training, client conversations improved and the team proactively handles NEC group AI inquiries.
  • The piece examines the SIer billable-hours dilemma, engineer responsibility for AI risk, and the FDE role.

Client: NEC Solution Innovators, Ltd., Third Transport & Service Solutions Division — Director: Yuto Iijima, Senior Professional: Tomoaki Makino Industry: IT solutions and system integration Theme: Generative AI (RAG, AWS Bedrock) × hands-on talent development training Services provided: A case-study-based training program using Dify and AWS Bedrock *Note: This interview was conducted when FDX Inc. was operating under its former name, Arrowsal Technology Inc.*

Introduction

NEC Solution Innovators has an extensive track record in the design and development of AWS serverless solutions. However, amid the rapid evolution of generative AI, the company faced a pressing challenge: "Even when requests for proposals came in, we had no choice but to ask other departments for support."

In this article, we explore how the company transformed generative AI from "knowledge" into "practical capability" through FDX's (formerly Arrowsal Technology) hands-on training program. From the background of the training to its results and the company's future outlook as a system integrator, we cover the full story.

01. Background — A Sense of Crisis: "Having to Turn Down Projects"

Even when requests for proposals came in, we had to rely on other departments for support

NEC Solution Innovators primarily handles system design and development using AWS serverless services for automotive manufacturers. Within this work, the use of generative AI remained limited to a narrow scope, such as coding assistance and research during troubleshooting.

The turning point came with a customer's request for a proposal related to generative AI. Requests began arriving for building RAG systems and AI agents, but the company lacked personnel capable of handling them, and it continued to be forced to pass on such projects.

Not being able to respond even when a request for a proposal came in—that was the biggest issue. We didn't even have an image of how to do it, and precisely because we couldn't imagine it, there was no way to develop the talent. It was also a real shame to be missing out on projects. — NEC Solution Innovators

At the time, technical information and implementation case studies for AWS Bedrock were limited compared to Azure-based services, and engineers capable of working with it were still scarce in the market. Given this situation, the company needed not merely to acquire knowledge of generative AI, but to accumulate practical know-how through case-study-based learning aimed at implementing it into customer operations.

We judged that once we became able to apply it to our customers' operations, we would naturally also acquire know-how applicable to our internal operations. — NEC Solution Innovators

Rising Expectations for AI on the Customer Side as Well

Another factor behind this was change on the customer side—the automotive manufacturers. In the automotive industry, the shift toward SDVs (Software Defined Vehicles) is advancing, increasing quality management costs that differ from the past, such as handling software-caused defects and providing continuous updates. Against this backdrop, quality departments were increasingly looking to AI-powered data analysis and anomaly detection to reduce quality costs and improve operational efficiency.

Our customers come up with plans like "we're going AI-oriented." We work with a sense of crisis that if we don't keep up with that, there will be no work for us. — NEC Solution Innovators

At customer companies, large-scale investments in generative AI and DX were progressing, and the market itself was expanding rapidly. To respond quickly to customer needs in these growth areas, strengthening technical capabilities and proposal capabilities related to generative AI had become an urgent priority.

02. Partner Selection — A "Supportive Attitude" Was the Deciding Factor

In selecting a training partner, the company reportedly compared and considered around four firms. Three of them were introduced through partners and centered on packaged proposals, with limited room for customization.

With your company, there was a sense that you would work closely with us. The other companies said, "We have a package, and it's difficult to deviate from it." You were quite flexible and quick to act, so we thought we'd definitely like to work with you. — NEC Solution Innovators

The goal of the training was clear: "For members of our own organization to become able to lead projects that apply generative AI to customer operations." Not mere technical acquisition—the goal was to develop people who could lead in real-world practice.

03. Conducting the Training — Building "Something That Works" with Dify × Bedrock

The training was conducted as a case-study program combining Dify and AWS Bedrock, in which participating members worked on actual field challenges as their themes. Rather than ending with lectures, the emphasis was placed on hands-on work to build "something that works."

The Surprise of "It's This Easy"—and the Question That Follows

From members using Dify for the first time, many expressed surprise: "You can build flows this easily?" The ability to visually construct workflows was also well received as easy to understand.

At the same time, that very simplicity raised a new question: since customers can use similar tools themselves, "where, as engineers, do we deliver value?"—a fundamental question.

I think the part where generative AI is used is a tool-like portion within the whole. It's confined to specific spots—for example, building things more cheaply—and we can't compete there. So we have to compete on being able to coordinate the whole, and on what form the generative AI portion within that should take. — NEC Solution Innovators

Generative AI itself is merely a technology that plays a part in the overall system. Simply competing on partial optimization—such as "using generative AI to develop more cheaply"—will make differentiation difficult going forward. What matters, we believe, is being able to design and propose where and in what form generative AI should be incorporated, based on a comprehensive view of the entire business and system.

The "sense of progress" and "next challenges" revealed through the hands-on sessions

In the hands-on sessions, each member worked on themes based on real field challenges. Although the period was short and functional issues remained, actually getting AI tools for problem-solving up and running was a major sense of accomplishment.

Actually getting AI tools for problem-solving up and running was a major sense of accomplishment. — NEC Solution Innovator

At the same time, the "next challenges" of applying this to actual projects also became clear: technical areas requiring deeper expertise, such as token volume issues, tuning speed and performance, and calling other tools instead of generative AI to save tokens.

When you really try to apply it to a project—for example, performance or token volume issues—we haven't gone that deep yet, so I think there are still plenty of challenges there. — NEC Solution Innovator

A firsthand sense of the speed of technological evolution

What came through strongly through the training was the speed of technological evolution in the generative AI field. From the time we were running training using Dify, a no-code AI app development tool, the Agent SDK appeared within a short period. Usable technologies and approaches kept expanding one after another, including sub-agents and business automation.

I thought it would be hard to do this in a systematic way, so while it wasn't exactly a breakthrough, I feel we managed to take the step of "just trying it." — NEC Solution Innovator

In a reality where "almost no one can judge what is optimal," the training approach of first getting hands-on experience became the first step toward change.

04. Perceived Changes — "Clearly More Knowledgeable"

The quality of conversations with clients changed

After the training, the number of projects itself did not dramatically increase. There are also cases where clients say they want to do something but it never becomes an actual project. However, a definite change did occur: the quality of conversations with clients itself changed.

Compared to a year ago, the knowledge is clearly different. When clients say something, we now have the intuition to picture things like "we could probably do it this way" or "this might be the bottleneck." Once we get to that point, things can generally move forward. — NEC Solution Innovator

In fact, regarding inquiries about generative AI projects coming from within the NEC Group, the team has shifted to a state where they can proactively approach projects that previously would have had to be passed over. Being able to say "we can do that" was cited as a concrete example.

From a "convenient tool" to an "essential one"

The perception of AI also changed significantly. Before the training, it was seen as merely "a convenient tool," but now the perception has shifted to "whatever form it takes, it's an essential technology we must engage with."

Rather than a convenient tool, it's now an essential one. I think the biggest thing is that we were able to try it out. — NEC Solution Innovator

At the same time, thinking along the lines of "if we assume AI adoption, how could we change this?" has begun to sprout within the organization across various processes. Some voices noted feeling an even greater sense of urgency than before, which is itself proof of the organization's changing mindset.

05. The Enduring Role of "People" in the Generative AI Era

The perception of AI as "an excellent assistant"

What was striking through the interviews was the balanced perspective on the division of roles between AI and humans. "Not using generative AI" is not a realistic option. But neither is leaving everything to AI. The recognition is that it is precisely in the middle ground that engineers' new value lies.

Not using generative AI is not a realistic option. The feeling is like having "gained an excellent assistant," and I actually feel that the roles people can take on have expanded. — NEC Solution Innovator

The risk and responsibility of endlessly pressing "approve"

That said, AI is not omnipotent. It sometimes repeats the same thinking or fails to produce the expected results. At such times, the criteria for escalating to the operator—this, they say, still requires "human sensibility."

In actual development settings as well, the problem of people endlessly approving operations proposed by AI assistants with "approve, approve" was pointed out. Are they approving with an understanding of the content? The argument is that designing these guardrails is precisely the engineer's responsibility.

Even when using Copilot in VS Code, it will just run CLI commands on its own, right? People start pressing "approve, approve." The ideal is someone who approves while understanding what's going on. Occasionally it even asks for approval of something that's actually no good. — NEC Solution Innovator

Designing at which stage the risks inherent in generative AI are detected and how humans intervene—that, the recognition goes, is the engineer's new value. Through the training, this understanding was shared across the entire organization. This also connects to the recently noted concept of "harness engineering": how to control AI and use it safely.

06. The SIer's Dilemma — Between Selling Man-Hours and AI-Driven Efficiency

What left a particularly strong impression during the interview was the candid discussion about the structural dilemma of being an SIer: the more you streamline development with AI, the more your man-hour-based revenue declines.

The more efficient we become, the smaller the pie for our existing business gets. That's the hard part. We need to present pricing that customers can accept, so we're still going through trial and error. — NEC Solution Innovators

While generative AI is improving development efficiency, balancing that with the traditional man-hour-based business model has become a major challenge. Although efficiency improvements can be pursued, when delivering to customers, we need to carefully consider quality, operational frameworks, and how value is provided.

This is a common challenge faced by many SIers. While large waterfall-style projects form their volume zone, the man-hour-based business model is the default assumption, so AI-driven efficiency gains directly translate into lost revenue.

It was also pointed out that there are fewer opportunities to assign junior members to projects. As AI-driven automation advances, simple development work alone may no longer be recognized as valuable by customers.

FDE — A New Role for Engineers

The concept that came up as a breakthrough for this dilemma was the Forward Deployed Engineer (FDE). Proposed by US-based Palantir and also adopted by OpenAI, this role involves "immersing oneself in the customer's business, with engineers discovering problems themselves and building solutions on the spot."

I heard the term FDE for the first time today, but I realized we've been thinking along the same lines. After all, we're currently hearing about problems from customers. What we really need to do is get inside their operations and discover the problems ourselves. — NEC Solution Innovators

However, there are realities unique to large enterprises. The FDE model makes it hard to scale, and it needs to align with the organization's business model. A pragmatic approach was described: "Enter from the upstream phase, propose splitting things up — keeping waterfall for some parts — and move forward while persuading stakeholders."

07. Looking Ahead — Toward a "Redesign the Whole Process" Mindset

Not "Partial AI Adoption," but Redesigning the Entire Process

Looking ahead after the training, they aim to make generative AI use in post-design phases the norm while also updating their proposal and upstream phases. Their vision goes beyond the idea of "replacing parts of existing processes with AI."

It's not about taking the existing process and saying, "We'll automate this part with AI." Rather, if we use generative AI across the entire flow, the process itself changes. That's what we want to aim for. — NEC Solution Innovators

At this point, generative AI adoption is gradually progressing, such as comprehensiveness checks for review tasks in each phase and reverse-engineering design documents from source code to update them. However, in the field, there were humble voices saying these efforts "can't yet be called full-scale adoption." Behind that lies a strong desire to push generative AI usage to an even higher level.

From "Hearing About Problems" to "Discovering Them"

Another major shift is the mindset change from "hearing about problems" to "discovering problems." Until now, they entered projects after requirements were already defined by the customer and specialized in realizing them. Going forward, they aim to immerse themselves in the customer's business, discover problems themselves, and turn them into proposals.

If you keep sending me case studies from other industries, even one that catches our attention could spark a new business. Since this was a valuable encounter, I'd like to build on it in that way. — NEC Solution Innovators

By combining the know-how gained through the training with use case information from other industries, they aim to uncover customers' latent problems — and they are ready to put this "upstream problem discovery" role into practice using the tools acquired during the training.

Summary

Key Takeaways

  • The trigger for adoption was a sense of crisis: they had been unable to meet customer requests for generative AI proposals and had no choice but to pass on projects
  • Among four companies compared, they chose flexible training tailored to field challenges rather than a "package-type" program
  • The hands-on experience of building "something that works" with Dify × AWS Bedrock directly translated into confidence in real work
  • After the training, the quality of conversations with customers changed. They can now proactively approach with "We can do that"
  • They view AI as a "capable assistant" and share the importance of risk management and control design handled by humans
  • They confront the dilemma between SIer man-hour-based business and AI efficiency, exploring a shift toward upstream problem discovery
  • A mindset shift emerged: not "partial AI adoption," but redesigning the entire process

From merely "passing on projects" to an organization that can say "We can do that" — NEC Solution Innovators' transformation is a great example of how hands-on training can change an organization's mindset and behavior. And that change extends to deeper questions, not just the technology learned in training, but the SIer business model and the role of engineers themselves.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q1. What kind of training program did you implement?

This is a case-study-style training program combining Dify and AWS Bedrock. It consists of six steps: AI fundamentals, demand creation and customer proposals, requirements definition, AI development and data preparation, operation and maintenance, and a summary/wrap-up, with an emphasis on participants building "something that works" based on real challenges from their actual workplace.

Q2. What prompted you to introduce this training?

While we began receiving customer requests for proposals related to generative AI (such as building RAG systems and AI agents), we lacked sufficient in-house talent to handle them. Technical information on AWS Bedrock and engineers capable of working with it were still scarce in the market, so we needed to accumulate practical know-how.

Q3. How was the training partner selected?

You compared and evaluated about four companies. Among many proposals that were package-based with limited room for customization, we understand that the "supportive attitude" of being able to flexibly adjust the content to fit workplace challenges was the deciding factor.

Q4. What changes occurred after the training?

While the number of projects did not increase dramatically, the quality of conversations with customers changed. We became able to envision implementation approaches and potential bottlenecks in response to customer requests, and we are now in a position to proactively engage with generative AI project inquiries coming from within the NEC Group.

Q5. Have any challenges become apparent after the training?

For applying this to actual projects, the next challenge is technical deepening, such as tuning token volume, speed, and performance. In addition, we are also confronting a dilemma unique to SIers: how to balance AI-driven efficiency with a labor-hour-based business model.
