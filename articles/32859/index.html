Few employers publish the process

If you are preparing for a forward deployed engineer interview, start with what is actually on the record. It is not much.

On November 20, 2026, we looked for interview material from six employers: Palantir, OpenAI, Anthropic, Databricks, Scale AI and Ramp. Only Ramp publishes hiring criteria specific to the role that we could read. Anthropic publishes general guidance for technical interviews. None of the 14 employer job ads we read that day describes interview stages.

Palantir and OpenAI have interview pages of their own, but neither could be read as text when we checked. So the stage-by-stage detail in circulation about Palantir’s loop comes from third parties. We label it that way below.

For which employers are advertising the role, see who is hiring forward deployed engineers.

What each employer publishes

Everything in this section is from the employer’s own page, read November 20, 2026.

Ramp. Its engineering blog post on forward deployed engineering says applicants are assessed on four things: drive and work ethic, engineering fundamentals, customer empathy and communication. On fundamentals, Ramp treats technical performance as a bar to clear and does not optimize for it beyond that. It calls drive the best single predictor of how someone performs on the job. The post gives no stages, exercises or timeline, and the text we received showed no author or date.

Anthropic. Its careers page covers technical roles in general, not this role. Interviews run over Google Meet. Technical roles use live coding tools, and the page names Colab and CodeSignal. Applicants can look things up, but should know basic syntax and standard libraries well enough that lookups do not use up the time. The page adds: “We care about what you can do, not where you learned to do it.” It gives no number of stages and no timeline. Applicants can re-apply after 12 months. Anthropic’s ad for a Manager, Forward Deployed Engineering has no interview-process text.

Scale AI. Its Frontier Agents Engineer ad gives two process facts and no stages. Salary is set during the interview process, and Scale’s policy is a 90-day wait before it reconsiders someone for the same role.

Palantir. Its Forward Deployed Software Engineer ad for New York does not describe the interview. It does warn that applicants will never be asked for payment or financial information during interviews.

Databricks. Its Sr. Forward Deployed Engineer ad has no interview information.

OpenAI. Its ad pages returned only a title to a text reader, and its interview guide could not be opened. We have nothing from OpenAI’s own pages.

What the job ads ask for

The ads say little about interviews, but a lot about what the employer wants. We counted requirements across 10 individual-contributor ads with “Forward Deployed” in the title or sub-title, all read on the employer’s own board on November 20, 2026: six from Palantir, two from Scale AI, one from Databricks and one from Vannevar Labs.

The sample is skewed. Seven of the 10 are government-facing ads, so the clearance count is not representative of the role in general.

One point holds across the whole sample: all 10 ads describe working directly with customers, even though only two write prior customer-facing experience into the requirements.

On experience, The Pragmatic Engineer (Gergely Orosz, August 12, 2025) reads the ads the same way: Palantir takes people about a year out of college, while Ramp prefers 5+ years for senior roles. That article describes no interview process.

If the role you want is government-facing, clearance changes the picture. See cleared forward deployed engineer roles.

What a third-party account says about Palantir

Nothing in this section is published by Palantir. We could not read Palantir’s own interview pages, so we cannot confirm or correct either account.

Aced, formerly Exponent, sells interview preparation. Its Palantir guide, written by co-founder Jacob Simon, says it is built from first-hand accounts by interviewers and applicants. It lists 5 interview experiences and 29 questions. The page shows no date beyond “Updated a day ago”. Read November 20, 2026, it reports these stages:

  • A 30-minute introductory call.
  • A technical screen: live coding in CodePair or Karat, or a HackerRank assessment.
  • An onsite of three 60-minute rounds, drawn from a pool of five: decomposition, learning, coding, re-engineering and system design.
  • A 60-minute round with the hiring manager.

Aced says the whole loop commonly runs 3-4 weeks. It describes three of the rounds this way:

  • Decomposition. Break a vague, real-world problem into parts and propose a solution. Little or no code.
  • Learning. Understand an unfamiliar system and extend it within the hour.
  • Re-engineering. Take unfamiliar code in CodePair and find and fix what is broken.

First Round Review published an article for founders on February 24, 2026, shown as updated October 2, 2026. It quotes two people who used to hire for Palantir. They are former staff speaking to an outside publication, not Palantir.

Shilpa Balaji, a former Palantir forward deployed engineer who later led hiring for the role there, contrasts Palantir with the pattern of alternating interviews and coding tests she associates with Google. Palantir, she says, used open-ended problem solving. The example in the article is designing a solution to an insider-trading problem: what data you would need, and what you would ask the customer.

Tiffany Siu, who also hired for Palantir, says applicants for the role had to pass the same interview loops as software engineers.

Balaji’s other remarks describe who she looked for: people who build compulsively, who do not arrive with a fixed playbook, and whom you would want beside you when a deployment gets hard. She also says she steered away from people with more than 10 years at a large tech company. One hiring lead’s view, not Palantir policy.

Preparing for the code half

This section and the next are our reading of the ads and accounts above. They are not statements by any employer.

Expect a real engineering bar. Two sources point the same way. Ramp treats engineering fundamentals as a bar you must clear. Siu says Palantir applicants passed the same loops as software engineers. Do not prepare for a lighter version of a software interview.

Pick the language the ad names. Python is named in 5 of the 10 ads and TypeScript or JavaScript in 4. If the ad lists several languages, use the one you are fastest in.

Practice with lookups allowed, and without needing them. Anthropic lets applicants look things up but expects fluency in basic syntax and standard libraries. Practice in a plain notebook or browser editor, not your own configured setup. Anthropic names Colab and CodeSignal; Aced’s account of Palantir names CodePair, Karat and HackerRank.

Practice on code you did not write. If Aced’s account is right, two of Palantir’s five onsite round types involve unfamiliar code: one extends a system, the other repairs it. That matches the job, which is building inside someone else’s environment. Take an open-source project you have never seen, give yourself an hour, and fix a bug or add a small feature.

Check the years line before you apply. Five of the 10 ads state a minimum, from 1+ year after college to 6+ years.

Preparing for the customer half

Practice scoping out loud. The insider-trading example in First Round Review and Aced’s description of decomposition describe the same skill: turning a vague problem into parts. Take a messy problem, and before proposing anything, say what data you would need, who uses the result, and what you would ask the customer first. Then propose a first version and say what you left out.

Prepare one story from your own work. James Honsa, co-founder of Genera and formerly head of legal engineering at Ironclad, told First Round Review that his most effective exercise was asking applicants to present a problem from their career and teach the panel how they used technology to solve it. Choose a problem where you dealt with users directly. Be ready to teach it, not just recount it.

Show the customer work you have done. All 10 ads describe working directly with customers. Two of Ramp’s four criteria, customer empathy and communication, are about this half of the job. Have specific examples: a requirement you pushed back on, a trade-off you explained to someone non-technical.

Take drive seriously. Ramp calls it the best single predictor of performance. Evidence helps more than assertion: things you built without being asked, and projects you carried to the point where people used them.

Questions worth asking the employer

The ads leave gaps that only the employer can fill. Ask early.

  • What are the stages, and how long does the process take? None of the 14 ads we read says.
  • How much travel, in practice? Six of the 10 ads give a figure, and the figures run from 20% to roughly 25-75%. Three say only “as needed”. Ask what the last year looked like for the team you would join.
  • What does “deployed” mean here? Ask how much of the week is spent on a customer’s site, whether you work with one customer or several, and whether the code you write ships in the core product. The answers separate this role from its neighbors; see forward deployed engineer vs solutions engineer.
  • On what basis is the pay range quoted? Scale AI’s ad says salary is set during the interview process. Ask whether a published range is base salary and what sits on top of it. Our pay by employer briefing lists what each ad states.
  • Does the work need a clearance? Seven of the 10 ads in our sample carry clearance wording, though that sample leans toward government roles.
  • If this does not work out, when can I apply again? Anthropic says 12 months. Scale AI says 90 days for the same role.

One caution from Palantir’s own ad: it will never ask applicants for payment or financial information during interviews. Treat any request like that, from anyone, as a warning sign.

In practice

Read the employer’s own pages first, and treat everything else as an account, not a specification. Prepare for a software engineering bar, in the language the ad names, on code you did not write. Practice scoping a vague problem out loud, and bring one story you can teach. Then ask about travel, pay basis and what “deployed” means before you go far into the process.

Questions

  • Do employers publish their forward deployed engineer interview process?
  • Mostly not. Of the six employers we checked on November 20, 2026, only Ramp publishes hiring criteria specific to the role, and it gives no stages or exercises. Anthropic publishes general guidance for technical interviews. None of the 14 employer job ads we read describes interview stages.
  • What is the Palantir decomposition interview?
  • We could not read Palantir’s own description of it. Aced, a company that sells interview preparation, describes it as a 60-minute round in which the applicant breaks a vague real-world problem into parts and proposes a solution with little or no code. That is a third-party account, and Palantir has not confirmed it.
  • Which programming languages should I prepare?
  • Python first. It is named in 5 of the 10 forward deployed engineer ads we read on November 20, 2026, and TypeScript or JavaScript in 4. Four Palantir ads did not return a language line in our read, so the true counts may be higher. Check the ad for the role you are applying to.
  • Can I look things up during the coding interview?
  • At Anthropic, yes. Its careers page says applicants for technical roles can look things up, and asks them to be comfortable with basic syntax and standard libraries so that lookups do not use up the time. That guidance covers Anthropic’s technical roles in general. We found no equivalent statement from the other five employers.
  • How much travel should I expect?
  • It varies by employer and team, so ask. Six of the 10 ads we read state a figure, from 20% at Databricks to roughly 25-75% on Palantir’s Tactical Edge team. Three Palantir ads ask for travel as needed without a figure, and one Scale AI ad did not mention travel in our read.
  • If I am turned down, when can I apply again?
  • Two employers say. Anthropic’s careers page says applicants can re-apply after 12 months. Scale AI’s ad says its policy is a 90-day wait before it reconsiders someone for the same role. We found no statement from the other employers we read.
