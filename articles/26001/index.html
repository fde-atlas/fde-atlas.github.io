Forward Deployed Engineer Interview Questions: The 9 I'd Ask Before You Hire One

Hayat Amin · Operator · 2026-11-02 · 9 min read

Summary

Hayat Amin, a former technology CFO of twenty years who now works as a forward deployed engineer, argues interviews for the role should demand proof rather than talent. He lists nine questions, five drawn from John Kim's Paraform hiring guide and four of his own: whether a system the candidate built still runs after they left, how they explain failures to executives, what they would do in week one on a real broken process, which number they would move by week four, and who owns the work after handover. He cites the MIT NANDA study finding only about 5% of AI pilots reach rapid revenue acceleration, and S&P Global data showing 46% of proofs of concept scrapped before production. The article ends with pre-interview preparation steps and a suggestion to replace two interview rounds with a paid working session.

The forward deployed engineer interview questions that matter ask for proof, not talent. Ask to see a system still running in someone else's company, ask for a week one plan on your own broken process, and ask who on your team owns it when they leave.

I'm Hayat Amin. I spent twenty years as a technology chief financial officer, sold three companies in that seat, and now work as a forward deployed engineer myself, so I've sat on both sides of this interview. Below are the 9 questions I'd ask if I were the chief executive hiring one, what a good answer sounds like, and what to do this week before you hire anyone.

Why a normal engineering interview misses

A software interview tests whether someone can write good code in a clean environment. A forward deployed engineer works in a dirty one: your CRM, your accounting system, the spreadsheet your operations lead keeps on her desktop, and a team that didn't ask for change. I explained the role in what is a forward deployed engineer. The interview has to test whether the work survives contact with your company.

That's where AI projects die. Fortune's report on the MIT NANDA study, built from 150 interviews with leaders, a survey of 350 employees and 300 public AI deployments, found about 5% of AI pilots reach rapid revenue acceleration. S&P Global found the average company scrapped 46% of its AI proofs of concept before production, and 42% of companies abandoned most of their AI initiatives, up from 17% a year earlier. I wrote about the pattern in why AI pilots never reach production. The person you hire is meant to be the fix for those numbers, so interview for exactly that.

The 9 forward deployed engineer interview questions

Five of these come from the hiring guide John Kim of Paraform published on 20 October 2026. The other four are mine, from the finance seat.

1. Show me something you built that's still running inside a customer's company after you left.

Paraform puts it as "show me code you've written that deployed to customer infrastructure". I'd push one step further and ask to speak to the person who owns it now. A good answer names the system, the company and that owner. A weak answer is a demo, a deck or a repository nobody uses.

2. Walk me through explaining a failed deployment to a non-technical executive.

Also from Paraform. You're listening for plain words, a number, and what they changed afterwards. If the answer blames the client's data without saying what they did about it, that's your answer.

3. Tell me about a customer conversation that changed what you built.

The third Paraform question. A forward deployed engineer earns the title by listening to the people doing the work. If nothing a user said ever changed their plan, they built to the spec and left.

4. Here's one of our broken processes. What would you do in week one?

Give them a real one. Orders arrive by email and someone retypes them into the ERP. Invoices sit in a shared inbox for 6 days. The pipeline in the CRM doesn't match the revenue in the accounting system. A good answer starts with sitting next to the person who does it, then asks what it costs in hours, then names the systems. A bad answer starts with a model name.

5. What number would you move first, and how would we know by week 4?

This is the CFO question, and it's the one most candidates find hardest. On my own engagements week 4 is when the first system goes into production, so I expect a candidate to name a number they could move by then: hours of retyping, days to close the month, invoices chased by hand. If they can't name one, you'll be paying for activity.

6. What did you do when a customer changed requirements halfway through?

Paraform suggests asking how they reset scope. Requirements will change in your company too. The good answer has a date, a conversation with the sponsor and a smaller first release. The bad one is "we absorbed it".

7. What happens when the model is wrong?

Every AI system gets things wrong. I want to hear where a person checks the output before it reaches a customer or the ledger, how the errors get logged, and how they test a change before it ships. "It's very accurate" isn't an answer.

8. Who on my team owns this when you leave, and what do they need?

If they can't answer, you're buying a dependency. A good answer names a role in your company, the access and documents that person gets, and the week the handover happens. On my own engagements that's week 12.

9. How much of your week can you spend inside our building and our systems?

Paraform asks what percentage of time a candidate can commit to customer locations. For a company hiring its first one, ask which days, which meetings they'll sit in, and whose desk they'll sit beside. Remote with weekly check ins is consulting.

The red flags

Paraform says candidates who answer "I just call the API" don't pass, and lists four more warning signs. I've added the last two.

  • A pure backend engineer with no customer interaction.
  • A solutions architect with no production code.
  • "I prefer to just focus on the code."
  • No flexibility on travel or time on site.
  • Only slides and diagrams to show. I covered that difference in is a forward deployed engineer just a consultant.
  • A long notice period or lock in. Someone confident in the work doesn't need one.

What to do this week, before you interview anyone

You can do most of the screening yourself, and you'll get a better hire for it. In order:

  • Pick one broken process and write it down in five lines: who does it, which systems it touches, how many hours a week it takes, and what goes wrong.
  • Put a number on it. If two people spend 6 hours a week retyping orders, that's 12 hours a week, about 600 a year.
  • Export a sample of the real data, 50 to 100 records, with anything personal removed. Candidates will work on this, not a toy problem.
  • Name the owner on your team now, before you hire. That person sits in every interview.
  • Replace two interview rounds with a paid working session of 2 to 3 hours on that process and that data. Ask for a week one plan, and the first number to move, by the end of it.
  • Call the owner of a system the candidate built before (question 1). Ask whether it still runs, and who fixes it when it breaks.
  • Decide fractional or full time. Paraform puts the Levels.fyi median total pay for a full time forward deployed engineer at $220,000, a full search at 8 to 12 weeks, and its own fee at 25% of first year base. Through Beyond Elevation, my firm, fractional work starts from $5,800 a month for one to two days a week, with no notice period. I set out that comparison in fractional forward deployed engineer.

Some companies get to step 2 and find the process costs 3 hours a week. Don't hire anyone for that. Fix it with a native integration you already pay for and keep the money.

If the list shows a pilot that never left the demo, three systems that won't join, or a number you can't see until month end, the job is bigger than a weekend fix, and that's when you bring in a forward deployed engineer. Questions people actually ask

What are good forward deployed engineer interview questions?

Ask for proof before talent. Show me a system you built that's still running inside a customer's company after you left. Walk me through telling a non-technical executive that a deployment failed. Here's our broken process, what would you do in week one? Who on my team owns this when you go? The answers tell you whether they ship into real systems or only into demos.

What is the forward deployed engineer interview process?

Paraform recommends a five stage loop: a recruiter screen on motivation, travel and pay, a practical integration task against a real system, system design under the customer's constraints, a decomposition case that turns a vague problem into scoped work, and a behavioural round on customer communication and ownership. For a company hiring its first one, I'd swap two of those stages for a paid working session on your own process.

How long does it take to hire a forward deployed engineer?

Paraform tells companies to budget 8 to 12 weeks for a full search, and charges 25% of first year base salary if you hire through them, which is $45,000 on a $180,000 base. A fractional engineer can start inside a week, which is why many companies test the work fractionally before they open a full time role.

What are forward deployed engineer job requirements?

Production code shipped into environments the engineer didn't control, the ability to explain a failure to an executive in plain words, comfort with a vague brief, and enough time on site to sit with the people doing the work. Paraform adds domain fit with your largest customers' industries. A degree list matters less than one system you can go and look at.

What are the red flags when interviewing a forward deployed engineer?

Paraform lists four: a backend engineer who has never spoken to a customer, a solutions architect with no production code, a candidate who says they prefer to just focus on the code, and no flexibility on travel. I'd add two from my own side of the table: someone who can only show slides, and someone who can't name who owned their last system after they left.
