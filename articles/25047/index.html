AI Business Efficiency Case Studies: Examples of Japanese Companies Featured in White Papers and the Order to Get Started

Original title: AI業務効率化の事例｜白書に載った国内企業の例と始める順番

FDE Consulting · 2026-10-30 · 10 min read

Summary

The author organizes Japanese corporate AI efficiency cases from the Ministry of Internal Affairs and Communications' 2026 (Reiwa 8) Information and Communications White Paper into three types by what work AI reduces: individual tasks, frontline procedures, and business processes. The author stresses distinguishing cases that are actually in use, sold as products, or merely company-reported results. A detailed SME example, Ainico Group, ran a one-year project with 21 volunteers from 10 business divisions, reporting 2,247 hours saved annually (company-reported, not independently measured). The article proposes a six-step sequence for applying cases internally and suggests limiting external help to judging whether a problem suits AI and connecting systems for production deployment, a role related to FDEs.

Key points

  • White Paper cases sorted into three types by AI's scope.
  • Cases mix in-use, product, and self-reported results.
  • Ainico Group's year-long project reported 2,247 hours saved.
  • Six-step adoption order: measure time, set rules, pilot small.
  • External help best for problem selection and production rollout.

It is natural for staff who want to improve business efficiency with AI to first look for other companies' examples. However, many of the case studies found through search are product adoption stories, and it is hard to tell how much is actually running and how much is just a concept.

Here, I have gathered examples of Japanese companies that the Ministry of Internal Affairs and Communications' 2026 (Reiwa 8) Information and Communications White Paper lists as "AI use cases in Japanese companies," and organized them by pattern of business efficiency improvement. The white paper is a public document, and each case comes with sources from the original announcements or press coverage. On that basis, I read the small and medium-sized enterprise example in detail and describe the order in which to apply the cases to your own company. How to choose tasks and the procedure itself are covered in Steps to improve business efficiency with AI, and how SMEs can get started is covered in AI adoption for small and medium-sized enterprises.

Domestic cases in the white paper

The white paper presents advanced AI use cases by business area of corporate activity. The white paper itself notes that, due to constraints on the survey period and space, it is difficult to comprehensively describe all cases. In other words, this is not a list of domestic cases, but the examples the white paper chose.

Extracting those close to business efficiency improvement gives the following.

In my view, making this distinction first when reading cases makes it easier to see what parts you can bring back to your own company.

Reading the cases as three patterns

The cases above can be divided into three patterns by the scope of work that AI reduces. The classification is my own organization.

PatternWork AI reducesWhite paper examplesWhat to decide internally
Individual workTime each person spends writing, researching, and summarizingNissin Foods Group, Chugai Pharmaceutical, Meiji Yasuda LifeWhat information may be used, usable environments, templates
Frontline proceduresTime frontline staff spend searching procedure manuals and records to make judgmentsAEON Retail, Daikin IndustriesSource documents, how to handle unanswerable questions, frontline verification
Business processesTime spent on handoffs spanning multiple people and stepsITOCHU Techno-Solutions, RaksWhich steps to delegate to AI and where humans verify

Individual work

This is the easiest pattern to start with, and the most common. Even in the white paper's corporate survey, "meeting minutes and email drafting assistance" was the most common task where Japanese companies use generative AI, at about 70%. What I focus on in the Nissin Foods Group example is that they did not stop at just distributing a tool: they selected target tasks by department, created prompt templates, and gradually expanded efforts to measure effects. The white paper notes that even if a general-purpose AI tool is introduced, only a few proactive employees may use it, and it may never lead to company-wide adoption.

Frontline procedures

The AEON Retail and Daikin Industries examples reduce the time frontline staff spend searching procedure manuals and past records. Both are based on documents accumulated within the company (manuals, equipment drawings, maintenance records). In my view, the effectiveness of this pattern is largely determined by how well the source documents are organized. If documents are outdated or written differently by each department, AI answers will also be inconsistent.

Business processes

The ITOCHU Techno-Solutions and Raks examples aim to shorten the processes themselves, such as requirements definition and expense reimbursement, which involve multiple people. Chugai Pharmaceutical, which the white paper cites as an advanced case, states that business process transformation, unlike individual work efficiency, should be pursued after sufficiently examining the division of roles between humans and AI. They emphasize "human-in-the-loop," determining which steps to delegate to AI and which steps humans are responsible for, and inserting human judgment and verification. This pattern involves work to connect with internal systems and data, so preparation takes longer than the individual work pattern. The approach is described in How to introduce AI agents.

An SME example: Ainico Group's approach

The white paper covers two companies in detail through individual interviews: the large company Chugai Pharmaceutical and the SME Ainico Group. For staff at small and medium-sized enterprises looking for AI adoption cases, I believe the latter's approach is the most useful reference.

Ainico Group is a company centered in Nara Prefecture and southern Kyoto Prefecture that handles custom homes, renovations, nursing care, childcare, and more. According to the white paper, AI tools such as ChatGPT were already in place internally, but usage remained individual, and the challenge was how to expand it across the business as a whole. From there, they proceeded in the following order.

PeriodWhat was done
BeginningPartnered with a specialized support provider and held study sessions for all employees. Rather than starting with business topics, they began with hands-on experience interacting with AI using familiar stories as material
Project startRecruited employees who wanted to take on the challenge from each department: about 2 people from each of the 10 business divisions, 21 participants in total. From each division's issues, they defined the issues and goals they wanted to solve with AI
1 yearProgress was checked at weekly study sessions of about one hour, with experts providing advice. Milestones for sharing results were set at 3, 6, and 12 months
Month 12At the final report meeting, it was reported that across all 10 business divisions, a total of 2,247 hours of work time had been saved per year
After thatThe study sessions continue once a month, and from April 2026 the company plans to select dedicated AI staff from each department

As a breakdown of the hours saved, the white paper gives three examples.

DepartmentWhat was doneAnnual hours saved
Public relations departmentAutomated multiple internal tasks880 hours
Nursing care business divisionCreated a system to optimize user transport routes, among other things170 hours
Real estate business divisionTemplated and automated inquiry handling897 hours

These figures were provided by the company to the white paper and were not measured by a third party. Even so, there is much to learn from how they proceeded. I believe there are three things I can take away.

  • Participants were people who raised their hands from each business division. They worked on this alongside their regular duties, and results were seen in every division
  • Challenges were raised by the divisions themselves, and goals and measurement methods were decided in advance. I believe this is why the saved hours could be totaled by department
  • Rules were kept to a minimum. They made known things like not entering personal information and setting opt-outs, aiming to avoid people not using the tools out of excessive fear

The white paper also cites as factors behind the results that mechanisms such as organizing workflows and checklists and turning procedures into videos had taken root within the company. As with the standard procedures in the field, organizing the underlying work came first.

The order for applying case studies to your own company

After collecting case studies, what should you start with in your own company? Based on my reading of the white paper's examples, the following order works well.

#What to doWhat can be read from the case studies
1Divide the case studies into the three patterns and determine which pattern the most time-consuming task in your company falls underWhat needs to be decided internally differs by pattern
2Choose one task matching the pattern in one department and measure the time currently spent on itAinico Group decided challenges and goals for each business division before starting
3Decide what information may be entered and what tools may be usedAinico Group kept rules to a minimum and made them known
4Create templates, recruit participants, and try on a small scaleNissin Foods Group gradually expanded its templates and effect measurement
5Set milestones to share numbers every three monthsAinico Group set milestones at 3, 6, and 12 months
6Decide whether to move on to the workflow patternChugai Pharmaceutical considers the division of roles between humans and AI before changing workflows

Steps 1 through 5 can often be carried out by internal people within the scope of the individual work and field procedure patterns. Once you enter step 6, the workflow pattern, you need connections to internal systems, design of where humans verify results, and production operation. In my view, this is where a stall tends to happen: the pilot shows results but never moves to production. I wrote about this kind of stall and how to get out of it in Breaking out of AI PoC stagnation.

If you rely on external help, whom should you ask?

What is interesting about the Ainico Group example is that they narrowed down to two situations where expert support was particularly effective. One is judging "whether this challenge should be solved with AI" when deciding on challenges, and the other is being able to consult "this approach won't work" after the project has started. Even after the project, they maintain an environment where they can consult the support vendor each time an issue arises.

In my view, when deciding the scope of external help, it is easy to decide by starting from these two. The individual work pattern can often be run internally once rules and templates are in place. External help is needed for determining which tasks to solve with AI, and for connecting to internal systems and running in production once you enter the workflow pattern. The role of getting deeply involved in the field and handling implementation for the latter is called an FDE (Forward Deployed Engineer). I wrote about what an FDE does in What is an FDE.
