How FDEs charge: PostHog's milestone-based pricing

Original title: FDE 怎么收费：PostHog 按里程碑定价

FDE Times · 2026-10-09 · 6 min read

Summary

This article examines PostHog's publicly documented pricing model for its FDE services: work is priced per milestone rather than by the hour, with a $5,000 minimum per milestone set via a pull request in the posthog.com repo, replacing an earlier rule of roughly 20% of a customer's first-year spend. Deliverables whose scope doubles must be re-quoted, and a milestone counts as complete only when a designated customer-side owner signs off. The author argues this makes sense because PostHog says AI has made output cheap, so the real value FDEs sell is judgment, which hourly billing cannot measure. The piece also covers writing narrow, checkable milestones, handling scope creep, and converting per-customer work into reusable templates and product improvements.

Key points

  • PostHog prices FDE work per milestone, not by the hour.
  • Each milestone has a $5,000 minimum, set via a public pull request.
  • Deliverables that double in scope must be re-quoted, not absorbed.
  • Completion requires sign-off from a designated customer-side owner.
  • The rationale: AI makes output cheap, so judgment is the real product.

Key takeaways

  • PostHog quotes its FDE services by milestone, not by the hour. Once a deliverable's scope doubles, it must be re-quoted.
  • The handbook doesn't explain the specific reason, but it states that an FDE's value lies in judgment rather than output, and hours can only measure output.
  • A milestone is only complete once a designated owner on the customer side signs off. Lessons accumulated along the way should be distilled into templates or product improvements.

PostHog's public handbook is explicit: Forward Deployed Engineers (FDEs) are quoted by milestone, not billed by the hour. The handbook also contains a stricter rule: any deliverable whose scope grows to twice its original size must be re-quoted—it can't quietly be absorbed as a delay.

This decision is also reflected on the public /services page. A pull request (PR) in the posthog.com code repository set the minimum price per milestone at $5,000, replacing a previous rule that fees should be roughly 20% of the customer's first-year purchase. The PR describes this as a deliberate choice by the FDE team.

PostHog doesn't explain why. However, on its overview page it writes that AI lets people build faster, so the real value a team provides is judgment, taste, and reasoning.

PostHog itself doesn't connect these two points. But if the real value is judgment, hourly billing becomes hard to defend, because hours can only measure output.

For engineers currently doing FDE work or aspiring to it, this model is worth a close read. It shows how to turn judgment—which is normally invisible—into something a customer can sign off on: a milestone, a quote, an acceptance.

Why hours are the wrong measure

PostHog's "How we work" page puts it plainly: an FDE's value lies not in how much they produce, but more in the judgment they bring to the work.

Imagine two FDEs given the same task. The first spends two weeks writing 3,000 lines of scripts. The second spends two days asking the customer questions, discovers that two-thirds of the events slated for migration are no longer used by anyone, and migrates only the remainder in one week.

Billed by the hour, the first earns more. Billed by the milestone, the second wins—and so does the customer. This pricing model therefore forces the whole team to answer "what should be done" before "how much should be done."

This view isn't unique to PostHog. IndiaNIC, discussing agency pricing, points out that once AI compresses the hours behind a deliverable, hourly billing punishes the fast and rewards the slow.

Customers have their own concerns too. Ghanemzadeh, who has written about FDE pricing from a practitioner's perspective, describes the common time-and-materials model: the work has no clear endpoint, and the total cost only becomes visible when the engagement ends.

Milestones must be narrow enough for the price to be credible

Fixed quotes aren't a panacea. Ghanemzadeh himself notes that "fixed outcome, fixed price" is only honest when the scope is narrow enough. That's why the unit of pricing must be a clearly defined milestone, not an entire vague project.

Suppose a customer wants to migrate analytics data from an old tool to PostHog. A bad milestone says "support the migration." A good milestone says: events from three main applications ingested into PostHog, the five most important dashboards matching the old system's data, completed in about three weeks.

The second version is measurable, bounded, and checkable, which is what makes it quotable. When the customer reads it, they either nod or say "actually, what we need most is a revenue dashboard." Both responses are useful, because the disagreement surfaces before anyone writes a single line of code.

PostHog's handbook also encourages FDEs to share unfinished work early so colleagues can weigh in on the direction. Applied to milestones, the practical advice is: have a colleague read the draft before you send the description to the customer. A misjudgment at this stage costs more than at any later stage.

What happens when scope expands?

Back to the migration example. Halfway through week two, the customer asks: since you're already doing this, could you also sync the data to a data warehouse? For many engineers, the instinct is to say yes and keep the customer happy.

PostHog's rule blocks exactly this instinct: a deliverable whose scope doubles must be re-quoted, not quietly absorbed as extra work. This rule protects both sides. The FDE doesn't get dragged into a project with no end, and the customer gets a clear decision instead of a surprise bill.

The capability behind this rule is being able to say "yes, but that's another milestone" without damaging the relationship. You can practice now, with internal teams that keep piling work on you.

"Done" means someone on the customer side signs off

According to PostHog's "Working with customers" page, a milestone is only complete once the deliverable is live and accepted by a designated owner on the customer side. Not "the customer seems happy," and not "the hours are used up," but a specific person being responsible for saying "this passes."

In the migration example, this person might be the customer's data lead, who opens the five dashboards and confirms the data matches. This requires you to clarify upfront: who signs off, and against what criteria.

A related piece of advice: write good documentation so the acceptor can check the work without you sitting next to them. If only the FDE knows how the scripts run, that sign-off will be hard to get.

What gets left behind after each milestone

A milestone can settle an invoice, but PostHog doesn't want the learning to end with it. The handbook requires FDEs to turn what they built for one customer—wherever it's useful to other customers too—into examples, templates, or product improvements.

The overview page adds that patterns recurring across multiple engagements get distilled into reusable assets and product improvements.

DataCamp highlights the capability involved: product judgment—deciding which parts should be customized for a single customer and which belong in the core product. Choose wrong, and you leave behind a pile of one-off code no one can maintain. DataCamp also notes that strong FDEs bring frontline experience back to the product engineering team, making the platform better for everyone.

In the migration example, the scripts written for the first customer, once cleaned up, might become a template for the tenth. That's a judgment call too: which parts are general enough to be worth investing in, and which should stay one-off.

Interviews and resumes can borrow PostHog's signals

PostHog's handbook offers a ready-made set of signals to check against: quote by milestone, re-quote when scope expands, have someone on the customer side sign off on acceptance, and turn experience into reusable assets. When reading any FDE job description, watch for traces of these mechanisms.

In interviews, you can borrow PostHog's rules directly and ask: when the deliverables double in scope, does the team re-quote, or quietly extend the deadline? You can also ask who on the customer side signs off on acceptance, and which parts of your work will be turned into templates for the next engagement.

When writing your resume, follow the lifecycle of a single milestone: before work began, what verifiable outcome did you agree on? When did you say "that's a different milestone"? Who on the customer side accepted the work? And how did what you left behind make the next job go faster?

If you're already an FDE, the lowest-cost exercise is this: start every engagement with a half-page definition of "done," and end it with customer sign-off. When coding speed no longer distinguishes one engineer from another, these two habits are where people are willing to pay.
