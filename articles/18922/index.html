How to Prevent Vendor Lock-In | Decide Ownership and Handover Before Signing the Contract

Original title: ベンダーロックインの対策｜契約前に名義と引き継ぎを決める

株式会社Sai · 2026-10-19 · 28 min read

Summary

This Japanese article explains how companies can prevent vendor lock-in by deciding ownership of accounts, rights, documentation, and handover arrangements before signing development contracts. Citing Japan's Fair Trade Commission's February 2022 survey of 1,000 government agencies, it notes 48.3% re-contracted with existing vendors because only they understood system functionality details—more than the 24.3% citing rights ownership. The author proposes checking existing systems in the order of account ownership, rights, documents, and handover destination, and recommends contracts specify source code delivery, transfer of copyright law Articles 27 and 28 rights, and standard-format data export. Staying with one vendor is not automatically lock-in if contracts preserve copies, data extraction, and handover terms. The article closes with the contract design of Sai's JISEKI service.

Key points

  • Decide naming, rights, documents, and handover before contracting.
  • 48.3% of surveyed agencies re-contracted because only the vendor knew system details.
  • Copyright transfer clauses must explicitly list Articles 27 and 28 rights.
  • Continuing with one vendor is not lock-in if exit conditions are contracted.
  • Audit existing systems in order: ownership, rights, documents, handover.

Key Takeaways

  • To guard against vendor lock-in, decide ownership and handover terms before signing the contract; for a system already in operation, verify in the order of ownership, rights, documentation, and handover destination who holds what.
  • In a Japan Fair Trade Commission survey, the most common reason government agencies re-contracted with their existing vendor was that only the existing vendor understood the details of the system's functionality, cited more often than rights ownership (accessed October 19, 2026).
  • Include the source code in deliverables and write into the contract who owns the copyright or the permitted scope of use; if you are receiving a transfer, explicitly state the rights under Articles 27 and 28 of the Copyright Act, because anything not written is presumed to remain with the transferring party.
  • Continuing with the same company is not immediately lock-in; where continuing is reasonable, leave only the source code, copies of documentation, how to extract data, and handover arrangements in the contract as conditions for exiting.
  • Our Jiseki ~JISEKI~ is designed so that rights and ownership of what is built are not left in one party's hands alone, and handover at termination is decided in the contract before it begins; it differs in form from the client-side ownership we recommend for commissioned development.

Vendor lock-in is a state where the cost and effort of switching away from a particular development company or product become so large that the client can no longer make a new choice

Vendor lock-in is a state where parties other than the company that originally built the system can no longer take on the modifications and maintenance needed to keep using it, leaving the client unable to choose a new partner. The cost and effort of switching are so large that there is no option but to keep relying on the same company.

The Japan Fair Trade Commission's February 2022 report defines vendor lock-in as a state where work needed to keep using an information system—such as functional modifications, version upgrades, and hardware maintenance—cannot be performed by anyone other than the company that implemented it, forcing continued use of a specific vendor (Japan Fair Trade Commission, "Report on the Fact-Finding Survey on Information System Procurement by Government Agencies" (PDF) / accessed October 19, 2026).

The essence of this definition lies in the part "cannot be performed by anyone other than the company that implemented it." Even if the work itself could be handled by other companies, if ownership, rights, and documentation exist only on the original company's side, no other company can touch it.

The most common reason for re-contracting is that only the existing vendor understands the system's contents

Lock-in does not arise only from choosing old technology or unusual products. It also arises from failing to decide ownership, rights, and where documentation will be kept at the contracting stage; trying to decide these after the build requires negotiation that needs the other party's consent.

In the Japan Fair Trade Commission's question to government agencies about why they re-contracted with existing vendors (multiple answers allowed; 1,000 valid responses), the most common answer was "because only the existing vendor could grasp the details of the existing system's functionality," at 483 agencies (48.3%). There were also 211 agencies (21.1%) answering that only the existing vendor could grasp the contents of the data stored in the system (Japan Fair Trade Commission, "Report on the Fact-Finding Survey on Information System Procurement by Government Agencies" (PDF) / accessed October 19, 2026).

On the rights side, 243 agencies (24.3%) answered that rights relating to the system's functionality (technology) belonged to the existing vendor, and 71 agencies (7.1%) answered that rights relating to data belonged to the existing vendor. The report notes that answers citing rights belonging to the existing vendor include agencies that implemented commercially available package software. It further cites as background the possibility that specifications were skewed toward a particular vendor or that rights processing was not handled appropriately (Japan Fair Trade Commission, "Report on the Fact-Finding Survey on Information System Procurement by Government Agencies" (PDF) / accessed October 19, 2026).

In an opinion from a national agency introduced in the same report, because copyright to the program belonged to the existing vendor when the system was modified, the operating entity could not be changed to another vendor, and the agency ended up re-contracting with the existing vendor (Japan Fair Trade Commission, "Report on the Fact-Finding Survey on Information System Procurement by Government Agencies" (PDF) / accessed October 19, 2026). In our view, the reason of "not being able to grasp it" is a documentation and handover problem, while the reason of "rights belonging to the vendor" is a contract problem. If the location of both had been decided before starting the build, they would be unlikely to surface for the first time at the switching stage.

When know-how and documentation accumulate on the development company's side, even holding the rights does not mean you can understand the system

The structure where know-how accumulates on the development company's side has also been pointed out. METI's DX Report (September 7, 2018) states that in Japan, most IT engineers belong to vendor companies rather than user companies, and that due to the commissioned development structure, information system know-how tends not to accumulate within user companies. It also writes that when outsourcing to external vendor companies is the norm, maintenance is performed in batches at intervals, causing know-how to accumulate on the vendor companies' side (METI, "DX Report: Overcoming the '2025 Cliff' of IT Systems and Full-scale Deployment of DX," main text (PDF, archived version on the National Diet Library WARP) / accessed October 19, 2026).

The Digital Agency's practical guidebook (DS-120, revised June 12, 2026) lists three causes of lock-in, noting that after design and development proceed, design information and the like can become a hostage held by the vendor, constraining subsequent work and procurement: design information is specialized, voluminous, and hard for anyone but its creators to understand; the ordering party does not properly manage documents such as procurement specifications and design documents; and exclusive proprietary technologies to which only a particular vendor holds rights are adopted (Digital Agency, "DS-120 Digital Government Promotion Standard Guidelines Practical Guidebook" (PDF) / accessed October 19, 2026).

Of the three, the two the ordering side can address through contracts and daily operations are document management and the handling of rights. Even the first one—design information being specialized and voluminous—leaves an entry point for interpretation if the ordering side keeps documents up to date.

The left column represents states that exist only on the development company's side; the more rows that fall into this column, the harder switching becomes. The right column represents states to be retained by the ordering side as well, and the lower band shows the items to write into the contract at each stage: before the contract, during development, and at termination. The following sections address how to close this left-right gap, in the order of the figure's rows.

Hold accounts and domains under the ordering party's name or with administrator privileges, and give the development company only the permissions needed for the work

Create accounts and domains under the ordering party's name, or at least keep administrator privileges with the ordering party, and give the development company only the permissions needed for the work. Accounts here means the entry points someone must hold for the system to keep running, such as domain registration (the name corresponding to the site or email address), cloud or server contracts, where source code is stored, and admin consoles for external services and analytics tools.

If ownership rests with the development company, ending the contract means asking the other party to change the owner of those entry points. Even without bad intent on their part, if the procedure is delayed by staff transfers or the company's circumstances, work to switch without taking the system offline cannot proceed during that time. Placing ownership with the ordering side is not a measure born of distrust; it is a measure to make the ending a smooth one.

Deciding this before work begins simplifies the procedure. Create the entry points under the ordering party's name, invite the development company's staff as members, and grant only the permissions needed for the work. At the end, simply remove the granted permissions; no ownership transfer procedure is needed.

The name on payments is also part of the entry points. If the development company advances payments for external service fees or advertising spend and bills them back, the contracts and admin consoles may end up under the development company's name as well. Which costs are contracted under whose name should be written into the contract as a separate item from development costs.

Three points go into the contract: that the entry points to be created will be under the ordering party's name, the scope of permissions granted to the development company, and the date permissions are removed at termination and the person holding the role of removing them. Including in the same clause that entry points must not be created with a staff member's personal email address saves searching for it later.

Include source code and design documents in the deliverables, and write the attribution of rights and the method of data extraction into the contract

Explicitly list source code, design documents, architecture diagrams, and operating procedures among the deliverables, and write into the contract whether copyright is transferred to the ordering party or retained by the development company with a defined scope of permitted use. For data, make it a requirement that it can be extracted in standard formats. This section organizes general contractual concepts from public templates and is not legal advice; for individual contracts, please consult a professional such as a lawyer.

For source code and copyright, write the attribution or the scope of permitted use, including the right to modify

IPA's (Information-technology Promotion Agency) "Information System Model Transaction and Contract" second edition was published on December 22, 2020, and the main text for commissioned development and maintenance/operation, plus the supplements for package and SaaS/ASP utilization, were replaced on April 8, 2025 with added commentary on international arbitration. The page's last update was September 11, 2026, reflecting updated contact information following the organizational restructuring of August 1, 2026 (IPA, "Information System Model Transaction and Contract (Second Edition)" / accessed October 19, 2026).

This template offers three options for copyright in deliverables. Option A vests all copyright in the development company (the vendor, in the template's terms); Option B retains copyright in generically usable programs and the like with the development company, vesting the rest in the ordering party (the user). Option C retains copyright in generic programs and the like with the development company and makes the rest jointly owned by the ordering party and the development company (IPA, "Information System Model Transaction and Contract <Second Edition> (Template)" (Word) / accessed October 19, 2026).

The commentary explains that the provision vesting copyright in the development company as a principle was made Option A (IPA, "Information System Model Transaction and Contract <Second Edition> (updated April 8, 2025)" (Word) / accessed October 19, 2026). If you choose Option A as the default, rights rest with the development company, so the ordering party must confirm before contracting whether leaving it as Option A is acceptable. The reason Option A was made the default is covered in the section dealing with cases where not switching is the more rational choice.

If copyright is transferred to the ordering party, check whether the right of adaptation (reworking the original work) is included. Article 61, Paragraph 2 of the Copyright Act provides that, in a contract transferring copyright, if the rights of Article 27 (translation and adaptation rights, etc.) or Article 28 (the original author's rights regarding use of derivative works) are not specially enumerated (individually listed) as the purpose of the transfer, those rights are presumed to be reserved by the transferor (e-Gov Legal Search, "Copyright Act" / accessed October 19, 2026). Because of this presumption, IPA's Option B explicitly states in its clauses that the rights of Articles 27 and 28 are also transferred (IPA, "Information System Model Transaction and Contract <Second Edition> (updated April 8, 2025)" (Word) / accessed October 19, 2026).

Whether the transfer clause includes the rights of Articles 27 and 28 is the first line to look for when opening the contract. The more a system undergoes continued modification, the more the handling of the right to modify matters later on.

Preparations for when the development company goes bankrupt are also decided near this clause. IPA's model contract issue analysis states that there is no system for satisfying requirements for perfection (requirements for asserting rights against third parties) regarding the whereabouts of copyright upon bankruptcy. On that basis, it says that disclosure and delivery of source code and accompanying documents can be handled by explicitly stating the source code in the deliverables or by using an escrow system (IPA "Information System Model Transactions and Contracts, Second Edition (updated April 8, 2025)" (Word) / accessed October 19, 2026). Escrow is a mechanism in which source code is deposited with a third party so that it can be received when agreed conditions are met.

Keep the configuration documents, architecture diagrams, and operation procedures up to date on the ordering side, and make data extractable in standard formats

Even if you have the source code and rights, without configuration documents, architecture diagrams, and operation procedures, it becomes difficult for other companies to decide whether to take over. Include these in the deliverables and keep them up to date on the ordering side.

As one measure to prevent situations where re-contracting is unavoidable, the Japan Fair Trade Commission's report introduces an example of a local government with a population of less than 50,000 that contractually requires the submission of materials such as information system configuration documents and architecture diagrams as deliverables (Japan Fair Trade Commission "Fact-Finding Survey Report on Information System Procurement by Government Agencies" (PDF) / accessed October 19, 2026). Configuration documents and architecture diagrams serve as a map for parties other than the company that built the environment to understand its contents.

Simply having the materials is not enough. The Digital Agency's DS-120, as a precaution against lock-in, requires the ordering party to manage up-to-date documentation, distinguishing between "storage" in folders and "management" to keep them current. It states that when specifications change, it is important to check for inconsistencies or omissions in the documentation and perform change management (Digital Agency "DS-120 Digital Government Promotion Standard Guidelines Practical Guidebook" (PDF) / accessed October 19, 2026).

Even if the materials are complete at the time of delivery, if they are not updated with each modification, discrepancies with the running system will accumulate. In the contract, in addition to making the materials deliverables, specify who updates the materials with each modification and who receives them.

For data, decide both the rights and the extraction method. DS-120 states that even if the data belongs to the ordering party in terms of rights, migration to another system is impossible if there is no function to extract data from the system or if data can only be extracted in a format readable only by specific products. Therefore, it says that the ability to extract data in standard formats must be defined as a requirement in advance (Digital Agency "DS-120 Digital Government Promotion Standard Guidelines Practical Guidebook" (PDF) / accessed October 19, 2026).

Treat records accumulated during operation the same way. Records such as inquiry histories and configuration change histories tend to be left out of handover unless the format in which they are delivered at termination is decided in advance.

Decide the ending in the contract made before starting development, and write down the arrangements for the handover partner and handover date

Decide the ending in the contract made before starting development, and write down the arrangements for who receives what, by when. If you try to decide this at the ending stage, the handover work itself becomes a new order, and you will have to start from negotiating the conditions.

The Japan Fair Trade Commission's report states that, to make it easier for parties other than the building company to perform modifications and operations, it is desirable to clearly state in the contract at the time of system construction in advance matters such as ownership of intellectual property rights, the approach to cost allocation, the preparation of materials needed to understand the system, and provision of source code. This is written for government agencies and is positioned as an important initiative also for avoiding vendor lock-in (Japan Fair Trade Commission "Fact-Finding Survey Report on Information System Procurement by Government Agencies" (PDF) / accessed October 19, 2026).

The four items to write in the contract as arrangements are as follows.

  • Handover partner: whether to return it to the ordering side's staff or hand it to the next commissioned company
  • Handover date: the date on which names and authority are transferred, and which party operates the system during the transition
  • What to hand over: for the four rows listed in the right column of the diagram, what to hand over and in what format
  • After handover: to what extent and by what method to respond to inquiries from the party that took over

The approach of deciding the scope of what to hand over as part of preparation for outsourcing was treated in the article on outsourcing new businesses as one of the items to write down before contracting. The arrangements in this article are about deciding when and to whom to hand over that scope.

Our Jiseki ~JISEKI~ is a contract that ties compensation to the business's revenue (the concept is covered in the revenue share article). External service usage fees and advertising placement costs are treated as actual expenses contracted under the ordering side's name, and this demarcation is clearly put in writing at the time of contracting (Jiseki ~JISEKI~).

Regarding ownership of what is created, the FAQ on the same page states: "The developed code and accounts belong to both companies. We do not create a situation where they remain with only one party. Handover at termination is also decided in advance in the contract made before starting." This design of joint ownership differs from the "entry held under the ordering side's name" form that this article recommends for commissioned development. What they share is, first, not creating a situation where things remain with only one party, and second, deciding handover at termination in the contract made before starting. What is written here is the design of our contract, not a track record of the business.

There are cases where not switching is the rational choice; in that case, leave only the exit conditions in the contract

Continuing to entrust work to the same company is not, by itself, lock-in. There are situations where it is rational to keep relying on the company that knows the system best, and what is needed then is not preparation for switching, but leaving only the conditions for exiting in the contract for when you want to switch.

The Japan Fair Trade Commission's report also states that cases of re-contracting with an existing vendor are not immediately cases of vendor lock-in. On that basis, it writes that depending on the reasons and circumstances, it is fully conceivable that some of these include situations of lock-in (Japan Fair Trade Commission "Fact-Finding Survey Report on Information System Procurement by Government Agencies" (PDF) / accessed October 19, 2026).

There are also reasons for leaving the rights with the development company. The IPA model contract made Plan A the default in order to promote software reuse, taking the position that even if copyright belongs to the development company, the leakage of the client's know-how can be prevented by imposing confidentiality obligations. The issue summary in the same document also presents the arguments for assigning copyright to the vendor: certain modifications, reproduction, and adaptation of a program work are permitted under copyright law even to the owner of a copy, so even a client that does not hold copyright can entrust maintenance and operation to an IT systems subsidiary or another external company (IPA "Information Systems Model Transactions and Contracts <Second Edition> (updated April 8, 2025)" (Word) / accessed October 19, 2026). Whether you can switch providers is not decided solely by whether rights are transferred; whether you have on hand source code and documentation that other companies can read also matters.

The guidance on choosing cloud services also contains language that does not treat continued use of the same provider itself as a problem. Digital Agency's DS-310 (decided by the Digital Society Promotion Council secretariat meeting on May 27, 2025) is the government's basic policy on cloud use, and it addresses the idea of actively using multiple providers out of concern for vendor lock-in. It then states that vendor lock-in does not apply as long as data portability and transparency, including a published, reasonable pricing structure and implementation process, are ensured (Digital Agency "DS-310 Basic Policy on the Appropriate Use of Cloud Services in Government Information Systems" (PDF) / accessed October 19, 2026). This applies only to cloud service selection.

In contracts with development companies, too, the right order is to first verify whether the conditions for getting out are in place, before deciding whether to change providers. If you decide to continue, the conditions to keep in the contract can be narrowed down to the following three.

  • The client receives copies of the source code and documentation with each revision
  • Data can be extracted in standard formats
  • Arrangements for handover when termination is decided

If these three are in place, the decision to continue becomes a choice rather than lock-in.

The order for verifying your current system: ownership, rights, documentation, handover destination

For a system already in operation, verify who holds what, in the order of ownership, rights, documentation, and handover destination. Start with ownership because its impact when things stop is the greatest, and verifying it does not require reading the contract.

Ownership: for each entry point, list the people who hold owner and administrator privileges

List the entry points mentioned in the accounts section, and for each one write down who holds owner and administrator privileges and who is the payer. If any entry point was created with a development company employee's personal email address, start negotiations to move it under the client's control first.

Rights: check whether the copyright clause and the deliverables list include the source code

Find the copyright clause in the contract, see which plan its wording is closest to, and whether it touches on the rights in Articles 27 and 28. If the deliverables list does not include the source code, consult the development company about receiving copies. For how to interpret the clauses, consult a professional such as a lawyer.

Documentation: verify whether the design documents and operating procedures match the current system

The IPA model contract states that when rebuilding a system that has been operated and maintained for many years, problems are not uncommon, such as having no personnel who understand the specifications, documentation being scattered or lost, or the running system not matching the design documents or source code. For this reason, it states that a survey of the current system must be conducted before requirements definition to clarify the specifications (IPA "Information Systems Model Transactions and Contracts <Second Edition> (updated April 8, 2025)" (Word) / accessed October 19, 2026).

METI's DX Report explains legacy-ification as a situation in which the contents of your own systems become invisible and you can no longer fix them yourself (METI "DX Report: Overcoming the '2025 Cliff' of IT Systems and Full-Scale Deployment of DX" main text (PDF, archived by the National Diet Library WARP) / accessed October 19, 2026). When considering a switch, too, before checking whether documentation exists, verify whether it matches the current system.

Handover destination: decide who will run the operation when the contract ends

If a member of the client's staff will take it over, make the condition for completed handover that this person can read the ownership records and documentation. If you will entrust it to another company, first verify whether the documentation that company needs in order to decide whether to take it on is complete. As written in the article on in-housing, a state where only the original builder can fix things takes the same shape as a state where only the vendor can fix things. Even when bringing it back in-house, keep it in a form that multiple people can read.

Frequently asked questions about countermeasures for vendor lock-in

Q. Can the copyright of an already-delivered system be transferred to the client afterward?

Article 61, Paragraph 1 of the Copyright Act provides that copyright may be transferred in whole or in part, so if you can reach agreement with the development company, there is room to conclude a contract transferring it after delivery (e-Gov Legal Search "Copyright Act" / accessed October 19, 2026). Even in that case, check whether the transfer clause includes a line on the rights in Articles 27 and 28. If agreement cannot be reached, consider whether you can receive copies of the source code and documentation without transferring rights and entrust maintenance to another company. For individual judgments, consult a professional such as a lawyer.

Q. When using SaaS, what should I check?

Since SaaS takes the form of using an application built by the provider, rather than rights to the source code, verify how data can be extracted. DS-120 states that because SaaS applications and data layouts belong to the provider, the client must choose a service that allows required data to be output in standard formats (Digital Agency "DS-120 Digital Government Promotion Standard Guidelines Practical Guidebook" (PDF) / accessed October 19, 2026). The supplement to the IPA model contract also lists, under "Data Handling" in the checklist for choosing cloud-provided services (SaaS/ASP in the supplement's terminology): whether rights to the data rest with the user (whether data can be extracted when the service ends), whether data migration tools exist, and whether data can be downloaded (IPA "Information Systems Model Transactions and Contracts <Second Edition, Supplement> (updated April 8, 2025)" (Word) / accessed October 19, 2026). Holding the administrator account under the client's name applies to SaaS as well.

Q. Is in-housing the only way to prevent lock-in?

In-house development is not the only option. METI's DX Report states that legacy issues stem not only from technical aspects but also significantly from management aspects, and that IT systems with proper maintenance management are less likely to become black boxes (METI "DX Report ~Overcoming the '2025 Cliff' of IT Systems and Full-scale Deployment of DX" full text (PDF, archived version on the National Diet Library WARP) / accessed October 19, 2026). Even if you continue relying on external parties, if the client side holds the ownership, documentation, and handover arrangements, you can choose a different partner. I wrote about preparing an in-house successor through hiring or partnering externally in the article on FDE hiring and external use, and about when to use external accompaniment to transfer work in-house in the article on where FDE support is useful.

Q. What is the difference between government procurement and contracts with private companies?

The Japan Fair Trade Commission's report is a survey targeting government agencies, but it states that the report's thinking is useful for similar issues in private-sector information system transactions as well. It also expresses the expectation that stakeholders involved in information system procurement broadly, not just in government, will work to prevent vendor lock-in (Japan Fair Trade Commission "Report on the Fact-finding Survey on Information System Procurement by Government Agencies" (PDF) / accessed October 19, 2026). The difference lies in procedures: government procurement rules such as bidding and publication of specifications do not directly apply to private contracts. This article covers only the issues of ownership, rights, documentation, and handover, which work in the same way in private contracts.
