What Forward Deployed Engineers at Tonic.ai Do: A Breakdown of the Data Masking FDE Role

Original title: Tonic.ai 前线部署工程师做什么：数据脱敏 FDE 岗位解析

FDE Times · 2026-10-26 · 6 min read

Summary

This article examines the FDE role at Tonic.ai, whose Textual product removes PII from text and audio. Because most customers self-host Textual in their own VPCs, data never leaves customer environments, so engineers must work inside customer infrastructure to install, verify, and report on de-identification results. Drawing on two job postings, the author shows the role combines deployment, error-type-specific output verification, and accountability through technical sections of client reports, with one-off fixes turned into reusable runbooks and product feedback.

Key points

  • Self-hosting in customer VPCs means Tonic engineers must work where the data lives.
  • FDEs check outputs for three error types: missed entities, wrong substitutions, and split or merged identities.
  • The official posting requires 3-5 years of customer-facing technical experience and up to 25% travel.
  • Writing the technical part of client reports makes data handling an accountable deliverable.
  • One-off fixes must become documentation, runbooks, and product improvements.

Tonic.ai's documentation on data collection contains a brief sentence: in self-hosted deployments, customer data never leaves the customer's environment. That sentence also explains why a company selling redaction software hires Forward Deployed Engineers (FDEs).

When the product can't see the data, the vendor's engineers have to go where the data lives. At Tonic, that means installing infrastructure, checking output quality, and writing part of the reports sent to customers.

For anyone doing FDE work or aspiring to it, Tonic showcases a role that rarely gets attention. The job isn't building agents or delivering polished demos. Customers need someone accountable for one question: is this data actually safe now?

When data can't move, engineers have to

Tonic describes Textual as a tool that removes personally identifiable information (PII) from text and audio, producing data that can be used to train models and run real-time agent workflows.

An FDE job posting the company published on Remotive states the business problem bluntly: the entire industry has to figure out how to hand enterprise data to AI labs without exposing the people in it.

The difficulty lies in where the software runs and where the data is stored. Tonic's documentation says most customers self-host Textual in their own VPC. The product page also recommends on-premises deployment with Kubernetes or Docker for "the highest level of data security and control."

The result is that the vendor can't see the data. The Tonic team can't open logs on their side to see which names the model missed. To know whether the product works correctly on real data, someone has to sit inside the customer's security perimeter. That person is the FDE.

The official posting on Tonic's careers page points in the same direction: the FDE embeds with customer teams to "deploy and operate" Tonic applications as part of an end-to-end solution, with travel up to 25% of the time. The work is primarily remote, but sometimes must be done on-site.

Two job postings, two sides of one job

Read either posting alone and you only see half the role. Put them together and it becomes clear that Tonic's FDE is responsible both for infrastructure and contract scope, and for the quality of every line of data.

The table makes two points. The official posting treats the FDE as someone who keeps deployments running and helps define the scope of services, which determines how the company sells and prices. The Remotive posting is far more specific: what the job delivers is verified results.

Three types of errors to check in every dataset

The Remotive posting explicitly lists the problems the FDE must look for in the output: missed entities, incorrect replacements, and identities that are wrongly merged or split. Engineers new to redaction might assume only the first type matters, but the latter two are harder.

Imagine a customer running a customer service call center that feeds a batch of call transcripts into the data pipeline. Within the same call, the customer is sometimes called "Nguyen Van Minh," sometimes "anh Minh" (an informal honorific in Vietnamese), and later "Mr Nguyen."

If the system replaces these three forms of address with three different fake names, one person becomes three. A model trained on such data learns the wrong conversational structure.

The reverse error is just as bad. In another call there are two people named Minh, one a customer and one an agent. If both are replaced with the same fake name, two identities are merged into one.

If a name is spelled out letter by letter over the phone somewhere and the system fails to catch it, that's a missed entity, and real PII is leaking.

None of these three error types show up in an aggregate "precision" number. So when you get a new dataset, the first thing to do is sample by document type and read the output with your own eyes. Then log errors strictly by these three categories rather than lumping them into one overall rate.

Customer reports are accountability, not paperwork

The most telling detail in the Remotive posting is that the FDE writes the technical sections of reports sent to customers. Handling sensitive data is therefore a deliverable someone is accountable for, not an internal step that gets forgotten once it runs.

The difference becomes obvious the moment you start writing. Anyone can write "the data pipeline has run on the full dataset."

"In the spot-checked sample, identity-splitting errors were concentrated in transcripts using informal forms of address, and have been fixed by adding new rules" — only someone who has actually read the data can write that sentence.

Customers pay for the second kind of sentence.

When data stays in the customer's environment, the vendor can't use its own systems to prove the work was done right. The technical section of the report is where the verification work becomes visible, and it deserves to be treated as seriously as code.

Every one-off fix should become a reusable asset

Every FDE team knows this risk: each customer becomes a separate branch, and the engineer becomes the only person who understands it. Tonic addresses this problem directly in the job description.

The Remotive posting requires turning every one-off fix into documentation, runbooks, and product improvements. The official posting requires the FDE to work with product and engineering teams to provide input on the core platform.

Back to the call center example. The rule mapping "anh Minh" and "Mr Nguyen" to the same identity shouldn't live forever in one customer's script. It should be written up as a runbook entry on conversational data; if it reappears at a second customer, it should be submitted to the product team.

This is exactly why Tonic needs FDEs rather than just technical support engineers. FDEs see error patterns every day that product teams in the office never see, precisely because the data sits behind the customer's VPC.

What skills can you practice before you get the title?

The requirement of 3-5 years of customer-facing technical work experience shows that Tonic isn't hiring fresh graduates for this role. But these skills can be practiced before you get the title, and in this kind of interview you should be able to demonstrate three of them.

The first is installing software in environments you don't control: using Kubernetes or Docker on a restricted network, with no internet access and very narrow permissions.

The second is to eval the entity extraction system's output by error type, rather than settling for a single aggregate number. The third is to write for the customer, using concrete sentences backed by data from spot-checked samples.

When reading any data company's FDE job posting, watch for phrases like "client report," "runbook," "self-host," or "customer VPC." They signal that the role prioritizes accountability for outcomes over giving demos.

On your resume, change "deployed system X" to "deployed within customer infrastructure, verified output by error type, and left behind an operations runbook other teams could reuse."

Tonic's "About Us" page states that the company believes data privacy is a human right. Any website can print that sentence. At Tonic, the person responsible for documenting it item by item and making it real inside customer infrastructure is the FDE.
