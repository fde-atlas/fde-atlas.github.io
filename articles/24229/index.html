What Is the Right Approach for a PoC? 5 Steps to Move from FDE Practice to Production and Success Criteria

Original title: PoCの進め方とは？FDE実務で本番化につなげる5ステップと成功基準

FDE総研 · 2026-09-30 · 12 min read

Summary

This Japanese article explains how forward deployed engineers should structure PoCs so they lead to production rather than ending as stalled proofs of concept. It distinguishes PoC, PoV, and MVP, lays out a five-step process (problem definition, hypotheses and success criteria, data and environment preparation, on-site validation, and evaluation with a Go/No-Go/Pivot decision), and identifies three failure causes: vague goals and criteria, missing field users and production data, and absent production planning. It contrasts FDE-style PoCs with conventional ones, advocates building with production-grade data, permissions, and logging from the start, and stresses organizational handover so operations survive without the FDE.

Key points

  • PoC tests feasibility, PoV tests business value, MVP tests continued adoption.
  • Five steps: define the problem, set success criteria, prepare data, validate on-site, decide Go/No-Go/Pivot.
  • Main failure causes: vague criteria, no field users or production data, no production plan.
  • Success criteria span business, operational, and technical layers, agreed before starting.
  • FDE-style PoCs embed engineers on-site and plan production transition from day one.

What is a PoC, and why does the approach matter?

A PoC is a process of testing hypotheses on a small scale before production investment, and depending on how it is run, it either stays at verification or moves to production.

A PoC (Proof of Concept) is an initiative to verify, within limited time and budget, whether a new technology or mechanism "really works for your company's problems." With the increase in generative AI and data utilization projects, starting a PoC itself is no longer unusual. On the other hand, terms like "PoC fatigue" and "PoC death," referring to a state where verification repeats without ever moving to production, are widely used.

FDE (Forward Deployed Engineer) is a role that spread from Palantir's practice of stationing engineers at customer sites. For an FDE, a PoC is not a place to verify "whether the technology works," but a place to verify "whether the customer's business changes." This article organizes, in 5 steps, the verification design that FDEs use in practice to connect PoCs to production.

How do PoC, PoV, and MVP differ?

PoC verifies "can it be realized," PoV verifies "does it produce value," and MVP verifies "will it continue to be used."

CategoryMain questionMain evaluation criteriaIntended users
PoC (Proof of Concept)Is it technically feasible?Accuracy, processing speed, feasibility of implementationDevelopment team, some staff
PoV (Proof of Value)Does it produce business value?Reduced workload, quality, changes in decision-makingField practitioners
MVP (Minimum Viable Product)Will it be used continuously?Usage rate, adoption, operating costsAll users of the target business process

In the field, these three are often collectively called "PoC." If you articulate in the first meeting "which question this verification answers," it becomes less likely that a gap arises afterward, such as "so, can we actually use it?"

Why do results change depending on the approach?

Because a PoC's conclusion is determined by the "question" and "conditions" of the verification, a vague design makes the results unusable for the next decision.

For example, if you start with the question "Can AI make inquiry handling more efficient?" then even if accuracy figures come out, no one can judge whether those numbers are good or bad. If you concretize the question to "Will drafting first responses reduce operators' correction time?", the same results reveal the next action to take.

Question granularityExampleUsability of results
Vague questionCan AI make inquiry handling more efficient?Even with figures, good or bad cannot be judged
Specific questionWill drafting first responses reduce operators' correction time?Next actions can be decided from the results

Ultimately, the quality of a PoC's approach comes down to the resolution of the question.

What steps should a PoC follow?

An FDE-style PoC proceeds in 5 steps: "problem definition → hypotheses and criteria → data and environment → field verification → evaluation and production decision."

StepWhat to doMain deliverables
1. Problem definitionObserve the workplace and narrow down the problem to solve to just oneProblem definition
2. Setting hypotheses and success criteriaAgree on "what must change for success," with numbers and deadlinesAgreed document of success criteria
3. Preparing data and environmentSet up a state where verification can be done with production-like data and permissionsVerification environment, access permissions
4. Implementation and verification in the fieldPractitioners use it in actual work, iterating feedbackRecord of improvements
5. Evaluation and production decisionJudge Go / No-Go / Pivot against criteria agreed in advanceDecision result

Steps 1-2: What is done in problem definition and setting success criteria?

In the first two steps, you observe the field to narrow down to one problem, and agree on success criteria with stakeholders in writing.

Of the 5 steps, these two most determine a PoC's success or failure. For problem definition, rather than relying only on request documents and interviews, we recommend actually observing the work alongside. A common story: following up on the complaint "filling out forms is a hassle" reveals the real cause is not the input itself but the confirmation flow before it.

Once the problem is narrowed down, write out the following items as hypotheses and agree on them with stakeholders.

Items to agree onContent to write out
WhoseThe users and departments affected by the change
Which workThe scope of the business process targeted
How muchThe magnitude of change considered success, and the deadline
Which questionWhether the verification answers PoC, PoV, or MVP

Steps 3-4: What are the key points of data preparation and field verification?

The key point is to conduct verification with production-like data and actual users, not just samples and developers.

In data preparation, confirm early the handling of personal information, access permissions, and data freshness and missing values. Since many organizations take time for approval from IT and security departments, submitting applications in parallel with Step 1 makes it easier to shorten the overall PoC period.

In field verification, repeat the cycle of "have them use it → hear their difficulties → fix it" in short 1-2 week cycles. This is the stage where an FDE's presence in the field delivers the most value.

Step 5: How is evaluation and the production decision done?

Evaluation is done against criteria agreed in advance, and the conclusion is clearly stated as one of three options—Go, No-Go, or Pivot—without deferring it.

Extensions framed as "we want to keep validating a bit longer" are often just postponing the decision. Even when extending, first redefine "what additional validation will be done, and by when will we know what in order to make the decision." How to concretely set decision criteria is covered later in "How should you define PoC success criteria?"

What causes PoCs to fail?

PoC failure causes can be grouped into three: "vague objectives and criteria," "absence of the field and real data," and "lack of productionization design."

In a July 2024 press release, Gartner predicted that a significant share of generative AI projects would be abandoned after PoC by the end of 2025. Getting stuck at the PoC stage is not a problem limited to a few companies. Typical failure patterns and preventive measures are summarized below.

Failure patternTypical symptomPreventive measure
Vague objectives/criteriaResults can't be evaluated even when producedDocument and agree on success criteria before starting
Overly technical focusAccuracy achieved but not used in operationsInclude practitioners in the validation team
Data diverges from productionAccuracy drops sharply on production dataSecure access to production-equivalent data early
Decision-maker absentDecision stalls after results are reportedMake a budget-holding decision-maker the sponsor
No productionization designFrozen because operations and costs undecidedDiscuss productionization conditions at PoC kickoff

What happens when objectives and success criteria are vague?

With vague objectives, evaluation criteria are retrofitted, and "success" gets interpreted differently by each stakeholder.

A common mismatch: field staff feel "it's more convenient," while management judges "we don't see returns on the investment." The cause is usually not deciding "success for whom" at the start.

What happens when the field and data are absent?

A PoC without field users or production data only works in the test environment, and its assumptions collapse at the productionization stage.

Demos built in a conference room usually run smoothly. But real operations involve numerous exceptions, notation variations, and implicit rules specific to each person. Whether the PoC is exposed to this "field noise" greatly changes how hard productionization will be.

What happens when decision-makers and productionization design are missing?

A PoC lacking a budget-holding decision-maker and productionization conditions gets frozen even with good results.

If no budget holder is present when results are reported, the next investment decision gets taken away and its priority declines over time. The same happens to PoCs that end without an operations plan or cost outlook. Put a budget-holding decision-maker in as sponsor, and discuss productionization conditions at PoC kickoff.

How should you define PoC success criteria?

Split success criteria into three layers—business, operational, and technical—and agree on thresholds and deadlines with stakeholders before starting.

What goes into each of the three success criteria layers?

Higher layers tie directly to management decisions, while lower layers are easier to measure during validation, so design the three layers to connect.

LayerQuestionExample metricsMain parties to align with
Business layerIs there value justifying the investment?Cost savings, revenue impact, risk reductionManagement, business owner
Operational layerDoes field work actually change?Work time, rework count, continued usage rateField managers, staff
Technical layerDoes it run stably?Accuracy, response time, error rate, operational loadIT department, development team

Judging success by technical metrics alone tends to yield "accuracy achieved but never used." Including at least one operational-layer metric helps reduce evaluation drift.

When should Go/No-Go criteria be decided?

Decide the criteria before the PoC starts. Deciding after seeing results invites convenient interpretations.

In practice, setting thresholds in three tiers makes agreement easier.

DecisionHow conditions are setNext action
GoExceeds agreed numbersProceed to productionization preparation
Conditional continuationFalls between Go and No-GoRedefine the scope and deadline of additional validation
No-GoFalls below agreed numbersStop, or pivot by changing the question

Concrete numbers vary greatly by business and industry. Rather than reusing other companies' figures, it's safest to set them from a baseline measured from your current operations.

How does an FDE-style PoC differ from a conventional PoC?

The biggest difference is that in an FDE-style PoC, engineers embed in the field and take end-to-end ownership from problem redefinition to productionization.

AspectConventional PoCFDE-style PoC
課題の起点 要件定義書・RFP 現場観察と対話による再定義
Engineer's locationImplementation per requirements from the development siteOn-site at the customer or frequent visits
Validation environmentExtracted sample dataProduction-like data and actual business workflows
Improvement cycleReporting and approval per phaseReflecting on-the-ground feedback daily to weekly
GoalReport on technical feasibilityProduction operation and changes to business processes

How does being on-site change the challenges?

Because FDEs can directly observe operations in the field, they can more easily discover the "real problems to solve" that lie behind the requirements document.

The value of the FDE model, which spread from Palantir, lies not in "building what you're told" but in "discovering together with the customer what should be built." For that reason, in FDE-style PoCs, it is not uncommon for the problem statement to be rewritten in the early stages. In fact, this is often seen as a healthy sign.

What does "building with production in mind" mean?

In the FDE approach, implementation from the PoC stage already takes production data, permissions, and operations into account, reducing the need to rebuild after validation.

If you validate with a throwaway prototype, you end up redesigning when moving to production, and by then enthusiasm on the ground has cooled. You don't need to build everything at production quality. However, if you build the following three items with the same approach as production, the transition becomes much easier.

Items to align with productionWhy they should be aligned
Data integrationAvoids redesigning when switching to production data
Authentication and permissionsPrevents assumptions from collapsing during security review
LogsTrack usage and quality in production just as they are

What is needed to move from PoC to production operation?

Moving to production requires both technical migration preparation and organizational/operational handover; if either is missing, the system won't take hold.

What should be prepared on the technical side?

On the technical side, review availability, security, monitoring, and cost against production standards, and resolve the provisional measures used during the PoC.

ItemTypical state at PoC stageActions required for production
AvailabilitySingle environment, manual recoveryRedundancy and recovery procedures for failures
SecurityProvisional permissions for a limited group of membersPermission design, audit logs, and compliance with internal policies
MonitoringDevelopers check manually by eyeAlert configuration and regular quality monitoring
CostUsage-based billing at validation scaleCost estimation and budgeting for expanded usage

When using generative AI, output quality may change due to model updates or changes in input data. Build in a mechanism to evaluate quality regularly even after operations begin.

What should be handed over on the organizational and operational side?

On the organizational side, designate a business owner, operations staff, and a contact point for inquiries, and hand over to a structure that works even without the FDE.

Note that the more deeply the FDE gets involved in the field, the more likely it becomes a situation where "things don't work without the FDE." At the production stage, put the following items in place and gradually transfer ownership to the customer's staff.

What to hand overDetails
RolesBusiness owner, operations staff, and contact point for inquiries
DocumentsOperations manual and how to handle frequently asked questions
MechanismsA route for receiving improvement requests
For usersBriefing sessions and updates to business manuals

User briefing sessions and business manual updates are easily forgotten, so make sure to state them explicitly in the migration plan.

The success or failure of a PoC depends largely not on the technical quality but on "question design" and "the path to production." For engineers interested in becoming FDEs, experience running these five steps becomes practical skills that apply regardless of job type.
