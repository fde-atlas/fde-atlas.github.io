Today's Judgment

What deserves the most attention today is not how many more agents have appeared, but the fact that three kinds of "capability proof" are beginning to stratify:

National-level application testing uses unified scenarios to filter out teams that can actually complete tasks (151 organizations and 251 teams competing side by side); industry field operations are writing SLAs and pay-for-results into commercial relationships (Neolix: customers no longer ask "can the vehicle run," but "can the task be handed to you"); vendor ecosystems are turning small business adoption into a distribution system with connectors, approval modes, and trainer networks (Claude for Small Business: 43 workflows + 750 community training sessions).

For FDEs: certificates only prove knowledge coverage, tests only prove performance on specific tasks, and true delivery capability must still be proven jointly by customer tasks, continuous operation, exception handling, sign-off, and repeat purchases.

Key Signals

National-level AI security application testing announces results: unified scenarios filter talent

  • Source: Jiangsu Cyberspace Administration website, republished from the National Computer Network Emergency Response Center | Original link
  • The results of the national-level AI-empowered cybersecurity application testing have been announced: the event set up 8 real security scenarios, with a total of 151 organizations and 251 teams registering; 32 teams achieved "good results," and the testing ran on Huawei's Ascend software and hardware environment. The selected teams were diverse—ranging from institutions like People's Daily Online and the People's Public Security University of China, to enterprises like Jiangsu Rural Commercial United Bank and universities like Fuzhou University Zhicheng College.
  • Why it matters: This is a capability screening mechanism of "unified tasks, unified environment, competitive samples, external results"—closer to real evidence of capability than certificates, plaques, or course completions. It provides a replicable middle layer for FDE talent development: between "knowing how to use tools" and "being able to do enterprise delivery," establish objective screening through unified scenario testing. University teams competing alongside professional institutions and achieving results also shows that the diversity of talent supply is increasing. Note the boundary: "good results" is not a championship or commercial acceptance, and test performance cannot be directly equated with enterprise delivery capability—the four-level ladder of capability evidence is: theoretical certification → unified scenario testing → real customer tasks → continuous operation/sign-off/repeat purchases.

Neolix: customers shift from "can the vehicle run" to "can the task be handed to you," SLAs begin entering contracts

  • Source: 36Kr | Original link
  • From a 36Kr industry roundtable transcript: Neolix's Executive President said that after scaling operations, customers began discussing SLAs around timeliness, cost, stability, and cargo damage control, with some requirements written directly into contracts; the business model has extended from selling vehicles to per-order and pay-for-results pricing. Moqi Intelligence added on the same panel: hotel customers don't care about the success rate of a single action, but about whether a room can be cleaned to standard within a time window, and how failures are coordinated with cleaning staff.
  • Why it matters: The shift from "feature acceptance" to "result accountability" is a key transition in enterprise AI deployment maturity. Only when customers' real tasks multiply and accountability becomes clear, and when enterprises cover richer exceptions and edge cases, can the data flywheel start spinning. The right way to write enterprise agent contracts is the seven-field results-based contract: task unit → service window → success threshold → quality/safety boundaries → human takeover → failure liability → pricing and renewal conditions. First define the results the customer is willing to pay for, then decide on the model and deployment approach—changes in contract structure say more about the industry maturing than any product release.

Claude for Small Business: 43 workflows, 27 new integrations, 750 community training sessions

  • Source: Anthropic official | Original link
  • Anthropic's September 15 update to its small business offering: it now includes 43 workflows and 27 new integrations (Shopify, Salesforce, Stripe, Zoom, etc.), with over 900,000 installs since launching in May. The deployment mechanism is noteworthy: every workflow starts in approval mode by default, waiting for user confirmation whenever sending, publishing, or payment is involved; permissions inherit from the original system and can be gradually granted per individual workflow. More than 150 approved training organizations plan to hold over 750 community workshops.
  • Why it matters: Adoption services for SMBs have been broken down into a four-piece kit of "ready-made workflows + connectors + permission controls + local trainers"—the vendor handles the first three, leaving the last mile to a network of local coaches. This is a clear lesson for training service providers: rather than teaching tool operation, offer "3 re-runnable industry workflow packages + 30 days of adoption accompaniment"—each package spelling out inputs, connectors, approval points, result metrics, and human responsibilities. The fact that the vendor still needs to train 150 local coaches after 900,000 installs proves precisely that the last mile of adoption cannot be absorbed by the product itself. Note: 900,000 is install count, not monthly active users or paying customers.

Implications for FDE Practitioners

  • Manage your own and your team's capability claims with the four-level ladder: theoretical certification → unified scenario testing → real customer tasks → continuous operation/sign-off/repeat purchases. External statements must strictly correspond to the evidence level—"made the good results list of a national-level test" can be said; "leading team nationwide" cannot. Mixing levels is a slow poison to trust.
  • Turn PoC contracts into task contracts: first define 1 priced task, service window, success threshold, human takeover, and failure liability, then discuss feature expansion. A provider willing to sign an SLA and one that only dares to list a feature inventory can differ by an order of magnitude in pricing.
  • Transform courses into an "adoption system": industry workflow packages + connector configuration + approval point setup + a 30-day re-run checklist—upgrading from "teaching operations" to "accompanying until it's actually used," which is also the path for training businesses to move from one-time revenue to recurring service fees.
