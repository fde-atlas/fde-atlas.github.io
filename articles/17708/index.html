FDE 30-60-90 Day Plan: Delivering First Value in Four Weeks

Original title: FDE 30-60-90 天计划：新人四周交付首个价值

FDE Times · 2026-10-08 · 5 min read

Summary

This guide lays out a 30-60-90 day plan for new forward deployed engineers (FDEs), arguing that the six-week window for showing customer value is really only about four weeks, since the first two are typically consumed by internal onboarding. It recommends picking a low-effort, high-impact "home run" task agreed on jointly with the customer, illustrated with an invoice-reconciliation scoring example. Weeks five and six are a likely "wall" period and should be reserved for fixes and production rollout. By day 30 you should be able to brief the account without notes; by day 60 you become first responder; day 90 means initial account ownership, since full ramp-up takes 3 to 9 months. The piece closes with resume advice and a re-scoring exercise.

Key points

  • Six weeks minus two weeks of internal onboarding leaves roughly four weeks of real customer time.
  • First value should be a jointly agreed home run: low effort, high customer impact.
  • Weeks five and six are a wall period; reserve them for fixes and production, not new features.
  • Day 90 means initial account ownership; full ramp-up takes 3 to 9 months.
  • Common failures: picking the biggest task first, letting the customer decide alone, tracking one metric.

Key Takeaways

  • The first two weeks are usually internal work, leaving only about four weeks of the six-week goal for actual time with the customer.
  • The first value delivered should be a "home run": low effort, high impact for the customer, and agreed on jointly with the customer rather than left for them to decide alone.
  • The day-90 goal is initial ownership of the account, not full ramp-up, since engineers typically take 3 to 9 months to get fully up to speed.

Imagine you've just taken over a customer account and your manager wants the customer to "see value in the first six weeks." That sounds generous—until you open your calendar and do the math.

In practice, a Forward Deployed Engineer's (FDE) first two weeks are usually spent on internal work: learning the company's deployment patterns, requesting system access, and shadowing senior FDEs. Subtract those two weeks, and only about four of the six remain for real work with the customer.

So the 30-60-90 day plan isn't a formality. Its purpose is to make sure you don't spend your precious four weeks on work that's "done correctly, but invisible to the customer."

Start from the customer's clock, not yours

Entrans defines an FDE's time-to-value as: the time from project kickoff to the customer's first real business outcome. The key words are "real business": a demo running on your laptop doesn't count.

Rocketlane offers a practical framing: ask what value can be unlocked for the customer at days 30, 60, and 90 from kickoff. Combine the two, and your plan should have two layers: one for your own learning, and one for the value the customer will see at each milestone.

The first two weeks: build your foundation, don't just wait

Tandem has an onboarding handbook written for the second FDE joining an in-flight project. It puts the customer briefing before the first-day office tour. If no one gives you one, write it yourself: who the customer is, where the pain points are, who makes decisions, and what the company has promised.

In week one, you attend every customer meeting alongside a senior colleague, with both of you using that briefing as a shared starting point. You don't need to talk much. Your job is to note down every piece of work the customer mentions, because that list is the raw material for the next step.

By the end of week two, you should have your access, your customer briefing, and a list of candidate tasks. If any of these is missing, that's the first risk to raise.

Example: picking a "home run" for an invoice reconciliation customer

Imagine you've taken over a distributor that wants an agent to automatically reconcile invoices. After two weeks of meetings, you have five candidate tasks. You score each on effort and customer impact, on a scale of 1 to 3.

Candidate taskEffortImpactDecision
Read PDF invoices from email and push them to accounting's reconciliation sheet13Home run, do first
Two-way ERP integration3360–90 day milestone
Overview dashboard for the director11Later
Fine-tuning a custom model for invoice templates31Not for now
Alerts via internal chat for invoices with pricing errors22Week 5–6 candidate

Rocketlane calls low-effort, high-impact tasks "home runs" and recommends prioritizing them for your first value delivery. In the table above, only the PDF invoice task qualifies.

The ERP integration is far more tempting—every engineer wants to bite into the hard problem. But with only four weeks, it will almost certainly be unfinished by the time the customer starts asking "do we have anything to use yet?" Push it to the 60-to-90-day milestone, when you've earned the trust to negotiate for more time.

The next step is neither deciding on your own nor letting the customer decide alone, but sitting down with the customer to jointly map the route to the first value. You might open with the head of accounting like this: "If three weeks from now your team no longer has to manually enter invoices from email, would that count as a meaningful outcome?"

If she nods, you have a measurable goal and a counterpart on the customer side sharing ownership. If she shakes her head and points to something else, you've just saved yourself three weeks of work in the wrong direction.

Weeks five and six: prepare for the wall

Writing about an FDE's first 90 days, FDE Academy notes that most new FDEs hit their "wall" around weeks five and six. Those who get through it smoothly receive the customer's first signal of trust.

What the wall looks like varies by account. But on the timeline in the example above, it most likely appears when the home run starts running on real data: the data is dirtier than expected, an access step is still missing, and users' actual practices differ from the process they described.

So reserve week six for fixing issues and pushing to production; don't schedule new features that week.

FDE Academy also notes that a new FDE's first task is rarely the team's highest-risk project. If your task is smaller than expected, that's normal. Do it cleanly.

Three checkpoints: 30, 60, 90

Day 30 is a readiness test: you walk a senior colleague through the entire customer account. Rehearse until you can explain the customer's pain points, who makes decisions, and where the home run stands without opening any notes.

By day 60, you become the customer's first responder, with senior colleagues stepping in only for exceptions. This is also the milestone set in Tandem's handbook. At this point, bigger work like the ERP integration starts to make sense, because the customer is used to coming to you directly.

By day 90, set expectations correctly. According to Tandem, engineers take 3 to 9 months on average to fully ramp up, so the day-90 goal is initial ownership of the account, not full mastery.

Mistakes that derail the plan early

The most common mistake is picking the biggest task first because it looks good on slides. By week six, there's plenty of progress but no business outcome for the customer.

The second is letting the customer choose the outcome without discussion, which lands you a requirement that can't be finished in four weeks. Another mistake is fixating on a single number.

Entrans recommends a balanced scorecard instead of a single metric, to avoid perverse incentives—like shortening time-to-value by delivering a solution nobody uses three months later.

The last mistake is treating the two weeks of internal work as dead time. This is the cheapest time to learn, ask questions, and take notes. Waste it, and you'll pay the price when you hit the wall.

How to show this skill on your resume

When reading FDE job descriptions, watch for phrases like "time-to-value," "first value," or "customer outcomes." For example, Palantir's Forward Deployed Software Engineer posting says the role works directly with customers to quickly understand their biggest problems.

When you see descriptions like this, be ready with a story about how you sequenced your work in the first weeks of a project. With only four weeks, "quickly" is exactly the pressure described above.

Don't just write "implemented X integration" on your resume. Write it as a sequence: which task you did first and why, what business outcome the customer got, and how long it took.

Exercise: re-score your last project

Pick a project you worked on in the past year—projects at product companies count too. Write out its first six weeks week by week, then mark the week when users got their first real business outcome.

Then rebuild a scoring table for that project's tasks, just like the invoice example. If the first task done wasn't a home run, reorder the sequence and estimate how many weeks earlier the first outcome could have arrived. That reordering is the story you bring into the interview.

Next time you take on a new project, work backward from the milestone the customer is waiting for. Four weeks sounds short, but it's enough to deliver a home run agreed on with the customer in advance.
