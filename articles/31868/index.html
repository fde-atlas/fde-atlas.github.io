Internal champion: how FDEs find and test one

FDE Times · 2026-10-09 · 5 min read

Summary

This article argues that a pilot's fate is usually decided in customer leadership meetings the FDE never attends, and survival depends on an internal champion. Champions stake their reputation and open doors to budget holders, while supporters go quiet under political pressure. The author recommends looking among respected end users, giving value before asking, and testing candidates by requesting access to the budget decision-maker. Champions also need an executive sponsor above them, honest data including failures, and ideally a champion group rather than a single person, so the project survives personnel changes.

Key points

  • A champion stakes their reputation; a supporter goes quiet when politics hit.
  • The quickest test: ask the candidate to open the door to the budget holder.
  • Look among respected end users, not the biggest titles.
  • Champions need an executive sponsor shielding them from above.
  • Share bad numbers and build a champion group to avoid over-reliance on one person.

In brief

  • A real champion stakes their professional reputation on the project; a supporter goes quiet once things turn political.
  • The quickest test: ask them to open the door to the person who decides the budget, then see whether it happens.
  • A champion needs an executive covering them from above and needs to be kept honest, or they become an evangelist for an outdated system.

The meeting that decides the fate of your pilot almost always happens without you. It is the customer’s leadership briefing, where someone asks whether that agent is worth continuing. The answer depends on one thing: whether anyone in the room speaks up for you.

Coding skill will not save you here. The integration can be clean and the evals can look good, but if nobody on the customer’s side will defend the project when budgets tighten or another department objects, your work ends at the demo.

The person who does that is called an internal champion. Finding, testing and developing champions is a learnable skill, much like debugging: you form a hypothesis, run a test and watch for signs you were wrong.

Someone who likes you is not necessarily a champion

A champion combines three things: a belief that the solution should succeed, real influence, and access to the decision-makers, as UserGems describes it. But the defining trait, according to Spotlight.ai, is that they stake their own professional reputation on the project’s success. Without that, all you have is a pleasant person.

The most important line, then, runs between champions and supporters. When a project gets caught up in internal politics, supporters go quiet while champions step in to defend it. At demo stage, the two look identical.

Supporter

  • Praises the demo, replies to messages quickly
  • Goes quiet when the project meets opposition
  • Apologises for not being able to introduce you upwards
  • Loses nothing if the project fails

Champion

  • May be sceptical at first, asks hard questions
  • Speaks up when the project hits politics
  • Opens the door to the budget holder themselves
  • Ties their personal reputation to the outcome

Where to look: at ground level, not at the top

Many engineers instinctively seek out the person with the biggest title. In practice, backing from a respected colleague often carries more weight than a meeting with a C-level executive, because that person opens a path to stakeholders the org chart does not show you, a point UserGems also stresses.

The first place to look is among end users: identify them early and you will spot the people who could become your strongest advocates, advice Close.com originally wrote for salespeople. FDEs have an even better position: you sit beside end users every day, and see who is tormented by the old process and whose opinion the whole team seeks.

In Tryolabs’ FDE model, the first week is for getting inside the customer’s real environment: mapping the systems and aligning the stakeholders. Do both at once. As you trace a data flow, you meet the very people who run it, and they form your first list of candidates.

Example: two candidates at a logistics company

Imagine you are deploying an agent that helps dispatch vehicles on the night shift. The first candidate is Minh, the IT team lead, who always answers Slack within five minutes and praises the demo in front of everyone. The second is Ha, the dispatch shift lead, who asks nothing but hard questions, yet every dispatcher with a tricky shift runs over to ask her.

Developing a champion is a two-way relationship: you treat them as a colleague and an expert, and offer value before asking for anything, in the spirit Close.com describes. So the first step with Ha is not to ask for anything at all. You fix the shift handover report she still has to stitch together by hand every morning.

Two weeks later, once you have trial figures, comes the test. The most practical approach is to ask the candidate to open the door to the budget holder and see whether it happens. The exchange might go like this.

You: Next week we’ll have two weeks of night-shift data. Do you think the operations director should see it before the quarterly review? Ha: Yes. He meets the shift leads on Thursday; I’ll ask for fifteen minutes. I’ll cover the operations side, you handle the technical part.

Ask Minh the same question and you get: “He’s very busy, let me see.” That is exactly the line Spotlight.ai draws: a real champion creates access, a supporter apologises for not being able to. Minh is still useful for system access, but the person to invest in is Ha.

A champion needs someone covering them from above

Finding Ha is not the end of it. Implementation champions often feel that leadership gives very little support to adopting new ways of working, according to a study by the Construction Industry Institute. A lone champion will burn out, or be brushed aside by their superiors in the first round of cuts.

Salesforce’s vice president of Forward-Deployed Engineering says that to get real commitment, you have to take the conversation to the right level. A champion and an executive sponsor are different roles: one pulls from below, the other shields from above. That is why the first thing you ask a champion for should be a meeting with that sponsor.

Do not let your champion become an evangelist

There is a risk in the opposite direction. A 2005 case study presented at the AMCIS conference warned that the subtle shift from champion to evangelist can hurt business outcomes, especially when that person is an executive. They keep defending a system that no longer fits.

The safeguard is to keep your champion honest. Give Ha the bad numbers too, the cases where the agent got it wrong, so she defends the project with data rather than faith.

A more durable approach is to turn one person into a group. Tryolabs states plainly that “an adoption metrics baseline and a trained group of internal champions” are deliverables of the adoption phase. A group with data lasts longer than one enthusiastic individual.

The mistakes that lose you a champion

The most common mistake is confusing the person who replies fastest with the person who has influence, as with Minh. The second is asking before giving: requesting a meeting with the director in the first week, before you have solved anything for them.

The third is leaving the champion to carry the project alone without a sponsor. The last is handing over to a single person. When that person changes jobs, the project is orphaned.

In FDE job descriptions, phrases such as “stakeholder management” or “drive adoption” usually point to exactly this skill. Prepare a specific story for your CV and interviews: how you recognised who the champion was, what test confirmed it, and what the champion group you left behind could do once you were gone.

Your code runs on the customer’s servers. Whether your project survives, though, lives in the head of one person on the customer’s side, at precisely the moment you are not in the meeting room.
