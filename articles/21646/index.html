A Product Manager (PdM) is a role that defines "what to build, why, and for whom" and bears ultimate responsibility for the product's success (business outcomes). Often called a "Mini-CEO," a PdM oversees everything end-to-end, from strategy formulation through development to post-release growth. The mission is not merely progress management but maximizing business value through the product.

With the spread of DX and SaaS, we have entered an era where excellent products directly determine a company's competitiveness. In this context, the importance of PdMs, who hold the key to a product's success, has never been higher.

A PdM is called a "Mini-CEO" because they take on strategy, decision-making, and accountability for a single product, just like a business owner.

The reasons a PdM operates like an executive are reflected in the following areas of responsibility.

  • Strategy setting: Envisioning what the product will achieve
  • Decision-making: Deciding what to build and what not to build within limited resources
  • Accountability: Taking responsibility for business outcomes such as revenue, usage rates, and customer satisfaction
  • Cross-functional coordination: Involving development, sales, marketing, and management to steer everything in one direction

As a professional observation, the term "Mini-CEO" can be misleading. A PdM does not have the strong authority of a CEO; in most cases, a PdM is required to exercise "leadership without authority." They must engage and move stakeholders without direct command authority—this difficulty is the essence of the PdM role. Note that "PdM" uses a lowercase "d" to avoid confusion with Project Manager.

The clear difference is that a PdM is responsible for "Why/What (why build it and what to build)," while a PjM is responsible for "How (how to build it)," and a PO is responsible for "delivery and prioritization decisions" in Scrum.

Here is a summary of the differences among the three roles.

RolePrimary responsibilityCore questionGoal
Product Manager (PdM)Product success (business outcomes)Why/What (why and what)The product wins in the market
Project Manager (PjM)Project completionHow (how to build)Achieving QCD (quality, cost, delivery)
Product Owner (PO)Optimizing the development backlogDelivery and prioritizationMaximizing product value (within Scrum)

As a real-world insight, while a PdM aims to "build a product that wins in the market," a PjM is responsible for "building what has been decided, on schedule." A PO, in the context of Scrum development, is the role that sets priorities for the development team. If this distinction remains ambiguous in practice, the "PdM in name only" problem described later arises. If you want to learn more about the PjM role, also see Can Project Management Side Jobs Be Lucrative? Rates and How to Land Them.

The reason demand for PdMs is rising in the BtoB space is that "with the spread of SaaS, the key to competitiveness has shifted from simply building a product to operating one that customers keep using."

The background behind the surge in demand can be summarized as follows.

  • SaaS subscription models: Revenue now depends on continued usage (churn prevention), not one-time sales
  • Product-led growth (PLG): We have entered an era where the product itself drives growth, not just sales power
  • Increasingly complex customer challenges: BtoB problems run deep, making specialists who can identify true challenges and translate them into features indispensable
  • The rise of generative AI: Product design premised on AI is now required, expanding the PdM's role

As a professional observation, in BtoB SaaS, "enabling customers to achieve outcomes" matters more than "adding features." Whether a company has a PdM who can refine the product from this perspective directly affects churn rates and LTV (customer lifetime value). That is exactly why companies are desperate to secure PdMs. For the big picture of BtoB product strategy, the fundamentals of BtoB marketing strategy are also a useful reference.

The work of a PdM comes down to four core responsibilities: "strategy formulation (Why/Who)," "roadmap planning (What)," "stakeholder management," and "impact measurement and growth." A PdM takes these on end to end and leads the product to success. This section explains each responsibility in concrete terms.

The first core responsibility is defining "why we are building this product (Why)" and "whose problems it will solve, and which ones (Who)." This is the starting point of everything and the most important task.

The elements that must be clarified in strategy formulation are as follows.

  • Market opportunity: which market to target and how big the chance is
  • Target customers: whose problems to solve, and which ones
  • Value proposition: why your company should solve those problems, and how you differentiate from competitors
  • Business model: how to generate revenue and make it viable as a business

As a common failure in practice, some teams leave Why and Who vague and rush into feature development for reasons like "a competitor launched it" or "sales wants it," ending up with a product that resonates with no one. An excellent PdM first rigorously defines "what problem truly needs to be solved." If this foundation wavers, everything that follows falls apart.

The second core responsibility is deciding "what to build, and in what order (What)" based on the strategy and translating it into a roadmap. This tests your decision-making on where to invest limited resources.

The key perspectives to prioritize in roadmap planning are as follows.

PerspectiveDescription
PrioritizationJudging development priority based on customer value and business impact
Selection and trade-offsClearly deciding "what not to do"
Resource allocationAllocating limited development capacity where it will have the greatest effect
Flexible revisionUpdating the roadmap in response to market and customer changes

As a real-world standard for technology choices, excellent PdMs avoid "doing everything." Cramming in feature after feature slows development, makes the product complex, and ultimately resonates with no customer. The ability to decide "what to cut" is the true proof of a PdM's skill.

The third core responsibility is acting as a "hub" that connects stakeholders with different positions—development, sales, marketing, and management—and guides them toward a single goal. As a leader without authority, the PdM brings stakeholders together.

The main stakeholders a PdM coordinates with, and their concerns, are as follows.

StakeholderMain concernsPdM's role
ManagementBusiness results and ROIBuilding consensus on strategy, reporting progress
Development teamTechnical feasibility and effortClarifying requirements, sharing priorities
Sales teamCustomer requests and ordersFiltering requests, explaining the roadmap
MarketingMarket appeal and customer acquisitionArticulating the product's value

As a professional's view, it is no exaggeration to say that most of a PdM's work is "coordination." Among stakeholders with different interests and languages, the PdM overcomes conflicts and builds consensus. This gritty coordination skill is the driving force that moves a product forward. For more on working on equal footing with technical teams, see What Is a Technical Architect? Role, Essential Skills, and How to Go Independent at High Rates.

The fourth core responsibility is not stopping at release, but analyzing data to verify impact, continuously running improvement cycles, and growing the product.

The activities involved in impact measurement and growth are as follows.

  • KPI monitoring: continuously measuring usage, retention, conversion, and more
  • Data analysis: interpreting the "why it isn't being used" behind the numbers
  • Forming improvement hypotheses: designing the next move based on data
  • A/B testing and validation: testing hypotheses on a small scale and improving while verifying their effect

As a common failure in practice, some teams treat a feature's release as "done" and neglect subsequent impact measurement. For an excellent PdM, release is not the goal but the start. Measuring "whether what we built is actually being used and producing results" and continuously improving is what sustains a product's growth. Organizations that produce "PdMs in name only" share three traits: "no decision-making authority," "responsible only for progress management," and "no accountability for business metrics." In most cases these are problems of organizational structure, not individual ability. This section explains failure patterns that occur frequently in practice and how to avoid them.

The first failure pattern is a PdM with no decision-making authority who simply relays requests from sales or management to development, becoming an "errand runner (message carrier)."

The typical symptoms of this pattern are as follows.

  • Converting requests brought in by sales directly into development tasks
  • Management's whims cutting in and overriding priorities
  • The PdM unable to decide "what to build" and stuck playing coordinator
  • Having neither the authority nor the bandwidth to question "why build it"

As a common failure in practice, requests from the loudest stakeholders keep getting inserted one after another, resulting in an incoherent patchwork of features. Unless the PdM has the authority to say "No," the product loses its strategic coherence.

The second failure pattern is "de facto PjM-ification": despite holding the title of PdM, the person actually handles only development progress management (How).

In organizations where the roles of PdM and PjM are conflated, the following situations arise.

Original PdM responsibilitiesReality of a PjM-ified PdM
Defining Why/WhatOnly managing progress on given specifications
Accountability for business resultsOnly managing deadlines and QCD
Strategic decision-makingTask assignment and coordination
Exploring customer problemsTracking the development schedule

As a professional's view, progress management is important work, but it is the PjM's role. When a PdM is swamped by it, they lose the time needed to think about "what to build and why"—their true job. Organizations where "a PdM exists but the product lacks strategic coherence" have almost always undergone this PjM-ification.

The third failure pattern is a PdM who does not commit to business metrics (revenue, LTV, etc.) and counts only "how many features were added" as results.

The states this pattern tends to fall into are as follows.

  • Treating "we released X features this quarter" as an achievement
  • Not measuring how features contributed to business outcomes (revenue, retention rate)
  • Unused features pile up, and the product becomes bloated
  • Confusing output (amount produced) with outcome (results generated)

As a common failure in the field, there are cases where features keep increasing while churn and revenue don't improve, resulting in a "feature-bloated product." PdMs should be evaluated not by "what they built" but by "what business outcomes they generated." Without this perspective, the product loses its direction.

To avoid being a PdM in name only, two organizational measures are essential: "proper delegation of authority to the PdM" and "redesigning evaluation metrics (KPIs) based on business outcomes."

Here is a summary of concrete measures for avoidance.

IssueOrganizational countermeasure
Becoming a goferGive the PdM decision-making authority over "what not to build"
Becoming a project managerSeparate the roles of PdM and PjM, and secure progress management separately
Overemphasis on feature additionsEvaluate based on outcomes (business results), not outputs
Ambiguity of authorityDocument responsibilities and authority explicitly, and get agreement from stakeholders

As a professional view, the PdM-in-name-only problem is often misunderstood as "a lack of individual ability," but its essence is an organizational structure issue. No matter how talented a person you hire, they cannot perform in an environment with no authority where they are evaluated on feature additions. The fundamental solution is for management to redesign "what to delegate to the PdM and how to evaluate them."

An excellent PdM needs a triangle of three skills—"business acumen," "technology skills," and "user understanding/UX skills"—plus the soft skills that support them. No single one is enough; balance and overall capability determine market value. Here we explain each skill in detail.

The first skill is "business acumen": understanding the market and connecting the product to business outcomes. Before being a technologist, a PdM is responsible for making the business viable.

The components of business acumen are as follows.

  • Market understanding: the ability to read market size, competitors, and trends
  • Revenue design: understanding of business models, pricing design, and unit economics
  • Collaboration with business development: a perspective for working with BizDev to expand partnerships and sales channels
  • Speaking in numbers: the ability to quantitatively demonstrate business impact, such as ROI and LTV

As a professional view, many PdMs are strong in technology or UX, but talent with business acumen is rare. Whether you can articulate "how much money does this product make" determines the trust you earn from management. For a business development perspective, Skills required for BtoB business development (BizDev) is also a useful reference.

The second skill is "technology skills": the ability to discuss on equal footing with engineers and define requirements accurately. While you don't need to write code yourself, a deep understanding of technology is essential.

The technology skills required of a PdM are as follows.

ElementDescription
Technical literacyUnderstanding how systems work and how development flows
Requirements definitionThe ability to translate ideas into implementable requirements
Feasibility judgmentThe ability to assess "whether that is technically difficult"
Understanding technical debtUnderstanding the trade-off between speed and quality

As a common failure in the field, there are cases where a PdM who doesn't understand technology makes unreasonable demands like "that's easy, right?" and loses the trust of the development team. A PdM who understands the technical essentials can produce realistic requirements and build good relationships with the development team.

The third skill is qualitative and quantitative research skills and UX design skills to uncover users' "true pain points." Capturing the essential problems customers don't put into words is the source of excellent products.

The main methods used for user understanding are as follows.

  • Qualitative research: deep understanding through user interviews and behavioral observation
  • Quantitative research: grasping trends through surveys and data analysis
  • UX design: designing usability and experiential value
  • Hypothesis validation: verifying user reactions with prototypes and MVPs

As a professional view, simply building the features users say they "want" is not enough. The PdM's true skill lies in uncovering the real pain points that users themselves haven't noticed. What's required is not "building what you're told" but the ability to "discern what is truly needed."

Fourth, as the foundation supporting the triangle of three skills, soft skills such as leadership, negotiation, and documentation abilities are essential.

The soft skills essential for a PdM are as follows.

SkillDescription
LeadershipThe ability to involve people and set direction even without authority
NegotiationThe ability to find common ground among conflicting interests
DocumentationThe ability to clearly articulate and share visions and requirements
CommunicationThe ability to convey messages appropriately to people in different positions

As a professional view, the PdM is a role that demands "leadership without authority." To move stakeholders without command authority, you need both logic (documents) and empathy (negotiation and dialogue). These soft skills convert specialized skills into actual results.

In AI product development, the collaboration between "PdMs who focus on Why/What" and "FDEs who rapidly turn How into reality in the field" creates a powerful synergy that maximizes product value. The two have a complementary relationship and are attracting particular attention in the era of generative AI. Here we explain the essence of this collaboration.

An FDE (Forward Deployed Engineer) is a field-focused, ultra-fast implementation engineer who deeply embeds in the customer's site and handles everything end-to-end, from problem discovery to implementation and production deployment. Established by US-based Palantir, demand has been expanding rapidly in recent years, especially among AI vendors.

The main characteristics of an FDE are as follows.

  • Near-resident-level immersion: deeply understanding the customer's business and implementing on the spot
  • Engineer × Consultant Hybrid: Combining technical skill with problem-solving ability
  • Support from PoC to Production: Not just "build and done" — taking responsibility through to results
  • Feedback into the Product: Feeding frontline insights back into product development

An FDE is not merely an "implementation support engineer" but someone who builds what the frontline needs on the spot and commits directly to the customer's business outcomes. For details on the FDE role, career path, and required skills, see What Is an FDE (Forward Deployed Engineer)? Roles, Careers, and How to Go Independent as a Freelancer — Explained by a Pro.

The reason collaboration between PdM and FDE is essential in AI product development is that "AI involves high uncertainty — you can't know until you build it — and the key to success lies in the back-and-forth between strategy (PdM) and rapid implementation and validation (FDE)."

The unique challenges of AI product development and the roles of each are as follows.

Challenge of AI developmentPdM's contributionFDE's contribution
Accuracy can't be predicted in advanceIdentifying valuable problemsRapid validation with real data
Prone to stalling at the PoC stageDesigning criteria for production decisionsCarrying through to production implementation
Frontline data is messyDefining the problem to solveData integration and preparation
Hard to embed into business operationsDesigning business valueImplementation and adoption in the field

As a professional view: AI products succeed with neither "strategy alone" nor "implementation alone." PdM defines "what should be solved," and FDE rapidly turns "how to make it work in the field" into reality. Only when this back-and-forth meshes well can you break out of the PoC stage and achieve production results.

The ideal division of roles is: PdM focuses on "Why/What," while FDE shapes the "How" based on the customer's real data.

Let's organize the division of roles between the two.

PerspectivePdMFDE
Area of responsibilityWhy / WhatHow (implementation and validation)
ViewpointMarket, business, strategyFrontline, data, implementation
Main deliverablesStrategy and roadmapWorking product and validation results
Distance from customersOverseeing the entire marketDeeply embedded in individual frontline sites

As the frontline reality: PdM looks at the whole market and decides "what to build," while FDE embeds into a specific customer's site and builds "something that actually works." This division of labor lets PdM focus on strategy and FDE focus on implementation. By leveraging each other's strengths, the speed and quality of product development improve dramatically.
