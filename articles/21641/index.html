However, surprisingly few can accurately explain what the role entails. A solution architect is a technical specialist who analyzes the challenges faced by customers and businesses, and designs and proposes the optimal IT technologies (system configurations, cloud, architecture) to solve them. They are engineers who decide "what to build," serving as a "bridge" with deep understanding of both business and technology. What sets them apart is that they are not merely designers—they are also accountable for the return on investment (ROI) of technology spending.

The essence of a solution architect lies in the ability to translate between "the language of business" and "the language of technology." Their job is to take abstract requests from management, such as "we want to increase revenue" or "we want to cut costs," and translate them into concrete technical designs: "which cloud services to combine, and in what configuration, to make it happen."

The "solutions" that solution architects handle do not refer to a single product or system. As shown below, they integrally combine the elements needed to solve a problem and derive a single "solution."

  • Business requirements: Management challenges to solve, KPIs, budget, deadlines
  • Technical elements: Cloud services, middleware, networks, databases
  • Non-functional requirements: Security, availability, scalability, maintainability
  • Constraints: Integration with existing systems, legal regulations, internal human resources

Weighing these factors and designing with the optimal balance—this responsibility for "global optimization" is the decisive difference from specialist engineers who go deep in a specific area.

It is easiest to understand solution architects as positioned "in between and above" SEs and IT consultants. While SEs play the role of "accurately building to specified requirements" and IT consultants play the role of "organizing management challenges," SAs play the role of "drawing the blueprint for how to solve problems technically and taking responsibility for technology selection."

The differences among the three roles can be summarized as follows.

A common misconception in the field is that "SAs are senior people who don't get their hands dirty." In reality, it's the opposite: the top SAs personally verify the latest technologies and get hands-on with PoCs (described later) to ensure technical validity. This "technical backing" is precisely what differentiates them from consultants who speak only in armchair theories. For a comparison with roles that have a strong technical sales aspect, also see A Complete Guide to Sales Engineers (Technical Sales): Job Description, Salary, and Required Skills.

There are multiple job titles containing "architect," each with a different scope. In conclusion, SAs design at the level of "individual projects and solutions," EAs at the level of "the entire enterprise structure," and cloud architects at the level of "the cloud infrastructure."

Enterprise Architect (EA)Company-wide IT strategy and system portfolioCompany-wide optimization, standardization, governanceAn urban planner designing an entire city

These are not mutually exclusive but form a continuous career path. The classic career route is to master the fundamentals as a Cloud Architect, then step up to SA, and from there, as an SA with a proven track record, advance to an EA who oversees the entire company.

The reason demand for Solution Architects is surging lies in a market structure where "technology options have exploded, and there is an overwhelming shortage of professionals who can identify the optimal solution."

The background behind this surge in demand can be summed up in the following three points.

  • An explosion of options driven by cloud adoption: AWS, Azure, and GCP alone offer hundreds of services, with virtually infinite combinations. Professionals with the discernment to choose the optimal solution from among them have become rare.
  • The full-scale arrival of DX (Digital Transformation): Many companies face the need to modernize legacy systems and strongly seek professionals who can bridge business and technology. Now that we have passed the "2025 Cliff" that METI warned about, modernization is an urgent management issue that cannot wait.
  • The insourcing trend: More companies are shifting from fully outsourcing to vendors toward in-house development, and core talent capable of leading technology selection is in demand both inside and outside organizations.

In short, the SA role is not merely a trend; it is a "structurally understaffed position" born from a structural change in how IT investments are made. This scarcity is precisely what underpins the high salaries and high rates discussed later.

The work of a Solution Architect proceeds through four phases: "Requirements gathering (requirements definition) → Design → PoC validation → Development governance." They lead technical decision-making end to end and bear responsibility for guiding the project to success. This section explains the practical work of each phase, along with real-world insights.

The first phase is visualizing the "real problem" through interviews with customers and business departments. In conclusion, the precision of problem definition at this stage determines 80% of the project's overall success or failure.

What a first-rate SA does in this phase is not take the customer's stated "requests" at face value, but dig into the "true problem (the gap between As-Is and To-Be)" behind them.

  • Customer's statement: "We want to introduce AI."
  • SA's question: "Why? What tasks do you want to improve, and by how much?"
  • The essence uncovered: "The real goal is reducing the workload of handling inquiries, and business processes themselves should be reviewed before turning to AI."

A common real-world failure is designing a high-spec system exactly as the customer asked, only for it to become a "white elephant" that no one uses because the field cannot handle it. To prevent this, SAs always invest time in "understanding the business" and "building consensus on the problem" before making technical proposals.

Once requirements are solidified, the technology stack and architecture to realize them are selected and designed. In conclusion, the SA's moment to shine here is coldly choosing "the technology best suited to the problem and constraints," not "trendy technology."

The main considerations during design are as follows.

Design considerationExamples of what to examine
ComputingServerless (Lambda, etc.), containers (ECS/EKS), or virtual machines
Data storeRDB (relational), NoSQL, or data lake
Integration methodAPI integration, event-driven (messaging), or batch processing
CostInitial cost vs. running cost, estimation of usage-based billing
Operational burdenDegree of reduction in operational workload through managed services

As a real-world criterion for technology selection, professional SAs have the courage to avoid "the newest and most feature-rich." For example, being able to deliberately choose a proven, mature architecture rather than an advanced microservices setup that the operations team's skills cannot keep up with is key to long-term project success. Those who want to deepen their cloud platform expertise may also find How to Go Freelance as a Cloud Engineer! Rate Benchmarks and How to Land High-Paying Projects helpful.

The PoC (Proof of Concept) phase is where you verify on a small scale whether the designed architecture "actually works." In conclusion, the SA gets hands-on here and eliminates technical risks before production investment.

Especially when adopting new technologies or unconventional architectures, there are always risks that cannot be seen through desk-based design alone. The main items to validate in a PoC are as follows.

  • Performance: Can it withstand the expected load? Does response time meet requirements?
  • Integration feasibility: Can it connect without issues to existing systems and external APIs?
  • Cost validity: Does the actual billing when running fall within the estimated range?
  • Operational picture: Is detection and recovery realistically possible when a failure occurs?

Being able to use the PoC results to make a "Go/No-Go (investment decision)" recommendation to management is a major part of an SA's value. Transforming "we won't know until we try" into "we can decide based on data" is the true hallmark of an SA with solid technical grounding.

The final phase is handing the design over to the development team and taking charge of technical governance across the entire implementation phase. In conclusion, the SA's responsibility does not end with the design; they must see through to the end that "the design philosophy is correctly implemented."

Specific responsibilities include the following.

  • Sharing design intent with the development team (explaining why this architecture was chosen)
  • Making decisions on technical specification changes and trade-offs that arise during implementation
  • Ensuring quality through code reviews and architecture reviews
  • Acting as the technical coordinator among stakeholders (customer, PM, development)

A common real-world problem is the "gap between design and implementation." When design documents become a mere formality and the development team changes the architecture on their own judgment, the original non-functional requirements (security and scalability) collapse. As the "technical watchdog" preventing this, the SA continues to play a vital role until the very end of the project.

What is required of a first-rate Solution Architect is the multiplication of four core skills: "technical ability," "business acumen," "non-functional requirements design ability," and "soft skills." Excelling in just one is not enough; balance and overall capability determine market value. This section breaks down each skill in detail.

The first core skill is "broad and practical technical ability" that is not skewed toward any particular domain. In conclusion, SAs are expected to have a "T-shaped," ideally a "π-shaped," skill set.

  • Horizontal bar (breadth): Knowledge that provides an overview of everything from cloud computing in general to infrastructure, networking, security, applications, and databases
  • Vertical bar (depth): Within that, a specialized area where you can say "no one can beat me in this" (e.g., AWS network design, data platform construction, etc.)

Cloud knowledge in particular is essential. Understanding the characteristics of the major AWS, Azure, and GCP services, and having a rich repertoire that lets you instantly say "for this requirement, use this service," becomes your weapon. The important thing is that while you don't need to be able to implement everything yourself, you must understand "the key points of implementation." If you design based solely on theoretical knowledge, it will fall apart in the development field with "this can't be built."

The second core skill is business acumen that converts technology into "business outcomes." In conclusion, "domain knowledge"—a deep understanding of the customer's industry and operations—and the ability to make proposals framed in terms of return on investment (ROI) elevate an SA from a mere technician to "a contributor to the business."

The specific components of business acumen are as follows.

Skill elementDescription
Domain knowledgeUnderstanding the business processes and practices of the customer's industry, such as finance, manufacturing, or retail
Problem discoveryThe ability to draw out latent issues that even the customer hasn't noticed
ROI estimationThe ability to quantitatively demonstrate cost reductions and revenue contributions relative to the investment
Proposal skillsThe ability to articulate technical benefits in language that resonates with management

A professional's view: When two SAs have equal technical skills, what ultimately makes the difference is "how much they understand the customer's business as their own." Technology is merely a means; the goal is the customer's business success—those who can embody this principle become the SAs who are chosen by name.

The third core skill is the ability to properly design hard-to-see "non-functional requirements." In conclusion, the true value of an SA lies not in an "ideal design that satisfies everything," but in the ability to judge the trade-offs of "what to keep and what to discard" within limited budgets and timeframes.

Non-functional requirements contain mutually conflicting elements.

  • Availability vs. cost: Demanding 99.99% high availability drives up costs with redundant configurations
  • Security vs. convenience: The more robust the system, the more user experience suffers
  • Scalability vs. simplicity: The more you prepare for future expansion, the more complex the initial configuration becomes

A common on-the-ground failure is designing for excessive quality with an "everything included" approach. This wastes money on availability that is never used, or excessive security brings operations to a standstill. First-class SAs discern the customer's business phase (startup or large enterprise) and have the judgment to determine "the level truly needed right now."

The fourth core skill is the soft skills to bring together stakeholders with different positions. In conclusion, an SA must serve as an interpreter and negotiator who engages "executives," "business divisions," and "development engineers"—people with different languages and concerns—toward a single goal.

The required communication changes significantly depending on the audience.

AudienceWhat to conveyLanguage to use
ExecutivesROI, business impactMoney and management language
Business divisionsHow operations will improveBusiness and field-level language
Development engineersWhy this design, technical rationaleTechnical language

Even a technically correct design won't be realized if you can't persuade stakeholders. In particular, the ability to "find common ground" in situations of conflicting interests—such as negotiations with customers over cost and deadlines, or coordination with development teams over technical debt—is what moves projects forward. This soft-skill barrier is the biggest reason why engineers with only technical skills cannot become SAs.

With the arrival of the era of generative AI and multi-cloud, the mission of the solution architect is expanding from "traditional system design" to "designing next-generation architectures that integrate AI, data, and multiple clouds." SAs who can adapt to this change hold overwhelming scarcity value in the market. This section explains the new roles based on the latest trends.

The first new mission is designing AI systems that leverage LLMs (large language models). In conclusion, demand is exploding for SAs who can translate corporate requests to "use generative AI" into safe and practical architectures.

Particularly noteworthy is the design of RAG (Retrieval-Augmented Generation). This is a mechanism that lets LLMs reference internal documents and databases, generating company-specific answers while suppressing hallucinations (generation of false information), and its design requires specialized judgments such as the following.

  • Model selection: Commercial APIs (Claude, GPT, etc.) or self-hosting open models
  • Data design: Selecting vector databases, optimizing chunking (document split granularity)
  • Security design: Boundary design to prevent sending sensitive data to external APIs
  • Evaluation design: How to measure the accuracy of generated results and continuously improve it

A professional's view: The reality is that very many companies' generative AI initiatives end up "stuck at PoC." The demo works, but it fails to meet the security, cost, and accuracy requirements for production operation. The SA who can design production architectures that overcome this "PoC wall" is exactly the talent the market is seeking most right now. For trends in AI-related specialist roles, also see A Complete Guide: What Roles Are Required of Palantir FDEs and FDRs?

The second new mission is designing "multi-cloud and hybrid cloud" environments that combine multiple clouds and existing environments. In conclusion, designing configurations that avoid vendor lock-in (dependence) while leveraging the strengths of each cloud is one of the most difficult challenges for the modern SA.

The background behind the demand for multi-cloud and its difficult points are as follows.

Purpose of multi-cloudDesign challenges
Avoiding vendor lock-inService specifications differ between clouds, making unification difficult
Leveraging each cloud's strengthsOptimizing network integration and data transfer costs
Strengthening availability and BCP (business continuity)Increased complexity of operations and monitoring, dispersed personnel skills
Cost optimizationUnderstanding the differences in billing systems across clouds

A common failure pattern is casually adopting multiple clouds on the assumption that "multi-cloud means safety," only to end up with more complex operations and increased costs and workload. Top-tier SAs start with the question "Is multi-cloud really necessary?" and identify a configuration that is neither excessive nor insufficient for the purpose. What is tested here is the judgment to calmly weigh added complexity against whether it delivers commensurate value.

The third new mission is balancing "governance" and "cost optimization." In conclusion, the SAs who will be highly valued in the next generation are those with a "FinOps" perspective who continuously optimize cloud costs while applying proper governance over AI and data.

The more cloud and AI usage spreads, the more companies face the following new challenges.

  • Data governance: Controls over who can access which data and what AI may be trained on
  • AI governance: Rules for using generative AI, accountability for outputs, and compliance assurance
  • FinOps: Mechanisms to visualize and continuously optimize cloud and AI usage fees, which tend to be open-ended

FinOps in particular is an area whose importance has been growing rapidly in recent years. SAs who can build in the perspective of "optimizing costs through ongoing use" rather than "build it and be done" from the design stage contribute directly to a company's cost structure, and are therefore highly rated by management.

In conclusion, the shortest route to becoming an SA is a three-step path: "hone your implementation skills as an engineer in a specific domain → prove broad knowledge with cloud certifications → accumulate upstream proposal and design experience."

Most SAs did not become SAs right away; they transitioned from backgrounds such as the following.

  • Build the foundation (implementation skills): Accumulate 3–5 years of hands-on implementation experience as an infrastructure, backend, or cloud engineer
  • Broaden (cloud knowledge): Obtain AWS/Azure associate-to-professional certifications to demonstrate the breadth of your technical knowledge
  • Move upstream (proposals and design): Get involved in requirements definition, technical proposals, and PoCs to gain client-facing experience
  • Cross boundaries (business): Learn ROI and industry knowledge to gain a business perspective

As a professional observation, the biggest differentiator is the "upstream experience" in step 3. Many engineers can implement but lack client-facing experience, and whether you can deliberately seek this out is the fork in the road to becoming an SA. If upstream opportunities are scarce within your company, getting involved in proposal phases through side work or freelance projects is also an effective strategy. For career development of those from backend backgrounds, also see Backend Engineer Career Paths and Salaries.

In conclusion, if you aim to become an SA, obtaining the top-tier "architect-track certifications" for AWS, Azure, and GCP is extremely effective as objective proof of your skills. Especially when changing jobs or winning freelance projects, certifications function as a "passport of trust."

The solution architect is one of the professions most likely to command high rates when going independent as a freelancer. Projects paying over 1.2–2 million yen per month are realistic, and the scarcity of professionals capable of upstream technical decisions underpins those rates. This section explains the reality of going independent and concrete strategies for winning high rates.

In conclusion, many companies seek "upstream technical judgment that they cannot secure with full-time staff" in the form of external freelance SAs or technical advisors, and demand far exceeds supply.

Why is demand for external SAs so high? The reasons are as follows.

  • Difficulty of hiring: Top-tier SAs are rare, and hiring them as full-time employees is extremely difficult
  • Highly spot-based nature: Technology selection and design are concentrated in the early stages of a project, so leveraging external resources is more rational than full-time employment
  • Expectations of an objective perspective: Third-party technical judgment that is free from internal politics is sought after
  • Support for in-house DX development: External experts are valued as "companions" for companies advancing in-house development

In other words, for companies, freelance SAs are a rational option that allows them to "leverage rare talent that they could not hire, only when needed." This structure supports the high rates that SAs can command when going independent. The overall picture of working as a freelancer is explained in detail in Benefits and Cautions of Going Independent as an IT Freelancer.

In conclusion, the key to winning high-rate projects is to quantitatively demonstrate not only "technical skills" but also "contribution to business outcomes" in your skill sheet. While the average monthly rate for freelance engineers overall is said to be around 700,000 to 760,000 yen, the reason SAs can aim for 1.2 million yen or more lies in this "way of presenting results."

Skill sheets and portfolios that are highly evaluated include the following elements.

As a professional perspective, what client companies really want to know is not "what you can do" but "how you will solve their problems." Whether you can describe past projects using the three-part set of "challenge, approach, and results" is the deciding factor in breaking through the rate ceiling.

In conclusion, the classic strategy that balances rate and stability is to secure stable projects through freelance agents in the early stages of independence, and then transition to long-term quasi-delegation contracts with direct (prime) clients once you have built a track record.

In this article, we comprehensively covered the role, job duties, essential skills, and salary ranges of solution architects (SAs), as well as going independent as a freelancer. Finally, let's review the key points.

  • What an SA is: A "bridge" that connects business challenges and IT technology, designing and proposing optimal system configurations
  • Workflow: Leading technical decisions across four phases: interviews → design → PoC validation → development control
  • Four Core Skills: The multiplication of technical skills, business acumen, non-functional requirements design, and soft skills
  • Latest Trends: Responding to generative AI (LLM/RAG), multi-cloud, and FinOps is a new mission
  • Salary Range: Generally 8 to 15 million yen, with over 20 million yen possible at foreign mega-cloud companies
  • Path to Independence: Thanks to their rarity, high-rate projects exceeding 1.2 to 2 million yen per month are realistic

Solution architects are a highly valuable profession whose demand will never dry up as long as technology continues to evolve. And that rare skill set is maximized when going independent as a freelancer, enabling a high-rate, highly flexible way of working. By combining technical skills with a business perspective and aiming to become one of a kind, great possibilities await in both career and income.
