The Word Is Growing Faster Than the Job

FDE Hub · 2026-09-08 · 7 min read

Summary

The author traces the provenance of widely cited FDE statistics and finds they measure different things. Christian & Timbers' figure of roughly 2,000 US FDEs who deliver enterprise value uses a Fortune 500 yardstick, while its 17,000 total and job-posting data essentially count the word, not the work. Postings are up over 1,000% year-on-year but on a small base; salary figures are ceilings from postings, not actual pay distributions. Many titled roles are pre-sales or implementation work. The author announces an anonymous FDE Hub practitioner survey, launching next week, designed with careful segmentation to answer questions no existing dataset can.

Key points

  • Only about 2,000 US FDEs meet a strict enterprise-value bar, per Christian & Timbers.
  • Job postings rose over 1,000% year-on-year, but Indeed's raw count is only thousands.
  • Median advertised salary exceeds $188,000; published figures are mostly ceilings.
  • Many roles using the title are pre-sales, implementation, or support work.
  • FDE Hub launches an anonymous, segmented practitioner survey next week.

A few weeks ago, on a call with a group of FDEs, I heard myself say that a proper analysis had found around 2,000 strict-definition forward deployed engineers in the United States. Someone asked where the figure came from. I couldn’t tell them.

It took me an evening to untangle. The 2,000 was the headline from a Christian & Timbers study that TechCrunch ran at the end of July. It had lodged in my head next to a different number, the 1,982 practitioner profiles I analysed for the first State of FDE report in January. Two figures, the same size, sitting side by side, and they measure entirely different things. One counts people a search firm believes can deliver enterprise outcomes. The other counts people who put the title on LinkedIn.

If I can conflate them, and I produced one of them, the market has no chance. And the market is now producing FDE numbers faster than anyone can check what they mean.

What is actually being counted

Start with the postings. Lightcast data published by Fortune last week has forward deployed job postings up more than 1,000% between January and August compared with the same period last year, and more than 4,600% against 2023. The rest of the tech job market grew 13% over the same period. The New York Times had the same shape a few days earlier: listings up twelvefold in a year on LinkedIn, up 730% on Indeed, with the detail that most coverage skipped, which is that Indeed’s own economist put the raw count of such listings in the thousands. A steep curve on a small base. In India, IIT Gandhinagar announced on 31 August that the second cohort of its residential AI diploma, starting this month, has had its curriculum re-curated around forward deployed roles, because industry partners told them that is what the market is hiring for.

A posting is a decision to use two words. It is not evidence that the job behind them involves discovery, or production code, or a customer’s operators, or anything else the original role was built around. I wrote earlier this summer about what thirty recruiter messages reveal when you read them as a dataset, and the short version is that a meaningful share of the roles wearing the title are pre-sales, implementation or support work with a new label. The curve is real. What it measures is how quickly the market has learned to say the words.

Then the salaries. The same Lightcast data puts the median advertised salary for the role above $188,000, against roughly $145,000 for a conventional software engineer. Frontier lab postings run to $400,000 at the top. Now take a single, well-documented job: Palantir’s forward deployed software engineer, the one the whole market is copying. Palantir’s own postings put the base salary at $135,000 to $200,000. Depending on which salary page you land on, the same job is quoted at a $211,000 to $280,000 median, a $238,000 average with a ceiling near $486,000, or “$750,000-plus” at the top of the ladder. One AI vendor has published a “compensation report” claiming that principal-level FDEs at frontier labs clear $1.2 million. These are all the same kind of number: a ceiling, or an estimate of a ceiling, for a posting in San Francisco or New York. Nobody has published what a forward deployed engineer at a Series B in Berlin is actually paid, or what the solo FDE at a 200-person logistics software company takes home, or how much of it is equity. The truth of FDE compensation is a distribution, and all we have are its most photogenic points.

Then the study itself. Christian & Timbers interviewed more than 250 hiring executives across 180 companies, surveyed 80 Fortune 500 executives and spoke to over 300 FDEs and applied AI engineers in the first half of the year. Their finding is that roughly 17,000 people in the US carry the title, about 2,000 have consistently delivered enterprise value, and demand is set to rise 2,100% by year end. At the start of the year, 5 to 10% of companies planned to hire FDEs. By the end of June it was 70%. Their bar for “delivered value”, by the founder’s own description, is impact in the tens of millions of dollars, which tells you the 2,000 were counted against a Fortune 500 yardstick and almost nobody deploying into the mid-market would qualify, however good they are.

That is real research, and the scarcity headline is also a very good advertisement for a firm that places exactly the people it counted. I don’t think that makes the number wrong. What it does mean is that the 300 practitioners were asked a buyer’s question: are you good enough to be sold? Nobody asked them what the job is. And the demand side of the same study is built on the same posting data from Indeed, Lightcast and LinkedIn as everything else, so even the most serious piece of work on this market is, underneath, counting the word.

What the first edition could and couldn’t say

I should be honest about my own contribution to the pile. The first State of FDE report was built on public profiles. Nearly 2,000 people across 776 companies, and of the companies outside the top employers, 625 had exactly one FDE. Those solo hires averaged 13.6 years of experience.

That told me who gets hired, where, and with what background. It was collection and analysis of what people chose to publish about themselves, and it was clear about that. It could not tell me what any of those people do on a Tuesday, what they earn, who they report to, or whether the title on their profile describes their week. The methodology had a ceiling, and I hit it.

It has since got worse. Nick Bloom’s team at Stanford tracks how people edit their own job histories on LinkedIn, and the New York Times reported a sharp rise this year in software engineers, data engineers and solutions architects retitling past roles as forward deployed engineer. The numbers are still in the hundreds, but the direction matters: the dataset I counted in January is being rewritten to match the market that is counting it. Bloom’s phrase for what the title does was that you are “upselling through a title”, and people are now applying the upsell to their own past.

The questions that decide what the title means

Here is what I want to know, and what no dataset in circulation can answer.

How is the week actually split between discovery and building? A role that is 80% context-gathering and 20% code is a different job from one that is 20% context-gathering and 80% code, and both are advertised under the same title at the same salary. The answer decides whether “engineer” is the right word at all.

Who does the FDE report to? Sales, product, services or the CEO directly. I have argued before that the pricing model decides what an FDE team is for. The reporting line is the operational version of the same question, and it is invisible in a posting.

Who owns the system after handover? Whether maintenance goes to the customer, to a support function, or quietly stays with the FDE tells you whether the role is a discovery investment or a services business with better branding.

The first edition found that most companies employing an FDE employ exactly one. If that holds, a large share of the people carrying the title are not on a pod of six with a 45-day clock. They are alone, senior, and improvising a function the company is still defining.

And what does the compensation distribution look like once you ask the person receiving it rather than the posting offering it, by geography, by company stage, by whether the number includes equity.

Every one of those questions has to be asked of practitioners, anonymously, by someone with no reason to shade the answers.

Why I think FDE Hub should be the one asking

FDE Hub has no product to sell into this market, no placement fee riding on scarcity, and no headcount plan that a particular answer would justify. That is the whole point of running it independently, and it is the one property a captive deployment organisation cannot replicate.

The survey will be anonymous by design, and I have spent several weeks on the methodology before opening it, mostly on segmentation. The single biggest way to ruin this data is to average a Palantir-style embed, a solutions engineer with a new title and an independent practitioner into one “FDE” and report the mean. Those decisions are made before the field opens, not after the responses come in.

It goes live next week. I’ll share the details then. Subscribers get the link first, and anyone who responds gets the results before they are published.

The next time someone on a call asks me where a number comes from, I’d like the answer to be: from the people doing the job.
