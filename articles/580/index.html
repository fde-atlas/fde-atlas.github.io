To be honest, the first time I noticed the term FDE wasn't because I read some industry report.

It was in a Builder group chat, where several founders of AI startups were discussing it: "Have you heard of FDE? Silicon Valley is hiring for it like crazy lately." Someone posted two or three job listings, someone shared a few English articles, someone said, "We now expect our engineers to develop toward the FDE role—a product manager plus engineer hybrid," and someone else sighed, "Isn't that just a fancier version of on-site development?" Everyone chimed in, but few could actually explain what an FDE is or why it suddenly became so hot.

Driven by that curiosity, I dug through a pile of material, and the deeper I went, the more interesting it became. And right in the middle of my research, in early May 2026, something intriguing happened in the AI industry—

OpenAI and Anthropic—two giants locked in fierce competition in AI—announced the creation of enterprise services companies within the same week. OpenAI formed a new company called The Deployment Company, raised over $4 billion, and acquired the UK AI consultancy Tomoro, bringing 150 AI experts on board and dispatching them to work on-site at client companies. On the very same day, Anthropic partnered with Wall Street giants such as Blackstone and Goldman Sachs to establish a joint venture for enterprise AI services. Two bitter rivals, on the same day, taking the same path.

And the role underpinning this path is exactly the one discussed in that Builder group chat—the FDE (Forward Deployed Engineer). It is not some newly invented concept, but an organizational model that Silicon Valley has validated over two decades. It's just that in the second half of AI implementation, it has suddenly become contested ground that no giant can afford to ignore.

In the plainest terms, an FDE is an engineer stationed at the customer's site, responsible for bridging the huge gap between "what the product can do" and "what the customer needs."

What makes this role special is that it combines the traits of a software engineer and a consultant: you must not only write code, but also go deep into the customer's site to solve real problems, guide enterprises on how to adopt AI technology, and teach AI models to adapt to specific business environments.

Bob McGrew, former Palantir executive and former Chief Research Officer at OpenAI, offered a more precise definition: an FDE is a technical person stationed at the customer's site who, with the existing product and with help from the product team, figures out how to deliver a valuable outcome—building use cases that create value for the customer and delivering software in a way that truly works.

"Forward Deployed" is a military term, originally referring to special units deployed to the front lines to carry out missions. FDE borrows this term to describe a type of technical talent that can both fight tough battles and go deep into the front lines.

Viewed within Palantir's framework, the FDE model operates internally through a two-person role pairing known as the "Echo-Delta" model: the Echo team digs into customer pain points and translates them into precise product requirements, while the Delta team rapidly builds, iterates, and deploys practical, usable solutions in the real environment of the customer's systems. Delta team members are the deployment engineers—they are typically highly skilled at writing code quickly, the people who bring ideas into the real world, building solutions and prototypes that ultimately land as products that actually run.

The FDE model was not born from theoretical deduction, but from a solution forced into existence by real-world difficulties.

The story begins in 2003. Palantir had just been founded, aiming to build analysis software for intelligence agencies like the CIA and NSA. The problem was that, given the nature of intelligence work, you simply couldn't ask spies directly, "How do you do your job?"—no one would tell you. Even if you found such a customer, they could never detail the specifics of their work.

Stephen Cohen, one of Palantir's founders, built a demo first and showed it to potential customers, asking what they thought. The customers' response was blunt: "This product is terrible—it has nothing to do with what we do." Cohen didn't give up; instead, he pressed further: "Then what would you want it to do differently?" He wrote down all the feedback and went back to revise. This process repeated over and over until the product could genuinely solve the customer's problems.

This difficult start taught Palantir a lesson: the traditional model of gathering requirements through sales and then having engineers build simply doesn't work. Someone had to plant themselves directly at the customer's site, explore alongside them, and iterate while they used it. That was the embryo of the FDE model.

The FDE model quickly proved its value in the field. On the battlefields of Iraq and Afghanistan, what the US military feared most was roadside bombs. Palantir engineers stationed there found that soldiers didn't need fancy intelligence dashboards at all—they just wanted a simple tool to mark "this road is suspicious" on a map. The engineers threw together a rudimentary map tool on the spot, letting soldiers flag risky road segments with a single click so others could immediately see them and avoid the danger zones. This seemingly crude tool immediately changed soldiers' daily routines and reduced casualties, and it was later refined into a standard feature of the Palantir platform.

Today, FDEs make up roughly half of Palantir's total workforce, and the company has grown into a giant with a market cap of nearly $400 billion thanks to this model.

The FDE model suddenly became one of the hottest topics in the global AI industry around 2025. According to data from the job platform Indeed, from January to September 2025, job postings for FDE-related roles grew by more than 800% year over year. As of April 2026, FDE-related job postings had surged from 643 in April 2025 to 5,330. More than 100 AI companies are recruiting FDEs on the YC incubator's job board, whereas three years ago that number was zero.

Why have FDEs suddenly become so important in the AI era?

The answer is actually quite simple: no matter how capable the models are, enterprise customers still don't know how to use them.

Customers across industries commonly face the same challenge: how to effectively use AI technology to achieve a return on investment. Research from MIT points out that up to 95% of enterprise generative AI projects fail to deliver a return on investment, while the 5% that succeed generally undertook deep customization and process integration.

Enterprise customers often have legacy systems, data silos, and security and compliance barriers, and deploying a full system in production is far more complex than the model itself. Customers' focus has long since shifted from "how high the model scores on benchmarks" to "how to use it to make money." Against this backdrop, what the FDE fills is precisely the gap between "what the product can do" and "what the customer needs."

On a consumer electronics production line in Shanghai, changeover and machine adjustment had been a headache for engineers for years: whenever the model changed, engineers would spend anywhere from two or three hours to most of a day tinkering beside the equipment. After the FDE team at Shiyuan Technology took over, they collected abnormal samples beside the production line during the day and returned to the lab at night to iterate on the model repeatedly. After months of this cycle, they ultimately compressed that time to 55 seconds. The number of product models handled per changeover also expanded from 5 per day to nearly 300.

An FDE doesn't sit at headquarters writing code; instead, they are stationed directly at customers' factories, banks, and hospitals, embedding the capabilities of large models into real business processes on the front lines.

OpenAI's FDE team once partnered with agricultural machinery giant John Deere, custom-developing an intelligent spraying system based on OpenAI's technology that helped farmers reduce chemical usage by 60% to 70%.

Beside 500-ton fermentation tanks and beneath a power plant's 200-meter-tall air-cooling tower, FDE engineers spent nearly nine months on site, truly "embedding" large AI models into the core of industrial production. Cases like these show that the value of an FDE lies not in how many lines of code they write, but in solving real problems in real business scenarios.

On the surface, FDEs do resemble China's "implementation consultants" or "on-site developers": both are technical personnel stationed at customer sites doing customized development. But the core difference lies in strategic positioning:

Traditional customization is a cost center, with the goal of generating revenue through service delivery; the FDE model, by contrast, is an investment, with the goal of product exploration through deep service.

Palantir founder Peter Thiel once said: "We need to do the unscalable at scale." This sentence captures the essence of the FDE: trading temporary unscalability for future scalability.

The fundamental difference between the two is that FDEs' on-site experience is systematically distilled into platform capabilities, so that each project is no longer "from 0 to 1," and the team's efficiency spins faster and faster like a flywheel.

Some will say: "Isn't this just consulting?" But FDE and consulting are fundamentally different. Palantir's approach is: enter with an existing product, fill the gap between the product's capabilities and actual needs, and first pave the route into a "gravel road." Then the product and engineering teams at headquarters abstract and generalize these on-site practices, turning them into a "highway" that can serve many more customers going forward.

Consulting is one-off project delivery, whereas FDE is scalable product exploration. An FDE conducts product exploration from inside the enterprise, rather than talking to customers from the outside.

To become a qualified FDE, you need to build a composite skill system of "technology + business + soft skills."

Programming and engineering: Proficiency in languages such as Python, along with solid software engineering practices, version control, and code quality awareness. Familiarity with modern AI development assistants such as Claude Code, Cursor, and GitHub Copilot to accelerate development iteration.

AI and ML: Familiarity with large language model application development (such as LangChain, LlamaIndex, RAG architectures, and Prompt Engineering) or computer vision frameworks (such as PyTorch and OpenCV), and the ability to fine-tune models for specific scenarios.

System deployment: Familiarity with containerization technologies (Docker, Kubernetes) and Linux development and operations environments.

Industry technologies and standards: Able to understand PLC control protocols in factory settings and to deploy lightweight edge inference models.

An FDE must be a true "bilingual speaker" between technology and business: able to understand model architectures as well as industry jargon; able to write code, and also to understand the jargon-filled requirements voiced in customer meetings, then translate between the two languages.

This means an FDE needs to go deep in at least 1-2 industries, becoming familiar with their business processes, common pain points, and decision-making logic.

Problem-solving ability, communication skills, and rapid learning ability are essential qualities for an FDE. In addition, since an FDE is the company's technical representative on the customer side and often needs to work independently at customer sites, self-motivation and resilience under pressure are also crucial.

Judging from actual job postings, companies' requirements for FDEs fall into two tiers:

Foundational tier: 1+ years of experience in system deployment, implementation, operations, or technical support; familiarity with basic Windows/Linux server operations; basic networking knowledge and troubleshooting skills; familiarity with basic SQL queries and data validation.

Advanced tier (AI track): Python programming skills; experience developing generative AI solutions based on foundation models; the ability to combine technical and business insight.

FDE compensation is highly competitive in the industry, even exceeding many traditional software engineer roles:

Internationally, total compensation (TC) for a Forward Deployed Engineer in the US is roughly $170,000-$340,000 per year, with a median of about $210,000. Industry insiders have even reported that Palantir FDEs can earn over $400,000 a year.

Domestically, annual salaries for FDE roles at leading companies generally range from 300,000 to 800,000 RMB.

Palantir's internal data also shows that the average total annual pay for FDEs is about $238,000.

The FDE career path is quite clear. Starting as a junior FDE, after gradually accumulating on-site customer experience and product exploration skills, one can be promoted to Senior FDE and Lead FDE. In the longer term, FDEs can advance toward roles such as AI Program Director or industry solutions expert.

Moreover, because FDEs accumulate frontline, customer-facing experience and industry insight, this path often offers faster career growth and greater irreplaceability than purely backend R&D roles.

According to industry data, FDE job postings grew by more than 800% year over year from January to September 2025. From April 2025 to April 2026, FDE-related job postings surged from 643 to 5,330. Leading companies such as OpenAI plan to expand their FDE teams to 50 people, while Anthropic is growing its Applied AI team (including FDEs) fivefold.

In terms of industry distribution, finance, manufacturing, healthcare, retail, and other sectors are all accelerating their AI deployments, generating massive demand for deployment work. Notably, New York has surpassed San Francisco as the largest hub for FDE hiring.

The FDE model is sweeping through Silicon Valley, but whether it can be successfully replicated in China remains a topic worth discussing. The core challenge lies in the fundamentally different ecosystems of the ToB markets in China and the US.

In terms of willingness to pay, Palantir's early customers were intelligence agencies like the CIA and NSA, solving core problems at the level of national security, with individual contracts often worth tens of millions of dollars—customers focused on value rather than cost. In China, by contrast, customers often demand "buy one, get three free," and per-person-day rates have not risen in a decade and have even declined. In this environment, the economic model of deep customization is hard to sustain.

In terms of product abstraction, Palantir uses underlying abstractions like Ontology to distill solutions from different domains into general-purpose capabilities, so every customized deployment strengthens its core product. In China, however, customization at most companies often degenerates into "patch-style" development. Lacking a powerful abstraction platform, every project starts from scratch, making it impossible to accumulate genuine product advantages.

Despite the challenges, China is actively promoting the cultivation of FDE talent. In March 2026, Shanghai launched a systematic FDE talent development initiative, accelerating progress across the board—from computing platforms to training systems, from university partnerships to policy support.

This shows that although implementing the FDE model in China requires overcoming more practical difficulties, its value has already been recognized by both industry and policy makers.

For Chinese ToB companies hoping to learn from the FDE model, a more realistic strategy is: focus on lighthouse customers and strategic customers, provide deep services selectively, and distill the solutions accumulated in each customization project into a reusable capability platform through low-code platforms, industry component libraries, and similar means. The approach of a domestic retail tech company is worth learning from: after every customization project is completed, at least two reusable business components must be abstracted from it.

An FDE takes on the dual role of engineer and consultant, needing to both write good code and communicate well with customers. These high demands mean the entry barrier for FDEs is significantly higher than for traditional engineers.

Take Palantir as an example: engineers spend about 25% of their time working at customer sites; at the healthcare AI company Commure, the on-site ratio for FDEs is as high as 50%. One industry observer vividly described FDEs as "earning a $400,000 salary while doing the least glamorous travel grunt work in all of Silicon Valley." Behind the high rewards lies an intense challenge to one's work and lifestyle.

The success of the FDE model depends heavily on the abstraction and platform capabilities of the product teams behind the scenes. If the experience FDEs gain in the field cannot be effectively distilled into reusable product features, the model degenerates into "premium outsourcing"—every project starts from zero, and efficiency can never take off.

From Palantir's secret weapon to the strategic moves of OpenAI and Anthropic, the FDE is becoming the key bridge connecting the lab and the market in the AI era. If we divide AI industrialization into two phases—the first half being model R&D, and the second half being scenario implementation and servitization—then the FDE is the core force of the second half.

What the FDE role represents is not just a new job title, but an entirely new way of working and a methodology for technology commercialization. It requires technical talent to step out of the comfort zone of coding and into the front lines of the customer's business, using technology to solve real problems and create real value. As the directive given to Palantir's Forward Deployed Engineers puts it—"Go There and Win."

For technical professionals, the FDE opens up a career path different from traditional R&D. It doesn't require you to be a top algorithm expert, but it does require broad technical vision, deep industry insight, and excellent communication skills. For companies, the FDE offers a possible path to finding balance between product standardization and customer personalization.

The story of the FDE is still unfolding. Its future depends on whether technical talent, companies, and customers can jointly drive a virtuous flywheel: the front line explores value, the back end distills capabilities, and ultimately AI truly enters every industry.
