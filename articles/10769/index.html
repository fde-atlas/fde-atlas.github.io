Alibaba's Latest AI-Native Organization Practices: Managers Dive in First, Special Zones Lead the Charge, and Workflows Get Rebuilt

Original title: 阿里最新AI原生组织实践：管理者先下水、特区打头阵、重构工作流

53AI · 2026-09-29 · 17 min read

Summary

The author reports from the AI Native Organization forum at the Yunqi Conference, where Alibaba's CPO Jiang Fang and CIO Jiang Linquan jointly presented for the first time. Core argument: AI can make coding ten times faster, but organizations haven't sped up accordingly. Jiang Fang proposes three moves—leaders go first, pilot in special zones, rebuild workflows. Jiang Linquan describes consolidating R&D roles into two Half-stack positions (PDFE and ABE) and opening business systems to agents, with agent traffic growing 1000x in four months. Bao Chenxing covers Alibaba Cloud's 600+ digital employees and manager avatars; Yuan Yimin stresses capturing frontline sales context into organizational data. Panelists agree AI should be measured by real business value, not token consumption.

Key points

  • Jiang Fang's three steps: leaders go first, special-zone pilots, rebuild workflows.
  • Jiang Linquan merged R&D roles into two Half-stack positions: PDFE and ABE.
  • Agent calls on Alibaba's business systems grew 1000x in four months.
  • Alibaba Cloud runs 600+ digital employees but remains in stage two of three.
  • Panel consensus: measure AI by business value; token use is only an indicator.

Full text

"Code can be 10x faster, but organizations don't necessarily speed up in sync. Alibaba pushed the question to managers, workflows, and organizational relationships."

On the afternoon of September 22, before the Mid-Autumn Festival, I attended the "AI Native Organization Forum" at the Yunqi Conference in Hangzhou, which was packed with attendees. I had been following the topic of AI-native organizations for a while before coming, and this time I especially hoped to share the forum's content with everyone as completely as possible.

The scene at the 2026 Yunqi Conference "AI Native Organization Forum"

This is already the fourth consecutive year that Alibaba's Yunqi Conference has held an organization-themed forum.

At this time last year, "AI Native" was rarely mentioned, but this year it has become one of the hottest topics in the industry, and the forum made it its theme outright.

What's even more noteworthy is that, as Jiang Fang, Chief People Officer (CPO) of Alibaba Group, pointed out in her opening remarks, this year's forum was jointly organized by the CPO and CIO lines for the first time. This aligns with a trend we've observed: some forward-looking companies have begun merging HR with information or intelligence centers. In the AI era, this may be the biggest difference in how organizations evolve compared to the past.

"Business systems are being rewritten by AI, and organizations will inevitably be rewritten too. The people who manage business systems and the people who manage organizations are sitting together for the first time."

In the past, when companies talked about AI transformation, the subject was often models, platforms, data, and tools. The AI Organization Forum asks a different set of more critical questions: how will job boundaries change, how will decision-making and collaboration be rewritten, what should managers do, how should talent be selected and developed, and once digital employees enter the organization, who provides the Context and who is accountable for the results.

What is the code to evolving an AI-native organization? After hearing the talks, no one on site offered a standard answer. The only certainty is that technology and tools are already moving fast, but organizations haven't sped up in sync. Let's take a look at the core content of the forum:

AI has moved from answering questions to executing tasks and delivering results, beginning to generate real business value in end-to-end processes. Job boundaries, ways of collaborating, and relationships of authority and responsibility are loosening accordingly.

Jiang Fang relayed two observations from AI companies. The head of Claude Code believes that Anthropic's technical leadership fundamentally comes from the leadership of its organizational processes and culture; the founder of Genspark said that the output of 70-plus people can match the output of the 2,000-person team he once worked with at a big tech company.

"When model capabilities converge, the organizational gap is precisely the moat that others cannot copy-paste."

Jiang Fang, Chief People Officer of Alibaba Group, delivering the opening talk

Last year, the forum proposed four things for organizational evolution: rebooting cognition, rebuilding capabilities, restructuring collaboration, and reshaping culture. After a year of practice, Jiang Fang offered a clearer judgment: "Organizational AI-ification is a certain choice." But the difficulty of transformation varies greatly across organizations.

She listed four conditions that make it relatively easier:

The top leader deeply understands AI and can get hands-on; work is already highly digitized; the organization has fewer than a hundred people; the core team has a high level of mutual trust. For large enterprises, these four points also serve as a reverse diagnostic checklist. Scale, legacy systems, offline information, and low trust all raise the cost of transformation.

The first three are easy to understand; the fourth, "high mutual trust," has rarely been placed into an AI transformation framework on its own before. Models and tools can be bought, but Context within an organization doesn't flow automatically. If important judgments remain stuck on personal computers, in meeting rooms, and in the minds of a few people for a long time, agents can't get the full context, and employees can't share processes with each other either. The closer AI gets to core processes, the more trust becomes like infrastructure.

"Individual productivity gains don't necessarily bring a leap in organizational efficiency."

An engineer's coding efficiency can improve 10x, yet end-to-end development cycles haven't shortened noticeably. A collection of super individuals doesn't automatically become a super organization.

Last year, Jiang Linquan, Vice President and Chief Information Officer of Alibaba Cloud Intelligence Group, had already seen the same problem from his CIO product and R&D team. After the team made heavy use of AI Coding tools, the AI-generated code rate kept rising, but the total time for end-to-end delivery of real requirements didn't shorten significantly. Developers actually spend only 20% of their total working time writing code, while the remaining 80% still goes into collaboration on requirements communication, confirmation, testing, release, and operations. The more code AI generates, the more core employees get "DDoSed" by verification work; moreover, mediocre code mass-produced by AI entering the system will very likely become a liability, and the system's burden grows along with it.

Jiang Linquan, Vice President and Chief Information Officer of Alibaba Cloud Intelligence Group, sharing on site

Two people saw the same bottleneck from different positions. The CPO cares about managers, mutual trust, talent, and authority-responsibility; the CIO faces data, permissions, platforms, and processes. No matter how skilled individuals become with tools, if work still flows through old pipelines, organizational efficiency is hard to change.

Returning to how to do it, CPO Jiang Fang then grounded the answer in three things: leaders get in the water first, special zones pilot first, and workflows get restructured.

Jiang Fang told a story about a product lead at Alibaba. Before going to Silicon Valley to learn, this manager felt the project was at a critical stage and he couldn't step away. After his mindset was opened up, upon returning to China he handed over the few things he couldn't bear to let go of to subordinates, picked seven or eight young people, rebuilt the business workflow from scratch, and immersed himself daily in how to help AI work better.

The change then spread to the team. People began proactively redoing their own workflows and began thinking about the business in terms of "delivering AI products."

The most critical move in this story is the manager's shift in self-awareness and thinking. Only when a manager first hands over the work they're most reluctant to let go of do they have time to enter a new, unproven process. If you only ask employees to learn tools while you still hold meetings, approve, and assign tasks the old way, the team will quickly return to its original track.

Jiang Fang herself is making similar attempts. Alibaba is advancing from individual AI efficiency gains to collaboration network efficiency, letting closely collaborating colleagues share interconnected Context through AI. The Qianwen Office team is exploring Context interconnection in the product, and she has started using the new version of Qianwen Office to accumulate work context and share it with her closest colleagues. She said this effort has just begun and she hopes to report concrete examples of overall efficiency gains next year.

Bao Chenxing, HR Vice President of Alibaba Cloud Intelligence Group: After 600-plus digital employees, Alibaba Cloud is still in the second stage

Bao Chenxing, HR Vice President of Alibaba Cloud Intelligence Group, sharing on site

Bao Chenxing divided organizational evolution into three stages.

The first stage is AI tools entering individual work, where employees start using Copilots, workbenches, or AI Assistants, and the human organizational network remains unchanged.

The second stage is humans and agents forming a hybrid work network, where digital employees become formal nodes that can directly serve customers and employees.

Only the third stage is the AI organizational brain: interactions, memory, and Context between humans and agents are deposited in a single hub.

His judgment was measured: Alibaba Cloud is still in the second stage, and the third stage remains a goal. Alibaba Cloud currently has roughly 600-plus digital employees. His business unit had about 2,500 people three years ago, originally run on a traditional functional structure, later reorganized into a three-tier network of business units. Now, digital employees and cross-team project agents are also beginning to enter this network.

The manager's digital avatar is the most vivid case in this part.

The team distilled management experience through corpus distillation and Harness engineering, and every day several hundred employees proactively consult the avatar. Bao Chenxing himself also used Qianwen Office to prepare quarterly OKR feedback for more than 20 people: AI read work materials and generated drafts, he first sent the content to employees for confirmation, then entered it into the system. Multiple colleagues told him, "This is the best feedback I've received since joining Alibaba," because it contained specific work data, no longer just a sentence or two like in the past.

This creates a new division of labor in a manager's work. AI expands information coverage, while the manager calibrates judgment, confirms boundaries, and owns the results. The span of management can expand, but evaluation cannot be handed over to a black box.

What large organizations are most familiar with is doing top-level design first, then decomposing it layer by layer and rolling it out comprehensively.

Jiang Fang believes this path is hard to apply directly to AI transformation. Large enterprises have diverse business forms, and much of the work has not yet been fully brought online, so neither humans nor AI have enough information to make a one-shot global design.

She made the second lesson explicit: "Set up special zones to try things first—don't expect everyone to march in lockstep." She used "renovating an old city district" to describe transformation in large organizations: rather than waiting for every condition to be ready, give pioneering teams an independent space and let the boldest people run first.

The Qoder team was her example. Five people built QoderWork in 7 days—discussing, deciding, and revising plans the same day, with no cumbersome approvals and no need to pull in collaboration from outside the team. Ideas that typically take months to validate in the industry took them one week per round, after which they immediately went back to practice to iterate.

The Qoder case directly illustrates the relationship between decision chains and experimentation speed. Problems, solutions, decisions, and revisions all stayed within one small team, so work didn't have to shuttle back and forth across departmental interfaces. The four conditions Jiang Fang listed earlier—a top leader personally involved, highly data-driven work, teams under 100 people, and mutual trust within the core team—are also more likely to coexist in such units.

Alibaba Cloud also runs larger-scale experiments internally. Bao Chenxing introduced that starting in February 2026, the team held a 100-day AI Maker Competition with company-wide participation—everyone submitted a project. Projects could be work-related or not, letting employees start from interest and get hands-on. This continues the principle he mentioned earlier: not making Token consumption a mandatory KPI for employees. The finals featured everything from a home companion robot dog to an AI video platform built by a Gen-Z management trainee.

Beyond the competition, new capabilities began entering real customer scenarios through two real cases. A short-drama platform had once hired an outsourced team of five for two months, yet the delivered results were still unsatisfactory. A TAM technical service engineer identified the problem, rebuilt the production platform in two weeks, re-orchestrated the workflow of models and video tools, and wrote a large number of Service modules.

Another salesperson was a liberal arts graduate with no coding experience. On a Friday, the customer's CTO asked to see a training platform proposal by the following Monday; two days later, he delivered a working Demo: hosts practiced in a simulated livestream room while AI evaluated, gave feedback, and scored on the spot. The customer then wanted to keep building a product around this Demo.

Kunlun Digital Tech offered another approach for large enterprises at the roundtable forum. Facing the new FDE business, they built a relatively independent "1+N+X" system: "1" is the Industrial ModelScope empowerment platform, "N" is each business unit building professional capabilities such as agents, MCP services, and Skills, and "X" plans to establish joint innovation centers for about ten oil and petrochemical domains. The framework is still under construction, currently piloting in Lanzhou, with plans for full replication once proven.

The "special zone" here isn't simply about having fewer people. Small teams need relatively complete ownership of goals, information, and decision-making so that work doesn't have to be hauled back and forth across departmental interfaces. Qoder-style small teams first compress experimentation and decision-making into a short chain to validate direction quickly; at the diffusion stage, proven methods must then be consolidated into shared platforms, professional capabilities, and business-adjacent scenario nodes. Without that second layer, small teams' experience is hard to reuse across other businesses.

"Special zones" also have boundaries. They can't remain showcase areas forever, and even less can they become an excuse to bypass safety and accountability. What a small team proves out must ultimately be integrated into real business, legacy systems, and formal structures of authority and responsibility.

At this point, the question moves into the third thing Jiang Fang discussed: restructuring workflows.

Jiang Fang compared "grafting AI tools onto old processes" to fitting a steam engine onto an old waterwheel: the power source changed, but the transmission stayed the same. She relayed OpenAI's research on site: frontier enterprises in the top 10% of AI usage consume 8.3 times more Tokens per capita than average enterprises. OpenAI just released a set of data: leading enterprises opened up an 8.3x Token gap within six months, and legal applications grew fastest, at 108 times—the gap doesn't just come from call volume. Frontier enterprises hand complete context, tools, and rules to agents, letting AI enter the workflow itself.

Jiang Linquan, Vice President and CIO of Alibaba Cloud Intelligence Group: product-R&D roles compressed into 2 types with Half-stack, and the CIO team's roles are also changing

At the workflow level, Jiang Linquan dissected the end-to-end product-R&D process in even finer detail.

As mentioned earlier, a developer's time actually spent on code is only 20%; breaking it down further, core and complex code accounts for only 20% of that, while the other 80%—routine code—takes up no more than about 35% of coding time. By this calculation, without changing other collaboration stages in product R&D, the portion of efficiency AI can improve affects only 7% of E2E time—extremely limited.

In early 2025, Jiang Linquan had already seen the limits of this kind of productivity improvement in product-R&D organizations. He returned to the essence of software engineering and rethought organizational questions for the AI era.

The classic software engineering work The Mythical Man-Month discussed a problem: adding people to a delayed software project often doesn't make it faster. Newcomers need to understand context, veterans have to re-split tasks, explain, and mentor, and as team size grows, communication links multiply rapidly.

Jiang Linquan believes AI is changing two key conditions: first, adding several agents to an engineer doesn't add several interpersonal collaboration relationships—while improving efficiency at a single node, it also reduces communication nodes in the organization. Second, moving from one role to another specialty usually requires long training, but AI can cut learning costs to as low as 1% of the original, which also creates conditions for reducing communication nodes.

These directly changed the original division of labor in product-R&D organizations. So the Alibaba Cloud CIO's product-R&D team took the lead last year in reshaping roles: using the Half-stack concept, they consolidated multiple roles into two—one customer-facing, one engineering-facing.

Customer-facing: product managers, designers, and frontend were merged into the PDFE role, responsible from user intent all the way to UI architecture—pushing requirements confirmation to the extreme "left." Engineering-facing: backend and architecture were also merged into the ABE role, responsible from data structures all the way to system stability—pushing quality responsibility to the extreme "left." The two sides connect through API contracts, which greatly improved the overall interaction chain.

Scaling up from product-R&D organizations to the company's entire business system is an even larger organizational problem.

Since this year's lobster craze began, general-purpose agents have been continuously exploding, and Jiang Linquan noticed the same problem: general-purpose agents have indeed boosted individual office productivity, but the organization's overall E2E has not significantly improved.

His CIO team began in February this year to fully prepare the foundation for opening business systems to agents, but opening systems brings another layer of pressure: general-purpose agents open browsers on their own, retry at high frequency, consume Tokens at high speed, and can "blow up" the company's business systems. The underlying permissions and security issues are critical. Jiang Linquan's principle for opening up is: when agents act on behalf of employees, they must inherit the employee's original permissions.

In four months, data from the business systems he oversees showed: general-purpose agent call volume grew 1,000-fold, quickly surpassing GUI interactions and the original first-party agents.

Here he gave an example of product commercialization: configuration that used to take two months now takes half a day using skills. He particularly emphasized that merely turning on MCP is far from enough—you must also reverse the process, return to the real business, re-sort workflows, unify business concepts, sort out platform permissions, and invest enormous effort in this invisible "dirty, tedious work." You must also help many previously "offline" business processes catch up by going "online."

Only by doing all this can business systems truly open up to agents, and long-pent-up business demands can be fully released.

Accordingly, the CIO's role is also changing: business demands used to be centrally routed to the CIO team to wait for scheduling; now business teams close the loop themselves on simple, vertical demands, while the CIO continues to support complex, cross-domain special projects. At the same time, a "developer operations" role has been added—Jiang Linquan treats business teams as new developers, and his team assigns one developer operations person to each business team to help them solve development challenges.

In other words, the CIO line must both maintain data consistency, permissions, and the open platform, and help these "broadly defined developers" turn their ideas into processes that run smoothly.

On the much-discussed topic of AI nativeness, Jiang Linquan's understanding is that an AI Native organization is more like a "state": as AI capabilities keep evolving, teams must continuously revolutionize themselves—breaking old mental seals from within, pushing the organization's effectiveness and value realization to new heights, and exporting battle-tested products outward to help more enterprises transform with AI. The Rui series is exactly such a product line, distilled from the internal practice of the Alibaba Cloud CIO team and now deliverable externally, including "Rui Recruitment" for hiring, "Rui Translation" for translation, and more.

Yuan Yimin, VP of HR at Alibaba Cloud Intelligence Group: Writing the context of the sales front line into organizational weights

Yuan Yimin, VP of HR at Alibaba Cloud Intelligence Group, sharing on site

Yuan Yimin turned the lens to Alibaba Cloud's international business. Her organization covers both product/R&D and sales, and the difficulty of AI-ifying these two types of work differs greatly: code can naturally be read by machines, while sales' key judgments are scattered across visit sites, a customer's moment of hesitation, and employees' experience.

She broke an AI organization into six interconnected elements: architecture, information, decision-making, learning, people, and supporting environment. In her words, "it's not six puzzle pieces, it's a chain."

At the architecture level, she proposed first determining a single objective function. In the past, online campaigns, offline events, and market intelligence each carried their own KPIs; local metrics were all met, yet it was hard to say how many business results the leads actually delivered. Alibaba Cloud's international business began making these links jointly point to lead conversion ROI, then adjusting task routing based on real contribution. Division of labor is no longer written only in job descriptions; it also changes with outcome data.

On information, Yuan Yimin used a memorable phrase: "From Facts to Contexts, written into the weights." CRM, BI reports, and approval forms mostly store structured conclusions, while the raw field context that decisions depend on has long stayed out of systems. Now, agents can cheaply handle listening, transcription, and summarization, with humans judging which information matters. Only then can an enterprise's unique unstructured context have the chance to become its post-training data.

The experience in employees' heads and on their local computers remains private weights. Yuan Yimin said: "When the person leaves, the weights reset to zero." Only by continuously writing field perception into organizational systems can an enterprise build its own long-term memory. This also connects to the context interconnection Jiang Fang mentioned.

Yuan Yimin also used numbers to explain decision cost: 5 people in a review produce 10 communication links, while 15 people produce 105. The most expensive hidden cost in an organization is attention. She advocated layered decision-making by scenario, compressing the management view, and drilling down into full context only when necessary.

For organizational learning, the most important thing is to find the steps that come with "built-in referees," which also relies on verifiable outcomes and controlled experiments; otherwise, retrospectives easily mistake correlation for causation.

On talent, she offered a sharp judgment: "Post-training is activation, not expansion." Products, tools, and processes can be taught, but judgment, reasoning density, and learning speed are hard to make up through onboarding. HR needs to identify what kind of "pre-training environment" candidates have been through and what environments have rewarded them; for the most cutting-edge roles, one must also look at a person's self-learning and transfer speed.

The final roundtable was moderated by An Xiaopeng, VP of the Public Affairs line at Alibaba Cloud Intelligence Group. Li Xianqi, Party Committee member and Deputy GM of PetroChina Kunlun Digital Intelligence, Ruan Zhongyou, CIO of Yihai Kerry Arawana, and Sun Dengke, co-founder and CFO of Galbot, discussed AI Native organizations from three vantage points: industrial agents, large-enterprise IT, and embodied intelligence.

Roundtable discussion at the AI Native organization forum

The three guests proposed very different ways of measuring. Li Xianqi looks at cost reduction, efficiency gains, output, and yield; Ruan Zhongyou looks at how much AI-generated code reaches production and how many marketing proposals are adopted by customers and proceed to sampling and transactions; Sun Dengke's North Star metric is more distant—he hopes that within five years, "one person, in one month, can build a commercially viable robot." Token consumption can be an observation item, but cannot directly serve as the report card of organizational transformation.

Ruan Zhongyou made a wry remark: "Colleagues who truly understand AI are now canceling weekly meetings and weekly reports, while those who don't are busy holding meetings about AI transformation." This points to how leaders work. Managers must use AI themselves and think clearly about which part of the process it enters and what business problem it solves; when a project starts, they must also set up evaluation first. He also described a hiring experiment: have candidates use AI on the spot to accomplish something they've never done before, to observe their learning and transfer abilities.

Galbot's approach picks up the "special zone" Jiang Fang mentioned. Small teams and young people can freely explore in different directions; once a direction shows value, the company quickly invests people, compute, and data. An Xiaopeng summarized this mechanism as "emergence, selection, replication": first let new ideas surface, then judge their commercial prospects, and finally scale up what works. When to switch between distributed exploration and concentrated resources still requires ongoing organizational judgment.

Li Xianqi's case pushed AI into industrial settings with very high safety requirements. On the very day the anomaly-diagnosis agent was connected to Lanzhou Petrochemical's 5.5-million-ton atmospheric-vacuum distillation unit, it produced a real alert. On-site experts judged that its root-cause analysis and handling recommendations were close to those of an engineer with more than five years of experience.

To bring the agent into this process, the team built tens of thousands of entity nodes and more than twenty thousand ontological relationships, and configured tools and permissions, verifiable loops, execution boundaries, human final decision-making, and full-process observability. The agent took 45 days to complete; under the old information-system development approach, it would have taken at least half a year, even seven or eight months. The cycle shortened, but safety standards did not loosen. What the AI can see, how far it can execute, and when it must hand back to humans all have to be written into the system in advance.

The roundtable consensus was clear: measure AI by real business value, have managers use it personally, redesign core processes, and let large enterprises run independent teams first. The challenges are equally clear: how high-risk industries handle model uncertainty, who is responsible for agent permissions and auditing, and whether FDEs should ultimately be priced by project, by tokens, or by business outcomes—all still being tested. Concluding the session, An Xiaopeng said the most basic unit of organization is expanding from person-to-person to person-and-digital-employee. Technology has already entered the workplace; organizational mechanisms are still catching up.

At the end of her speech, Jiang Fang brought the topic back to talent. She relayed two important observations that Alibaba colleagues brought back from a recent study trip to Silicon Valley: NVIDIA, still rigorously assessing values at the $5-trillion NVIDIA, uses these 5 values—worth a look for every company and every person—with business output and values each accounting for 50% of the total performance score; Anthropic trains its internal interviewers through multiple rounds, including how to judge whether a candidate's values align with the company's mission.

From this she proposed "Talented but small ego." The AI era needs judgment and practical ability, and also people willing to pursue collective success and collaborate back-to-back with their teams. In the coming year, human colleagues will work alongside digital colleagues. Digital colleagues have no ego; humans must learn to share context, cede some control, and help their digital colleagues do better.

Thus the AI Native organization becomes a problem for both the CPO and the CIO. The CIO must make systems, data, and permissions agent-friendly; the CPO and HR must adjust mechanisms for managers, talent, collaboration, and evaluation; business leaders provide real scenarios and are accountable for results. Missing any one of the three, AI remains stuck at local efficiency gains.

The signal from this forum is more concrete: AI is moving from employees' toolboxes into managers' daily work, and is beginning to touch enterprises' processes, systems, talent, and accountability. The CPO and CIO co-hosting a forum for the first time is only a beginning. The gap that follows will show in who can make people, agents, data, permissions, and business goals run within the same working system.

Technology keeps advancing, and organizations keep evolving. Let us close with CPO Jiang Fang's speech, for mutual encouragement:

"No one has the answers; only by throwing ourselves fully into this exploration will we eventually find a path that lets AI truly reach into the fabric of the organization and into everyone's work."
