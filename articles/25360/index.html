What Is a DPA (Data Processing Agreement): How FDEs Handle Vendor Reviews

Original title: DPA 数据处理协议是什么：FDE 如何应对供应商审查

FDE Times · 2026-10-09 · 6 min read

Summary

This skill guide teaches forward deployed engineers (FDEs) to read Data Processing Agreements (DPAs) the way they read technical specs. Under GDPR Article 28 and Vietnamese law, processors may only handle personal data per contract and written customer instructions, making the DPA effectively the FDE's scope of work. Using a mid-project switch to an overseas model provider as an example, the article walks through sub-processor approval, SCCs for cross-border transfers, retention questions, and end-of-project deletion duties, offering a six-column data-flow table. It also covers Vietnam's Personal Data Protection Law (effective January 2026): a 60-day DPIA milestone, six-month reassessment cycles, and fines up to 5% of annual revenue for transfer violations. Finally, it urges FDEs to prepare vendor-review answers on sub-processors, regions, retention, and SOC 2 scope before legal asks.

Key points

  • GDPR Article 28 and Vietnamese law limit processors to contract and written customer instructions.
  • Adding an overseas model provider mid-project requires written customer approval and possibly SCCs.
  • Vietnam's new law adds a 60-day DPIA milestone and six-month reassessment cycles.
  • Vendor reviews commonly stall on sub-processors, data regions, and SOC 2 report scope.
  • FDEs should request the DPA in week one and prepare data-deletion checklists early.

Key Takeaways

  • Under GDPR Article 28 and Vietnamese law, a processor may only do what the contract and the customer's instructions allow. The DPA is effectively your scope of work.
  • Adding a new model provider or tool mid-project may require the customer's written approval. If the provider is located outside the country, SCCs or a transfer assessment may also come into play.
  • Vendor reviews will ask about sub-processors, regions, retention periods, and the scope of SOC 2 reports. FDEs who have the answers ready in advance can move projects forward faster.

Imagine you are on-site in week three. The data pipeline is running smoothly on sample data, and you want to switch to a different model provider for customer support ticket summarization. The customer's engineer agrees on the spot. Then legal sends an email asking just one question: Is this provider on the approved sub-processor list?

This kind of blocker isn't in the code. You don't need to become a lawyer. But you are the one with direct access to customer data, so you need to be able to read the clauses that determine what you can touch.

This article teaches one specific skill: reading a data processing agreement (DPA) like a technical spec, and preparing the answers to vendor review before the customer asks.

The Data Contract Is Your Scope of Work

Under the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR), the customer is the controller and your company is the processor. GDPR Article 28 requires processors to process personal data only on the controller's documented instructions.

Vietnamese law requires almost the same thing: a processor must process data according to the agreement or contract signed with the controller.

The practical implication is simple. Whenever you run a script on real data, export a table for debugging, or send a piece of text to an external API, first ask whether it is within the scope of the contract, then ask whether it works.

And this "contract" must be in writing: GDPR requires the processing agreement to be in written form, including electronic form. Four types of clauses in a DPA directly affect engineering work.

Sub-processors. A processor may not engage another processor without the controller's prior written authorization (specific or general).

End of service. All personal data must be deleted or returned, at the customer's choice.

Audits. The customer has the right to conduct audits, including on-site inspections.

Cross-border transfers. If data leaves the EU, safeguards are required, such as Standard Contractual Clauses (SCCs), which are model clauses pre-approved by the European Commission.

Worked Example: Adding a Model Provider Mid-Project

Back to that email from legal. Suppose the customer is an EU retailer and the provider you want to try has servers outside the EU. Here is a step-by-step walkthrough.

Step one, determine whether the new provider counts as a new processor. If you send tickets containing customer names and emails to its API, the answer is yes. That means you need the customer's written authorization. Check whether the existing DPA comes with a general authorization with a sub-processor list, or requires approval for each one.

Step two, cross-border transfer. Servers outside the EU mean a transfer of data to a third country, and legal will ask about SCCs. If you understand how the provider's DPA works, you can answer quickly.

Take Anthropic as an example: according to its help pages, accepting the Commercial Terms of Service constitutes acceptance of Anthropic's DPA, which includes SCCs, with no separate signature required. Sending legal a link to that clause is far faster than promising to chase down a signature.

Step three, data retention. The buyer will ask how long the provider retains data. Apptension's due diligence guide puts it bluntly: if a vendor cannot demonstrate clear retention controls, assume the data will be kept longer than you would like. Have the answer before you walk into the meeting.

The final step, and the most often forgotten: end of project. The obligation to delete or return data applies not only to the main database, but also to the CSVs you exported for debugging, the notebooks on your laptop, and the logs containing the original ticket text.

If the Customer Is in Vietnam, the Project Timeline Gains New Milestones

First, distinguish the old rules from the new. Vietnam's Personal Data Protection Law takes effect on January 1, 2026, and the accompanying Decree 356, issued on December 31, 2025 to guide its implementation, replaces the previous Decree 13 framework.

For project planning, the new framework adds the following.

60-day milestone. According to DLA Piper's overview, data processing impact assessment (DPIA) documentation must be submitted, and the competent authority must receive it within 60 days of the start of processing. Put it in the launch plan as a real milestone.

Six-month cycle. According to Tilleke & Gibbins' analysis, the new law requires DPIAs and cross-border transfer impact assessments (TIAs) to be updated on a six-month cycle when there are changes. Adding an offshore model provider falls squarely into this category.

Cross-border transfer risk. Violations of cross-border transfer rules can be fined up to 5% of annual revenue. So whenever the flow of data changes, inform the customer early.

Your paperwork. Under Decree 13, processors, like controllers, must also prepare and maintain their own impact assessment documentation, and cross-border transfers require a separate filing with the cybersecurity authority. The old rules have been superseded, so ask the customer's legal team: under Decree 356, which documents are your responsibility.

What Vendor Reviews Ask, and Why to Answer First

Procurement processes at large companies usually include a vendor review. According to Apptension's guide for buyers, such reviews should start with data flows and data retention.

Vendors who cannot answer clearly get classified as high risk. The same guide notes that if you cannot state your sub-processors and the regions where data resides, you cannot convincingly answer "where did the data go" during an audit.

The question that most often stalls a review is the scope of the SOC 2 report. A SOC 2 report is only valid for the products and environments listed in its scope section.

Apptension's advice: to reduce SOC 2 review risk, read the scope section first. If you are deploying a dedicated environment for the customer but the report only covers the shared SaaS product, that is a gap, and you should raise it proactively before the customer discovers it.

Action Steps and Common Mistakes

For every new customer, follow a fixed order. Request the DPA and sub-processor list in week one, and map the data flows before touching real data. Mark every external tool that will receive data, put milestones such as sub-processor approval and DPIA documentation into the plan, and draft the data deletion checklist for delivery day in advance.

The most common mistake is treating legal as someone else's job. The result is an FDE wiring in an observability tool or a new API based solely on the customer engineer's verbal approval.

Another common one is forgetting that logs and exported files are also personal data. And there's the case of handing over a SOC 2 report without knowing which product it covers.

Turn it into an interview story

When reading an FDE job description, watch for terms like security review, procurement, or customer compliance: once they appear, this skill belongs on your resume. When asked about a deployment that got stuck, the example above is great material.

A concise way to tell it: in week three, the customer's legal team blocked the onboarding of a summarization service provider because it wasn't on the sub-processor list and its servers were outside the EU. You drew a six-column data flow diagram, sent the provider's DPA link with SCCs, set a TTL on the logs, and wrote a checklist for deleting debug files.

Result: the customer approved by email, with no extra meeting. The story shows you understand both sides.

The next time the customer's legal team sends questions, reply with a complete six-column table instead of a promise.
