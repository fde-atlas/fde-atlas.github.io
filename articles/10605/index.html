Six judgments on the FDE role for bosses and job seekers: don't deify it, and don't miss it

The FDE role has recently been highly sought after in the AI field, but there is a huge gap between its real value and market expectations. This article sharply dissects the underlying logic of the FDE role—it is essentially a hybrid of on-site outsourcing, pre-sales and post-sales, and customer success—and points out that companies should not place the burden of AI implementation on a single role. From organizational transformation and business thinking to technical understanding, it offers 6 core judgments to help bosses avoid decision-making pitfalls, while revealing to job seekers that this role is a high-value entry point for understanding technology, product, and the real needs of major clients.

Core judgments

The FDE role itself is overrated, but the work stage it corresponds to is very important. Bosses should not place the core competitiveness of AI implementation on an externally hired role; job seekers can treat it as a high-value entry point for understanding technology, product, and the real needs of major clients.

  • Role logic: The underlying functions of an FDE are close to on-site outsourcing, pre-sales, post-sales, delivery engineer, and customer success.
  • Boss's perspective: Hiring one FDE will not automatically solve AI implementation; the person who can truly solve it is already close to partner level.
  • Organizational perspective: Prioritize internal transformation, focusing on business thinking, basic technical understanding, and willingness to communicate with clients.
  • Job seeker's perspective: This role lets you enter real client sites, and your growth will be fast.

FDE is hot right now, but I advise bosses not to overthink it

Anyone following AI should know that the FDE role has been very hot recently.

Many people in management, or bosses of To B companies, start wondering: Now that our company is doing AI business, should we also hire an FDE to help our product truly land and get implemented?

If you are a job seeker, or you were already on the business side and an FDE opportunity has now appeared at your company, you might also wonder: Should I take this role or not?

I offer 6 clear judgments, hoping to give you some advice.

1. FDE has been hyped up by some people; in essence it is still on-site outsourcing

FDE is absolutely not as amazing as everyone claims.

To put it bluntly, its role logic is on-site outsourcing.

Note that I'm talking about the role logic.

It still operates within the framework of a service company, sending someone who is closer to the client and communicates better with them to understand client needs, implement them, coordinate internal resources, and monitor the progress of the entire project.

It's just that in the past this person might have been called pre-sales, post-sales, delivery engineer, customer success, or on-site consultant.

Now they are working on AI products, private deployment, and enterprise applications, so people have given it a trendier name.

You can say it understands the product better, understands technology a bit more, or is closer to the client site.

But in terms of role functions, it is still a frontline service and project advancement role.

There is nothing derogatory about this in itself; To B business has always needed people like this.

The real problem is that the market has made this role sound too magical, as if clients failing to adopt a company's AI product is due to a lack of an FDE—as if once you hire an FDE, clients will start using it, the product will start running, and the AI business will naturally generate revenue.

Some self-media and media outlets hype up the FDE in every way, which is completely misleading. After all, on-site outsourcing is just one way to improve the delivery experience and delivery efficiency.

2. FDE has value, but bosses should lower their expectations of hiring

If you are a boss, I suggest you first lower your expectations of the FDE.

Don't think that just because you see Silicon Valley companies talking about FDEs, or everyone in the market saying FDEs can help clients implement AI products, get them adopted, and generate revenue, your own company can solve this problem with a single role.

It must be acknowledged that in 99% of cases, clients fail to adopt AI products because our product/service capabilities themselves need iteration and polishing, not because of a missing FDE. The value of the vast majority of products can only be demonstrated through implementation, training, scenario breakdown, and continuous pushing.

Perhaps such a miracle exists: you hire an FDE who really can get your AI application implemented and used well, and can produce visible results for clients.

Then the question arises: this shows that this person is impressive, not that your product or your business is impressive.

At that point, the most valuable asset rests with this person, namely the ability to "implement AI capabilities into applications." They truly understand clients' real needs, they know how clients can start using the product, and they can even push back to drive your product improvements.

Then they could absolutely take these needs and choose a better, more mature, more competitive product in the industry. They could even organize their own delivery team and directly provide AI implementation services to clients.

So why would they work for you?

The scarcest capability in the AI industry right now is implementation-driven efficiency. One could even say that what people lack is an entire FDE company, and what they lack is definitely not your AI product.

How clients use it, who uses it, in which workflow, how to change the original way of working, how to prove efficiency gains... these are all core questions. And expecting an ordinary externally hired FDE to fully handle this is unrealistic in itself.

So a more reasonable positioning for the FDE is to see it as an engineer who can be deployed to the frontline.

They may lean toward pre-sales, helping clients understand the product and validate scenarios.

They may lean toward post-sales, helping clients with configuration, launch, training, and problem-solving.

They may also lean toward customer success in the SaaS industry, pushing clients from having purchased the product to actually using it.

They are important, but they cannot carry the entire company's delivery capability and efficiency-improvement capability.

Never place so-called AI implementation, client efficiency gains, and business growth on this role. Not even a little.

3. The kind of FDE you want is hard to find in the market

So how do you actually find FDE people?

The person you want understands business, clients, technology, and products, can coordinate resources, advance projects, and help clients achieve results.

That is already close to the capability of a small company.

What you want is not really an employee, but a company, or rather a partner. Relying on external hiring is unrealistic.

Of course, we must also acknowledge that the work stage the FDE corresponds to is very important. For an AI product to truly land, someone must stand at the client site, connecting needs, product, technology, and business results.

There is nothing wrong with this stage.

For any emerging role, what a company should consider first is often not immediate external hiring, but which people in the existing team can transition into it: pre-sales can transition, planners can transition, product people can transition, R&D can transition too...

Even some people in customer success, delivery, and operations roles might transition into it.

Because in this new environment of AI implementation, hardly anyone can claim to have particularly rich experience.

At this point, what matters most is actually personality, ability, and cognition.

4. The core is business thinking

I think the most important thing is whether a person has genuine business thinking.

The biggest difference between business thinking and R&D thinking is that business thinking always focuses on how to solve the problem in the simplest way.

When many people encounter a client need, their first reaction is: record this requirement, go back and have the product team add a feature, and have R&D schedule it.

This kind of person is not suited for frontline AI implementation.

Because problems at the client site are never that standard.

Data is incomplete, processes are unclear, the person in charge keeps changing, and the clients themselves can't articulate what they really want.

If every problem has to be solved at the product feature level, the project will quickly get stuck.

A truly business-oriented person will first determine what the client's real problem is.

Can we first work around it with existing features?

Is this thing really required? Can I leave it blank?

Can I get another part of the process working first?

……

This kind of person will find ways to work around problems and solve them by setting up whatever scaffolding is needed, pushing things forward.

That's what business-oriented thinking looks like.

AI implementation especially requires this ability.

Because AI applications are often not purely technical problems, but business process transformation problems.

You need to know where you can compromise, where you must hold firm, where to build a small closed loop first, and where to fill in system capabilities later.

5. A basic understanding of technical principles is enough

The second thing to look at is whether they understand some technical principles.

But I believe that in the AI era, the technical requirements for frontline personnel are actually not as high as many people imagine.

In the past, delivering enterprise-level technology meant digging through many low-level details yourself, slowly asking questions and slowly building understanding.

Now, many details can be asked directly of AI.

More complex engineering implementations are handled by specialized R&D engineers behind the scenes.

What a frontline FDE really needs to master is the most basic operating logic at the technical level.

They need to know roughly where AI capabilities come from, where the boundaries are, which scenarios are suitable, which scenarios are prone to failure, and which requirements sound simple but carry high engineering costs.

They also need to translate customer language into language that internal R&D can understand.

At the same time, they need to translate internal technical constraints into terms customers can understand.

Getting to that level is already enough to support a lot of frontline work.

So when looking for FDE transition candidates within the company, I recommend focusing on two traits.

One is strong business thinking.

The other is a basic understanding of technology.

If the person isn't averse to deep engagement with customers, and is willing to go on-site to hear requirements, handle ambiguous problems, and drive customer adoption, then they're well suited for this transition.

This is usually far more reliable than hiring an FDE from outside who looks great on paper.

6. Job seekers should seriously consider it

If you're a job seeker, my view of this role is more positive.

I think the FDE role has a very promising future.

The reason is simple: you get an excellent opportunity to understand technology, product, and real customer needs all at once.

Many people studying product can only hear product managers talk about features.

Many people studying technology can only look at demos or documentation.

Many people doing business can only look at things from the angle of sales leads and customer relationships.

An FDE pushes you into the customer's field environment.

You'll see how customers actually understand AI, how they raise requirements, how they resist new tools, how they push an application forward within their organization, and you'll see how many problems lie between a product demo and real adoption.

This is extremely valuable.

Because what the AI industry lacks right now is exactly implementation and application.

Whoever truly understands customer needs, whoever knows why customers don't use the product, whoever can connect product capabilities with business scenarios—their growth will be extremely fast.

Especially for young people, if you have the chance to enter such a role, I think it's worth serious consideration.

It may not be as miraculous as the outside world makes it sound, but it genuinely gives you a high-density growth environment.

You'll understand what AI business really looks like earlier than many people who only stay in the office making proposals, writing PPTs, and watching product training.

A rational view

So, my judgment on FDEs can be summed up in two sentences.

For bosses: don't deify the FDE.

It can improve frontline service and project execution capabilities, but it cannot replace the company's own product capabilities, delivery systems, and customer understanding.

For job seekers: the FDE role is absolutely worth considering. It puts you on the front line of AI implementation, where you see real customers, real needs, and real resistance.

The term FDE will probably fade, but people who can actually put AI to work in business will, of course, become more and more valuable.
