Don’t be an FDE snowflake, says Kinaxis’s Chief of Agentic Solutions

Diginomica · 2026-11-26 · 10 min read

Summary

Manik Sharma, Chief of Agentic Solutions at Kinaxis, argues that forward deployed engineers should assemble 'personalized' solutions from standard platform components rather than produce fragile, customer-specific 'customized' snowflakes. Gaps found in customer work feed back into reusable 'Lego bricks' built by dedicated teams. His long-term ambition is a supply chain operating system, with implementation eventually handed to customers and partners. The author warns that services revenue could skew incentives, and customers should avoid lock-in to bespoke solutions.

Key points

  • Sharma distinguishes personalization (platform tooling) from customization (fragile snowflakes).
  • FDEs work in three teams: customer delivery, incubation of new bricks, and core engineering.
  • His aspiration is a supply chain operating system, likened to the 2007 iOS launch.
  • Kinaxis plans to hand delivery over to a customer and partner ecosystem over time.
  • The author cautions that services revenue risks distorting product incentives.

One of the words that has been thoroughly over-used in the last decade of culture wars — at least by a certain kind of individual — is ‘snowflake’.

And so when Manik Sharma, Chief of Agentic Solutions at Kinaxis, started throwing it around in a recent interview I started to wonder what I had gotten myself into.

But for Sharma the term has an entirely different and more interesting meaning — one that is of particular importance during the current industry trend du jour of embracing the Forward Deployed Engineer (FDE) cult [sorry] shared value delivery model.

Because for Sharma, there's a fine line between personalizing a standard offering and customizing a unique one — and when FDEs step over it they end up lumbering organizations with what I understand young people call ‘a melt’. He explains:

When I go beyond a packaged solution, I'm at a personalized solution for the customer. And I use the word personalized versus customized for a reason. Customization is where I'm tweaking any part of the platform — which is like a snowflake.

Essentially Sharma is arguing that any company that uses FDEs has to still follow the basic architectural principles of mass customization — such as modularity, assembly of personalized behavior from standard components, and the integration of enhancements back into the core product — rather than simply hack the underlying codebase until it somehow, sort of, meets the specific needs of each individual organization.

And this matters to Sharma because Kinaxis has a pretty ambitious strategy to extend beyond its current supply chain planning business and encompass the end-to-end orchestration of its customers’ supply chains — which only works if it can build a sufficiently composable platform to both bring together a much broader range of capabilities and adapt them to each customer’s individual operational environment.

From product to platform

And that’s where my discussion with Sharma begins — because assembling and deploying solutions using a composable platform is… somewhat different from deploying a single packaged software application like Kinaxis’s longstanding planning software.

Because such a platform doesn’t only require ‘deployment into’ an environment but ‘deployment amongst’ everything else — which means being able to adapt to the particular characteristics of each customer's supply chain while also successfully integrating its own particular rat’s nest of data systems, physical infrastructure and ornery participants.

And this, Sharma explained, is where his army of FDEs is intended to step into the breach:

The forward-deployed engineer motion is about having tooling in place and going in to understand the customer's problem and stitch the puzzle together for them. It's not coming to a customer with a packaged solution. For Kinaxis the solutioning aspect is to stitch this puzzle together with what we have in the platform.

But what, I ask, is in the platform? Sharma elaborates:

With Kinaxis, our tooling is the platform. We have a data fabric and semantic tooling for building objects and relationships. Part of that tooling is Maestro, because it can solve some very complex supply chain problems. On the agent side we have orchestration skills that FDEs can configure for particular outcomes. And on the experience side, widgets, process maps and drill-throughs are all standard components of the platform.

But Sharma acknowledges that even with this toolbox there's still a considerable amount of customer-specific engineering to be done:

There will be gaps. The first job for the FDE is the data fabric — moving data into the reference database, building the semantic objects and relationships, and putting life into those objects, with trigger functions, action functions and constraints. Then, getting to the outcome flow — the FDE works out how to connect workflows and decision engines through APIs or other protocols so decisions can feed back into the semantics and the next actions can be taken. And then the FDE has to ensure we're serving data back and forth to the right surfaces — green screens, copilot or whatever — so that orchestration happens across all those functions.

But at this point I’m suffering from a little cognitive dissonance — because all of this sounds like customer-specific work, with the FDEs both configuring what exists but also building whatever is necessary but missing.

And isn't that the very definition of what Sharma means by a ‘snowflake’ — a customer-specific customization that needs constant care and attention to stop it melting?

From customization to personalization

I put this confusion to Sharma and ask how he intends to avoid the risk that he will end up with a significant number of unique solutions to support — like snowflakes — that are each perhaps beautiful in their own way but utterly fragile.

And this is where he returns to the dividing line between customization and personalization:

I use the word personalized versus customized. Personalized is where I have capabilities and tooling in the platform. Think of Excel. A billion people log into Excel and make macros. Their macros are very custom, but they use the tooling and dropdown menus within it. That's exactly the line we're trying to draw. What is the tooling we expose and the Lego bricks of menu items for people to build that? So that [like] Excel, when it upgrades, people are not affected.

In other words Sharma is determined to ensure that customer solutions are built from what he continually calls “Lego bricks” — standard modules that would be supported by Kinaxis and assembled into ‘personalized’ solutions rather than ‘customized’ snowflakes.

And to support this he says he views the work of the FDEs across three connected teams:

There are the customer FDE teams — these guys are responding to customer requirements and feeding things back in. If the Lego bricks fall short, an incubation FDE team keeps building the bricks. And then there's engineering… [for] things which we think cannot be bricks and have to be core platform.

In other words, FDEs don't simply assemble individual customer solutions, but instead discover what's missing from the platform and feed those requirements into teams responsible for extending its reusable capabilities — which is why Sharma stresses that the resulting Lego bricks are productized and supported, rather than being experimental customizations which get left behind when an engagement ends. In this way he suggests that he can use the FDE engagements themselves to progressively expand what can be assembled for the next customer.

But this only leads to another level of cognitive dissonance for me. Because while I understand the idea, it also shifts a considerable services burden onto Kinaxis — especially given the ambition to co-evolve solutions with customers as part of a long-term relationship.

Which begs a different question — doesn't this leave Kinaxis facing a significant business model shift? One which takes it away from infinitely replicable software and into finite-capacity custom service delivery?

From platform to ecosystem

While Sharma acknowledges that Kinaxis's commercial model will change, he denies that the FDE push will ultimately turn it into a high-touch services organization:

We are not going to grow this FDE business to be a services business. Our FDEs are right now penetrating the market. But a lot of customers and partners are coming in and saying ‘I have my own teams.’ And the skills to build these solutions are very easily available. Anybody who knows Python, who knows basic databases, can pick this up and start building.

All of which is to say that Sharma envisages his FDEs gradually handing over the work to a coalition of the willing as the platform matures — Kinaxis’s customers and partners:

Today I have my hands on the steering wheel. You're the passenger. Tomorrow, you move into the driver's seat. That's why this is a big ecosystem play for me — I'm building a new highway system here. I just collect the toll for the analytics and all that. People can build this stuff.

And the value of that highway, Sharma argues, lies not in owning the individual applications or even the underlying data, but in understanding how supply chain processes fit together — what he calls the "ontology of the flow." And he believes that the domain knowledge, decisioning capabilities and reusable components Kinaxis has built up in this area give it a strong advantage:

I can go into an SAP customer where the customer has no desire to move away from APO [SAP's Advanced Planning and Optimization software] and build the semantic model for them. Maestro was a great starting point, but I can go to any planning system, because I understand flows.

Which takes us quite a long way from the original discussion about FDEs.

I suggest to Sharma that his explanation makes the FDE model feel like a stepping stone to a much bigger prize — something closer to an operating system for supply chains. He responds:

100%. That's exactly what I'm looking for. My aspiration is the 2007 moment when iOS got released. The Walkman, the camera, all became an app. I want to be the operating system where warehouse management, everything becomes an agentic flow. It's only when iOS has been able to pull things together that you can get CarPlay — the camera, the music, the directions working together. I want to be that system where customers can build that across warehouse management systems, across inventory systems, across planning systems.

Essentially, then, Sharma wants to be the Steve Jobs of supply chain.

So, uh, no pressure then.

My take

It's become a bit of a trope — even before the mania for FDEs — that the best way to develop products is to do it together with customers. To listen attentively, to learn from their needs, and to build what they want.

But having worked in both product and service companies I would say that the reality is significantly more nuanced.

Because as Clayton Christensen pointed out, while listening to customers might work for existing offerings, new offerings need to be focused on jobs-to-be-done — essentially resisting specific customer feature demands in favor of building something that meets a more general bucket of needs better than existing approaches.

And having worked in large systems integrators I know the almost super-human discipline required to maintain a portfolio of supposedly reusable solutions when the customer voice dominates the discussion — and revenue comes from doing what they want right now.

Sharma starts in a different place — because of Kinaxis's product heritage — and so has an existing product organization to draw on. But I still feel that Kinaxis needs to be alert to how much services engagements can skew incentives once they start delivering significant revenue — because expensive developers building product one step removed from the market can suddenly start looking like under-utilized opportunities to make a quick buck. And if the company is successful with its FDE program — and it really does become a vehicle for high value, complex engagements — then it may find a significant portion of its revenue starting to come from those services rather than its products, at which point the danger could be amplified ten-fold.

The company's plan to gradually cede the field to customers and partners feels like a reassuring potential counterweight — but will just need to be kept front and center if it is to evolve without drama from product to composable platform via services-led orchestration. But if it can hold the line — and has the strength of product management necessary to treat its own FDE organization as a prototypical systems integrator customer of the platform, rather than as a core commercial offering in its own right — then the model can potentially work. In practice, it just means ensuring that its own FDEs work with the same tools, components and constraints as any external systems integrator so that the gaps they uncover lead to a flywheel of reusable capabilities that make the platform more useful to others — and clear a path out of delivery for Kinaxis.

But interestingly the danger of drifting into services is perhaps even more pertinent for the company’s current and future customers.

Because when starting on the FDE journey with Kinaxis, organizations should also recognize the dangers that could flow from their own behavior — because it would be easy for them to compromise their own interests by pushing Kinaxis to create the kind of fragile and expensive snowflake Sharma talked about.

Because at the end of the day, for Kinaxis, getting embedded in a series of large custom services engagements for the next several decades might be a minor pain in the ass — and a distraction from their product business — but it would also be pretty lucrative. But for customers, getting locked into a bin fire of bespoke workflows, integrations and dependencies that require expensive specialist intervention every time something needs to change would be… a major one. So organizations should definitely go into these kinds of FDE engagement with their eyes open — treating it not as another systems integration opportunity ripe for gold plating, but an opportunity to shape the roadmap of the product provider.

But to return to Sharma, while the overall ambition is large, the route to getting there seems coherent — not just in his explanation but in the wider vision Kinaxis laid out at its Kinexions event in Barcelona recently.

As I observed at the time, however, it's going to take some skill to thread the needle — because it’s never easier to shift technology, positioning, and delivery model all at the same time.

Challenges which, I guess, explain why Sharma seems to hate snowflakes so much.
