Exclusive Analysis | Daily Pay Up to 170,000: Why Is Wall Street Scrambling for "AI Implementation Officers"?

Original title: 独家解读丨日薪高达17万：华尔街为何疯抢「AI落地官」？

雷峰网 · 2026-06-03 · 8 min read

Summary

This article examines why two former hedge fund traders, Felipe Sinisterra and Dave Wang, can charge $25,000 per day for AI consulting to Wall Street firms. It argues the price reflects a supply bottleneck: enterprises have bought AI tools but cannot embed them into core business processes due to fragmented data, legacy workflows designed for humans, and a shortage of talent combining business and AI expertise. Examples include a cross-border e-commerce firm whose agent failed on old supply-chain processes, and Uber burning its annual AI budget in four months with little visible output. In response, tech giants are racing to hire Forward Deployed Engineers: OpenAI formed a $14 billion deployment company and acquired Tomoro with 150 FDEs, Google compressed FDE interviews to two days, Anthropic partnered with Blackstone and Goldman Sachs, and Meta built an Enterprise Solutions unit. The author concludes the high fees signal a genuine shortage, not a bubble.

Key points

  • Two ex-traders charge $25,000/day teaching Wall Street to use AI, booked two months out
  • AI adoption stalls on fragmented data, legacy processes, and lack of hybrid talent
  • Uber burned its yearly AI budget in four months with minimal perceived gains
  • OpenAI launched a $14B deployment company and bought Tomoro; Google, Meta, Anthropic are also hiring FDEs
  • Author argues the fees reflect a supply bottleneck, not a bubble

"The truth about large model deployment is being revealed." Jerry Lu, a partner at a Silicon Valley AI investment fund, candidly told Leifeng.com (WeChat account: Leifeng.com). His remark stems from two seemingly unrelated news stories that have recently stirred heated discussion in Silicon Valley's AI circles.

One is a sky-high bill on Wall Street: two AI consultants charge as much as $25,000 per day (about 170,000 RMB), with client bookings already scheduled two months out.

The other is that tech giant Meta has reportedly formed a new "Enterprise Solutions" department, planning to station engineers at major clients to provide customized AI services.

In Jerry's view, these two seemingly unrelated stories actually reflect the same thing: when entering enterprises, there is still a gap between large models' APIs and business logic.

What the sky-high bill buys is precisely the "scarcity premium" of being able to close that gap.

In fact, this has almost become the hottest topic in Silicon Valley's AI circles recently.

Giants like Google, OpenAI, and Anthropic are not only setting up large numbers of FDE (Forward Deployed Engineer) positions, but even establishing dedicated subsidiaries or joint ventures, and making big acquisitions, to quickly build up FDE staff.

Meanwhile, domestic AI cloud giants have also been "frantically poaching talent" recently; one major company even hired a vice-president-level executive from outside to lead FDE-related teams. For more insider details, add the author's WeChat xf123a.

Sky-high daily rates, giants poaching talent, capital M&A... What exactly are these Wall Street financial behemoths and AI tech giants anxious about?

$25,000, about 170,000 RMB. Even on Wall Street, that figure is jaw-dropping.

And that is just one day's pay for the two AI consultants, Felipe Sinisterra and Dave Wang.

Both are former hedge fund traders. Last year, they founded a company that teaches Wall Street elites how to use AI; its daily service fee has now soared to $25,000, with financial institution clients lining up.

What are they teaching? Why is it so expensive?

Some fund managers who attended recall that they teach how to use Gemini to analyze entrepreneurs' pitch videos, and use ChatGPT and Claude to perform sentiment analysis on earnings calls, identifying key statements that could move markets.

"At $25,000 a day, on the surface it looks like AI training is expensive, but it actually reflects the anxiety of financial institutions: the tools have been bought, but they don't know how to use them in core business." Former AWS chief architect Fei Lianghong told Leifeng.com.

"Using AI to analyze pitch videos and earnings call sentiment isn't simple 'prompting skills'—it's about truly embedding AI into investment research, risk judgment, and trading decision processes. What enterprises lack most right now are people who can turn AI into business results."

"It's essentially FOMO. Many traditional companies aren't steeped in Silicon Valley's AI circles, so when facing the impact of large models, they feel a mix of shock, anxiety, and confusion—they're stunned by AI's capabilities, but can't quite understand it or use it." said Jiang Shuomiao, CEO of Guanceyun (Observability Cloud).

A technology executive at a commercial bank headquartered in Shenzhen told Leifeng.com that as the "top paymasters" of technology deployment, financial institutions are often the richest and most motivated to try new technologies, and thus the most willing to pay big money for "outside help."

That's because AI technology iterates so fast—so fast that these companies can barely keep up. And this is exactly the arbitrage window that AI consultants Dave and Felipe have seized.

How big is this window? Just look at the long queues of anxious Wall Street giants.

Wall Street financial institutions don't lack money, data, or use cases—the only thing they lack is the ability to "use AI well."

This is not an isolated case, but a "systemic dilemma" faced by the vast majority of enterprises when deploying AI.

"Don't just look at tech giants. Currently, most enterprises' use of AI remains shallow, still at the level of meeting notes, PPTs, and writing some code." Fei Lianghong explained.

"But what's truly hard and important is getting AI into enterprises' internal data, business systems, and decision-making processes."

In his view, many AI projects don't fail because of AI's capabilities, but because of the enterprises' own data, processes, and organizational coordination.

"There are four core reasons why enterprises 'can't use AI well or deeply': scattered data, unrestructured processes, a lack of people who understand both business and AI, and the absence of clear accountability and ROI mechanisms within the organization." Take, for example, the most core issue: process restructuring.

Unlike the standardization of C-end business, B-end enterprise business is often as complicated as a tangled mess.

For example, a cross-border e-commerce company in Shenzhen once tried using an AI agent to replace manual handling of returns and exchanges during major promotions. In theory, when the AI agent detected a batch of returns, it could automatically coordinate with the warehouse to adjust inventory in time, helping the company avoid losses.

Their idea at the time was: "Can we delegate authority to the AI agent?"

But after running through the process, they found that the existing supply chain processes were designed for "humans," and the AI agent couldn't use them.

Running the AI agent on the old process meant: the AI computed a solution, but under traditional internal control processes, it first had to send it to the customer service supervisor, the supervisor had to log into the intranet and click confirm, then report to the warehouse manager for a second authorization via QR code scan, and finally a human employee had to manually copy and paste mismatched product codes between the two systems into the logistics system.

A new car running on old rails—the AI agent's advantages were basically impossible to realize.

"The integration and connection between AI tools and existing business systems is still insufficient, causing AI deployments to suffer from 'acclimatization' issues. This is a key problem enterprises and service providers need to focus on solving next." added Chen Yunwen, CEO of Daguan Data.

And hidden behind the processes is an even bigger "knot": data fragmentation.

Take banking as an example: a bank's customer data may be spread across four or five isolated platforms such as CRM, email systems, transaction systems, and compliance systems.

For AI to run an automated "customer risk assessment" process, it first has to connect these 4 to 5 data silos. Connecting one usually takes weeks of data cleaning and interface development. Connecting all of them takes months.

As Chen Yunwen put it: "The core reason enterprises can't use AI well is weak data infrastructure and too much historical data debt. Many old problems—data cleaning, rule sorting, data fragmentation—have become even more exposed in the AI era, limiting the depth of AI applications."

With enterprise data scattered every which way and silos everywhere, even the most powerful AI model will inevitably get lost—the model doesn't understand your business, let alone get into your systems.

Moreover, facing AI, a rapidly evolving new technology, many companies lack people who understand both business and AI.

"Right now, many enterprises lack 'dual-talent' who understand both advanced AI systems and the business." Chen Yunwen told Leifeng.com that AI talent represents a brand-new skill set—such as model evals, process reshaping, prompt engineering, data permission management, and hallucination control—but such talent is almost entirely absent from enterprises' existing traditional talent pools.

Just like 20 years ago, when enterprises bought SAP systems and still needed a consulting team to spend six months on implementation before they could be used, the "last mile" of technology deployment still needs engineering implementation to fill the gap.

"Large model technology is only the first step; the subsequent long-cycle application deployment, such as model selection and personnel training, is key to using AI well and making it truly take root in the business." Jiang Shuomiao added.

And amid this awkward fumbling of "not knowing how to use it," enterprises are facing unprecedented "value anxiety" internally.

Earlier, Uber COO Andrew Macdonald bluntly stated at a tech conference that controlling AI spending is "increasingly hard to justify," because there is no visible direct relationship between investment and productivity. Uber's compute and token consumption grew exponentially, burning through its entire annual AI budget in just four months, yet the functional improvements users actually perceived were minimal.

This harsh reality of "high input, low output" is sparking huge controversy across the entire industry. Gary Marcus, standard-bearer of the LLM bubble theory, promptly published a warning: "If enough companies experience the same situation, the bubble will burst."

On one side is an intense desire to use AI to cut costs and boost efficiency; on the other is the siege of legacy systems and sky-high token bills. This brutal forcing mechanism ultimately pushed the pressure onto the LLM vendors.

Do the LLM companies know about their customers' AI anxiety?

The answer is yes. Otherwise they wouldn't be frantically hiring FDEs (Forward Deployed Engineers).

Recently, a fierce battle over FDEs has been raging in Silicon Valley.

To ramp up FDE hiring, Google compressed its interview process to just two days; OpenAI announced the creation of the "OpenAI Deployment Company," valued at $14 billion, dedicated to enterprise-level AI deployment, and swiftly acquired Tomoro, a UK company with 150 FDEs.

Meanwhile, Anthropic partnered with Blackstone and Goldman Sachs to form an independent AI services company, plugging Claude into the critical business workflows of "mid-sized enterprises across all industries"; Meta set up an Enterprise Solutions division, dispatching engineers to help customers clean their data and embed AI tools directly into their workflows.

The giants finally had to admit a reality: the dream of making easy money by just selling APIs is over.

"FDE roles are hot precisely because the tech giants have realized that selling APIs and AI models alone isn't enough—customers need someone to help them actually get AI up and running," explained Fei Lianghong, former chief architect at AWS.

In his view, Meta setting up an Enterprise Solutions division and stationing engineers with major clients is essentially transforming AI companies from "product suppliers" into "deployment service providers." The value of an FDE isn't just writing code—it's going deep into the customer's site, understanding business processes, connecting data systems, quickly building usable solutions, and pushing PoCs into production environments.

"The core contradiction of enterprise AI deployment has changed. In the past, everyone competed on whose AI model was stronger; going forward, the competition is over who can turn models into customer efficiency, revenue, and profit faster. What's truly scarce isn't AI tools, but people who combine 'business understanding + engineering implementation + organizational drive' in one person," he emphasized.

Chen Yunwen, CEO of DataGrand, likewise believes the FDE boom signals that the AI competition has officially entered its second half—shifting from competing on models to competing on deployment.

"The core value of FDEs is acting as the 'translator' between technology and business. They understand both AI capabilities and business scenarios, and can truly close the loop of product-evolution-driven deployment. This is really a shift in mindset—from a technology mindset to a business-value mindset—and a key step for AI moving into real-world deployment."

Back to the original question: is a $25,000 daily salary a bubble signal, or an inevitable growing pain of the industry?

The conclusion is clear: this is not a bubble, but a price signal of a supply bottleneck.

What does a real bubble look like? Fake demand.

For example, during the 1999–2000 dot-com bubble, the logic behind .com companies burning cash was "everyone else is burning money." But today's market is different—enterprises' demand for using AI to optimize processes and boost productivity is real and hard-hitting.

The current awkwardness is this: LLM technology capabilities are in place, but the last mile of product deployment is not.

LLMs crush humans on benchmarks. But their capabilities are wrapped inside APIs, and to turn them into gears that run smoothly within enterprise systems, countless deep trenches made of legacy data and legacy processes must be filled in.

The people filling those trenches—like Dave Wang, Felipe Sinisterra, and a growing number of FDE teams—are currently the most highly priced and most sought-after talent in the global tech market.

These people are expensive, and this deployment road is painful, but it's very hard to avoid.
