What is a Forward Deployed Engineer? Why are AI labs competing to hire FDEs?

Original title: 前线部署工程师是什么？AI 实验室为何抢招 FDE

FDE Times · 2026-10-08 · 5 min read

Summary

The article explains why forward deployed engineer (FDE) roles are surging. A MIT NANDA study of 300 enterprise AI deployments found 95% had no measurable profit impact, showing the gap between impressive demos and working production systems. OpenAI acquired Tomoro, a UK firm with 150 FDEs, and Anthropic launched an FDE consulting arm backed by Blackstone and Goldman Sachs. Lightcast data shows FDE job postings grew over 1000% year-over-year in the first eight months of 2026, with median salaries above $188,000 versus roughly $145,000 for traditional software engineers. The piece distinguishes FDEs from consultants and solutions architects, and advises developers to demonstrate they can make systems work with messy data and real business processes.

Key Takeaways

  • In the first eight months of 2026, FDE job postings grew by more than 1000%. OpenAI, Anthropic, and Google are all hiring, and OpenAI even acquired a company with 150 FDEs.
  • The reason: demos win contracts, but only production deployments keep customers. Meanwhile, of the 300 enterprise AI projects surveyed by MIT NANDA, 95% had no measurable impact on profit.
  • For developers, the path into the FDE field is proving you can get systems running amid messy data and real business processes—not just in a notebook.

OpenAI recently founded a new deployment company, and its first acquisition was neither a research lab nor a model startup, but the UK-based company Tomoro, which has 150 Forward Deployed Engineers (FDEs). Anthropic has taken the same path, launching an external FDE consulting firm backed by Blackstone and Goldman Sachs.

Both AI labs are spending their money on more people, not more GPUs. Gergely Orosz, author of The Pragmatic Engineer newsletter, reports that Google, OpenAI, and Anthropic all have strong demand for this role. Lightcast data cited by Fortune shows that from January to August 2026, FDE job postings grew by more than 1000% year over year.

If you're a developer with a few years of experience wondering why this role suddenly got so hot, the answer lies in a rather grim number.

The Hard Part Isn't the Model—It's the Customer

A study by MIT NANDA examined 300 publicly documented enterprise AI deployments and found that 95% of them had no measurable impact on profit. Put that number next to the hiring data, and the demand becomes clear. Today's models are good enough to produce dazzling demos, but the distance from demo to running in a real business is as far as it ever was.

Paraform, a recruiting firm that tracks this role, summed up the business logic in two sentences: demos win contracts, production deployments keep them. Dice president Paul Farnsworth described it from the enterprise side: companies struggle to turn AI models into something that actually runs within their own business, and FDEs fill exactly that gap.

Consider a typical case. A logistics company bought an AI platform to automate processing of shipping documents. In the demo, the model read clean sample invoices and extracted every field correctly. At deployment, the real documents were scattered across three different systems, half of them photos taken with a phone, and the approval process required sign-off from two departments that had never worked together.

None of this is visible over Zoom from the vendor's headquarters. What is visible is an engineer sitting in the customer's office. That engineer can open the customer's database, ask the data-entry staff why they keep skipping a certain field, and then write code to make the system work with the real data. That engineer is an FDE.

Leaving the Palo Alto Office to Sit Beside the Customer

Palantir's Meline von Brentano explained in a Fortune interview that the company recruits the best software engineers and then pushes them out of the comfort of the Palo Alto office to work on-site with customers. The key word here is "comfort." The real problems only show up where engineers would usually prefer not to go.

Paraform defines the role as sitting at the intersection of hands-on problem-solving for customers and deploying AI systems. The two halves cannot be separated. Someone who only talks to customers will promise things that can't be built; someone who only writes code will build things nobody uses. An FDE must do both, often in the same week.

There is one objection worth hearing. A summary of a Pragmatic Engineer article argues that today's FDE roles are actually closer to consultants or Solutions Architects. This view is useful because it gives you a question to apply to every job description: does the role require you to write code yourself and be accountable for the outcome when the system breaks at the customer's site? If not, it's consulting, not FDE work.

What It Takes to Move Into This Role

FDE work exists to bridge the gap between demo and production, so the strongest evidence you can offer is that you have bridged such a gap before. That makes the first skill integrating messy systems. Developers doing outsourcing or in-house product development in Vietnam often have an underrated advantage here: they're used to working with legacy systems, handling non-standard data, and dealing with clients who change requirements halfway through a project.

The problem is that your resume rarely tells that story.

Pick one project and rewrite it the way FDE recruiters want to see it: what problem users faced before you arrived, which systems you had to integrate with, and which metric changed after launch. One line like that beats ten lines of framework lists.

The second skill is customer requirements discovery, and it can be learned. Instead of asking users what features they want, practice sitting next to them, observing how they actually work, and then translating that workflow into a data flow yourself. If the role really sits between the customer and the system, as Paraform says, then this is the half that most developers rarely get to practice in their day-to-day work.

The third skill is harder to teach: tolerating ambiguity. Return to the logistics example. Nobody will hand you a ticket for three disconnected systems and two departments that never talk to each other. You arrive at an unfamiliar office with nothing but an open-ended problem and must judge for yourself what's worth doing. If that sounds more exciting than frightening, you already have the most important trait.

On pay, Lightcast data shows the median salary in FDE job ads exceeds $188,000, compared with about $145,000 for traditional software engineers. Another analysis by Recruiting From Scratch of 924 job postings put the median at $195,000. That premium is the market's price for the stretch of work between demo and production.

Demos still win contracts, but the labs are putting serious money into the people who can keep them. What's scarce today is someone who can make the model actually run in the customer's office—and in the coming years, that someone could be you.
