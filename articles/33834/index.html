Annual salary of 1.7 million yuan: this AI role suddenly became a hot commodity

Follow Qibidao

Author | Qibidao, Songe

Editor | Qibidao, Zou Wei

Cover image by Qibidao

Recently, an AI role called FDE suddenly went viral.

On recruitment platforms, ByteDance's "Doubao AI Large Model FDE" pays up to 70,000 yuan per month with 15 months' salary; Ant Digital Technologies' B2B FDE pays up to 60,000 yuan per month; Zhipu's FDE lead pays up to 80,000 yuan per month.

In Silicon Valley, the median annual salary for FDE-related roles exceeds $250,000 (close to 1.7 million yuan), and companies like OpenAI, Anthropic, and Salesforce are all investing heavily.

FDE stands for Forward Deployed Engineer, referring to people who go deep into customer sites and are responsible for integrating AI technology into enterprise business processes. They not only write code and build systems, but also communicate with bosses and employees, turning a vague request like "we want to use AI" into a workflow that actually runs.

But there is also considerable controversy about this role. Some say it is the most valuable new profession of the AI era, while others believe it is nothing more than "high-end outsourcing."

-01- Founded recently, valued at 94.5 billion

FDE is not a role that just appeared.

As early as around 2010, the US data company Palantir established this role. After taking orders from the military and intelligence agencies, Palantir sent engineers directly to customer sites—even to the frontlines in Iraq and Afghanistan. When a soldier said "this road looks suspicious," the engineer would turn it into an annotation tool on the map on the spot, and useful features would later be incorporated back into the platform. To this day, about half of Palantir's employees are in FDE roles.

So why did FDE suddenly become hot in 2026?

Zhan Bingqiang, founder of AIGCLINK, believes "the hype element is actually bigger." He told Qibidao that the FDE group has existed for years, just under a different name—"high-end outsourcing." There is even a joking saying in the industry: FDEs call themselves "high-end outsourcing," while OPCs call themselves "freelancers."

But behind the hype, there is indeed real demand driving it.

The core reason is: the AI industry has moved from "selling models" to "selling results."

Over the past two years, buying models and AI agents became fashionable among enterprises, but many projects stalled at the demo stage and hit various walls once they entered production—legacy systems that couldn't connect, data scattered across WeChat and Excel, bosses who couldn't articulate specific requirements, and compliance and permission issues that couldn't be passed. Large models are standardized products, but enterprise data, processes, and systems vary widely. Someone has to be on-site to smooth out the "last mile" from demo to something actually usable.

According to a report by Dingjiao, an FDE named Lawted walked into the office of a freight forwarding company in Shenzhen and saw more than thirty employees all repeating the same task: constantly opening shipping PDFs sent by customers via WeChat and email, finding the port and shipping schedule, replying with confirmation, and then manually entering the data into the system. He single-handedly automated this entire process using AI tools.

That is the value of an FDE. In Zhan Bingqiang's words: "In the past, when people went to enterprises to implement AI, there was never a particularly clear identity. Now with FDE, it's like giving a clearer identity to the people who help enterprises with AI transformation."

Moves by overseas giants have further fueled the FDE craze.

In May 2026, OpenAI announced the establishment of The Deployment Company, valued at $14 billion (about 94.5 billion yuan), with initial funding of over $4 billion, while acquiring the UK consulting firm Tomoro (about 150 experienced FDEs) as the cornerstone of its deployment team.

Almost at the same time, Anthropic, together with Blackstone and Goldman Sachs, formed a $1.5 billion joint venture for enterprise AI services, focusing on implementation in heavily regulated industries such as finance and healthcare. Salesforce publicly recruited 1,000 FDEs to serve its Agentforce product line. AWS, the world's largest cloud computing company, is preparing to invest $1 billion to build an FDE team of several thousand people.

Compared with industries like finance and the internet, manufacturing—with its thin profits and difficult transformation—is precisely the "main battlefield" where AI implementation has the greatest potential for efficiency gains and where FDE value is most evident. Frontline FDEs' practices in industrial manufacturing, construction engineering, and cross-border e-commerce have already proven: once enterprises see efficiency leaps with their own eyes, their attitude shifts from wait-and-see to actively seeking more AI transformation opportunities.

Palantir AIP product interface, source: gateex.com

There is also policy-level push domestically. Beijing and other places have incorporated concepts like OPC, FDE, and Token into relevant government programs, causing an already warming topic to completely break into the mainstream. Li Yachong, founder of Putao Teng Technology, also observed: "Because AI genuinely has implementation needs, no matter what this role is called, the underlying logic is that enterprises will increase their use of AI. The name doesn't affect the demand itself."

With two forces combined—on one side, the real pain points of enterprise AI implementation, and on the other, the push from capital and policy—FDE has gone from a niche engineering role to a hot profession discussed by everyone.

-02- A new opportunity for IT outsourcing companies

Zhan Bingqiang has a metaphor: if we see the past information age as the agricultural age, then AI is like the industrial age. Today's FDEs are a group of "people driving harvesters to farm."

In the past, writing a piece of software required ten people working for months; now a few people with Claude, Codex, and AI agents might be able to get it done.

"In the past, on-site development meant writing code yourself; now AI writes the code or helps clients use AI." Li Yachong also believes the essence hasn't changed, only the tools have.

Indeed, when no one else has a harvester, the person who can operate one is naturally extremely valuable.

"But this window period is actually very short—maybe until around the first half of next year, and the opportunities will become narrower and narrower," Zhan Bingqiang said.

This also leaves a window open for traditional IT service providers. "FDE is actually the easiest direction for practitioners in various industries to transition into," Zhan Bingqiang said.

Traditional IT outsourcing personnel already know how to deal with clients, know how to deliver projects, and are familiar with enterprises' legacy systems. If they then add AI capabilities and transition into FDEs, that is far more realistic than having a pure model researcher relearn the realities of factories, finance, or ERP.

Anthropic is taking this path to the extreme.

Claude by Anthropic, source: winbuzzer.com

In June this year, it announced a partnership with the veteran IT service provider DXC. DXC plans to train tens of thousands of Claude-certified FDEs and then place them in banks, airlines, insurance companies, manufacturing enterprises, and government agencies.

DXC itself has 115,000 employees and has long operated critical systems for these clients. Before partnering with Anthropic, it had already involved Claude in developing its own AI-native operations platform, OASIS, with over 95% of the code generated by Claude.

When 95% of code can be generated by AI, it hasn't made some far-sighted IT service companies disappear; on the contrary, they have begun training FDEs at scale, because what clients buy has never been just code.

Zhan Bingqiang believes this is the most noteworthy change in the FDE business model.

Traditional outsourcing likes to "sell people"—ten people working for six months means charging for ten people for six months. In the future, FDEs may see two completely different pricing models.

The first is helping enterprises deploy an "AI employee."

The FDE enters the enterprise, sets up the AI employee, and teaches the client's own staff how to use it—equivalent to "helping them onto the horse, then giving them a ride."

The second is more radical: selling results directly.

"You don't need to worry about what the intermediate process looks like," Zhan Bingqiang said, giving an example.

Suppose that in the past, an enterprise needed to spend 30,000 yuan to complete a task; now a Forward Deployed Engineer with their own AI employees can finish it for 10,000 yuan, so the customer only buys the final result.

Whether it was done by three people in the background, or by one person plus ten agents, the customer doesn't care.

Next, some companies may simply sell a "completed task."

This is also why Zhan Bingqiang believes small and medium-sized IT companies must transform quickly. "In the future, every boss must themselves be a Forward Deployed Engineer."

If the boss of a software or AI company doesn't even know what AI can currently achieve for the business, and still operates under the old headcount- and project-based logic, he believes such companies will sooner or later lose their competitiveness.

Going one step further, enterprises should become AI Native companies; at the very least, managers themselves must have the ability to use AI to reorganize delivery.

-03- Business Skills Matter More Than Technical Skills

"I have one fairly certain conclusion," he said. "In the future, Forward Deployed Engineer will be a guaranteed role at AI companies. The internet era gave rise to roles like product manager, operations, and engineering; in the future's AI Native companies, the FDE will likewise be a role that is certain to emerge, just like those."

His logic is: no matter how agents develop, enterprises undergoing AI transformation need customization tailored to specific scenarios, which naturally requires a group of people doing this "dirty, tiring work" on the front lines. AI Native companies can focus on models and data, but reaching customers on the front line and completing the last mile of work still has to be handed to Forward Deployed Engineers.

But he believes Vibe Coding (AI-assisted programming) ultimately won't become a real barrier. "Gradually, everyone will probably be able to do it."

What truly sets people apart is two things: industry expertise and business skills.

Zhan Bingqiang listed a capability checklist for a qualified FDE: first, industry know-how; second, vibe coding ability; third, customer-facing delivery and implementation ability; and fourth, a certain level of business skill.

"In the end, when it comes down to it," Zhan Bingqiang said, "what an FDE really competes on is two abilities: one is expertise, that is, how deep your industry know-how truly is; the other is business skill."

OpenAI's recent hiring changes already show signs of this.

It is not just hiring general FDEs, but has begun to segment into fields like healthcare, life sciences, and government.

Healthcare FDEs need to understand hospitals, insurance, electronic medical records, and the regulatory environment; life sciences teams work directly with pharmaceutical companies, biotech firms, and CROs, participating in drug discovery and R&D processes; government roles even require the corresponding security clearance.

The FDE is becoming less and less like a technical job and more like an "industry expert + engineer."

This also means that not everyone flooding into the FDE role today will land a million-yuan annual salary.

The Chinese hiring market has already become stratified. A survey by Jiemian News found that some ordinary FDEs earn a monthly salary of about 20,000 to 30,000 yuan, while senior-level personnel mostly receive annual packages above 400,000 yuan; the high-paying positions offered by ByteDance, Ant Group, and Zhipu are concentrated more among senior talent.

On the surface, the rise of the FDE is the birth of a new profession, but what it reflects behind the scenes is actually an important pivot in the AI industry.

How far this role can go ultimately depends on how deep AI implementation goes. If AI truly permeates every industry and every enterprise the way electricity did, then demand for FDEs will only grow larger—just as the electrification era needed large numbers of electricians, and the information age needed large numbers of programmers.

In the future, the ones who truly remain will be those who understand both technology and business, and who can also connect well with customers.
