FDE Project Handover Checklist: When People Leave, the System Must Stay Alive

Original title: FDE 项目交接清单：人走了系统还得活着

FDE中国 · 2026-09-04 · 4 min read

Summary

This article from FDE China addresses a structural risk in FDE work: one engineer often holds the client relationship, system details, and undocumented know-how simultaneously, so departures can leave systems running with no one understanding why. It provides a 14-item handover checklist in four groups—documentation and knowledge, systems and operations, client relationships, and risk confirmation—covering key rotation, alert ownership, stakeholder maps, and three-party sign-off. The core argument: tacit knowledge and trust chains cannot be crammed in a final week, so reviews should happen mid-project and quarterly, and handover is complete only when the receiver independently performs end-to-end operations.

Key points

  • FDE departures risk losing tacit knowledge and client trust chains.
  • 14 checklist items span docs, ops, client relations, and risk.
  • Keys, alert routing, and data access must not stay on personal accounts.
  • Handover counts as done only via receiver's independent end-to-end execution.
  • Run checklist mid-project and quarterly instead of a last-week scramble.

The problem this piece addresses: an FDE single-handedly carries the client relationship, the system details, and a pile of undocumented tacit knowledge—and once that person leaves, the system keeps running but no one knows why it works. The most valuable takeaway is the set of checklist items on "making tacit knowledge explicit": why a prompt was written this way, what to check first when the system misbehaves in production, which phrasing the client is sensitive to—things that vanish completely without a handover and are far harder to reconstruct than code and documentation. The most common misuse is turning the handover into a document move: tossing over a link to the knowledge base and calling it done, only for the receiver to discover three months later that the content behind that link is long outdated. Compared with the in-site content on metrics and lifecycle, this piece's added value lies in "the complete boundary of handover"—a transfer checklist ranging from key ownership to the chain of trust. What needs layering on in the Chinese context: handovers are often rigidly tied to personnel rotation cycles, so we recommend making quarterly handover self-checks a routine mechanism to reduce reliance on individual conscientiousness. One thing to do after reading: assume you'll vanish from your current project tomorrow—write down the three things that would break first, and complete their documentation within this week. — FDEChina Editorial Team · Practitioner's Edition

FDE projects have a brutal characteristic: one engineer is often simultaneously the client's most trusted interlocutor, the person with the deepest grasp of system details, and the sole carrier of all tacit experience. Whether a handover is done well shows not in the offboarding paperwork but three months later—when the client reports an issue, they know whom to contact; when the system misbehaves, someone knows what to check first; and the next iteration doesn't repeat every pitfall originally stumbled into. This checklist has fourteen items in four groups, designed to be checked off, and applies to three scenarios: personnel rotation, project handover to the client's team, or delivery to a successor colleague. Handover preparation should proceed in sync with the project lifecycle, not as a last-minute scramble the week before departure; the eval sets and scripts among the transfer artifacts should build on the outputs of eval baseline building and the PoC acceptance checklist.

Group 1: Documentation and knowledge—so someone who never participated can take over

  • □ System design documentation is up to date: architecture diagrams, module responsibilities, and key design decisions with their rationale (why A was chosen over B) are complete, and the docs contain no references to dead links or deprecated systems.
  • □ All prompts, model parameters, and workflow configurations are under version control with change notes: every key change can be traced to "when it was changed, why, and how much the metrics moved afterward."
  • □ Tacit troubleshooting experience has been written down: what order to check things when production misbehaves, which error types actually share the same root cause, which alerts can be deferred—compiled into a one-page "incident response quick reference."
  • □ The known issues and technical debt list has been handed over: things currently known to be impossible, workarounds, and suggested future directions are listed item by item, so the receiver doesn't have to rediscover them by stepping into the same pits.

Group 2: Systems and operations—the machine level depends on no one

  • □ All keys, accounts, and certificates have completed ownership transfer or rotation: no production resource remains bound to a personal account, and the transfer record (what was transferred, when, and who confirmed it) is archived.
  • □ Deployment and rollback procedures can be executed independently by others: during the handover period, the receiver has fully run through one deployment and one rollback, and the operations docs have been revised based on that hands-on test.
  • □ On-call ownership for monitoring and alerting is clear: who receives alerts, what the escalation path is, and the response-time expectations for each alert have all been updated to the receiver's information and verified as actually delivered.
  • □ Data access permissions have been reconfirmed on a least-privilege basis: departing or rotating personnel no longer have access to production data, and the client has written acknowledgment and confirmation of the current permission state.

Group 3: Client and relationships—the chain of trust must be handed over too

  • □ The client relationship map has been handed over: each stakeholder's role, concerns, communication preferences, and historical sensitivities (which commitments were made under what circumstances) are captured on one page, and key stakeholders are introduced in person jointly by the outgoing and incoming parties.
  • □ Current external commitments and boundaries have been synced to the receiver: promised features and metrics, explicitly out-of-scope items, and in-flight change requests are covered one by one, so the newcomer doesn't inadvertently overturn old commitments.
  • □ The service cadence has a transition plan: the client's accustomed response rhythm (weekly meetings, daily reports, instant-message groups) is maintained in parallel by both parties for at least one full cycle during handover, with the newcomer leading and the outgoing person observing.

Group 4: Risks and confirmation—the handover must be verifiably complete

  • □ In-flight risk items are handed over one by one: each open risk (dependency delays, metrics near thresholds, client-side organizational changes) comes with current status, actions already taken, and recommended next steps, and the receiver confirms understanding of each.
  • □ Handover acceptance criteria have been executed: the receiver independently completes one end-to-end critical operation (reproducing and resolving a typical incident, or running an eval regression), with "having done it" rather than "having understood it" as the criterion for completion.
  • □ Handover completion is confirmed by three parties: the transferor, the receiver, and the client or project stakeholder confirm the checklist item by item; outstanding items are tagged with an owner and a deadline, and the record is archived for reference.

Usage notes

The most common failure mode for this checklist is opening it one week before departure. Tangible assets like docs and configs can be roughly patched together in a rushed week, but the chain of trust in Group 3 and the tacit experience in Group 1 simply cannot be written under time pressure—they need to exist in an "handover-ready at any moment" state all along. So the recommended rhythm is: go through the checklist once at the project's midpoint, log the gaps, and work them into daily routines; then run a routine self-check every quarter; and when the handover formally starts, go through the checklist a third time, handling only the deltas. If your team runs multiple projects in parallel, institutionalizing the quarterly self-check is far more reliable than relying on individual conscientiousness.

Another usage point: the criterion for handover completion is "the receiver can do it," not "the transferor has explained it." The end-to-end hands-on acceptance arranged in the checklist (the last item of Group 2 and the second item of Group 4) are the two items best at preventing problems before they occur—many handovers look perfect on paper, and the first production incident reveals that the receiver has never independently executed anything. For continued observation of post-handover outcomes, use the metrics from delivery quality measurement to check whether trends remain stable; for team-level knowledge accumulation and rotation mechanism design, see the FDE knowledge guide.
