Chapter 6 · The Temptation of Customization, the Ledger of Project-Based Work

Original title: 第 6 章 · 定制化的诱惑，项目制的账

FDE 的来路 · 2020 · 6 min read

Summary

This chapter examines the economic tension between customization and productization in enterprise software. Customer demands are often genuine, but accepting each one leads to unmanageable code branches and a drift toward labor outsourcing. The author distinguishes three sources of customization, argues that go-live, acceptance, adoption, and business outcomes are four unrelated events, and shows how per-person-day pricing rewards more customization. FDE without a product base and reuse discipline risks becoming pricier on-site development; the test is whether business outcomes, accumulated capabilities, and delivery speed actually improve.

Key points

  • Each customization decision can be individually right while the overall business fails.
  • Customization stems from regulation, competitive processes, or legacy baggage—hard to separate on-site.
  • Go-live, acceptance, adoption, and business results have no automatic connection.
  • Person-day pricing rewards longer projects and undermines productization incentives.
  • Palantir's FDE model works because engineers configure an existing platform, not blank projects.

Customers want tailored solutions; product companies want scale and reuse. This chapter examines the economic tension between customization, project work, and productization—a conflict that never quite goes away.

In enterprise software projects, there is one kind of request that is hardest to refuse:

"Other customers may never use this feature, but we genuinely need it."

And the customer is often not wrong.

Their organizational structure, approval rules, legacy systems, and customer contracts are all right there. If the standard product ignores them completely, the project can't land. And to win the contract, the vendor easily agrees: sure, we can customize.

The first customization leaves both sides satisfied.

Then the second customer has another set of "genuinely unique" rules. The third customer demands compatibility with an old version. As projects pile up, the product grows a thicket of branches no one dares to delete.

Sales says it's a key-account requirement; delivery says without the change, acceptance can't pass; engineering says if this continues, the version becomes unmaintainable. Everyone has a point, yet the company increasingly resembles a labor outsourcing firm.

This is the most troublesome thing about customization: each individual decision may well be right; strung together, the business may not add up.

Don't Rush to Blame Customization

Explaining the problem as a bad habit of Chinese customers doesn't hold up.

Enterprises buy software precisely to make their own business run. Tax rules, compliance, industry regulations, and organizational permissions differ, so of course products must adapt. Manufacturing plants operate differently, and banks and hotels don't share a single business process.

Customization has at least three sources.

The first is genuine differences in industry and regulation, where the product is unusable without it.

The second is when an enterprise has developed a distinctive, competitive process, and forcing standardization would erase that advantage.

The third is mere historical baggage. Whatever the old system did, the new one must preserve exactly; a certain executive likes looking at one particular table, so dozens of fields become "core requirements."

The first two may be worth doing; the third is the easiest way to drag the vendor into a pit.

The trouble is that on the project site, the three are hard to distinguish at once. The customer calls all of them "business needs," and the vendor may not have the courage to probe. After all, saying "we can do it" wins the contract, while saying "it's not worth doing" may mean instant elimination.

Go-Live, Acceptance, Adoption, and Results Are Four Different Things

Project work loves clear boundaries.

When requirements are confirmed, when the system goes live, which features count as delivered, what counts as a defect. The clearer the boundaries, the easier it is for both sides to sign contracts and make payments.

There's nothing wrong with this approach. Without milestones, complex projects spiral out of control.

The problem is that what's easy to accept isn't necessarily the outcome the customer most wants to buy.

A system going live only means it has entered the environment; passing acceptance only means the contracted features are largely complete; user adoption means the work is truly starting to migrate; business results occurring is what shows the investment may have been worth it.

There is no automatic link between these four things.

A feature can be fully launched while users keep using Excel; users can log in every day simply because the company mandates it; usage can be high while profits don't change, because the system merely makes an already-correct process faster.

If the vendor is only accountable for the first two, it will pour its energy into feature lists and acceptance documents. If the customer only gets serious at acceptance and no one keeps pushing after the project ends, they will eventually blame the failure on the system too.

Both sides fulfilled their contractual obligations, yet the enterprise never got the outcome it originally wanted.

What Behaviors Grow Out of Billing by Person-Day

The easiest project cost to calculate is people.

A few consultants, a few developers, so many months—multiply it out into a quote. Customers find it intuitive, and vendors find it easy to manage.

But pricing models quietly shape behavior.

If revenue comes mainly from how many person-days are invested, then the more requirements and the longer the timeline, the higher the short-term revenue. Turning a repetitive task into a product means sending fewer people next time—which may actually reduce the project's value.

The company talks about productization, but its sales targets and delivery revenue reward customization, so teams end up back on the old path.

This doesn't mean billing by person-day is necessarily bad. When requirements are uncertain and accountability is hard to quantify, person-days may be the most acceptable arrangement for both sides. The problem is that a company that only ever sells person-days will struggle to prove why it's worth more than another team that can work overtime just as hard.

Complexity becomes revenue—and it also becomes debt.

The extra money collected today may be the bill to pay tomorrow, in upgrades, operations, and personnel handovers.

FDEs Can Also Become More Expensive Outsourcing

The FDE model emphasizes staying close to customers and quickly solving real problems, which naturally wins goodwill.

But without a product foundation and a discipline of reuse, it is more dangerous than traditional project work.

A highly capable engineer arrives at the customer's site and takes on everything: if an interface doesn't work, they write one; if a process is missing, they build one; if the data is dirty, they clean it by hand. The customer is delighted, because someone finally got things done.

Only after this person leaves does the team discover that all the knowledge lives in their head, the code fits only this one customer, and the product's mainline has absorbed none of the on-site work.

That's not the FDE model succeeding—it's just a more expensive, harder-to-replace on-site developer.

Palantir's description of its own Forward Deployed Engineers contains a premise that is easy to overlook: the engineers are configuring and extending an existing software platform, not starting from a blank project at every customer. On-site work can produce scale not just because the engineers are good, but because there is something behind them that can be used again and again.

Without that premise, the stronger the front line, the emptier the back office may become.

Leave Something Behind from Every Project

Customization and productization are not an either-or choice.

A more realistic approach is to break down the differences in every project:

  • Data mappings and permission configurations that belong only to the customer stay on the customer side;
  • Objects, states, and exceptions that recur across an industry get distilled into industry models;
  • Integration methods for common systems become connectors;
  • Methods for verifying results become eval sets;
  • Go-live, rollback, approval, and monitoring become general-purpose control capabilities;
  • Pitfalls encountered in the field get written into the delivery handbook.

The first time may still be heavy. If the second time is just as heavy as the first, you have to ask what was actually accumulated.

Reuse doesn't mean copying customer A's code for customer B. What can be reused is the skeleton for solving the problem; customer differences enter through configuration, mapping, and limited extension.

This requires the front-line team to be willing to hand experience back to the product, and the product team to be willing to admit that problems discovered in the field matter more than the original roadmap.

Neither side finds it easy.

The Antidote to Project Work Is Just a New Disguise

FDE brings one genuinely promising change to project work: it shifts the focus from feature completion to customer outcomes.

If a team agrees on business metrics from the start, validates in real environments, follows through to adoption and results, and then distills field learnings back into the product, hands-on delivery can indeed compound.

But FDE also provides a very attractive new wrapper.

What used to be called on-site development is now called forward deployment; what used to be "whatever the customer asks for" is now "co-creating with the customer"; what used to be billed per head now costs more under new terminology.

The way to tell the difference lies not in job titles, but in three questions:

After this engagement ends, has the customer's business outcome changed? What verifiable capability did the team leave behind? Can the next similar customer be served faster and more reliably?

If none of these three questions can be answered, no matter how new the name, the business is still the old one.

Part One covered how enterprises can own their data; Part Two covered why data doesn't naturally turn into action. At this point, the problem is laid bare: what the last ten meters lacks is not just software features, nor just a clever model, but an accountability arrangement that can cross the boundaries of requirements, organization, and delivery.

Next, AI will make this both more possible and more dangerous.
