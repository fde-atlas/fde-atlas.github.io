FDE Customer Data Retention and Deletion Guide

Original title: FDE 客户数据保留与删除指南

FDE Times · 2026-11-14 · 5 min read

Summary

This guide explains how forward deployed engineers should handle copies of customer data across email, Slack, cloud storage and notebooks. Paid Slack plans retain data for the workspace's lifetime by default unless an admin sets a deletion period, content sent via Slack Connect by external members follows the other organization's policy, and Google Vault retains deleted emails under rules and holds. The author recommends agreeing on a single transfer channel in week one, requesting only necessary columns under data minimization, and maintaining a per-copy tracking table with location, purpose, deletion owner and deadline. It cites ICO guidance, GDPR Article 5(1)(e), and NIST SP 800-88 Rev. 2, and warns against changing Slack retention settings or promising deletions you cannot control.

Key points

  • Paid Slack retains data for the workspace's lifetime unless someone sets a deletion period.
  • Slack Connect content from external members is governed by their organization's retention policy.
  • Google Vault rules and holds keep deleted emails until rules expire.
  • Track each copy, not each file, and only promise deletions you control.
  • Cites ICO guidance, GDPR Article 5(1)(e), and NIST SP 800-88 Rev. 2.

Key Takeaways

  • Paid Slack retains data by default for as long as the workspace exists, so someone must specifically set deletion deadlines.
  • Messages posted by clients via Slack Connect follow the client organization's retention policy; even after a user deletes an email, Google Vault still retains it.
  • The ICO requires standard retention periods to be set in writing and reviewed regularly, with data that is no longer needed deleted or anonymized.

On day three of the project, the client emails a file

customer_export_full.csv

with a single line in the body: "So you can take a look at real data." That same afternoon, one of the client's engineers sends an updated version to the shared Slack Connect channel.

You download both files to your laptop, push them to a test bucket, and run a notebook. Every step has a justification; everyone wants to move fast.

Now count how many places this dataset exists: your inbox, the inboxes of everyone CC'd, the Slack channel, your Downloads folder, the bucket, and possibly the notebook's output. Three months later when the project ends, or when the client asks "Has our data been deleted?", can you answer?

For a Forward Deployed Engineer (FDE), this is not just the legal team's business. You received the files, and you know best where the copies are. The rules for receiving, storing, and deleting data should be set in the first week, while there are still few copies.

How many copies does one client file produce?

Treat every file a client sends as a set of copies, each with its own deletion rules. The difficulty is that the rules differ from place to place, and most are not in your hands.

Slack is the most obvious example. Slack's help documentation states: on paid plans, data is retained by default for as long as the workspace exists.

Retention settings apply to all messages, files, canvases, and lists, but only if an admin has specifically set a period. If no one sets one, that CSV will still be there long after the contract has expired.

And usually the person who controls this setting is not you. So the first question to ask is: who in the company manages Slack's retention settings?

Slack Connect is more complicated. According to Slack's documentation, your organization's retention settings only apply to content sent by members of your organization; content posted by external people in shared channels is retained or deleted according to the other organization's policy. So you have no authority to delete that updated version sent by the client's engineer.

Email is not simple either. With Google Vault, even if a user deletes an email or empties the trash, retention rules still preserve the data, and holds take precedence over rules.

Data is only purged from Google's systems after the last applicable rule expires. So don't tell the client "it's all been deleted" just because you clicked delete in your inbox.

Someone needs to own the retention period

The guidance from the UK data protection regulator, the Information Commissioner's Office (ICO), is blunt: personal data must not be kept longer than necessary. Organizations need a policy that sets standard retention periods where possible, reviews the data they hold regularly, and deletes or anonymizes what is no longer needed.

Article 5(1)(e) of the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) has a similar requirement: data that can identify an individual must not be kept longer than necessary for the purposes of processing. Many FDEs have a folder in their laptop

old_exports/

kept "just in case it might be useful someday," which is hard to reconcile with these principles.

The ICO's data minimization principle goes further: do not hold more data than is needed for the stated purpose. If the problem you are solving is inventory forecasting, there is no reason for a customer phone number column to appear in the file.

Example: building a tracking sheet for that day-three CSV

Back to

customer_export_full.csv

. Don't open it right away; first spend ten minutes registering it in a data tracking sheet. The tracking sheet can be as simple as a spreadsheet shared with the client, but each row corresponds to one copy, not one file.

The "who can delete" column is the most critical. It forces you to admit that the second row is outside your control. The right move is to tell the client that this copy is governed by their own retention policy.

Then send the client a short message setting the rules for next time. A template:

"To keep your data from being scattered across multiple places, please send it going forward via [the agreed single channel], and include only these columns: [list of column names]. We will register every file in a shared tracking sheet with a deletion date, and send confirmation once it is deleted."

This message shows the client you take this seriously, and it prevents the next round of email attachments.

Five things to do in the first week

  • Before the client sends the first file, agree on a single transfer channel and write it into the kickoff meeting notes.
  • In the spirit of data minimization, request only the columns needed for the project's purpose.
  • Register every copy in the tracking sheet as soon as it appears, including temporary copies on your laptop.
  • Ask your company's Slack and Workspace admins three questions: what the current retention period is, who can change it, and whether the team mailbox has a hold.
  • Schedule regular reviews, say every two weeks, to delete expired rows and record the deletion dates.

When the project ends and a hard drive or server needs to be retired, device-level erasure has its own dedicated guidance. NIST's SP 800-88 Rev. 2 recommends establishing a media sanitization program with appropriate technologies and controls. Forward that document to the IT team; don't invent your own erasure method.

Mistakes that cost an FDE the client's trust

The most dangerous mistakes often come from good intentions: an engineer wants to tidy things up and unilaterally shortens Slack's retention period.

Slack warns that data deleted under retention settings is permanently gone, and deletion jobs run once a day. Changing one number can wipe out conversation history that other teams still need.

The second mistake is forgetting about backups. The ICO notes that copies in backup systems may not be immediately overwritten; the key is to make that data unusable. So your deletion commitments to the client must account for these copies too.

The third mistake is promising to delete data the client sent via Slack Connect, which you cannot control at all. The fourth is reporting "deleted" while the email is still retained by a Vault rule or hold.

These mistakes share one thing in common: the commitment exceeded what you could control. The tracking sheet clearly shows which parts are yours to manage, and you only commit to those.

How to put this skill on your resume

If you've handled real customer data, don't just write "security-conscious." Write something specific, like "established a customer data intake and deletion process for a project, including a copy-tracking sheet and deletion confirmation at handoff," and employers will see how carefully you treat this kind of data.

When asked in an interview "a customer emails you a sensitive file—what do you do?", answer in order: copies, deletion permissions, retention period, confirmation. With this answer, the interviewer will see that you clearly know what you can and cannot promise the customer.

Don't wait for a security meeting to deal with this. Have the six-column tracking sheet ready before the first file lands in your inbox.
