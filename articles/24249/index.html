How to Choose Operations That Recover AI Investments Even If the AI Bubble Bursts | Formulas and Evaluation Sheet

Original title: AIバブル崩壊でもAI投資を回収できる業務の選び方｜計算式と判定シート

FDE総研 · 2026-11-06 · 14 min read

Summary

Written from a forward deployed engineer's practical perspective, this article offers a framework for choosing AI use cases that pay back even if the AI market crashes. It avoids predicting the bubble and instead defines four recovery conditions—task volume, measurable unit cost, error tolerance, and vendor/model replaceability—rating tasks on each. Payback period is calculated as initial cost divided by monthly net savings (gross savings minus operating costs), with 12 months as the author's benchmark. A hypothetical example (1,000 tasks/month, 10 minutes cut to 3 including review, ¥120k monthly operating cost) yields roughly 8.7 months. Sensitivity analysis shows that if API fees triple, projects stay profitable only when API costs stay below about a third of monthly savings. The article compares FDE-style, SIer, IT consulting, and in-house approaches on recovery speed and exit ease, and provides a monthly scorecard (net savings, remaining payback months, API dependency, fallback options) for continue/shrink/exit decisions, stressing that exit readiness—monthly contracts, data exportability, and preserved manual procedures—must be designed before deployment.

Key points

  • Four recovery conditions: volume, unit cost, error tolerance, and replaceability; meeting three or more aids recovery.
  • Payback formula: initial cost ÷ monthly net savings, with 12 months as the benchmark.
  • API dependency ratio determines resilience; above one-third of savings, a 3x price hike erases profits.
  • FDE-style work recovers fast and exits easily; SIers suit large, fixed-specification projects.
  • Exit design—monthly contracts, data export, manual fallback procedures—must precede deployment.

Every time AI-related stocks plunge or headlines about an "AI bubble collapse" appear, consultations increase about PoCs stalled in approval and generative AI contracts facing renewal decisions. This article, written from the practical perspective of an FDE (Forward Deployed Engineer: an engineer role that embeds in customer sites and handles everything from identifying business issues to implementation and adoption), compiles formulas and an evaluation sheet for choosing tasks whose investment can be recovered even if the market collapses. It does not predict whether the AI bubble will burst. It focuses solely on how to choose tasks that pay off either way.

What kinds of tasks recover AI investment even if the AI bubble bursts?

Tasks meeting three or more of FDE Soken's "four payback criteria" are likely to recover their investment even when the AI market wobbles.

What is thought to happen first in an AI bubble collapse is price revisions by model providers, shrinking free tiers, service discontinuation, and business withdrawal due to funding difficulties. Whatever change occurs, for tasks where "work previously done by humans is reliably decreasing every month," AI's benefit remains as cost reduction. Conversely, tasks that "might lead to future revenue" become impossible to justify the moment the market cools.

The four payback criteria: volume, unit cost, error tolerance, and replaceability

Whether a task can pay back its investment can be judged on three levels (○△×) across four axes: volume, unit cost, error tolerance, and replaceability.

The following table turns the criteria that practitioners at FDE Soken and VACAN use in initial project assessments into a form anyone can use.

CriterionQuestion to checkStandard for ○
① Volume (repeatability)How much work of the same type occurs each monthA certain amount or more every month, not disappearing seasonally
② Unit cost (measurability)Can work time and labor cost per task be measuredWork logs or measurements can show time down to the minute
③ Error toleranceWhen AI makes a mistake, can human review absorb itA reviewer can judge correctness within a few minutes
④ ReplaceabilityCan you switch to another model or vendorNot dependent on features specific to a particular model

Of the four criteria, ① and ② examine "whether savings can be calculated," while ③ and ④ examine "whether savings remain even if the market changes." Tasks meeting only the first two perform well in normal times but are vulnerable to price increases and service discontinuation.

Typical patterns of tasks that are hard to pay back

Tasks whose benefits cannot be measured in monetary terms, and tasks where AI errors reach customers or legal affairs directly, are the first to be halted in an AI bubble collapse.

Applying the four criteria to representative tasks yields the following breakdown. The judgments reflect general tendencies based on task nature; actual judgments vary with your company's actual work conditions.

Reading and entering documents and invoices○○○△Easy to pay back

What stands out in the field are tasks like "meeting minutes" and "internal search" that have high user satisfaction but haven't measured ② unit cost. When the market worsens, they can show they're being used but cannot show savings, and tend to be cut from budgets. Before implementation, we recommend measuring work time per task, even if only for one week.

How do you calculate the payback period for AI investment?

The payback period for AI investment is calculated as "initial cost ÷ (monthly savings − monthly operating costs)," and FDE Soken uses 12 months or less as a guideline.

Twelve months is FDE Soken's criterion, based on the fact that many companies review annual budgets on a yearly cycle. It is not an industry standard. Companies with different capital investment thinking should adjust, for example using 24 months as the benchmark.

Formula and definitions of each term

In calculating the payback period, the biggest key to avoiding optimistic estimates is to include "review work time" in monthly savings before subtracting.

  • Monthly savings = Monthly volume × (pre-implementation work time per task − post-implementation work time per task [including review time]) × hourly labor cost
  • Monthly operating costs = API/SaaS usage fees + maintenance and modification labor costs + monitoring and evaluation labor costs
  • Monthly net savings = Monthly savings − monthly operating costs
  • Payback period (months) = Initial cost ÷ monthly net savings

We often see cases where the post-implementation work time does not include the time for humans to read and correct AI output. The time to review generative AI output never becomes zero.

Sample calculation with hypothetical figures

If a task of 1,000 items per month at 10 minutes each drops to 3 minutes including review, and operating costs are 120,000 yen per month, the payback period is about 8.7 months.

How do you choose tasks that stay profitable even if API prices rise 2-3x?

Whether you remain profitable even if API prices triple depends on the share of API costs in your operating expenses. Check unit prices on the official pages of Anthropic and OpenAI.

As of November 2026, we have found no information that major providers have officially announced simultaneous price increases. On the other hand, there are reports of cases where new higher-tier models are offered at higher unit prices than previous models. The "2-3x" mentioned here is a hypothetical worst-case scenario that combines factors such as shrinking free tiers, the end of discounts, and forced migration to higher-tier models.

Calculate sensitivity by price multiplier

In the previous chapter's example calculation, even if API prices tripled, a net monthly savings of 170,000 yen would remain, and the payback period would only extend to about 11.8 months.

API price multiplierAPI cost (monthly)Monthly operating costMonthly net savingsPayback period
1x (current)30,000 yen120,000 yenApprox. 230,000 yenApprox. 8.7 months
2x60,000 yen150,000 yenApprox. 200,000 yenApprox. 10.0 months
3x90,000 yen180,000 yenApprox. 170,000 yenApprox. 11.8 months

This calculation remains profitable because API costs account for just under 10% of the monthly savings. Conversely, for tasks where API costs exceed one-third of the savings, a 3x price increase reduces net savings to nearly zero. FDE Soken calls "API cost ÷ monthly savings" the API dependency ratio and makes it a mandatory pre-adoption check to determine whether net savings remain positive under the 3x scenario.

Four safeguards for price-increase-resilient task design

By incorporating four elements into your design—a model-switching layer, combined use of smaller models, caching, and batch processing—you can reduce the impact of price increases.

  • Insert a model-switching layer: Do not call a specific vendor's API directly from the application; route through an internal common interface
  • Use smaller models appropriately: Route simple tasks like classification and extraction to low-cost models, and use higher-tier models only for difficult tasks
  • Use caching: For processes that repeatedly send the same instructions or reference documents, make use of the prompt caching offered by each provider
  • Switch to batch processing: For tasks that do not require immediate responses, consider the batch pricing tiers listed on the official pricing pages

Since pricing tables are subject to revision, always record the latest unit prices from official pages and the date of verification in your calculations. Specific cost optimization measures are covered in detail in LLM Cost Optimization in Practice.

Risks beyond pricing: service discontinuation and terms-of-service changes

During an AI bubble collapse, model discontinuation and terms-of-service changes can cause business stoppages even more than price increases.

Old models are discontinued periodically regardless of AI market conditions, but if the market deteriorates, discontinuation may be accelerated by providers' business restructuring. Having evaluation test data on hand and a setup that can verify within a few days whether accuracy drops when switching to another model serves as practical insurance. For how to build evaluations, see How to Conduct LLM Evaluations.

Do the tasks you should choose change based on company size, organizational structure, and the person in charge's experience?

Under FDE Soken's decision criteria, the tasks you should choose vary by company size and internal structure, and for companies without a dedicated person, it is safest to start with one department and one task.

By company size: guidelines for the first task to choose

The smaller the company, the more you should choose tasks that require little maintenance and can be completed with SaaS to keep exit costs low.

Should AI investment be pursued via FDE, SIer, IT consulting, or in-house development?

In FDE Soken's comparison, ease of exit and speed to payback are the dividing lines: the FDE model suits short-term payback, while SIers suit large-scale development with fixed specifications.

FDE is known as a role that Palantir popularized by stationing engineers at customers' sites. Rather than comparing which structure is superior, we compare them by how easily they allow "stopping or scaling down" decisions in the event of an AI bubble collapse.

Comparison table of the four approaches

If you assume an AI bubble collapse, the basic rule is to choose an approach that is less likely to become fixed costs and that leaves both business understanding and code in-house.

Companies and people the FDE model does not suit

The FDE model does not suit companies whose business owners cannot spare time for validation, or people who want to handle only design without entering the field.

  • Unsuitable companies: business owners cannot participate in 1-2 hours of validation per week / measurement of actual work time is refused / approval rules require fully finalized specifications before ordering
  • Unsuitable people: find it painful to talk directly with field staff to draw out business knowledge / prefer to build a highly polished design in one pass rather than rebuilding in short cycles / do not want the responsibility of explaining with numbers, such as calculating savings

The FDE model works best when the back-and-forth with the field is fast. When this premise does not hold, deciding priorities with an IT consultant and then handing off to an SIer or in-house development may result in faster payback. How to decide whether to continue or withdraw AI investment during a collapse?

Whether to continue or withdraw AI investment is judged by monthly net savings and remaining payback months; the FDE Research Institute uses three consecutive months of losses as a guideline for considering scale-down.

The mere appearance of headlines about an "AI bubble collapse" is not a reason to withdraw. The only basis for judgment is the figure of how much your own operations are saving each month.

Decision sheet for continue, scale down, or withdraw

The decision sheet can be used regardless of market conditions as long as you record four metrics monthly: net savings, remaining payback months, API dependency rate, and fallback options.

MetricContinueConsider scaling downConsider withdrawing
Monthly net savingsMaintaining a positive figureNegative for 3 consecutive monthsNegative for 6 months despite improvement measures
Remaining payback monthsWithin the original planMore than 6 months behind planNo prospect of payback
API dependency rate (API cost ÷ monthly savings)Net savings remain positive even in a 3x scenarioNet savings nearly zero in a 2x scenarioNet savings negative at current pricing
Fallback optionsCan switch to another model within daysSwitching requires weeks of reworkNo alternative available

These metric thresholds are the FDE Research Institute's judgment criteria; adjust them according to the importance of the work. Scaling down does not mean "stopping"; it includes adjustments such as focusing on higher-volume work or reducing the scope of top-tier model usage.

Design ease of withdrawal before implementation

Whether you can make a sound withdrawal decision is determined by whether you secured three things before implementation: contract terms, data, and manual procedures.

  • Contract terms: before annual contracts, set up a period where you can cancel monthly for validation
  • Data export: store input data, output logs, and evaluation data in a form that can be exported to your own environment
  • Manual procedures: do not discard the legacy procedure documents so work can run by hand on the day AI stops

A common situation in practice is knowing a withdrawal is warranted but being unable to stop because the manual procedures have been lost. To ensure business continuity even if AI stops, it is reliable to test once during normal times by shutting down AI for just half a day. Continuing to use this decision sheet serves as a means of fulfilling accountability for the investment, whether or not an AI bubble collapse occurs.
