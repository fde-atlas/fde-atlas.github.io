AI Fundamentals · 28 min read

Summary

Drawing on a Tencent Research Institute report, Fan Bing's open-source FDE book, and a first-hand account by Jove Zhong (Head of FDE at Cresta), this article argues that as models commoditize, the scarce resource is people who can push models into production. It frames the gap via an 80/95/99 rule: prototypes are easy, but the 80%→99% stretch toward enterprise-grade reliability is where Forward Deployed Engineers earn their pay. The piece distinguishes FDEs from pre-sales, onsite outsourcing, and consulting, explains Echo/Delta capability split, dual distillation, and ontology layers, shows how AI lowered three cost barriers, and offers an L0–L4 maturity framework plus China–US market comparisons, concluding that China's binding constraint is economics, not technology.

Key points

  • MIT's GenAI Divide found ~95% of enterprise GenAI spend produced no measurable financial value; the gap lies between demo and production.
  • FDEs embed at customer sites, write production code, and own outcomes—distinct from pre-sales, onsite outsourcing, and consulting.
  • AI lowered three cost barriers—knowledge distillation, custom development, and hybrid talent supply—reviving the FDE model in 2024–2026.
  • Scaling requires ontology layers plus Skills, connectors, and industry knowledge bases; the first customer costs roughly 3x a normal project.
  • China has far fewer FDE postings and 30–50% lower pay; the core challenge is unit economics, not technology.

I. Getting from 0 to 80% Takes Just One Engineer Who Can Write Prompts

In 2025, MIT's NANDA lab released "The GenAI Divide." The report's conclusion has been cited countless times: of the $30-40 billion that enterprises worldwide invested in generative AI over the past three years, 95% failed to produce value that could be written into financial statements.

Before citing this number, its methodology needs to be explained. After the report's release, criticism focused on three points: it defined "failure" as having no measurable financial-statement impact within six months, a standard under which almost all early investments in the internet and cloud computing would count as failures; it measured value only by profit, cost, and revenue, ignoring leading indicators such as process speedups and employee adoption; and its sample was dominated by large enterprises, with failure data coming only from the project sponsors' side. There was also a named conflict of interest: the NANDA lab itself researches the agentic internet, and the report's proposed solution happens to point in its own direction.

These criticisms are all valid. But the directional judgment has been repeatedly corroborated by independent sources: Stanford's same-year aggregate data shows that 88% of organizations are using AI, while only a single-digit percentage of agentic applications have actually made it into production; McKinsey adds that only 6% of enterprises can claim AI contributes more than 5% to their profits.

The Tencent Research Institute's report reframes this phenomenon with a sharper yardstick, called the 80/95/99 rule:

RangeWhat it takes to cross it
0 → 80%General-purpose large models + simple prompts. An experienced engineer can build a "pretty good" prototype in a day
80 → 95%The model makes errors in specific scenarios, misunderstands industry terminology, and can't handle edge cases; this requires extensive prompt optimization, rule supplements, data annotation, and test set construction
95 → 99%The last mile of enterprise-grade scenarios. A single wrong customer-service answer can become a brand incident, and errors in finance, healthcare, or government can trigger compliance risks

The report's exact words: FDEs don't do the work of going from 0 to 80%; they do the work of going from 80% to 99%. Customers pay for FDEs not because they can't see a demo, but because there is a long gulf between a demo and production.

The most counterintuitive part of that yardstick is the 99% ceiling. Why 99% and not 100%? Because LLMs are fundamentally probabilistic systems with an open output space, invisible edge cases will always exist. The marginal cost of pushing from 99% to 99.9% grows exponentially while marginal returns diminish. The report frames this as the optimal solution in engineering economics rather than a technical compromise: AI covers the scalable 99%, while humans guard the 1% that cannot be automated.

Across the three stages of AI adoption, FDEs engage differently: in the individual productivity stage, they run "operational bootcamps"; in the organizational transformation stage, they need cross-industry organizational change experience; in the business restructuring stage, it's about system building, distilling tacit industry knowledge into ontologies.

An often-overlooked prerequisite: AI projects must first produce a runnable demo

In traditional IT projects, solution documents, prototypes, and reference cases are enough to build customer confidence. AI projects are different. Model performance, boundary conditions, and business benefits all carry uncertainty, and customers can hardly judge from a PPT alone whether a system will run stably under their own data, processes, and organizational constraints.

The report lays out this chain of mechanisms:

需求异质化与效果不确定
        ↓
必须做可运行 Demo 验证
        ↓
售前交付能力前置
        ↓
AI 降低原型与定制成本
        ↓
FDE 成为更可行的组织形态

The point of a demo is not to replace the production system, but to surface risks early: whether the model understands the business language, whether the data is usable, whether the interfaces connect, whether users are willing to use it, and whether there's room to improve business metrics.

II. What an FDE Is, and What It Is Not

A one-sentence definition

Fan Bing's book adopts Bob McGrew's definition. McGrew was an engineer at PayPal in his early years, later an early executive at Palantir, and then OpenAI's Chief Research Officer — both ChatGPT and GPT-4 came out of the teams he led:

A Forward Deployed Engineer is an engineer stationed at the customer's site, filling the gap between "what the product can do" and "what the customer needs."

Broken down, each of the three words carries a qualifier. "Stationed at the site" means the work context is embedded with the customer: joining the customer's group chats, reading the customer's data, attending the customer's meetings, and getting to know the person who "knows why the process is the way it is" — not necessarily sitting in the customer's office every day. "Gap" is the reason this role exists: where the product works out of the box, you're not needed; the deeper the gap, the more you're needed. "Engineer" is the most crucial qualifier: you write production code, not reports. When Palantir coined the term, it deliberately kept the words "software engineer" to emphasize to the world that this is not a consulting role.

As for "Forward Deployed," it's a military term for troops deployed on the front line: putting the most combat-capable people closest to the problem.

What it is not

Not sales engineering. A Sales Engineer's work ends before the contract is signed; the goal is winning the deal, and the deliverable is slides. An FDE's work enters deep water only after the contract is signed; the goal is winning the outcome, and the deliverable is a system running in production.

Not on-site outsourcing. This distinction matters especially for Chinese readers — "engineers on-site" has a long and inglorious history in China. Qimeng Technology, one of the first domestic service providers to fly the FDE flag, draws the line in three sentences: on-site work is billed by the hour, while FDEs deliver by phase and are accepted by results; on-site work is written from scratch, while FDEs do engineering on top of a product foundation; on-site engagements drag on longer and longer, and when the people leave, the system stops — FDEs leave when the work is done, leaving the capability in the system and the customer's team.

The Tencent Research Institute's report offers the same judgment from another angle: the essential difference between FDEs and traditional on-site staffing is whether the project leaves behind reusable assets:

只留给客户一个系统          → 外包
带回经验却无法复用          → 项目制交付
把现场经验转化为
Skill / 模板 / 测试集 / 产品能力 → FDE
沉淀后显著降低下一个客户成本  → 可规模化 FDE

Not a consultant. Consultants deliver recommendations per project and are not responsible for execution; FDEs are responsible for the system's final operation, with the endpoint being "the customer's team can use it independently." Anthropic's collaboration with the fintech company FIS is a specimen case: engineers embedded with FIS to co-build an anti-money-laundering agent, compressing investigations from hours to minutes, but the stated goal of the collaboration was not to hand over a system — it was "to transfer knowledge so that FIS can build agents on its own in the future." A consultant's business is built on the customer's continued need; the standard for whether an FDE has succeeded is exactly the reverse: only when the customer no longer needs you one day does it mean you've succeeded.

Not a traditional product engineer. Product engineers face abstract users (personas, funnels, DAU); FDEs face concrete customers (a bank's risk control department, a farm in Iowa, a patrol unit on the outskirts of Baghdad). Palantir's official blog post "Dev versus Delta" gave the official division between these two roles: platform engineers are responsible for "one capability, serving many customers," while Forward Deployed Engineers (internal codename "Delta") are responsible for "one customer, mobilizing many capabilities." Nabil Kureishi, who spent nearly eight years doing forward deployed engineering at Palantir, distilled this discipline into a blunt phrase: "Screw generalizability" — first save the customer in front of you; whether it can be reused for the next one is the platform team's problem.

How practitioners draw this line

The "nots" above are the outside view. Jove Zhong, head of FDE at the North American AI agent company Cresta, offers a more engineering-oriented version, in three points:

  • FDEs write production code directly. SEs focus on technical communication and prototype demos in pre-sales scenarios, SAs focus on architecture design and technology selection; FDEs, however, must both design and implement, integrate solutions into the customer's real systems, and take responsibility for launch quality.
  • FDEs own results all the way to the "last mile." Whether the launch meets its targets, whether it scales stably under SLOs, and how to diagnose and roll back when things break are all part of the daily job.
  • FDEs are hybrid players. They can discuss business loops with customer executives as well as debug issues with the customer's engineers over logs; they handle requirements clarification and scope control, and also personally write code on the critical path.

His response to "is FDE just old wine in a new bottle" is "no AI, no FDE"—he has interviewed several Palantir FDEs, and if they weren't working on AI projects, he would rather call them "delivery engineers." By this definition, today's FDE is more accurately called an AI delivery engineer.

When asked "how is this different from on-site development at domestic SaaS companies," his answer was: there are many non-standard things in AI that need seasoned experts to polish, and these things need to be turned back into the product.

Where did this playbook come from

In 2003, Peter Thiel and several Stanford-educated young people founded a company named after The Lord of the Rings: Palantir, the seeing-stone. The premise of what they set out to do could make any product manager break down on the spot. McGrugh later recounted on YC's podcast:

"When we started the company, our goal was to build software for the intelligence community—to put it bluntly, software for spies. One challenge of building software for spies is: I don't know any spies, and you probably don't either. Even if you happen to find a spy and ask them 'how do you actually do your work day to day,' they usually won't tell you."

No user interviews, no requirements documents, no usability testing. Co-founder Stephen Cohen's approach was charmingly crude: build a demo prototype and show it to them; they would bluntly say "this is terrible, it has nothing to do with what we do," he would ask "then how do you wish it were different," pull out a notebook and write down every point, go back and revise, then deliver it again.

Hidden in this loop were two intuitions later proven priceless: customers in complex domains don't know what they want until they see something usable; and the fastest way to learn what customers want is to put the people who build things next to the people who use them.

It was employee number 13, Shyam Sankar, who upgraded this intuition into company strategy. As Palantir moved from its first customer to its second and third, the team discovered a counterintuitive fact: what each customer wanted differed in subtle but critical ways. The standard solution is to extract commonalities, build a general-purpose product, and say no to differences. But Palantir's customers were the CIA, the FBI, and the US military on the battlefield—saying no meant being out.

Sankar did the opposite: build a flexibly customizable platform, station engineers on-site at customers, and finish the last mile. His most critical move was changing the accounting of this activity: in the software industry's ledger, "customization for a single customer" is called services, the enemy of profit margins; he flipped it around: on-site customization should be booked as product discovery—every pitfall engineers stepped into on-site was a signpost for the platform's next evolution.

Around 2007, the biggest source of US military casualties in Iraq was roadside bombs. Sankar took a small team and a "still very rough" product into a SCIF for two weeks of joint work. A SCIF is a physically isolated classified space where even speakerphones are banned. He strapped a phone to his head with an elastic band to free his hands for coding, one ear listening to analysts' feedback, the other listening to Silicon Valley headquarters. He worked nineteen hours a day for two weeks. At the end, the analysts said "this thing is useful," but Sankar himself was worn out and called CEO Karp saying "this isn't sustainable, we're done for." Karp's answer later became company culture: turn this kind of "unsustainability" into an institution.

III. Why now: three cost barriers lowered by AI

The FDE model is not a new concept. Palantir has practiced it since 2003, but over the past decade only a handful of companies have truly made it work. The core judgment of the Tencent Research Institute report is: FDEs resurging in 2024–2026 is not because enterprises suddenly need more on-site staff, but because AI has simultaneously changed three cost structures of this model.

Cost itemPastWhat AI changed
Industry knowledge distillation costAfter on-site teams discovered real pain points, they could only write requirements documents and queue for product teams to evaluate, design, develop, and launchFrontline FDEs can write scripts, package tools, build prototypes, and generate test cases directly on-site, moving forward work that previously had to wait for the back office
Custom development cost"Understand the business, write requirements, schedule development, test and launch"Becomes "understand the business, abstract semantics, generate prototypes, validate on-site." Small-scale scenario development, rapid prototyping, and customer-side process adaptation can be done by smaller teams
Hybrid talent supply costPeople who understand technology don't understand the business, people who understand the business don't understand codeAI tools compensate for some engineering, documentation, and product capabilities, enabling more frontline staff to work across business and technical boundaries

The decline in the first two is easy to understand. The third deserves elaboration: lowered engineering execution barriers mean Delta-layer costs are compressed, but Echo-layer capabilities—judging scenario value, understanding industry logic, and driving organizational adoption—do not become easier to acquire as a result.

Capabilities split into two types: Echo and Delta

The report splits this cross-boundary capability into two types for analysis. Echo handles "what to do," Delta handles "how to build it."

Echo's core is understanding the customer's business: where the money comes from, where processes get stuck, and which link AI can leverage to create value. It cannot just listen to what the customer says it wants; it must also judge whether what the customer says is actually the problem: often the customer asks for a feature when what's really needed is restructuring a process; the customer wants to buy a set of tools when what's really missing is an organizational mechanism for putting them to use.

Delta's core is rapidly building prototypes, integrating systems, tuning models, and iterating continuously in real environments. AI coding has lowered Delta's technical barrier but hasn't eliminated the need for engineering judgment: rough prototypes are fine, but there must be a quality baseline; rapid iteration is fine, but security, permissions, and auditing cannot be ignored.

In domestic organizations, these two are often scattered across different teams: industry architects understand customers but can't necessarily deliver, delivery engineers can implement but can't necessarily define problems, and product teams understand the platform but are too far from the customer's site. The value of the FDE model is precisely reconnecting these capabilities through organizational mechanisms. In actual operations, Echo and Delta are not necessarily two positions but two roles. In early teams, one person may hold both capabilities, and as project scale grows, they naturally diverge.

Bidirectional distillation: how delivery becomes learning

If Echo and Delta explain what capabilities FDEs need, bidirectional distillation explains how these capabilities become organizational assets.

The first layer is distillation for the customer: converting business experience, process rules, and tacit knowledge scattered in customer employees' heads into knowledge bases, rule systems, workflows, SOPs, and application capabilities that AI can invoke. The customer thereby gains more stable, more replicable business execution capability.

The second layer is distillation for the vendor itself: consolidating industry knowledge, system interfaces, testing methods, scenario templates, and failure cases gained from projects into product capabilities, industry templates, internal tools, and platform components. The vendor thereby improves delivery efficiency and service depth for similar scenarios.

The common carrier for both layers of distillation is the Ontology Layer. Ontology theory is not complicated: entities, relationships, and attributes—essentially a structured business knowledge graph. Within a customer's systems, the same business object may have different field names, different codes, and different process representations. If AI faces these messy systems directly, it is hard for it to reliably understand business meaning. What the ontology layer does is translate these system differences into a unified business language: this is a customer, this is an order, this is an approval, this is a risk event; what relationships exist between them, what actions they can trigger, and under what conditions human intervention is needed.

An ontology summarized from one customer can potentially be replicated to another customer in the same industry. It is not about using customer data to train models, but about distilling industry knowledge into reusable abstractions. These concepts belong to no single enterprise, but to the shared business domain.

With the two layers of distillation stacked together, the old model—heavy, expensive, and heavily dependent on elite teams—can finally become a path adoptable by more industries.

An easily underestimated engineering constraint

The report specifically warns about one thing: even with AI-assisted ontology extraction (reverse engineering from code, automatic generation from design documents), the cost of human confirmation remains high. AI-generated results may be 98% correct, but to find that 2% of errors, a human must read through 100%.

We must not create the illusion for managers that "AI is here, so ontology modeling is now cheap." Technical means can accelerate initial extraction, but final confirmation and governance still require domain experts' investment. This is also why the Echo team's cost is unavoidable.

There is also a push from the demand side: no one can afford to wait

The cost structure explains "why this has become feasible," but not "why it is so urgent right now." Jove Zhong gave a more blunt answer from both the buyer and vendor sides: no one can afford to wait.

We cannot wait for AI platforms to become so complete that even novices can develop production-grade agents, cannot wait for customers to spend three months in training before barely learning to use them, cannot wait for the next generation of models, and certainly cannot wait for competitors to seize all the high ground. His judgment on this window period is quite restrained—"in three to five years, when AI models are extremely intelligent and can be used mindlessly, FDEs should move on to other roles; maybe it won't take three to five years, three to five months is not impossible." But precisely because no one knows when that day will come, we must hire aggressively right now: "I review hundreds of resumes a week."

Incidentally, his description of the current stage is even more biting—and more vivid—than "proof-of-concept purgatory": AI agents are like teenage sex—everyone wants it, everyone claims they've done it, but hardly anyone actually knows how.

IV. What a Delivery Looks Like

Six Stages

Fan Bing's book is organized around the complete journey of a real delivery, with six interlocking stages:

StageCore Question
Finding the right problemMany AI projects terminate halfway—Silicon Valley calls this the "proof-of-concept purgatory." Use MVD (Minimum Viable Deployment) to find the fit between business pain points and solutions
Winning the customerPrinciples for screening lighthouse customers; beware of "requirement locusts" who only take solutions without implementing them; passing customer due diligence, procurement, legal, and security reviews
Activating deploymentBreaking the "day-one curse" of enterprise deployment; driving hot-fix rapid iteration in the customer's real environment; defusing organizational resistance to change
Securing renewalOptimizing system performance and stability; accepting lossy service—don't obsess over perfect technical metrics, prioritize business availability; building system health scores and churn early warnings
Growing revenueStarting with free validation to lead into paid engagements; billing based on actual business outcomes; going deep in one department first, then expanding to multiple departments
Scaling replicationThe three-tier leverage of knowledge replication, component replication, and product replication; reviewing failed projects; building cross-customer propagation loops

Among these, the "securing renewal" stage deserves a pause, especially for engineers. Accepting lossy service (not obsessing over perfect technical metrics, prioritizing business availability) is counterintuitive in engineering culture, but it directly determines whether the project survives to renewal.

Where Does a Frontline FDE's Time Go

The Perspective AI survey cited in the report gives a set of numbers: 47% on customer engagement (discovery interviews, on-site deployment, design reviews), 31% on writing or reviewing code, and 22% on internal coordination and research synthesis; 71% of FDEs travel at least once a month, and 64% primarily work in the mode of "deploying existing products into new customer environments with extensive customization."

Jove Zhong offered a finer breakdown from the Cresta frontline, with two cuts:

CutAllocation
By counterpartAbout 1/3 of the time aligning vision goals and specific processes with customers; 1/3 building the AI solution; 1/3 coordinating feedback with internal teams
By deliverableAbout 1/3 writing code (mainly Python, also JS/Go/Java); 1/3 writing prompts; 1/3 writing tests and running evals

The two sets of numbers corroborate the same thing: writing code is only one third. For engineers accustomed to measuring themselves by code output, this is the expectation to build before switching roles.

He used three words to describe people suited for this role: fast, precise, relentless.

  • Fast means speed on multiple levels: quickly grasping the customer's pain points and constraints, delivering quickly, learning new things quickly. And not pursuing the most elegant solution—"a workable Python solution built in 2 days is often more practical than a polished Rust solution built in 20 days"
  • Precise means focusing on the big picture, letting go of the small, dynamically adjusting priorities, while finding the right internal resources for support
  • Relentless means admitting this is not an easy job: changing requirements and personnel turnover are the norm; travel when travel is needed, work overtime when overtime is needed

There is also one hard requirement: about one third of the time on the road. His exact words were "if it's not too far, talking with the customer onsite in the early project phase is more efficient and builds better rapport, with lots of online meetings afterward"—but he deliberately added, "after all, I'm not a resident engineer."

Business Understanding Comes Before Writing Prompts

The Tencent Research Institute report distills a four-step playbook from frontline experience in China, and the first step is worth noting: after the FDE team arrives at the customer's site, they should not immediately write prompts or build agents, but should first figure out where the customer's money comes from, where the business is stuck, and which link AI can leverage to create value. This stage requires industry business experts to participate, sorting out SOPs, knowledge bases, business chains, and key metrics.

The following three steps are: business experts and FDEs together convert scenarios into agents, workflows, or application prototypes, so technical staff no longer carry the burden alone; use tools to ensure quality, generating test sets from real chat logs, historical tickets, and business data for automated evals and A/B testing; validate value with business outcomes, comparing the AI group and the human group link by link (using metrics such as reach rate, response rate, conversion rate, and per-capita efficiency) to convince the customer to keep investing.

The report summarizes this principle in one sentence: deliver real outcomes, don't sell software. Customers don't pay for "using AI," they pay for the business actually getting better.

Land and Expand

In a public YC interview, Bob McGrew made a judgment widely overlooked by teams in China: "Either be among the top five priorities of the executive team, or fail." If the scenario an FDE targets is not on the CEO's or business owner's priority list, IT or process inertia will prevail, and the project will ultimately be shelved or downgraded.

The business model behind the FDE model is not the traditional "one-off large contract delivery" but Land and Expand. The initial project can be small, and may not even be profitable in the short term: first use a small-scale pilot to prove value and build trust within the customer's organization; once the customer sees results, expand from one department to multiple departments and from one scenario to multiple scenarios, with contract value and renewal rates steadily rising. The FDE's task is to ensure the first batch of users actually use the system, generating business data and internal word-of-mouth, rather than ending after a demo for leadership.

L0–L4: A Maturity Assessment Framework

Many AI projects don't die because they "can't be built," but because they remain stuck at the Demo stage and never reach production. The report provides a maturity framework to help determine which stage you're at and what to do next:

StageMarkerCore TaskTypical Duration
L0 Scenario IdentificationCustomer articulates pain points, FDE assesses AI feasibilityBusiness diagnosis, value prioritization, feasibility assessment1–2 weeks
L1 Prototype ValidationA runnable Demo with the core path working end to endRapid prototyping, customer sees results, feedback collection2–4 weeks
L2 Pilot OperationSmall-scale use in real businessConnecting real data, handling exceptions, building test sets1–3 months
L3 Production DeploymentFormally embedded in business processes with an SLAPermissions, auditing, monitoring, rollback, training1–2 months
L4 Scale ExpansionReplication across departments, scenarios, and customersTemplating, Skill reuse, partner enablementOngoing

Most projects die between L1 and L2. The cause is usually not technical but three types of blockers: pilot users are not tied to performance metrics, so "using it or not doesn't matter" and usage declines; data quality is glossed over at the Demo stage, and problems erupt all at once in the real environment; organizational approval processes are not cleared in advance, and permissions and compliance become the last-mile roadblock. The report defines FDE professionalism as: the ability to identify these blockers in advance and design countermeasures, rather than firefighting after problems appear.

V. The Hurdle of Scaling

A Cost Curve

The most common objection to the FDE model is: isn't this just high-end custom outsourcing? The report answers this objection with a cost comparison: both appear to work on-site at the customer, but their organizational logic is completely different: the former only cares whether this contract passes acceptance, while the latter also cares whether this contract can make the next one cheaper.

Model1st Customer10th CustomerOrganizational Outcome
Project-based delivery1×8–9×More people, revenue and costs rise together
Ontology-based delivery3×4–4.5×Heavier upfront, but reuse increases significantly later

Ontology-based delivery requires investing more effort in abstraction and validation on the first customer; the first contract costs roughly three times a typical project. But as long as the abstraction is correct, similar customers only require mapping and adaptation, and marginal costs decline.

Behind this is the combination of delayed returns and economies of scale. Delayed returns mean the investment in building the ontology is not recovered within the current project: the first project, or even the first few, will cost more than pure project-based delivery, because in addition to delivering the customer's system, knowledge abstraction, structuring, and validation must also be completed. Economies of scale mean that if the vertical has enough similar customers, the ontology's value is released at scale: after completing one benchmark enterprise, dozens or even hundreds of similar enterprises can reuse the same ontology, and delivery costs drop significantly. In a typical vertical, after seven or eight customers, the ontology basically stabilizes, and subsequent updates become significantly smaller.

The report therefore offers this judgment: the FDE model is essentially an invest-first, recover-later business logic, and it is not suitable for all scenarios. If a field has only one or two customers, the cost-effectiveness of building an ontology is low; if the field is large enough with enough customers, the upfront investment yields long-term compounding returns. This also explains why platform companies are better suited to undertake ontology building: they have the strategic patience of deep industry engagement, and enough customers to amortize the upfront costs.

Three Levels of Replication Leverage

To avoid turning FDE into a pile of custom projects, you must have the ability to replicate at scale. The book offers three levels of leverage: knowledge replication, component replication, and product replication; the report describes current forms of accumulation more concretely.

In industry practice, the vehicle for knowledge accumulation is evolving rapidly. Early knowledge graphs and ontologies were highly structured, but extremely costly to build and maintain; later workflow orchestration lowered some barriers and could solve many process-type problems, but still required strong technical capability; Multi-Agent further enhanced collaboration on complex tasks, but system design and debugging complexity remained high.

What deserves more attention now is the combination of Skills and connectors, and these three forms of accumulation correspond to different levels:

Asset TypeProblem SolvedMain Risks
SkillSolidifying task experience into executable capabilityLong-term project experience and scenario abstraction
ConnectorConnecting AI into enterprises' real systemsTool ecosystem, permissions, security, and stability
Industry knowledge baseProviding professional content and business contextData sources, industry know-how, and continuous updates

The report's exact words: the ontology layer solves "how business objects are understood," Skills solve "how capabilities are reused," and connectors solve "how systems are operated," corresponding respectively to knowledge accumulation, capability accumulation, and system-connection accumulation. Only the combination of all three constitutes a reusable industry solution.

There is an easily overlooked security reminder here: a single Skill does not constitute a moat. A Skill is essentially a process and rules described in natural language; it is plaintext and easily copied once placed on a platform; large language models can also be prompted to "spit out" a Skill's full content. In practice, a more feasible protection approach is to package the Skill with connectors and a proprietary knowledge base into a complete application, exposing only a usage interface or conversational interface, not the underlying Skill description directly.

Five Risks and Early Signals

The report condenses the risks in implementation into a table, and points out that all risks essentially point to the same question: is marginal cost being continuously reduced?

RiskEarly SignalResponse Focus
Degeneration into outsourcingCustomer keeps buying person-days, project cannot be reusedInclude Skills, templates, and test sets in deliverables
Unclear organizational ownershipSales, delivery, and product goals pulling against each otherEstablish dual metrics for customer value and knowledge accumulation
Talent bottleneckA few strong individuals firefighting long-termTiered development of Echo, Delta, and FDPM
Missing ontology layerEvery project re-interviews and re-models from scratchBuild an industry asset library and reuse mechanisms
Difficulty expandingDemo looks good but usage declinesExpand from IT to the business layer, prove value with metrics

The most insidious is the first, the vicious cycle of a missing ontology layer: when projects pile up and staffing is tight, the team prioritizes delivering to customers, and accumulation always comes last; without accumulation, the next project starts from zero again; efficiency drops, people get more exhausted, and there is even less time to accumulate. Once this cycle forms, FDE loses its scaling narrative.

The report deliberately clarifies the scope of this point: the "missing ontology layer" mentioned here does not mean that a complete ontology has yet to be built (that requires the preconditions of customer scale and deal size). It refers to a more fundamental problem: the team has not even done the most basic Skill accumulation, industry templates, or terminology standardization, and every delivery starts completely from scratch. This level of deficiency is more fatal than ordinary delivery problems, because it directly affects the business model: management sees that more people means more profit and fewer people means less profit; investors see something that cannot scale; customers see that a few particular engineers are capable, rather than that the platform itself has value.

VI. The Reality for Chinese Teams

The China-US differences are not just about market size

The report uses a table to break down the structural differences between the two markets:

DimensionUS MarketChina Market

But the differences go beyond the numbers. The report points out that many large US enterprises have accumulated years of processes, data, job division, and system governance, with relatively mature ERP, CRM, ITSM, and other systems, and data has a strong standardization foundation. AI is more like "installing an intelligent brain onto a precision machine." Many Chinese enterprises, by contrast, are more like "conversation-driven": requirements are often raised in unstructured ways, collaboration relies on instant communication, decision chains depend heavily on key stakeholders, and processes are often written not into systems but into tacit understanding and personal relationships.

This difference increases the difficulty of landing FDE on one hand, but also brings a leapfrogging opportunity on the other. Traditional software required filling in forms, drawing workflows, and configuring rules first; AI's entry point is natural language, and natural language is precisely the collaboration method such organizations are most familiar with. Many customers may not be able to map out their processes clearly before adopting a system, and are instead better suited to "running while building the road" with an FDE alongside.

Four premises that do not fully hold

The report conducts a sober feasibility review of the Palantir model. For this model to work, three conditions must hold simultaneously: a high-quality feedback loop between FDEs and product R&D, customer-site experience being absorbed by the platform, and platform capabilities in turn improving delivery efficiency for the next customer. Missing any link, FDE easily degenerates into high-end consulting.

However, several premises of Palantir's technical system do not fully hold in China:

  • Foundry is built on customers having accumulated years of structured data, while most domestic enterprises still lack standardized governance at the data foundation level; the ontology is not "extracted" from systems but must be "sorted out anew" within projects
  • Palantir's Ontology took over a decade and hundreds of dedicated engineers to refine; under the deal sizes and payment habits of the domestic 2B market, it is hard to replicate directly
  • Gotham's customers naturally have extremely high switching costs due to security clearance requirements, which does not exist in commercial scenarios
  • Palantir's acceptance rate is about 0.3%; this level of talent density is hard to replicate at scale

The report therefore suggests the strategy: domestic teams should not copy Palantir's full technology stack, but should grasp the core logic: establish a unified semantic layer between data and business, letting AI and FDEs operate on this semantic layer together, rather than directly facing raw systems. Use a lighter approach (Skills + connectors + industry knowledge bases) to gradually approximate the effect of the ontology layer.

The most realistic constraint is economics

The report characterizes the core domestic challenge as economics, not technology: high-value Silicon Valley customers can support annual contracts of six to seven figures in US dollars, while many domestic projects remain in the range of RMB 100K to 1M.

If FDE relies entirely on high-cost expert input, the math is hard to make work; if it is handed entirely to low-cost outsourcing, product feedback and experience accumulation are lost. Therefore, Chinese FDEs must solve two problems: first, use AI and platform capabilities to reduce delivery costs; second, use Skills, connectors, industry templates, and partner ecosystems to increase reuse rates. The report's judgment is: whoever solves this problem first may gain a structural advantage in China's enterprise services market.

VII. How to Use These Three Materials

The perspectives of the three materials barely overlap: one covers mechanisms, one covers the role and processes, and one covers how people inside it think. Each is explained below.

Tencent Research Institute's "FDE Model Industry Observation and Practice"

83 pages, 10 chapters, public commercial edition v5.0 released in July 2026. It is the mechanism perspective, answering "why FDE is heating up at the current stage, which structural problems of LLM deployment it solves, and whether it can scale." Its core contributions lie in three areas:

  • The role division of Echo / Delta / FDPM, along with the theoretical framework of bidirectional distillation and the ontology layer
  • Tencent Cloud's practice samples: the WorkBuddy → LearnBuddy incubation path in education, ISV collaboration in media, the full-chain practice of the CodeBuddy/WorkBuddy customer success team, and the "platform / tools / partners" three-layer enablement methodology
  • The complete operations manual in Chapter 9: using a fictional ERP industry case, it walks through the entire process of the Echo-Delta collaboration mechanism from cold start to scaled reuse—what each of the five stages should do, produce, how to assess, and how to distribute benefits—all at the operational level

Note its self-imposed limitations: Chapter 9 explicitly states it is "a fictional case for methodology illustration purposes" and "some mechanisms (such as the internal settlement model) are based on consulting designs; no complete public implementation case exists domestically or abroad"; Appendix A.3 also lists five research limitations (timeliness of recruitment data, limited domestic samples, differences in salary measurement, bias in publicly posted job samples, and the boundaries of Tencent Cloud practice content). Read these boundaries along with the content.

Fan Bing's "Forward Deployed Engineer (FDE): The Secret to Delivering Customer Value in the AI Era"

The publisher calls it "the first book to fully break down FDE." It is also one of the main sources for §2 and §4 of this article.

ItemContent

It answers three questions: What is an FDE? How to do it? Who is doing it? The book is divided into three parts.

Part One: The Origin and Development of the FDE (Chapter 1). Starting from the 95% figure from MIT's NANDA Lab in 2025, it tells how Palantir forged this playbook into a moat over two decades, then presents the FDE concept, responsibilities, and three layers of traits, along with the fourfold identity facing customers, product lines, sales, and the organization. The four cuts of "what it is not" in §2 are in this chapter, which also covers companies' specific requirements for hiring FDEs, the tech stack an individual needs to become an FDE, and a practical toolbox.

Part Two: The Full FDE Workflow in Practice (Chapters 2–7). It unfolds along the complete journey of a real delivery across six stages, each offering methods rather than principles:

Part Three: Case Studies from Home and Abroad (Chapter 8). It reviews the practice paths of different types of overseas companies such as Palantir, OpenAI, Anthropic, Harvey, Sierra, and Databricks/Snowflake, analyzes the attempts of major Chinese companies (Volcengine, Huawei) and early movers, provides comparison cases, and includes a 180-day hands-on retrospective from a Chinese AI startup team.

This book was adapted from an open-source project, and the author did four things for the published edition: polished it paragraph by paragraph and verified all details and data; improved the layout, redoing lists and headings by content hierarchy; added many visual charts; and substantially revised Chapter 8—compared with the open-source edition's case compilation, it streamlines descriptions and distills reusable lessons. The companion resources also add an FAQ on high-frequency FDE questions. So different uses call for different choices: for systematic learning, the published edition saves more time; for quickly building awareness, the full open-source text is enough.

The three parts I find most valuable:

  • The "what it is not" in Chapter 1: it separates the FDE from sales engineers, on-site outsourcing, consultants, and product engineers one by one. These four cuts build accurate understanding better than a positive definition, and the cut on "on-site outsourcing" in particular is essential for Chinese readers
  • The complete case collection in Chapter 8: the point is not enumeration but discerning which lessons can be replicated and which cannot be copied wholesale
  • The metrics system in Appendix A: divided into four groups—delivery, customer, business, and organization—it is one of the few attempts to turn "is the FDE doing well" into a measurable question

Jove Zhong, "I Was Reborn as an FDE in the AI Isekai"

The first two materials take a research perspective; this one is a first-person account from a frontline practitioner. The author, Jove Zhong, is Head of FDE at Cresta, a North American AI agent company; he previously spent four years as a cofounder of a streaming database startup, joined Cresta in August 2025, published this account in the Superlinear Academy community on September 28, and in December added a retrospective on his first three months.

Its value lies not in being systematic—by the author's own words, "this piece is not necessarily objective, and not authoritative at all"—but in providing data points. The three most worth reading:

  • The three differences from SE / SA (see §2): writing production code directly, being accountable for outcomes down to the last mile, and being a hybrid player. Closer to everyday judgment than the research perspective's "four things it is not"
  • First-hand numbers on time allocation (see §4): the two-way split, and the conclusion most striking to engineers—"writing code is only a third"
  • The judgment on the window of opportunity (see §3): "no AI, no FDE," "no one can afford to wait," and that biting metaphor about the current state of AI agents
