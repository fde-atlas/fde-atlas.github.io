FDE Interview & Selection Process | Typical Flow, Common Questions, and How to Prepare | FDE board

Summary

This FDE board article explains the typical hiring process for Forward Deployed Engineers and how to prepare. The most common failure, it argues, is preparing as if for a standard software engineering interview: FDE selection evaluates technical skill, problem decomposition, and customer-facing ability, including case-style interviews with ambiguous problems and role-play with a interviewer acting as a client. A typical flow runs from resume screening through technical screening, implementation or system design, case interviews, customer-facing rounds, and a final interview, often taking several weeks to about a month and a half. The recommended preparation is not new study material but an inventory of past projects—clarifying what was ambiguous, why designs were chosen, and how agreement was reached with customers.

Key points

  • FDE interviews assess technical skill, problem decomposition, and customer handling—not just coding.
  • Typical process spans several weeks to 1.5 months across about six stages.
  • Case interviews are the biggest pitfall: confirm assumptions and narrow scope before proposing solutions like RAG.
  • Best preparation is reviewing past projects, not grinding algorithm puzzles.
  • AI coding tool policies vary by company; confirm with recruiters beforehand.

The most common mistake in FDE selection is preparing the same way you would for a regular software engineer interview. Coding is certainly assessed, but that alone won't get you through. What distinguishes this role is the inclusion of interviews where you structure ambiguous problems and interviews simulating customer interactions. This article organizes the selection process as far as it can be confirmed from public information at the time of writing (2026-08), along with how to prepare.

The Bottom Line

  • FDE selection evaluates you in three directions: technical skill, problem decomposition ability, and customer-facing skills. Even if you can code, you won't pass if you can't explain how you would proceed when requirements are ambiguous.
  • The typical flow is: documents/casual interview → technical screening → implementation or system design → problem decomposition or case-format interview → customer-interaction interview → final interview. The number of rounds and their order vary by company.
  • The core of preparation is not new tactics but taking stock of your past projects. The fastest approach is to articulate, for each project, "what was ambiguous," "why you chose that design," and "how you reached agreement with the customer."

How Is This Different from a Regular Engineer Interview?

A regular software engineer interview focuses mainly on whether you can solve given problems correctly and quickly. The interviewer defines the problem for you. In FDE selection, that premise is removed. The problem itself is handed to you in an ambiguous state, and how you structure it and decide where to start is part of the evaluation.

The reason lies in the nature of the work. An FDE starts from a state where the customer can only say "we want to do something with AI," and is expected to verify available data, select business processes likely to yield results, and deliver something working in a short period. The interview is a miniature version of this.

Another difference is that communication is assessed as a mandatory requirement, not a bonus. In a regular engineer interview, you can sometimes pass with slightly clumsy explanations as long as your implementation is correct. In FDE selection, the clarity of your explanations and your attitude of confirming the other party's assumptions as you proceed are themselves evaluation criteria. Since this role involves facing customers, the ability to explain things only to internal colleagues is not enough.

The Typical Selection Flow

The structure varies by company and year, but summarizing the elements that commonly appear in candidate-facing guides and experience reports as of the time of writing (2026-08), the flow is roughly as follows. Domestic companies tend to have fewer rounds, while foreign AI companies tend to have more.

The overall duration is often a few weeks to about a month and a half. However, this is a trend gathered from publicly available candidate-side information, not something officially disclosed by companies. Please confirm the actual number of rounds and content directly with the recruiter at the company you're applying to.

What Coding Interviews Really Assess

FDE coding interviews are not necessarily in the format of solving difficult algorithm problems quickly. Rather, they often use practical, work-like material—for example, writing code that handles dirty data, or calling an API and assembling the results.

What's being evaluated is not just whether you reached the correct answer. Can you ask questions when requirements are missing? Can you point out weaknesses in your own code? Can you make the judgment to get to "something that works" within the time limit? In actual FDE work, delivering something the customer can touch by the deadline matters more than a perfect design. The interview is looking at that too.

Note that policies on using AI coding assistance tools vary by company. Some selection processes prohibit them, so it's safest to check in advance.

The Problem Decomposition Interview—Where Most People Fail

This is the distinctive part of FDE selection. You're given a broad question like "A company in this industry has this kind of problem. How would you proceed?" This format is known as a representative stage in Palantir's selection process, and AI companies often include similar interviews as well.

The typical way to fail is to start talking about solutions immediately. If you jump in with "let's build a RAG" without confirming assumptions, your evaluation drops right there. First, confirm who is struggling, how things currently run, what data actually exists, and how success will be measured. Then, deliberately narrow the scope and show concretely what you would deliver in the first two weeks. Whether you follow this order is what's being assessed.

For preparation, rather than memorizing anticipated Q&A, it's more effective to practice re-explaining projects you actually handled in this same format. If you can summarize verbally in three minutes what was ambiguous at the time, what you confirmed, and why you started with that scope, you'll be able to talk through unfamiliar material using the same framework.

Preparing for Customer-Interaction and Role-Play Formats

Some interviews include a format where the interviewer plays the customer, making requests or objecting to your proposals. Rather than testing how you handle difficult customers, you should assume they're assessing whether you can convey that something is technically infeasible while still moving things forward without making an enemy of the other party.

What works here is honesty. Don't claim you can do what you can't; say you don't know what you don't know and take it back to investigate; show what you can do instead. If you can do these three things naturally, that's usually sufficient. Conversely, evasive answers that paper over the moment cost you points. Since FDE work runs on customer trust, interviewers scrutinize this closely.

Whether English is required depends on the employer. At foreign-affiliated companies, it is common to speak Japanese with Japanese clients while using English internally, so part of the interview may be conducted in English. Be sure to check how English requirements are stated in the job posting before applying. Jobs currently open in Japan are compiled in the FDE board job listings.

How to prepare—start by taking inventory of your past projects

Rather than buying a new prep book, organizing your past projects is more effective. Choose three projects you worked on and write out the following items for each. The act of writing them out itself creates the language you will use in the interview.

In addition, prepare questions to ask the interviewer. Questions such as "What would be considered success six months after joining?", "What is the ratio of time spent at client sites versus working in-house?", and "Is there a path for feeding issues found at client sites back into the product?" demonstrate your level of understanding while also reducing mismatches after joining. In particular, the frequency of business trips and on-site assignments is often not stated in job postings, so we recommend confirming it during the selection process.

  • What was undecided at the start? Who did you ask about what to fill in the gaps?
  • How many options were there, and why did you choose that design? Which alternatives you discarded and why
  • How did you reach agreement with the client or the user department? How did you handle objections?
  • After going to production, was it actually used? If not, what was the cause?
  • Which parts did you write yourself? Can you distinguish between the team's results and your own contributions?

Frequently asked questions

  • How far should I go in preparing for algorithm problems?
  • You should have a grasp of basic data structures and algorithms, but in most cases you don't need to drill to competitive programming level. What matters more is being able to keep making progress on practical, work-like problems and being able to explain the weaknesses of the code you wrote yourself.
  • I have no experience dealing with clients. Will I fail the selection process?
  • It doesn't have to be an external client. Experience refining requirements with an internal user department, or explaining technical constraints to non-engineers and gaining their agreement, serves as proof of the same ability. Recall such situations and prepare them as concrete examples.
  • Am I allowed to use AI tools during the selection process?
  • Policies vary by company, and some selection processes explicitly prohibit them. Check with the recruiter in advance. Even when not prohibited, relying entirely on the tool and being unable to explain your own decisions will lower your evaluation.
  • If I am rejected, can I apply again?
  • At many companies, you can reapply after a certain waiting period. The period varies by company, so the surest way is to ask when you receive the rejection notice. Before your next application, gaining experience that addresses the stage where you failed will change your pass rate.
