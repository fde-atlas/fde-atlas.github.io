Is the FDE a thing of the past? Unveiling Palantir's next "super soldier": the full-stack engineer

Original title: FDE已成过去式？揭秘Palantir的下一个“超级士兵”：全栈工程师

网易版权所有 · 2025-09-09 · 17 min read

Summary

The author analyzes Palantir's newly promoted Full Stack Engineer (FSE) concept, arguing it is not a rebranding of FDE but a strategic move to internalize the FDE frontline ethos into product development. FSE is a subset of Dev, organized into three platform-based roles, emphasizing end-to-end ownership, cross-functional collaboration, and user-centricity. The article describes the Frontline rotation program that sends developers to customer sites, and offers Chinese IT service firms advice on hiring, culture, and incentives, while flagging structural constraints in China's talent supply, management habits, and client-vendor dynamics.

Key points

  • FSE is a subset of Dev, not a rebranded FDE
  • Three platform-based FSE roles stress end-to-end ownership
  • Frontline program rotates developers into customer sites
  • Chinese firms need changes in hiring, culture, incentives
  • China faces talent, management, and commercial constraints

Full text

In the tech world, concepts iterate faster than codebases. The birth of a new term always stirs up waves, drawing countless interpretations, hype, and of course, no shortage of skepticism. Just as the industry was still digesting the impact of FDE and gradually demystifying it, Palantir—known for its mystique and elitism—has thrown out another concept that seems familiar yet different: the "Full Stack Engineer" (FSE).

Is this just another case of "old wine in new bottles" marketing hype, or does it represent a brand-new way of thinking about the role and value of engineers? Drawing on Palantir's recently published article "Building End-to-End, Full Stack Engineering Experience at Palantir," I will try to interpret the talent "open scheme" behind the new term, as well as the lessons and reflections it offers for domestic IT service companies and engineers.

A rebranding of Dev, or the internalization of the FDE spirit?"

To FDE, Or FSE

To understand this FSE, one must first answer a sharp question: isn't this just their internal "developers" (DEV), who have always been building products, wearing a trendy new outfit?

The answer is: yes, but far more than that.

First, let's clarify a fundamental fact: Palantir doesn't only have FDEs; like other solution vendors and system integrators, it also has architects, designers, software development engineers, and many other technical roles. Broadly speaking, its business operations rely on two core technical forces:

  • One is the Forward Deployed Engineer, or FDE. They are the implementers of solutions. Like technical special forces, FDEs are deployed to customer sites, using the company's ready-made powerful arsenal—tool platforms such as Foundry, Apollo, and Gotham—to tailor solutions for specific customers and solve their most thorny business problems. Their battlefield is external, and their success criterion is solving customer problems.
  • The other is Dev, i.e., product development engineers, who belong to the Product Development (PD) team and focus on core product development and infrastructure building. To continue the military analogy, PD is the creator of the powerful arsenal that FDEs use. They are Palantir's core product R&D team, responsible for designing, building, iterating, and maintaining the Foundry and Gotham platforms themselves from scratch. Their battlefield is internal, and their success criterion is product excellence. The Full Stack Engineer mentioned in Palantir's latest article, judging by the role and job description, should be a subset of Dev.

Another analogy can illustrate the relationship between FDE and FSE: the FSE is the person who designs and builds the F1 race car, while the FDE is the driver and on-site engineer who races it to win specific competitions.

With this background understood, we can dig one level deeper into Palantir's "open scheme": why give its core "development" team the new title of "Full Stack Engineer"? There may be three possibilities:

Possibility one: the "internal transplant" of FDE's successful experience. FDE and Palantir's success are tightly bound together; from a fancy title to a widely celebrated model, packaging certainly played a part, but the market's recognition of FDE is surely still supported by culture-driven practice, and it has unique value. Given that product R&D organizations and personnel are relatively far from customers, with the resulting inertial risk of drifting away from customer value, Palantir may want to fully inject its FDE "frontline spirit" into the bloodstream of product R&D. They don't want their product developers to merely "write code based on product managers' requirement documents"; instead, they require them, like FDEs, to become "product creators" who deeply understand the essence of problems, possess an end-to-end sense of ownership, and are oriented toward end-user value. If the new title "Full Stack Engineer" is already being promoted internally, it may be that Palantir's management is using it as a lever to reshape the mindset and capability model of the development team, so as to align end-to-end with customer value across its business.

Possibility two: a "brand upgrade" in the battle for top talent. In Silicon Valley, the war for talent is often fiercer than product competition; Meta's sky-high poaching from OpenAI this year is an extreme example. "Software Engineer" or "Developer" is a generic, almost "code monkey" title that evokes no feeling, whereas "Full Stack Engineer" is undoubtedly a more attractive name that gives its holder greater professional pride. It sends top talent a strong signal: here, you are not a "code monkey" tightening screws in a vast machine; you are a "mini CTO" granted enormous trust and autonomy, with full ownership from understanding the problem to creating the solution. For top engineers who crave massive impact, this is undoubtedly highly appealing.

Possibility three: the "redefinition" of an industry buzzword. FSE is not an entirely new term. The concept of Full-Stack Developer—and even the role and position—has long existed, and there have long been discussions of Full Stack Engineer as well. But as the original article notes, the traditional FSE is defined from a technical angle of being proficient in both front-end and back-end, whereas Palantir, drawing on its own practice, has given it a new meaning: elevating the concept of "full stack" from the "tech stack" to the "business stack." Palantir's "full stack" means: you are responsible for the entire lifecycle of a problem—from business user interviews, requirement definition, architecture design, coding and implementation, testing and evals, deployment and launch, to subsequent iteration, and even value realization, you need to be deeply involved and in control. This is both a high-profile showcase of its own elite culture and an attempt to seize the final right to interpret the term "full stack."

Therefore, "Full Stack Engineer" is not merely a repackaging of Dev. Perhaps FSE is more like a strategic move by Palantir to systematically "internalize" the success principles of FDE into its product R&D system. It is not the invention of a new position, but a philosophical elevation of the core development team.

The real stories of six "mini CTOs"

Tell Your Stories

Abstract definitions are always pale; through the personal experiences of several engineers, Palantir's article paints a vivid picture. They are not cogs on an assembly line, but rather "mini CTOs" each entrusted with weighty responsibilities.

Joe: An explorer seeking value in ambiguity

Joe joined Palantir's London office in 2018, and he admits that the work state he enjoys most is an environment rich in information but unclear in instructions. This may sound unbelievable—most engineers expect clear requirements and well-defined tasks. But Joe's joy lies precisely in the fact that no one directly tells him "do this" or "do that." His desk is piled with all kinds of information: customer feedback, data reports, fragments of team discussions. His task is to keenly discern from these complex signals what is most effective and critical for improving the product, then step up proactively and take on that unassigned work. This requires not only technical ability, but also deep insight, independent judgment, and strong inner drive. He is like a captain navigating through fog, relying on compass and stars to chart the right course for the team. One might say that Joe's way of working is a perfect reproduction, on the product R&D side, of the FDE's ability to find key problems in ambiguous information and define the direction of battle.

Philip and Tim: Transformers from real problems to product value

Philip joined in 2017, and his motivation is very pure: "play to my strengths, take ownership, and create great products." Tim, an early engineer who joined in 2013, turned that passion into something concrete: "solve real problems in the here and now, and turn them into products." Together, their stories reveal the core of Palantir's "full-stack engineer" role — problem-oriented, value-driven. Their work never starts from a technical framework or a trendy algorithm, but from a specific, thorny pain point a customer encounters in the real world. The original text describes FSE in exactly the same way as FDE: they go deep into the front lines, understand the full context of a problem, then return to the product with that still-warm, firsthand experience, thinking about how to use technology to turn a temporary solution into a reusable, scalable product feature, thereby creating value for a broader user base. This process is a complete closed loop from 0 to 1, and then from 1 to N. Tim's passion clearly shows how FSE plays the bridge role: forging the "raw battlefield reports" (real problems) that FDEs bring back from the front lines into "standard-issue weapons" (product features) that all FDEs can use.

Rory: A bottom-up influencer driving change

Rory joined the company in 2019, and what he values most is Palantir's culture. He says that at Palantir, engineers have ample space and freedom to express "what should be built and why," and this is not just empty talk. At many large companies, product direction is decided by executives or product managers, and engineers are mostly executors. But at Palantir, Rory and his colleagues are encouraged to become contributors of ideas and drivers of change. They can, and should, challenge the existing roadmap based on their own understanding of users and technology, and propose bolder visions. Of course, their opinions are not always adopted, but this open, equal, debate-encouraging atmosphere is the soil in which the "full-stack engineer" plays their "mini-CTO" role. It requires engineers to have not only technical depth, but also clear logic, the ability to persuade others, and the resilience to make things happen.

Seth: A newcomer who is highly trusted and grows fast

Seth only joined the New York office in 2023, and as a newcomer, his experience is enough to surprise many of his peers. He recalls that the level of trust he felt here far exceeded that of any company he had worked at before. Usually, newcomers must go through a long training and observation period before they can touch core systems. But at Palantir, engineers are given enormous autonomy from day one. Seth and his colleagues are empowered to manage their own product areas end to end, which means they have great flexibility and decision-making power in everything from technology selection and architecture design to final implementation. Behind this "hire with trust" culture is high confidence in people's abilities — and, in turn, an extremely high hiring bar. It sends a clear signal: the team trusts you, so go ahead and create.

These six stories together paint a complete picture of Palantir's "full-stack engineer": they are proactive value discoverers, bridges connecting the real world and the product world, change drivers who dare to speak up, and fully trusted and empowered decision-makers. They participate in the entire project lifecycle, from scoping, planning, and design to building, merging multiple traditional roles — product manager, architect, and development engineer — into one.

Beyond the article: FSE is not mysterious, but it is not simple either

Simple, Yet Powerful

In fact, after reading the original text, the author was somewhat confused about the roles and boundaries of FSE and FDE. For example,

  • Phillip described rebuilding the alerting and triage system in a customer's hosted IT infrastructure; he and a few core engineers worked with users to design the solution, then delivered product iterations quickly.
  • Tim mentioned the typical FDE work environment of switching within short periods of time / multi-threading across vastly different industries such as hospitals, rocket manufacturing supply chains, and tax fraud.
  • Joe's mention of "going to the customer site to talk directly with users, then returning to the hotel, delivering the features they asked for, and demoing them to them the next day" is also a description of the typical FDE working state.

At first glance, FSE seems to be just Palantir's rebranding of FDE — or is there some kind of functional synergy or overlap between Palantir's product developers and FDEs in the field?

Actually, no. Finding the answer is not hard (today's AI still can't automatically plan and research this out — you still need some basic desk-research thinking, manually breaking things down and trying them yourself). Currently, Palantir's FSE positions are basically divided by platform product, into three categories, clearly distinguished from FDE, and there are still parallel Software Engineer positions. The responsibilities of the three FSE categories differ somewhat, but are broadly the same:

Mission Manager (a new product) Full-Stack Software Engineer

  • Work closely with FDEs, platform and customer engineers to understand user workflows, product gaps, and define short-term and long-term product features
  • Collaborate with product designers and customers to develop the concepts, features, workflows, and designs of our platform
  • Support the long-term technical roadmap to build high-quality application interactions, polished and reusable user experiences, reduce operational burden, ensure scalability, and mitigate risk
  • Own the end-to-end user experience across the entire stack, including debugging core backend systems and driving API improvements, with a focus on quality and performance
  • Work closely with your team and stakeholders to deliver technical solutions that meet business needs in a sustainable and timely manner

Apollo Full-Stack Software Engineer

  • Collaborate with product designers and internal and external customers to develop and refine Apollo's overall look, design, and functionality
  • Develop and grow the core libraries and components used to render user interfaces and build backend services
  • Define and implement APIs that support frontend functionality and provide backend services for our platform
  • Make end-to-end contributions to both frontend and backend codebases to achieve business outcomes
  • Regularly test and debug web applications and backend services, both manually and with automation, to maintain high quality and high performance

Application Development Full-Stack Software Engineer

  • As a member of the engineering community, build shared frontend tooling to support Foundry teams
  • Design and build large-scale, data-intensive APIs, among other things
  • Develop interactive workflow UIs
  • Build products designed to enable non-technical users to understand technical concepts
  • Create low-code/no-code WYSIWYG tools that enable application builders within Foundry to build products for their users

As you can see, Palantir requires its FSEs to be not just implementers of code, but co-builders of the product. They need end-to-end problem-solving ability, deeply involved in everything from understanding user needs and participating in product design, to frontend and backend development and API building, to final quality assurance. Strong cross-team collaboration skills and user-centric product thinking are the foundation of all the roles. The common requirements across the three positions include:

  • End-to-end full-stack ownership: engineers are explicitly required to own the entire technology stack, from the frontend user interface to core backend systems, with responsibilities spanning the entire software development lifecycle from concept to delivery.
  • Close cross-functional collaboration: all emphasize close cooperation with product designers, customers, and other engineering teams (FDE, platform engineers, etc.).
  • User- and product-centricity: all roles place understanding user workflows, solving user pain points, and improving user experience at the core.
  • Core API design and development: building and optimizing APIs is the key to connecting frontend and backend, requiring engineers to consider not only functionality but also API performance, scalability, and ease of use.
  • High quality and high performance standards: a strong focus on quality, performance, and scalability. Ensuring product excellence through debugging, testing (automated and manual), and building robust systems.

Of course, the three roles each have their own focus. The full-stack role for new products emphasizes product exploration and strategic building from 0 to 1, the mature Apollo focuses more on polishing the platform's appearance, experience, and core components, while the more general application-development full-stack role is inherently about "empowerment"—that is, "building tools for developers." Overall, however, we can see that the FSE is not simply a DEV; it requires more comprehensive capabilities—and these capabilities lie not, or not only, in technical breadth, but rather in emphasizing communication, understanding, and collaboration with internal and external users. So, borrowing the FDE special-forces analogy, the FSE is the special forces of product development.

Here I also want to debunk some myths about Chinese companies imitating Palantir. Some self-media may have overestimated or underestimated Palantir's practices and model, misleading business managers. For example, some believe Palantir's ontology technology is worthless, outdated and useless, while others believe the ontology has some mystical power that allows a team to hunker down for a hundred days of intensive closed-door development and produce from scratch a universal killer weapon that works everywhere. As for the FDE, some rush to copy this excellent model wholesale, while others insist "whatever others can do, we Chinese can do too" (that dates me, but it's only to illustrate a phenomenon, with no disrespect to the older generation of scientists)—yet few bother to explore the business model, product DNA, and cultural foundation that match the FDE.

So let's return to the FSE and see how Palantir passes the FDE spirit down to the FSE, which may also help dispel the myths above. Palantir doesn't just shout slogans; it has a clear frontline rotation mechanism, internally known as the Frontline Program. In this program, development engineers are sent to customer sites, where, on real projects, they learn how the products they are or will be developing are actually used and what challenges customers face. Like FDEs—and perhaps shoulder to shoulder with them—they immerse themselves in complex and ambiguous problem environments, discussing technical architecture with customers, running business workshops, setting strategy with executives, witnessing firsthand how customers interact with their products, personally experiencing customer pain points, and gaining first-hand feedback and ideas for solving problems on the customer's factory floor. After the frontline rotation ends, the FSE returns to their original role to continue development.

Whether it's the FSE or the FDE, what matters most is resource allocation and execution; personnel screening at entry and cultural drivers are, of course, even more fundamental, and I will discuss this later in the article. In any case, it's not one radish filling eight holes, let alone one master with eight onlookers.

What Can Local IT Service Companies Learn from Palantir?

What to Learn

For the many IT solutions and systems integration service companies in China, Palantir's "full-stack engineer" model is undoubtedly like a lighthouse, pointing the way toward improving delivery efficiency and customer value. However, simply copying a job title is meaningless; the real task is to think about how to inject the "ownership of problems" culture into one's own R&D and delivery systems. This means that, beyond special programs like Frontline and the related cost and performance mechanisms mentioned earlier, the key lies in learning the talent philosophy behind it and transforming one's own organizational soil.

First, change must start at the entry point for talent—with the philosophy. The focus of recruiting and training must shift from finding "obedient executors" to discovering and empowering "problem solvers with ideas." We need to go beyond the traditional "technical interview": in interviews, carefully designed open-ended business scenario questions should be used to assess candidates' ability to break down complex problems, think in a structured way, and propose innovative solutions. We should look for "T-shaped talent" with strong curiosity about business, product thinking, and a strong sense of ownership, rather than "I-shaped talent" who merely check boxes on a technical list. Soft skills—such as communication, learning agility, and resilience under pressure—should be given equal weight with hard skills.

Second, the more arduous task lies in reshaping the organization and culture. We must tear down the walls between R&D teams, business teams, and delivery teams. R&D teams must not become a closed "resource pool" that passively receives requirements. Mechanisms and opportunities must be created for R&D engineers to face customers directly, to "hear the gunfire," to hear users' complaints and praise with their own ears, and to see with their own eyes how the code they write runs in real scenarios. Companies can try building cross-functional "virtual squads" around key customers or major projects, tightly binding together roles such as pre-sales, R&D, delivery, and operations, sharing goals and risks together. At the same time, effective mentorship systems and knowledge-sharing mechanisms should be established, so that seasoned "problem-solving experts" can pass down their experience and methodology, allowing the team to grow together through real-world practice.

Finally, all of these changes need to be supported by career development paths and incentive mechanisms. Companies need to design an attractive career track for such versatile talent, showing them that beyond becoming a technical expert, they can also grow into more influential roles such as Solutions Architect or technical product owner. More importantly, companies must dare to delegate, handing part of project decision-making authority to frontline teams within a controllable scope, letting them learn how to "be their own masters" through practice. Correspondingly, compensation and bonus systems should also be adjusted—not based merely on lines of code or completed feature points, but tied more closely to project success, customer satisfaction, and the business value ultimately created.

Reflection: Between Myth and Reality, Where Does Our Path Lie?

Myths and Misconceptions

Returning to the question at the start of this article: is the "full-stack engineer" an entirely new role, or is it Palantir's beautification and packaging of its elite culture?

The answer is probably both. In essence, this "craftsman" role combining product, design, and development is not a new species; it is more like a "throwback" and tribute to the image of the excellent engineer from the early days of software engineering. It's just that in today's industry of ever-finer division of labor, Palantir's approach is, to some extent, "counter-division-of-labor": it attempts to reintegrate, within its core product R&D team, the capabilities that have been split apart—essentially requiring its "car-building engineers" to possess the mindset and even the experience of a "race car driver" in order to tackle extremely complex customer problems. This undoubtedly places extremely high demands on talent quality, but Palantir, through its unique culture and business model, has scaled and realized this idealized role at relatively low cost and in a systematic way. It is both a true portrait of its team and culture, and carries a carefully crafted aura of "myth."

However, to make such a "myth" shine into reality at scale is enormously challenging, especially in the current environment of enterprise digitalization in China.

First, China faces a structural problem on the talent supply side. The current promotion and training systems for enterprise digitalization talent favor "specialists" over "generalists"; most software or IT engineers have their career paths confined from the very beginning to narrow tracks such as front-end or back-end, lacking opportunities and awareness for cross-domain development. Second, the inertia of corporate culture and management models poses enormous resistance. Many Chinese IT companies, especially project-based service firms, remain accustomed to hierarchical, directive-driven management. Truly empowering managers to delegate and giving engineers sufficient trust and room for trial and error requires a disruptive shift in mindset. Finally, the current state of the business environment and customer relationships is also a constraint. Under the traditional "Party A and Party B" relationship, customers prefer to pay for clearly defined feature lists and person-days of effort rather than for the process of "jointly exploring and solving problems," making it difficult for engineers to gain the soil to go deep into the business and influence decisions.

Even so, Palantir's "full-stack engineer" philosophy still offers valuable inspiration for Chinese software companies. Perhaps we won't suddenly see many Palantir-style super-successful companies spanning domains and industries, nor can we fully replicate its model in the short term. But what matters is that it reminds the industry to return to the essence of technology: the ultimate value of technology lies in solving people's problems.

For enterprises, this means deliberately creating an environment that better unlocks individual potential, encouraging engineers to "look up at the road" rather than just "keep their heads down pulling the cart." For every engineer, it means maintaining an ever-burning curiosity, proactively understanding the business, thinking about the "why" behind the technology, and striving to grow from a "code implementer" into a more well-rounded "problem solver."

This road is destined to be long, but every attempt and effort in this direction will bring us one step closer to creating real value.
